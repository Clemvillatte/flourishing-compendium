Early Buddhist discourse, with a narrative frame · Samyutta Nikaya 7.2, in Thanissaro Bhikkhu's translation, cross-read with Bhikkhu Sujato's.

Near Rajagaha the brahman Bharadvaja hears that a man of his own clan has gone forth to follow the Buddha. He is angry. He walks to the Bamboo Grove, finds the Buddha and insults and curses him with rude, harsh words ([Thanissaro, SN 7.2](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn07/sn07.002.than.html)). The sutta names him Akkosaka, "Insulter," which Sujato renders "the Rude" ([Sujato, SN 7.2](https://suttacentral.net/sn7.2/en/sujato)).

The Buddha does not return the insult. He asks the man about guests: do relatives and friends sometimes come to your house, and do you serve them food? Yes. "And if they don't accept them, to whom do those foods belong?" The food is still the host's. Then the Buddha says that what the brahman has thrown at him he does not accept: "It's all yours, brahman. It's all yours" ([Thanissaro](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn07/sn07.002.than.html)). Whoever returns an insult, he adds, is "eating together" with the insulter. Sujato has the same image: "it still belongs to you" ([Sujato](https://suttacentral.net/sn7.2/en/sujato)).

Bharadvaja objects: the king and his court say Gotama is an arahant, and yet he is angry. Thanissaro's note says the man thinks the Buddha is cursing him, when the Buddha is simply stating how the law of kamma works. The Buddha answers in verse: "You make things worse when you flare up at someone who's angry. Whoever doesn't flare up at someone who's angry wins a battle hard to win" ([Thanissaro](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn07/sn07.002.than.html)).

The brahman then takes refuge and asks to go forth. After some time alone and diligent, the sutta says, he becomes an arahant. In Sujato's version he is "no longer called 'the Rude'" ([Sujato](https://suttacentral.net/sn7.2/en/sujato)).

The compendium reads this beside Dhammapada 3-5. The verse says hatred does not end through hatred. The scene shows one way hatred ends: the insult has nobody to catch it. The anger Bharadvaja arrived with belongs to him, and he is left holding it until his own question answers it.

Limits: the sutta describes how a Buddha handles abuse, with a calm already in place, and it gives no practice for an angry person who has no such calm. The ending is the tradition's own, an arahant in a short time, and a modern reader may take it as devotional shorthand.

**Principles:**
- [[B117 — Don't hold the coal — anger returns to harm the holder]]: the refused gift leaves the insult with the person who offered it.
- [[B162 — Hatred never ceases by hatred]]: the Buddha does not return the abuse, and the exchange ends in a request for ordination.
