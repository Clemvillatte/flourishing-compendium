Traditional Chan account · ninth-century China; Dōgen, Shin Fukatoku (1241) and Shinji Shōbōgenzō, Book 2, kōan 4; Wu, The Golden Age of Zen (1967)

A scholar who had mastered the Diamond Sutra could not answer one question from a rice-cake seller ([Shin Fukatoku, pp. 189-190](https://www.thezensite.com/ZenTeachings/Dogen_Teachings/Shobogenzo/017shinFukatoku.pdf)). The scripture he carried says the mind of the past, the present and the future cannot be held onto; which mind, she asked, do you propose to refresh with my cakes? ([Shin Fukatoku, pp. 189-190](https://www.thezensite.com/ZenTeachings/Dogen_Teachings/Shobogenzo/017shinFukatoku.pdf)) Deshan Xuanjian (Tokusan in the Japanese rendering the sources use), the Diamond Sutra lecturer, later burned his commentaries ([Shinji Shōbōgenzō, kōan 4](https://www.thezensite.com/ZenTeachings/Dogen_Teachings/Shobogenzo/017shinFukatoku.pdf)).

Deshan was heading south to challenge a teaching he had heard was "bragging of pointing directly at the mind of man" ([Wu, pp. 150-154](https://terebess.hu/zen/deshan.html)), carrying his commentaries. Resting by the road, he met an old woman selling pastries that in Chinese "were, and still are, called 'mind-refreshers'" ([Wu, pp. 150-154](https://terebess.hu/zen/deshan.html)). Asked what he carried, he answered: "I am the Fully Perfected Lord of the Diamond-Cutting Scripture. I have so mastered this Scripture that there is nothing in It that I do not understand." ([Shin Fukatoku, pp. 189-190](https://www.thezensite.com/ZenTeachings/Dogen_Teachings/Shobogenzo/017shinFukatoku.pdf)) Quoting back his own scripture, that the mind of the past, present and future cannot be held onto, she asked: "Which mind do you think you will refresh with these rice cakes? If the reverend monk is able to say, I will sell you some rice cakes. If the reverend monk is unable to say, I will not sell you any." ([Shin Fukatoku, pp. 189-190](https://www.thezensite.com/ZenTeachings/Dogen_Teachings/Shobogenzo/017shinFukatoku.pdf)) Dōgen reports that Tokusan "was so flabbergasted that he did not recall how he would have customarily responded"; she "dismissed him with a flick of her wrist" and sold him nothing ([Shin Fukatoku, p. 190](https://www.thezensite.com/ZenTeachings/Dogen_Teachings/Shobogenzo/017shinFukatoku.pdf)).

He continued toward the master Longtan, called Ryutan in Japanese. The night scenes come from a second text of Dōgen's, the Shinji Shōbōgenzō: Deshan received a paper candle from his teacher, and "Just as Tokusan touched it, Ryūtan blew out the flame, whereupon Tokusan suddenly had a great awakening and immediately bowed in respect" ([Shinji Shōbōgenzō, kōan 4, in the fascicle's addendum](https://www.thezensite.com/ZenTeachings/Dogen_Teachings/Shobogenzo/017shinFukatoku.pdf)). Afterwards he burned his manuscripts before the Dharma Hall, saying all his penetration of obscure speech had been "like sending a single hair into the great void of space" ([Shinji Shōbōgenzō, kōan 4](https://www.thezensite.com/ZenTeachings/Dogen_Teachings/Shobogenzo/017shinFukatoku.pdf)).

The compendium's reading for B129: her question bypassed the commentaries he carried and asked about the mind he proposed to refresh, which they could not answer. The candle scene repeats the point as an event, in the dark, as his hand reaches for light. Dōgen's own caution marks its limit: "To merely see the blowing out of a paper candle is insufficient for the Transmission of the Torch" ([Shin Fukatoku, p. 192](https://www.thezensite.com/ZenTeachings/Dogen_Teachings/Shobogenzo/017shinFukatoku.pdf)).

**Principles:**
- [[B129 — Point directly at experience|B129 Point directly at experience]]: the seller's question asks which mind Deshan proposes to refresh, which his mastery of the text cannot answer.
