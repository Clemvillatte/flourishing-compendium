*United States, 1958 · Leonard Read's essay "I, Pencil", first published in The Freeman*

In December 1958 Leonard Read published a short essay narrated by an ordinary wooden pencil. It is a family tree, told from inside the family, and it opens with a confession of mystery:

> I am a lead pencil—the ordinary wooden pencil familiar to all boys and girls and adults who can read and write. ... Simple? Yet, not a single person on the face of this earth knows how to make me.

The pencil then walks through its ancestry. The graphite is mined in Ceylon. The logger is in Oregon, the chemist at the factory, the worker in the oil field, since paraffin is a by-product of petroleum. The chain keeps widening: the logger drinks coffee, so the picker of a coffee berry in far-off Brazil belongs to the tree too. Read's count: "millions of human beings have had a hand in my creation, no one of whom even knows more than a very few of the others." Not even the president of the pencil company holds the design, because "there isn't a single person in all these millions... who contributes more than a tiny, infinitesimal bit of know-how."

The essay's point is not a policy argument. The point is that the pencil exists, and no one made it. Nobody owns the blueprint because there isn't one. Thousands of local tasks, each understood by the person doing it, linked up into an object no single mind contains. The essay closes on wonder rather than policy, quoting Chesterton: "We are perishing for want of wonder, not for want of wonders." The pencil asks to be looked at slowly: the order is real, it feeds you, and it never needed a director.
