*United States, 1958 · Leonard Read's essay "I, Pencil", first published in The Freeman*

In December 1958 Leonard Read published a short essay narrated by an ordinary wooden pencil. It is a family tree, told from inside the family, and it opens by presenting itself as a mystery:

> I am a lead pencil—the ordinary wooden pencil familiar to all boys and girls and adults who can read and write. ... Simple? Yet, not a single person on the face of this earth knows how to make me.

The pencil then walks through its ancestry. The graphite is mined in Ceylon. The logger is in Oregon, the chemist at the factory, the worker in the oil field, since paraffin is a by-product of petroleum. The chain keeps widening: the logger drinks coffee, so the picker of a coffee berry in far-off Brazil belongs to the tree too. Read's count: "millions of human beings have had a hand in my creation, no one of whom even knows more than a very few of the others." The essay's claim is about knowledge, not about the absence of makers: "There isn't a single person in all these millions... who contributes more than a tiny, infinitesimal bit of know-how." No one person holds the whole of how a pencil comes to be.

Read's own point was political, and he said so: the essay argues that becoming "aware of the miraculousness which I symbolize, you can help save the freedom mankind is so unhappily losing," and its "fact still more astounding" is "the absence of a master mind, of anyone dictating or forcibly directing these countless actions which bring me into being." The compendium's use of the essay is a cross-tradition analogy rather than that argument. The pencil is a worked example of an order nobody commands: thousands of local tasks, each understood by the person doing it, linked into an object whose making no single mind contains. The compendium pairs it with the Daoist trust in emergent order and with process philosophy's expectation that wholes exceed their parts.
