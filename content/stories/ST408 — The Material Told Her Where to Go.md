Biographical account · Barbara McClintock (1902-1992), maize geneticist at Cold Spring Harbor, via Evelyn Fox Keller's biography, a 2024 history-of-science study and the National Library of Medicine's profile.

In 1944, the year she was elected to the National Academy of Sciences, McClintock began the experiments that led to transposition ([Keller, excerpt p. 4](https://epistemologies.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2007/10/a-feeling-for-the-organism-comp.pdf)). Geneticists in the 1950s accepted that some elements could move, but treated the events as random and meaningless, and control was not appreciated ([Wu 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11393105/)). McClintock watched them instead, and the watching had a method Keller records: one must have "the time to look, the patience to 'hear what the material has to say to you,' the openness to 'let it come to you'" ([Keller, p. 198](https://epistemologies.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2007/10/a-feeling-for-the-organism-comp.pdf)). In her own words: "The material just tells you where to go. The maize cases were so spectacular that I just had to follow them" ([Wu 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11393105/)).

The skill stayed; the expectation went. She followed each plant from seedling on: "I don't feel I really know the story if I don't watch the plant all the way along. So I know every plant in the field. I know them intimately" ([Keller, p. 199](https://epistemologies.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2007/10/a-feeling-for-the-organism-comp.pdf)). "No two plants are exactly alike. They're all different, and as a consequence, you have to know that difference" ([Keller, p. 198](https://epistemologies.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2007/10/a-feeling-for-the-organism-comp.pdf)). She distinguished kernels with spots from those with blotches, and recognized different systems in them ([Wu 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11393105/)).

What she found was that the controlling elements did not occupy stable chromosomal loci; they moved, and she called the capacity to change position "transposition" ([Keller, excerpt p. 9](https://epistemologies.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2007/10/a-feeling-for-the-organism-comp.pdf)). When she made these ideas public in 1951, 1953 and 1956, "few listened, and fewer understood," and she was described as "obscure," even "mad" ([Keller, excerpt p. 10](https://epistemologies.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2007/10/a-feeling-for-the-organism-comp.pdf)). Attendees at the 1951 symposium later recalled that "it was the control theory, not transposition per se, that they could not understand" ([Comfort 1999, quoted in Wu 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11393105/)). In a 1978 interview she said: "I never gave another seminar, and haven't. Since 1960. On that aspect of my work" ([Wu 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11393105/)). In notes for her Nobel acceptance speech she answered the question directly: "If they had had the same fantastic experiences with the maize plant as I was having, they would have drawn the same conclusion. The evidence and logic were incontestable" ([NLM, Profiles in Science](https://spotlight.nlm.nih.gov/spotlight/ll/feature/nobel)). The prize came in 1983, "for her discovery of mobile genetic elements" ([NobelPrize.org](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1983/mcclintock/facts/)), when she was 81, the first woman to receive an unshared Nobel in that category ([NLM, Profiles in Science](https://spotlight.nlm.nih.gov/spotlight/ll/feature/nobel)).

The connection with D045 is receptiveness within expertise: knowing a material well need not mean deciding in advance what it can show. The deviations were there for everyone; hers was the cup that let them arrive as news.
