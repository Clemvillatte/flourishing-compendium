Alabama, 1880-1903 · Helen Keller, The Story of My Life (1903), her account with letters from her teacher Anne Sullivan · an autobiography, Project Gutenberg raw text

Deaf and blind from infancy, Helen Keller did her thinking through touch, vibration and muscle sense, and her 1903 autobiography records that fact in detail. The book is a record of a body that is not a broken machine, with missing inputs to be replaced, but a whole that reorganized itself as the medium of her perception, her speech and her contact with other minds. ([The Story of My Life](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2397/pg2397.txt))

Learning to speak, she read her teacher's lips with her fingers ([The Story of My Life](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2397/pg2397.txt)):

> "In reading my teacher's lips I was wholly dependent on my fingers: I had to use the sense of touch in catching the vibrations of the throat, the movements of the mouth and the expression of the face; and often this sense was at fault."

> "In such cases I was forced to repeat the words or sentences, sometimes for hours, until I felt the proper ring in my own voice."

The same body took in the world at large. At Niagara she did not see the falls ([The Story of My Life](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2397/pg2397.txt)):

> "It is difficult to describe my emotions when I stood on the point which overhangs the American Falls and felt the air vibrate and the earth tremble."

Her teacher's letters describe the same organization of the whole body ([The Story of My Life](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2397/pg2397.txt)):

> "Indeed, her whole body is so finely organized that she seems to use it as a medium for bringing herself into closer relations with her fellow creatures."

> "She has learned to connect certain movements of the body with anger, others with joy, and others still with sorrow."

A later account adds the bodily pleasure she takes in other people's voices ([The Story of My Life](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2397/pg2397.txt)):

> "Sometimes she puts her hand on a singer's throat to feel the muscular thrill and contraction, and from this she gets genuine pleasure."

My reading: P143, drawing on Bergson, places the body between the matter that influences it and the matter it acts on, a center of action rather than a representation-maker behind the eyes. Keller's autobiography is that picture lived out at the limit. Incoming impressions arrive as touch, and they become movements: throat vibrations caught by the fingers become her own speech, corrected "until I felt the proper ring in my own voice." Her contact with other people runs the same route, through hands, clothing, floor vibrations and the play of muscles, which she reads closely enough to ask, when a boy's thrown torpedo startled her mother, "What are we afraid of?" Nothing in the book presents the body as a vehicle she pilots; the thinking happens in the touching. The story does not carry Merleau-Ponty's lived-body theory or the interoception research the principle page lists as parallels; it carries the plain record of one body doing the work that sight and hearing do for others.
