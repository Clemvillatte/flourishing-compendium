Phrygia and Lydia, mythic time · Ovid, Metamorphoses 11 · a Greek myth retold by a Roman poet, Brookes More translation

King Midas of Phrygia was given his choice of reward by a god, and chose the thing he had always valued most. The wish was granted completely, and it ruined the conditions of his life in a single day. Ovid's version of the myth is the sharpest early statement of what P080 claims: value is not in the thing desired but in what acting on it actually brings, and the test only runs once the consequences ensue. ([Ovid, Metamorphoses 11](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidMetamorphoses11.html))

The set-up: Midas had returned the god Bacchus's foster-father Silenus after ten days of hospitality, and Bacchus "allowed the king to choose his own reward, a welcome offer, but it led to harm" ([Ovid, Metamorphoses 11](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidMetamorphoses11.html)). The wish, and the granting ([Ovid, Metamorphoses 11](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidMetamorphoses11.html)):

> "Cause whatsoever I shall touch to change at once to yellow gold."

> "Bacchus agreed to his unfortunate request, with grief that Midas chose for harm and not for good."

The test begins at the table ([Ovid, Metamorphoses 11](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidMetamorphoses11.html)):

> "but when he touched the gift of Ceres with his right hand, instantly the gift of Ceres stiffened to gold; or if he tried to bite with hungry teeth a tender bit of meat, the dainty, as his teeth but touched it, shone at once with yellow shreds and flakes of gold. And wine, another gift of Bacchus, when he mixed it in pure water, can be seen in his astonished mouth as liquid gold."

The observation matures the valuation. Midas, starving in the middle of his treasure, prays ([Ovid, Metamorphoses 11](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidMetamorphoses11.html)):

> "Oh pardon me, father Lenaeus! I have done wrong, but pity me, I pray, and save me from this curse that looked so fair."

Bacchus sends him to wash in the stream near Sardis, and the myth closes with the river itself carrying the residue ([Ovid, Metamorphoses 11](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidMetamorphoses11.html)):

> "And even to this hour adjoining fields, touched by this ancient vein of gold, are hardened where the river flows and colored with the gold that Midas left."

My reading: the myth runs Dewey's test in miniature. Gold was Midas's valuation of ends-in-view; the wish is the valuation converted into a policy; the meal is the observation of results; the plea is the valuation matured by consequences that actually ensued, in Dewey's phrase. The myth's details are chosen for the flourishing point: what stiffens in Midas's hand is the gift of Ceres, bread, and what fills his mouth is Bacchus's gift turned to liquid gold. The things of value were never the metal; they were the eating and drinking it prevented. Ovid underlines the diagnosis later in the same book, when Midas alone misjudges a music contest and "blaming Tmolus called the award unjust": the man whose valuations keep failing the test. Read alongside P080, the story carries the test-by-consequences whole; it does not carry the inquiry that precedes good valuation, since Midas chooses his reward without examination.

**Principles:**
- [[P080 — Value is what fulfills conditions of flourishing|80. Value is what fulfills conditions of flourishing]] — the wish tests gold as a valuation, the meal is the observation of consequences, and the plea is the valuation matured by them; the conditions of flourishing, bread and wine, are what the gold destroys
