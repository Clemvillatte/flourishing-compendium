*Meiji-era Japan, 1882 · the founding of judo by Kanō Jigorō*

Kanō Jigorō was a small, bookish boy, and he was bullied at school often enough to go looking for an answer. A family friend named Nakai Baisei, one of the shōgun's guards, showed him a few jujutsu techniques "by which a smaller man might overcome a larger and stronger opponent," and warned him the art was out of date and dangerous. Kanō wanted it anyway. He found his first teacher, a bonesetter named Fukuda Hachinosuke, who taught in a ten-mat room beside his practice.

He trained, studied what remained of the old schools, and in May or June of 1882 started the Kodokan with twelve mats in space belonging to the Eishō-ji, a Buddhist temple in Tokyo. He called the art he taught there judo, the gentle way. In 1888 he and Reverend Thomas Lindsay presented a lecture to the Asiatic Society of Japan whose theme was "the main principle of judo involved gaining victory by yielding to strength." The doctrine is spelled out plainly:

> A main feature of the art is the application of the principles of non-resistance and taking advantage of the opponent's loss of equilibrium; hence the name jūjutsu (literally soft or gentle art), or judo (doctrine of softness or gentleness)...

The broader principle he built the whole art on was what he called the Maximum Efficiency in Use of Mind and Body. Yielding was not weakness or surrender; the condition is carried in the sentence itself, as he put it to a reporter in 1938: "When yielding is the highest efficient use of energy, then yielding is judo." He had only a handful of students at the start, and they improved their technique through regular contests with local police jujutsu teams.

The compendium pairs the story with the Daoist preference for the soft over the hard as an analogy, not a claimed lineage: the same observation about strength appears in a Tokyo dojo and in the Dao De Jing's comparisons of the way with water.
