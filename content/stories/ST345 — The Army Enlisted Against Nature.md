*United States, 1910–1942 · William James's "The Moral Equivalent of War" and the Civilian Conservation Corps*

In 1906 William James gave an address at Stanford University, and four years later, in the last year of his life, the argument appeared as the essay "The Moral Equivalent of War." James refused to talk to the war party like a pacifist scold. He granted its premise in full: the martial virtues were real, they had been bred by war, and a peace kept merely as a pleasure-economy would never hold. His evidence was blunt: "History is a bath of blood," and "Martial virtues must be the enduring cement; intrepidity, contempt of softness, surrender of private interest, obedience to command, must still remain the rock upon which states are built" ([James 1910, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Moral_Equivalent_of_War)).

The question was where those virtues should live once war ends. James's answer carried his own signature on it:

> "If now—and this is my idea—there were, instead of military conscription a conscription of the whole youthful population to form for a certain number of years a part of the army enlisted against nature, the injustice would tend to be evened out, and numerous other goods to the commonwealth would follow." ([James 1910, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Moral_Equivalent_of_War))

The draftees would go to coal and iron mines, freight trains, fishing fleets in December, road-building and tunnel-making, "to get the childishness knocked out of them, and to come back into society with healthier sympathies and soberer ideas."

The idea outlived him. In the first days of his presidency in 1933, Franklin Roosevelt received a report that half a million young men were out of school and out of work; the secretaries of Labor, Agriculture, Interior and the Army took up the plan, and a bill creating the Civilian Conservation Corps, for up to 275,000 young men aged 18 to 25, was signed on March 31 ([Democracy: A Journal of Ideas](https://democracyjournal.org/magazine/33/can-national-service-become-integral-to-our-culture/)). The Army built the camps. By the time Congress ended the program in 1942, three million men had passed through it, planting three billion trees, clearing 125,000 miles of trails and developing 800 state parks ([Democracy: A Journal of Ideas](https://democracyjournal.org/magazine/33/can-national-service-become-integral-to-our-culture/)).

James had forecast what such a spark could do: "It is only a question of blowing on the spark till the whole population gets incandescent, and on the ruins of the old morals of military honor, a stable system of morals of civic honor builds itself up" ([James 1910, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Moral_Equivalent_of_War)). He kept the word conscription; the compendium reads the equivalent as voluntary service and difficult craft. The CCC, for all its imperfections, is the nearest thing his spark got to a trial run, and the tree lines it left behind are still standing.
