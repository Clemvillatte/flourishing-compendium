United States, 1910–1942 · William James's "The Moral Equivalent of War" and the Civilian Conservation Corps

In 1906 William James gave an address at Stanford University, and four years later, in the last year of his life, the argument appeared as the essay "The Moral Equivalent of War" ([Democracy: A Journal of Ideas](https://democracyjournal.org/magazine/33/can-national-service-become-integral-to-our-culture/)). James did not argue as if the war party were simply wrong. The war-party is assuredly right, he wrote, "in affirming and reaffirming that the martial virtues, although originally gained by the race through war, are absolute and permanent human goods" ([James 1910, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Moral_Equivalent_of_War)). His evidence was blunt: "History is a bath of blood," and the martial virtues "must still remain the rock upon which states are built" ([James 1910, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Moral_Equivalent_of_War)).

The question was where those virtues should live once war ends. James's answer carried his own signature on it:

> "If now—and this is my idea—there were, instead of military conscription a conscription of the whole youthful population to form for a certain number of years a part of the army enlisted against nature, the injustice would tend to be evened out, and numerous other goods to the commonwealth would follow." ([James 1910, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Moral_Equivalent_of_War))

The draftees would go to coal and iron mines, freight trains, fishing fleets in December, road-building and tunnel-making, "to get the childishness knocked out of them, and to come back into society with healthier sympathies and soberer ideas" ([James 1910, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Moral_Equivalent_of_War)).

The idea outlived him. In the Democracy Journal's account, James's ideas "were first turned into reality when Franklin D. Roosevelt became President in 1933"; on Roosevelt's desk in the Oval Office was a report showing that half a million young men were out of school and out of work, and during his first days in office he told the secretaries of Labor, Agriculture, the Interior and the Army that he wanted a Civilian Conservation Corps ([Democracy: A Journal of Ideas](https://democracyjournal.org/magazine/33/can-national-service-become-integral-to-our-culture/)). Within a few weeks of taking office he sent Congress a very short bill authorizing a corps of up to 275,000 young men between the ages of 18 and 25, unmarried and unemployed; the Army built the camps, and he signed the bill on March 31 ([Democracy: A Journal of Ideas](https://democracyjournal.org/magazine/33/can-national-service-become-integral-to-our-culture/)). The corps was open only to men, and the Black enlistees served in segregated camps ([Democracy: A Journal of Ideas](https://democracyjournal.org/magazine/33/can-national-service-become-integral-to-our-culture/)). By 1942, when most of the CCC men graduated into military service in the Second World War, more than three million of them had planted three billion trees, cleared 125,000 miles of trails and developed 800 state parks ([Democracy: A Journal of Ideas](https://democracyjournal.org/magazine/33/can-national-service-become-integral-to-our-culture/)).

James had forecast what such a spark could do: "It is only a question of blowing on the spark till the whole population gets incandescent, and on the ruins of the old morals of military honor, a stable system of morals of civic honor builds itself up" ([James 1910, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Moral_Equivalent_of_War)). He kept the word conscription; the compendium reads the equivalent as voluntary service and difficult craft.
