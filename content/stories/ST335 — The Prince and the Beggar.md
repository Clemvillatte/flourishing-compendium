*Therapeutic metaphor · 2013 · retold from Janina Scarlet, as presented in The Big Book of ACT Metaphors*

A prince and a beggar, alike as twins, meet and trade places for a day. The beggar puts on the royal robes and is treated as royalty — and shares the fine food with other beggars, as he always did. The prince puts on the rags and is shoved through the streets — and talks down to the other beggars, steals bread from a child, refuses to eat with them, as he always did.

The clothes changed. The treatment changed. The habits travelled with the wearer.

The metaphor is aimed at the assumption that roles, moods, and circumstances are the whole self — that becoming the anxious one, the failed one, the promoted one, actually replaces who is having the experience. The narrow claim is that a role or label does not describe the whole person. It is not a claim that character cannot change; it is a claim that the costume is not the character.

Retold from Scarlet (2013), as presented in Stoddard & Afari, *The Big Book of ACT Metaphors* (2014), pp. 121–122.
