[[T009 — Zhuangzi|Zhuangzi]] parable · China, 4th century BCE · [Legge, The Texts of Taoism, vol. 2 (1891), Zhuangzi ch. 20](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/daoist/zhuangzi/outer-chapters/zhuangzi-the-tree-on-the-mountain)

If a man is crossing a river in a boat, and another empty vessel comes into collision with it, even though he be a man of a choleric temper, he will not be angry with it. If there be a person, however, in that boat, he will bawl out to him to haul out of the way. If his shout be not heard, he will repeat it; and if the other do not then hear, he will call out a third time, following up the shout with abusive terms. Formerly he was not angry, but now he is; formerly (he thought) the boat was empty, but now there is a person in it. If a man can empty himself of himself, during his time in the world, who can harm him? — [Legge, The Texts of Taoism, vol. 2, Zhuangzi ch. 20](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/daoist/zhuangzi/outer-chapters/zhuangzi-the-tree-on-the-mountain)

The parable is a precise anatomy of anger. It locates the fuel not in the event but in the story of a responsible offender. Practise seeing the boats that hit you as empty, and most quarrels lose their engine.

**Principles:**
- [[D002 — Practice ziran — naturalness|2. Practice ziran — naturalness]] — the collision changes nothing; the anger comes from the story of a responsible offender, which the parable asks you to drop
- [[D003 — Act through wu wei — non-forcing|3. Act through wu wei — non-forcing]] — cursing the imagined offender adds no force that changes the event; the empty-boat seeing is the non-forcing response
- [[D007 — Hold yin and yang together|7. Hold yin and yang together]] — compendium reading: the same collision, two inner readings; holding both sides of the event dissolves the rage
- [[D010 — Return to pu — the uncarved block|10. Return to pu — the uncarved block]] — compendium reading: "empty himself of himself" is the uncarved state, before the wound of being blamed is carved in
