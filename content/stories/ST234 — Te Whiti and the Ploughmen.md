*Historical/biographical narrative · New Zealand, 19th century · [Parihaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parihaka)*

In the 1870s and 1880s, the settlement of Parihaka, under the prophets Te Whiti o Rongomai and Tohu Kākahi, responds to the government's confiscation and surveying of their land with a distinctive campaign: ploughing. Teams of ploughmen, unarmed, plough the confiscated land, asserting their relationship to it by working it. When the survey pegs are pulled up, they are replaced with pegs the Parihaka people carried. When armed constabulary finally invade in 1881, they are met not with resistance but with children carrying baskets of food and singing.

The invasion's aftermath, the arrest and imprisonment of hundreds without trial, was long suppressed in New Zealand historiography, and the government's formal apology came in 2017. The campaign's interest is its grammar: every action was chosen to demonstrate the relationship rather than to win, and the tradition treats this as the highest form of the Māori principle of mana and the relational resolution of dispute.
