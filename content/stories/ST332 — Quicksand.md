*Therapeutic metaphor · 2005 · retold from Steven C. Hayes, Get Out of Your Mind and Into Your Life*

Most of what people know about quicksand comes from films: someone steps in, starts to sink, and does the human thing — struggles. Kicks, claws, pulls at the ground. And in the films, the struggling is what takes the person under.

The metaphor borrows that image for one narrow purpose. A struggle against something that cannot be fought — the ground, the water, the weather — does not make the situation better and can make it worse: the person in the quicksand sinks deeper with each effort to escape it. The psychological parallel is specific: fighting an inner experience that will not be fought away can leave a person more entangled in it than the experience itself warranted.

It is an image, not a claim that feelings are harmless ground. It is used to raise a question: in this situation, what does the struggling itself cost?

Retold from Hayes (2005, pp. 3–4), as discussed in Stoddard & Afari, *The Big Book of ACT Metaphors* (2014), pp. 17–18, 59.
