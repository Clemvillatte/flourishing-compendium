*Philosophical narrative · Denmark, 1844 · [Søren Kierkegaard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%C3%B8ren_Kierkegaard)*

Kierkegaard's The Concept of Anxiety defines anxiety as the dizziness of freedom. It is not fear, which has an object. Anxiety is the vertigo that comes from standing at the edge of one's own possibility, from seeing that nothing determines the choice, and from the discovery that one's self is something one is, in freedom, always in the process of becoming. Adam's anxiety in the garden, Kierkegaard notes, was not about the apple; it was the possibility of possibility.

The book is the founding text of anxiety as a philosophical category rather than a symptom, and its practical yield is the reframing: anxiety is not a malfunction but the accurate perception of one's condition. Read the sentence as a practice instruction, not just a definition: when the vertigo arrives, treat it as information about the size of the choice in front of you. The tradition's later treatments, including Sartre's anguish and Heidegger's Angst, are its descendants, and the clinical literature's use of anxiety as information rather than noise is its distant heir.
