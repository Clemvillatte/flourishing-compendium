Real person, documented experiment · James Lind, HMS Salisbury, 1747, from his Treatise of the Scurvy and two later historical studies.

In May 1747 the Salisbury had been at sea for weeks, and scurvy had taken hold: of 350 sailors, 80 were struck down ([Tröhler, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1276007/)). Many cures had been recommended, and none had been tested against another. The ship's surgeon, James Lind, wrote: "On the 20th of May 1747, I selected twelve patients in the scurvy, on board the Salisbury at sea. Their cases were as similar as I could have them" ([Lind, Treatise, 3rd ed.](https://archive.org/download/bim_eighteenth-century_a-treatise-on-the-scurvy_lind-james_1772/bim_eighteenth-century_a-treatise-on-the-scurvy_lind-james_1772_djvu.txt)). They shared the same quarters in the fore-hold and the same diet.

He gave them to six pairs. Two drank cider, two took elixir of vitriol, two took vinegar, two the sea water, two a medicinal paste, and two had "two oranges and one lemon" a day. The oranges and lemons ran out after six days. Lind's verdict: "The consequence was, that the most sudden and visible good effects were perceived from the use of oranges and lemons." One man was fit for duty after six days, and the other "was appointed to attend the rest of the sick." "Next to the oranges, I thought the cyder had the best effects" ([Lind](https://archive.org/download/bim_eighteenth-century_a-treatise-on-the-scurvy_lind-james_1772/bim_eighteenth-century_a-treatise-on-the-scurvy_lind-james_1772_djvu.txt)).

Then nothing much happened. The year after publication, the Navy's Sick and Hurt Board rejected a proposal to supply sailors with fruit juice ([Tröhler](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1276007/)). In the end Gilbert Blane, by then a commissioner on the Board, persuaded the Admiralty in 1795 to issue lemon juice generously, 42 years after the Treatise ([Tröhler](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1276007/)). The delay is more complicated than a story of stubborn officers. Iain Milne notes that "there is little evidence ... that Lind ever clearly recommended the use of lemon juice to the Admiralty or to others," and Lind believed scurvy had several causes, including foul air and lack of exercise ([Milne, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3536506/)).

The compendium sets this beside Peirce's four ways of fixing belief in P025. In 1747 the fair test was the method of experiment. The decades after show authority, habit and a preferred theory holding off the result, as well as the practical problem of keeping fresh fruit at sea. Limits: the trial was small, with two men per remedy, and Lind did not say how he assigned them. The story does not show that experiment was strong enough to settle the matter. It took decades of ship records.

**Principles:**
- [[P025 — Fix belief by experiment, not tenacity, authority, or taste]]: a fair test of six remedies gave a clear signal, and the years afterwards show what the other ways of fixing belief do to it.
