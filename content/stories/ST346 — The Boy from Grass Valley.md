*California and Massachusetts, 1855–1916 · Josiah Royce*

Josiah Royce was born in 1855 in Grass Valley, a remote mining town in California. His parents were English emigrants who had come west as Forty-Niners and never found gold ([UCLA Newsroom](https://newsroom.ucla.edu/magazine/josiah-royce-hall-philosopher-building-university)). Royce would look back on his home state as "the sad state wherein I had the odd fortune to be born … a philosophically barren area." He grew up poor, stocky and lonely, and still became the first major American philosopher born west of the Mississippi ([UCLA Newsroom](https://newsroom.ucla.edu/magazine/josiah-royce-hall-philosopher-building-university)). His mother, a devout Christian who headed a primary school, directed his early education with his older sisters; at eleven he was sent to school in San Francisco ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/royce/)).

The decisive act came in 1882. William James was taking a one-year sabbatical from Harvard, and Royce was offered the chance to replace him. Royce did not hedge: "he accepted the offer of half of James's salary, resigned his California appointment altogether, and moved his wife and newborn son across the continent in the summer of 1882" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/royce/)). A one-year replacement post, at half pay, three thousand miles from home, with a wife and a baby. The barren-state boy bet everything on the community of minds, and the bet held: the position was made permanent in 1884, and Royce taught at Harvard until his death in 1916.

What he made of the crossing became his philosophy. In his later ethical writings he stressed "the centrality of action intended to realize a sound ideal that one has freely embraced" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/royce/)). His name for that binding was loyalty, and he gave it the plainest possible test: "Loyalty, he wrote, gives life meaning and cures hesitancy; happiness and satisfaction come from devotion to a purpose outside oneself" ([UCLA Newsroom](https://newsroom.ucla.edu/magazine/josiah-royce-hall-philosopher-building-university)). He believed in "tightly binding oneself to others to benefit a noble cause, which would give direction as to what to do and how to live" ([UCLA Newsroom](https://newsroom.ucla.edu/magazine/josiah-royce-hall-philosopher-building-university)).

The story is a worked example of the marriage James described between ideals and virtues. An ideal held alone, in a barren town, by a lonely boy, gives no reality; the risk-taking devotion alone gives no direction. Royce's significance was created at the point where the two fused: a freely embraced ideal, and a man who resigned his post and crossed a continent to serve it.
