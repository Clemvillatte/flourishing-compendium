California and Massachusetts, 1855–1916 · Josiah Royce

Josiah Royce was born in 1855 in Grass Valley, a remote mining town in California. His parents were English emigrants who had come west as Forty-Niners and never found gold ([UCLA Newsroom](https://newsroom.ucla.edu/magazine/josiah-royce-hall-philosopher-building-university)). Royce would look back on his home state as "the sad state wherein I had the odd fortune to be born … a philosophically barren area." He grew up poor, stocky and lonely, and still became the first major American philosopher born west of the Mississippi ([UCLA Newsroom](https://newsroom.ucla.edu/magazine/josiah-royce-hall-philosopher-building-university)). His mother, a devout Christian who headed a primary school, directed his early education with his older sisters; at eleven he was sent to school in San Francisco ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/royce/)).

The decisive act came in 1882. William James was taking a one-year sabbatical from Harvard, and Royce was offered the chance to replace him. Royce did not hedge: "he accepted the offer of half of James's salary, resigned his California appointment altogether, and moved his wife and newborn son across the continent in the summer of 1882" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/royce/)). A one-year replacement post, at half of James's salary, with a wife and a newborn son. From 1882 until his death in 1916, Royce taught at Harvard ([UCLA Newsroom](https://newsroom.ucla.edu/magazine/josiah-royce-hall-philosopher-building-university)).

His later ethical writings are summarized by the Stanford Encyclopedia as stressing "the centrality of action intended to realize a sound ideal that one has freely embraced" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/royce/)). His name for that binding was loyalty. The UCLA account of his work reports that, in his writing, loyalty gives life meaning and cures hesitancy, and that happiness and satisfaction come from devotion to a purpose outside oneself; it describes him as believing in tightly binding oneself to others to benefit a noble cause, which would give direction as to what to do and how to live ([UCLA Newsroom](https://newsroom.ucla.edu/magazine/josiah-royce-hall-philosopher-building-university)).

The compendium reads the 1882 decision as a worked example of the marriage between ideals and virtues. An ideal held alone, in a barren town, by a lonely boy, gives no reality; the devotion alone gives no direction. On this reading, Royce's significance was created at the point where the two fused: a freely embraced ideal, and a man who resigned his post and crossed a continent to serve it.
