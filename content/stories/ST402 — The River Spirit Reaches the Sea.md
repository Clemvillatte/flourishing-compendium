Philosophical allegory · Zhuangzi, chapter 17, in James Legge's translation, cross-read with Herbert Giles's.

Autumn floods fill the hundred streams and the Yellow River swells until, in Legge's wording, "one could not distinguish an ox from a horse" across its channel. Giles has a cow in place of the ox ([Giles, ch. 17](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/59709/pg59709.txt)). The river's spirit, the Earl of the He, laughs with delight "thinking that all the beauty of the world was to be found in his charge" ([Legge, ch. 17 §1](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)).

He walks east along the current until he reaches the North Sea and cannot see where its waters begin. He turns his face toward Ruo, the spirit of the sea, and sighs that the proverb about a man who learns a hundred points and thinks no one his equal "was surely spoken of me." He admits he once discounted people who belittled Confucius and Bo-yi. Now: "If I had not come to your gate, I should have been in danger (of continuing in my ignorance)" ([Legge](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)).

Ruo says the river is now fit to be talked with: "A frog in a well cannot be talked with about the sea - he is confined to the limits of his hole." The same goes for a summer insect and ice. Ruo does not boast about the sea. "Between heaven and earth I am but as a small stone or a small tree on a great hill. So long as I see myself to be thus small, how should I make much of myself?" The whole of the Middle States, he says, is smaller than a grain of rice in a granary ([Legge §2](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)).

The Earl then asks whether he should take heaven and earth as the standard of what is great and a hair's point as the standard of what is small. Ruo says no: "The (different) capacities of things are illimitable" ([Legge §3](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)). The sea does not offer itself as the new measure.

The compendium reads this beside D106 and D128. The river is corrected by seeing something larger, and he says so plainly. The correction does not leave him with a bigger idol: his teacher declines to be one.

Limits: the chapter ends the scene in dialogue, and the river's later life is not narrated. Telling someone their trouble is small beside the sea can shut down real pain, and the text does not address that use. Ruo's own speech is a long argument about relative size, and it is not clear that a person in distress could live by it.

**Principles:**
- [[D106 — Live at your own scale]]: the flood-pride and the sea's refusal to be the new standard.
- [[D128 — Learn from everyone]]: the river learns from an unexpected teacher and admits his ignorance.
