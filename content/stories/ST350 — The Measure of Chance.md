Cambridge and Milford, 1839–1914 · Charles Sanders Peirce, "The Doctrine of Necessity Examined" (The Monist, 1892), in The Essential Peirce vol. 1; biographical facts from the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy

Charles Sanders Peirce spent thirty-two years in exact measurement: from 1859 to the last day of 1891 the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey employed him, measuring the intensity of the earth's gravitational field with swinging pendulums, often of his own design ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/peirce/)). Alongside the Survey he taught logic at Johns Hopkins from 1879 until 1884, when the post was suddenly terminated for reasons connected to his second marriage: his wife Juliette was Romani, and his divorce came only two days before the wedding ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/peirce/)). It was the only academic post he ever held. When the Survey work ended too, on the last day of 1891, he lived on the edge of penury, eking out a living from intellectual odd-jobs and consulting, sometimes surviving only through the charity of friends, above all William James ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/peirce/)).

That first year out of employment, he wrote the essay arguing the universe is a gamble. In "The Doctrine of Necessity Examined" he grants the necessitarians their case, then answers it. Everywhere, the main fact is "growth and increasing complexity," while "death and corruption are mere accidents or secondary phenomena" ([Essential Peirce, I 308](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1L3jvBMstbqfeNxOVCmm-z1ZRB56qw7ko)). His conclusion: "By thus admitting pure spontaneity or life as a character of the universe, acting always and everywhere though restrained within narrow bounds by law, producing infinitesimal departures from law continually, and great ones with infinite infrequency, I account for all the variety and diversity of the universe" ([Essential Peirce, I 308](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1L3jvBMstbqfeNxOVCmm-z1ZRB56qw7ko)). The point of the wager: "By supposing the rigid exactitude of causation to yield, I care not how little, — be it but by a strictly infinitesimal amount, — we gain room to insert mind into our scheme" ([Essential Peirce, I 309](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1L3jvBMstbqfeNxOVCmm-z1ZRB56qw7ko)). Chance is not the enemy of order; "variety itself, beyond comparison the most obtrusive character of the universe: no mechanism can account for this" ([Essential Peirce, I 310](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1L3jvBMstbqfeNxOVCmm-z1ZRB56qw7ko)).

The man of the pendulums did not abandon method; he moved it. "I make use of chance chiefly to make room for a principle of generalisation, or tendency to form habits, which I hold has produced all regularities" ([Essential Peirce, I 310](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1L3jvBMstbqfeNxOVCmm-z1ZRB56qw7ko)). Law itself, on his account, is habit grown from chance.

The compendium reads the life and the essay as one pattern. Peirce's own existence turned aleatory: employment lost, income a series of fortunate and unfortunate throws, survival sometimes a friend's charity. His response was neither denial nor fatalism but the cultivation of method: the logic, the classification of the sciences, some 12,000 printed pages published in his lifetime and roughly 80,000 handwritten ones left unpublished ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/peirce/)). That is what the principle asks of an aleatory world: accept the gamble, and inside it, build the habits.
