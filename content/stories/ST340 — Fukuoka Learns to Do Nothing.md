*Shikoku, Japan, 1937–1975 · Masanobu Fukuoka and the invention of natural farming*

Fukuoka was a laboratory man before he was a farmer: a trained microbiologist and plant pathologist, inspecting imports for the Yokohama customs office, until a hospital stay with pneumonia in 1937 changed his mind about his whole profession. What he saw when he recovered no longer looked like science helping nature. It looked like a fight with it. He resigned his research post and went back to his family's farm on Shikoku with a question most farmers would consider insane: what happens if you stop?

His first attempt at the answer is the part people forget. He took over a patch of his father's citrus trees and simply stopped pruning them, letting everything take its course. The result:

> the branches became intertwined, insects attacked the trees, and the entire orchard withered away in no time.

He had mistaken doing nothing for non-interference, and he said so plainly:

> I had acted in the belief that everything should be left to take its natural course, but I found that if you apply that way of thinking all at once, before long, things do not go so well. This is abandonment, not "natural farming."

So he started again, this time subtracting practices one at a time and watching what the land did. Each removal had to be tested against the grain of the place; the difference between working with nature and abandoning it had to be learned orchard row by orchard row.

> I was aiming at a pleasant, natural way of farming which results in making the work easier instead of harder. "How about not doing this?" "How about not doing that?" — that was my way of thinking. I ultimately reached the conclusion that there was no need to plow, no need to apply fertilizer, no need to make compost, no need to use insecticide.

Rice and barley went into the same fields in rotation, and the rules he arrived at were four negatives: no plowing, no prepared fertilizer or compost, no weeding by cultivation or herbicide, no pesticide. He called the method shizen nōhō, natural farming, and wrote it up in 1975 in a book whose title became a slogan: The One-Straw Revolution. Its most quoted line puts the farm in its place: "The ultimate goal of farming is not the growing of crops, but the cultivation and perfection of human beings."
