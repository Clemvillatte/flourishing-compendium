*Shikoku, Japan, 1937–1975 · the development of Masanobu Fukuoka's natural-farming method*

Fukuoka was a laboratory man before he was a farmer: a trained microbiologist and plant pathologist, inspecting imports for the Yokohama customs office, until a hospital stay with pneumonia in 1937 changed his view of his own profession. The experience he described was a spiritual one that led him to doubt modern agricultural science. He resigned his research post and went back to his family's farm on Shikoku with a question: what happens if you stop?

His first attempt is the part of the story worth keeping. From 1938 he experimented with techniques in the family's citrus orchards, and in one patch he simply stopped pruning. The result:

> the branches became intertwined, insects attacked the trees, and the entire orchard withered away in no time.

He had mistaken doing nothing for non-interference, and he said so plainly:

> I had acted in the belief that everything should be left to take its natural course, but I found that if you apply that way of thinking all at once, before long, things do not go so well. This is abandonment, not "natural farming."

He took up natural farming again in 1947 with success, using no-till methods to raise rice and barley, and the rules he arrived at were four negatives: no plowing, no prepared fertilizer or compost, no weeding by cultivation or herbicide, no pesticide. He called the method shizen nōhō, natural farming, and described its direction of travel like this:

> I was aiming at a pleasant, natural way of farming which results in making the work easier instead of harder. "How about not doing this?" "How about not doing that?" — that was my way of thinking. I ultimately reached the conclusion that there was no need to plow, no need to apply fertilizer, no need to make compost, no need to use insecticide.

He wrote the method up in 1975 in The One-Straw Revolution, translated into English in 1978. Its line on the point of all of it, as Wikiquote reproduces it with a citation to chapter 3 (p. 119) of the book, reads: "The ultimate goal of farming is not the growing of crops, but the cultivation and perfection of human beings."
