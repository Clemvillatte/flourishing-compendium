Daoist literary parable · Liezi, Book II, "The Yellow Emperor," in Lionel Giles's 1912 translation.

A sailor swims among sea-gulls every morning, and a hundred or more gather around him ([Liezi, Book II, p. 49](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tzu;_(IA_taoistteachingsf00liezrich).pdf)). One day his father asks him to catch one or two as pets ([Liezi, Book II, p. 49](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tzu;_(IA_taoistteachingsf00liezrich).pdf)). On the following day the sailor goes down to the sea as usual, and the encounter is no longer available on its usual terms; the account ends with the gulls refusing to alight ([Liezi, Book II, pp. 49–50](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tzu;_(IA_taoistteachingsf00liezrich).pdf)):

> "the gulls only wheeled about in the air and would not alight." ([Liezi, Book II, p. 49, exact excerpt from Giles](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tzu;_(IA_taoistteachingsf00liezrich).pdf))

An attached commentary describes "disturbance in his mind, accompanied by a change in his outward demeanour" and says the birds "became conscious of the fact that he was a human being" ([Liezi, Book II, p. 50](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tzu;_(IA_taoistteachingsf00liezrich).pdf)). Giles identifies that commentary as Chang Chan's, a fourth-century officer of the Banqueting Court, and marks its excerpts with inverted commas ([Giles, introduction, printed pp. 15–16](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tzu;_(IA_taoistteachingsf00liezrich).pdf)); it should be read as explanation, distinct from the narration itself.

For D133, the compendium reads the change that follows the father's request as a warning about possessiveness entering a freely shared encounter. The birds' closeness existed only while it was given, which is the parable's version of the Dao De Jing's warning that "he who would hold it in his grasp loses it" ([DDJ 29, Legge](https://www.sacred-texts.com/tao/taote.htm)). The narration describes no capture attempt, apology or reconciliation; the loss is the whole outcome ([Liezi, Book II, pp. 49–50](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tzu;_(IA_taoistteachingsf00liezrich).pdf)). A parable supplies no evidence about parenting outcomes or about how gull cognition works, and it questions the demand to secure another being's participation without pretending to settle those questions.

**Principles:**
- [[D133 — Don't dominate — the world can't be controlled]]: the gulls' freely given closeness ends when they are wanted as possessions, so the encounter the sailor valued could no longer be had on its own terms.
