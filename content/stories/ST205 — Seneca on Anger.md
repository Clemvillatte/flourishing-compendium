*Historical/biographical narrative · Rome, 1st century CE · [Seneca the Younger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seneca_the_Younger)*

Seneca's De Ira opens with the claim that anger is temporary madness, and the treatise proceeds as a study of its mechanism: the impression of injury, the assent, the heat. His examples of the disease's extremes include a Roman noble who had a slave killed for breaking a crystal cup, and a host who crucified a slave for speaking freely. The cure is not suppression, which Seneca treats as impossible, but the refusal of the first judgement: nothing needs to be done, and a delay is available.

The treatise's practical core is the pause: count to ten, in effect, and the delay is not for calming down but for the assent to be examined. Its modern descendants, anger-management protocols and [[Cognitive reappraisal|cognitive reappraisal]], preserve the structure Seneca described: the passion is a judgement, and judgements can be interrupted before they become fuel.
