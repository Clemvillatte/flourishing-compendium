Cambridge, Massachusetts, 1870 · William James's diary

William James spent years in a state of physical suffering and depression that was to last nearly six years. A sense of moral impotence plagued him; while suicide seldom seemed like a live option, thoughts of taking his life never wholly departed from his mind ([The Harvard Crimson](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/1963/5/8/cosmopolite-cosmologist-the-life-of-william/)). One night, walking into a darkened room, he had a sudden hallucinatory vision: a young man he had once seen, a patient at a mental hospital, black-haired, with "greenish" skin, sitting on a bench against the wall, his legs pressed up against his chest, moving only his eyes. "That shape am I," James wrote. After it, "the universe changed entirely," and he woke each morning with dread in his stomach ([The Atlantic](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/1996/05/the-nitrous-oxide-philosopher/376581/)).

Two diary entries mark the turn. On February 1, 1870, he wrote: "Today I about touched bottom, and perceive plainly that I must face the choice with open eyes: shall I Frankly throw the moral business overboard, as one unsuited to my innate aptitudes, or shall I follow it and it alone...? I will give the latter alternative a fair trial. Who knows but the moral interest may become developed" ([The Harvard Crimson](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/1963/5/8/cosmopolite-cosmologist-the-life-of-william/)). Three months later, on April 30, having finished the first part of Charles Renouvier's second Essais, he recorded the entry: "I think that yesterday was a crisis in my life... At any rate, I will assume for the present—until next year—that it is no illusion. My first act of free will shall be to believe in free will" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_James)).

The Crimson account describes a gradual return: only very slowly did enthusiasm and buoyancy become dominant ([The Harvard Crimson](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/1963/5/8/cosmopolite-cosmologist-the-life-of-william/)). His programme for the remainder of the year was to abstain from "the mere speculation & contemplative [meditation]" in which his nature took most delight, and to "voluntarily cultivate the feeling of moral freedom, by reading books favorable to it, as well as by acting" ([The Atlantic](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/1996/05/the-nitrous-oxide-philosopher/376581/)). Two events of the decade that followed did much of the therapeutic work: he started teaching at Harvard, an offer he welcomed as a stabilizing vocation, and he married Alice Howe Gibbens ([The Harvard Crimson](https://www.thecrimson.com/article/1963/5/8/cosmopolite-cosmologist-the-life-of-william/)).

A resolve in a diary is not a cure, and the compendium does not offer the story as evidence that belief ended James's depression. It offers it as a comparison for its own principle: not a refutation of the suffering but a provisional commitment made inside it, with a programme of reading and acting built around the resolve. Years later James gave the pattern its lasting image, the mountain climber in a tight place: "the part of wisdom as well as of courage is to believe what is in the line of your needs, for only by such belief is the need fulfilled" ([The Atlantic](https://www.theatlantic.com/magazine/archive/1996/05/the-nitrous-oxide-philosopher/376581/)).
