Autobiographical account, translated · Hakuin's great-doubt passage from his letter to an old nun of the Hokke sect, in Yampolsky's translation of the Selected Writings.

At nineteen Hakuin reads that the enlightened master Yen-t'ou was killed by bandits, his cries resounding for over three li. "I wondered why such an enlightened monk was unable to escape the swords of thieves. If such a thing could happen to a man who was like a unicorn or phoenix among monks, a dragon in the sea of Buddhism, how was I to escape the staves of the demons of hell after I died? What use was there in studying Zen? What a fraud Buddhism!" ([Hakuin, Selected Writings, Yampolsky translation](https://terebess.hu/zen/hakuin1.html)). For three days he cannot eat; his faith is completely lost, and the Buddha's statues look like mud and dirt to him ([Hakuin, Selected Writings, Yampolsky translation](https://terebess.hu/zen/hakuin1.html)).

The account then jumps ahead to a different register, and what it reports is no longer loss. At twenty-four, deep in the Mu koan, he writes: "Night and day I did not sleep; I forgot both to eat and rest. Suddenly a great doubt manifested itself before me. It was as though I were frozen solid in the midst of an ice sheet extending tens of thousands of miles. A purity filled my breast and I could neither go forward nor retreat." He hears lectures as though from outside the hall; at times he seems to float through the air ([Hakuin, Selected Writings, Yampolsky translation](https://terebess.hu/zen/hakuin1.html)). Then: "I chanced to hear the sound of the temple bell and I was suddenly transformed. It was as if a sheet of ice had been smashed or a jade tower had fallen with a crash." His cry follows: "Wonderful, wonderful. There is no cycle of birth and death through which one must pass. There is no enlightenment one must seek" ([Hakuin, Selected Writings, Yampolsky translation](https://terebess.hu/zen/hakuin1.html)).

Read beside B047, Hakuin's retrospective account places an experience he calls great doubt immediately before the bell-triggered breakthrough. This page reads that sequence as an illustration of the principle's claim that doubt is the mechanism by which the seeing is produced, not proof of what happened between his two crises. What keeps the account honest is what Hakuin reports next, in the same breath: "My pride soared up like a majestic mountain, my arrogance surged forward like the tide. Smugly I thought to myself: 'In the past two or three hundred years no one could have accomplished such a marvelous breakthrough as this.'" ([Hakuin, Selected Writings, Yampolsky translation](https://terebess.hu/zen/hakuin1.html)) The breakthrough does not end the account; the pride that followed it is part of what Hakuin chose to report, and a tidy success story is not what the page gives.

**Principles:**
- [[B047 — Keep the great doubt alive]]: Hakuin's own account runs from a faith-destroying question at nineteen through a frozen, sleepless absorption at twenty-four to a bell-smashed breakthrough, and reports the arrogance that followed it; the doubt immediately precedes the awakening in his telling, and the aftermath is included rather than edited out.
