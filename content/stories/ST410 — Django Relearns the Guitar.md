Biographical account · France, 20th century; Benjamin Givan, The Music of Django Reinhardt (University of Michigan Press, 2010), chapter 1 (book pages 7-10); the Britannica entry on Reinhardt

After a caravan fire in 1928, Django Reinhardt relearned the guitar with what Givan calls "an entirely new playing technique" ([Givan, ch. 1, p. 7](https://press.umich.edu/pdf/9780472114993-ch1.pdf)). Givan describes almost two years of recovery: Reinhardt regained the use of his leg, while the third and fourth fingers of his left hand remained permanently damaged ([Givan, ch. 1, p. 7](https://press.umich.edu/pdf/9780472114993-ch1.pdf)).

The fire is documented in the chapter's opening. "On the night of October 26, 1928, the eighteen-year-old musician returned from a playing engagement to his caravan at a gypsy encampment outside Paris." ([Givan, ch. 1, p. 7](https://press.umich.edu/pdf/9780472114993-ch1.pdf)) A candle's flame ignited celluloid flowers his wife planned to sell; the right side of his body was burned so severely that a surgeon recommended amputating his leg, which Reinhardt refused ([Givan, ch. 1, p. 7](https://press.umich.edu/pdf/9780472114993-ch1.pdf)). The outcome of the recovery is stated in one sentence:

> During a recovery period of almost two years he regained the use of his leg, but the third and fourth fingers of his left hand were permanently damaged. That Reinhardt managed to relearn his instrument with an entirely new playing technique has been a source of awe and mystery ever since. ([Givan, ch. 1, p. 7](https://press.umich.edu/pdf/9780472114993-ch1.pdf))

The chapter is careful about how far the damage went. Photographs show the two fingers "bent backward at their base at an abnormal angle, and the upper joints were partially flexed." ([Givan, ch. 1, p. 10](https://press.umich.edu/pdf/9780472114993-ch1.pdf)) Givan rejects the stronger claims of some writers: "the view of some authors that two of his digits were 'useless' or 'paralyzed' is misleading" ([Givan, ch. 1, p. 10](https://press.umich.edu/pdf/9780472114993-ch1.pdf)).

What he rebuilt is documented in a 1944 discographical booklet that Givan quotes ([Givan, ch. 1, p. 10](https://press.umich.edu/pdf/9780472114993-ch1.pdf)):

> [Reinhardt] uses the first and second left-hand fingers most of the time in single-note work; in chord work he can make use of the third and fourth fingers to a limited extent on the first two strings. He plays his famous octave passages on any two strings, with a 'damped' string in between. . . , avoiding that frenzied rushing up and down the fingerboard which would otherwise be necessary. His famous chromatic runs, if played in the first position, are fingered; if played up the fingerboard, they are glissed with one finger. He plays unusual chord shapes because of his handicap. ([Givan, ch. 1, p. 10](https://press.umich.edu/pdf/9780472114993-ch1.pdf), quoting the 1944 Billy Neill and E. Gates discographical booklet)

The compendium reads this as an illustration of P192: new musical possibilities developed while a physical limitation persisted ([Givan, ch. 1, p. 7](https://press.umich.edu/pdf/9780472114993-ch1.pdf)). The technical description includes both limited use of the damaged fingers and different ways of distributing the playing across the movement available ([Givan, ch. 1, p. 10](https://press.umich.edu/pdf/9780472114993-ch1.pdf)). This is a case of practical adaptation, not evidence establishing Bergson's metaphysics or showing that every injury can be turned to benefit ([Givan, ch. 1](https://press.umich.edu/pdf/9780472114993-ch1.pdf)).

**Principles:**
- [[P192 — Life is freedom inserting itself into necessity|P192 Life is freedom inserting itself into necessity]] — the permanently damaged hand is the necessity, and the rebuilt technique (damped-string octaves, one-finger glissandi, chord shapes because of the handicap) is freedom inserted into that necessity rather than the necessity removed. ([Givan, ch. 1](https://press.umich.edu/pdf/9780472114993-ch1.pdf))
