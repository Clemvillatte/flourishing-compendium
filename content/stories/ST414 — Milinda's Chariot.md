Northwest India, ~2nd century BCE (traditional setting) · Milindapañha, Book II · a canonical dialogue, T. W. Rhys Davids translation (Sacred Books of the East 35)

A Greek king who ruled in India sat down to cross-examine a Buddhist elder, and the elder opened by disclaiming his own name. The exchange that followed, over the chariot the king arrived in, is the classic one-scene demonstration of what B122 calls using two truths: analysis dissolves the chariot, the king puts it back together as a designation, and the monk approves both moves. The dialogue works the registers so cleanly that an essay writer can retell the whole principle from it. ([Milindapañha II, Rhys Davids](https://sacred-texts.com/bud/sbe35/sbe3504.htm))

The frame: "Milinda is supposed to be the Menander, who appears in the list of the Greek kings of Baktria," a "king of the Yonakas reigning at Sâgala" ([Rhys Davids' introduction](https://sacred-texts.com/bud/sbe35/sbe3502.htm)). Nagasena's opening move hands over his own identity as the first exhibit ([Milindapañha II](https://sacred-texts.com/bud/sbe35/sbe3504.htm)):

> "I am known as Nâgasena, O king, and it is by that name that my brethren in the faith address me. But although parents, O king, give such a name as Nâgasena, or Sûrasena, or Vîrasena, or Sîhasena, yet this, Sire,--Nâgasena and so on--is only a generally understood term, a designation in common use. For there is no permanent individuality (no soul) involved in the matter."

The cross-examination then takes the king's own chariot apart. Asked how he came, the king said in a carriage, and Nagasena pressed: is the pole the chariot? the axle? the wheels, the framework, the ropes, the yoke, the spokes, the goad? All the parts together? Anything outside them? To each, no. Then the punchline ([Milindapañha II](https://sacred-texts.com/bud/sbe35/sbe3504.htm)):

> "Then thus, ask as I may, I can discover no chariot. Chariot is a mere empty sound."

The charge of untruth follows, the five hundred Yonakas applaud, and the king answers ([Milindapañha II](https://sacred-texts.com/bud/sbe35/sbe3504.htm)):

> "I have spoken no untruth, reverend Sir. It is on account of its having all these things--the pole, and the axle, the wheels, and the framework, the ropes, the yoke, the spokes, and the goad--that it comes under the generally understood term, the designation in common use, of 'chariot.'"

> "Very good! Your Majesty has rightly grasped the meaning of 'chariot.'"

Nagasena then turns the same analysis on the name he had disclaimed, "the thirty-two kinds of organic matter in a human body, and the five constituent elements of being," and closes with the verse of the nun Vajira ([Milindapañha II](https://sacred-texts.com/bud/sbe35/sbe3504.htm)):

> "Just as it is by the condition precedent of the co-existence of its various parts that the word 'chariot' is used, just so is it that when the Skandhas are there we talk of a 'being.'"

My reading: the scene runs both registers of B122 in order. Ultimate analysis, pressed alone, dissolves the chariot into "a mere empty sound" and lands the king with a charge of falsehood, which is the nihilism reading Nagarjuna later answered. The king's reply restores the conventional register: the word "chariot" works, in dependence on parts, as a designation in common use. Nagasena approves both: the analysis was correct, and the designation is correct. The Vajira verse states the hinge in one line: when the conditions are present, we talk of a being; the talk tracks the conditions, and nothing else needs to be found. Nagarjuna's verse makes the registers interdependent: without the conventional truth, "the significance of the ultimate cannot be taught" ([MMK 24.10, Garfield](https://pages.uoregon.edu/munno/OregonCourses/REL4400002/nagarjuna_middleway24.pdf)). The story does not carry the Madhyamaka dialectic itself; its argument works by designation analysis, not by the tetralemma.

**Principles:**
- [[B122 — Use two truths (conventional and ultimate)|122. Use two truths (conventional and ultimate)]] — the exchange dissolves the chariot by ultimate analysis, restores it as a conventional designation, and has the monk approve both, with the Vajira verse giving the dependence of the two registers in one line
