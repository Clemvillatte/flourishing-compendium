Early Buddhist discourse with a narrative frame · Majjhima Nikaya 86, in Thanissaro Bhikkhu's translation, cross-read with Bhikkhu Sujato's.

A bandit named Angulimala has turned villages into non-villages and wears a garland of fingers. Cowherds, shepherds and farmers tell the Buddha three times not to take that road, and he keeps going in silence. Angulimala sees one man coming alone, takes up his sword and follows, and by the sutta's account cannot catch him though he runs with all his might. "Stop, contemplative! Stop!" "I have stopped, Angulimala. You stop" ([Thanissaro, MN 86](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.086.than.html)). The Buddha explains in verse that he has stopped, "once & for all," having cast off violence "toward all living beings." The bandit throws his sword and weapons over a cliff and asks to go forth.

King Pasenadi arrives at the monastery with about 500 horsemen to deal with the bandit. The Buddha asks what he would do if he saw Angulimala with head shaved, in the ochre robe, refraining from killing, living on one meal a day. The king says he would bow, rise to greet him, give him robes and food, and arrange protection. Then the Buddha points to the monk beside him, and the king is terrified. "Don't be afraid, great king." and "He poses no danger to you." The king later says that what "we could not tame even with blunt or bladed weapons, the Blessed One has tamed without blunt or bladed weapons" ([Thanissaro](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.086.than.html)).

Not everyone in Savatthi is of the king's mind. On his alms round Angulimala is hit by a clod, a stone and a potsherd, and arrives with his head broken open and his bowl smashed. The Buddha says: "Bear with it, brahman! Bear with it!" Sujato has "Endure it, brahmin!" ([Sujato, MN 86](https://suttacentral.net/mn86/en/sujato)). Later, alone in his release, Angulimala speaks of his enemies: "May even my enemies hear talk of the Dhamma." He adds that his name is now Doer of No Harm, "but I used to be a doer of harm" ([Thanissaro](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.086.than.html)).

Limits: the sutta is told from inside the tradition. It reads the stones as the fruit of kamma and gives no voice to the families of the people he killed, and none to the crowd. The king's willingness to bow to the changed monk is conditional ("suppose you were to see..."), and the sutta does not show whether the crowd's anger ever lifted. Angulimala's wish for his enemies is not forgiveness from the people he wronged.

**Principles:**
- [[B104 — Practice patience (kshanti)]]: "Bear with it" after being struck.
- [[B107 — Forgive — release the coal]]: Angulimala's wish for his enemies is the only forgiveness the text shows, and it comes from the man who was hurt.
