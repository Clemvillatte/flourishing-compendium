Traditional biography, translated · the Mo-Kia-Yen desert crossing in The Life of Hiuen-Tsiang, the biography by his disciples Hwui Li and Yen-Tsung, in Samuel Beal's translation.

In 629, an imperial rescript forbade going abroad; the others who had planned the journey gave it up, and he went alone ([Beal, Life of Hiuen-Tsiang, Book I](https://archive.org/stream/lifeofhiuentsian030569mbp/lifeofhiuentsian030569mbp_djvu.txt)). His guide leaves him, and "alone and deserted, he traversed the sandy waste; his only means of observing the way being the heaps of bones and the horse-dung," into a desert with "no birds overhead, and no beasts below; there is neither water nor herb to be found" ([Beal, Life of Hiuen-Tsiang, Book I](https://archive.org/stream/lifeofhiuentsian030569mbp/lifeofhiuentsian030569mbp_djvu.txt)). A hundred li in, he loses his way, then his water: the vessel "slipped from his hands, and the water was wasted; thus, a supply enough for 1000 li was lost in a moment" ([Beal, Life of Hiuen-Tsiang, Book I](https://archive.org/stream/lifeofhiuentsian030569mbp/lifeofhiuentsian030569mbp_djvu.txt)). He turns back east, and after ten li stops himself: "It is better to die in the attempt to go to the West, than to live by returning to the East." He turns his bridle, invokes Kwan-Yin, and proceeds northwest ([Beal, Life of Hiuen-Tsiang, Book I](https://archive.org/stream/lifeofhiuentsian030569mbp/lifeofhiuentsian030569mbp_djvu.txt)).

Through the crossing he carries the Heart Sutra, given him years before by a sick man he had fed and clothed ([Beal, Life of Hiuen-Tsiang, Book I](https://archive.org/stream/lifeofhiuentsian030569mbp/lifeofhiuentsian030569mbp_djvu.txt)). The biography reports that demon shapes surrounded him on the road, that invoking Kwan-Yin did not drive them all away, and that "when he recited this Sutra, at the sound of the words they all disappeared in a moment" ([Beal, Life of Hiuen-Tsiang, Book I](https://archive.org/stream/lifeofhiuentsian030569mbp/lifeofhiuentsian030569mbp_djvu.txt)). Then the thirst: "for four nights and five days not a drop of water had he to wet his throat or mouth." Unable to advance, he lies on the sands invoking Kwan-Yin, until the middle of the fifth night, when a cool wind touches his body; rousing himself, he pushes on ten li, and his horse "suddenly started off another way and could not be brought back or turned," bringing him to green grass and "a pool of water; sweet, and bright as a mirror," where he drinks without stint ([Beal, Life of Hiuen-Tsiang, Book I](https://archive.org/stream/lifeofhiuentsian030569mbp/lifeofhiuentsian030569mbp_djvu.txt)).

Read beside B122, the episode illustrates a limited point; this reading is the page's, not the biography's. The book he recites teaches that "form is emptiness, emptiness is form; emptiness is not separate from form, form is not separate from emptiness" ([The Heart Sutra, Red Pine translation](http://oaks.nvg.org/heart-sutra.html)). The biography pairs that recitation with an undiminished bodily need: thirst and drinking remain consequential within the narrative. A teaching of emptiness does not make the water irrelevant, and the pilgrim who carries it still turns his bridle at the tenth li, still drinks without stint at the pool. The account does not explain the two truths or show Xuanzang distinguishing them; that argument stays with the principle's own Nagarjuna verses.

**Principles:**
- [[B122 — Use two truths (conventional and ultimate)]]: The biography pairs the Heart Sutra's emptiness with a body that still needs water; the pairing shows that a teaching of emptiness leaves thirst and drinking consequential, while the two truths as an argument stay with Nagarjuna's verses.
