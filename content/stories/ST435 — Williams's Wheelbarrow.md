Poems with biographical context · United States, 20th century · Poetry Foundation, Poets.org, Britannica, and the Annals of Thoracic Surgery

William Carlos Williams spent his working life as a family doctor in Rutherford, New Jersey, and his poems gave that life's smallest objects their full attention: a red wheelbarrow glazed with rain water, some plums in an icebox ([Carter](https://www.annalsthoracicsurgery.org/article/S0003-4975(99)00293-3/fulltext); [Teicher](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/articles/68731/william-carlos-williams-the-red-wheelbarrow)). He took his M.D. at the University of Pennsylvania in 1906 and, in Britannica's phrase, "returned in 1910 to a lifetime of poetry and medical practice in his hometown of Rutherford, New Jersey" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/William-Carlos-Williams)). In Rutherford, in Carter's words, "he spent nearly all his life, including 40 years of practicing medicine," most of it from the family house at 9 Ridge Road, "where he practiced and the family lived for the remainder of his life" ([Carter](https://www.annalsthoracicsurgery.org/article/S0003-4975(99)00293-3/fulltext)).

Teicher's account of how the poems got written is one sentence: "In between house calls, in the midst of delivering countless babies and treating the ailments of Rutherford's working-class population, Williams wrote tiny poems on prescription pads or holed up late into the night in his upstairs study, from which his wife, Flossie, could hear the clatter of his typewriter as draft after draft raced through it" ([Teicher](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/articles/68731/william-carlos-williams-the-red-wheelbarrow)).

He had, in Teicher's words, a famous maxim, "No ideas but in things," and one of the things was a wheelbarrow: "so much depends / upon // a red wheel / barrow // glazed with rain / water // beside the white / chickens" ([Teicher](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/articles/68731/william-carlos-williams-the-red-wheelbarrow)). Nowhere does Williams tell us why so much depends; "he leaves us to ask, and answer, that question" ([Teicher](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/articles/68731/william-carlos-williams-the-red-wheelbarrow)).

In "This Is Just to Say," the speaker reports eating plums that someone else may have reserved for breakfast, asks forgiveness, and recalls them as "so sweet / and so cold" ([Poets.org](https://poets.org/poem/just-say)).

In his Autobiography, as quoted by Carter, Williams describes medicine and writing as complementary: "they are two parts of a whole, that it is not two jobs at all, that one rests the man when the other fatigues him" ([Carter](https://www.annalsthoracicsurgery.org/article/S0003-4975(99)00293-3/fulltext)). Britannica describes his imagery as making "the ordinary appear extraordinary" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/William-Carlos-Williams)).

I pair him with D180, the Daoist delight in the ordinary, as a cross-tradition reading of its own; it does not attribute Daoist commitments to Williams. What the pairing rests on is the operation the principle describes, attentive sensory pleasure in ordinary things: the plums are tasted as "so sweet and so cold," the wheelbarrow is seen "glazed with rain water." What the story does not establish is the principle's consequence: the Autobiography passage speaks to work rhythm, and the plum poem's speaker acts on appetite and eats food someone else may have reserved, so contentment and the end of chasing remain outside what the opened sources show. The principle's classical frame, DDJ 80's village and [[T009 — Zhuangzi|Zhuangzi]]'s paradox, also stays outside this story.

**Principles:**
- [[D180 — Delight in the ordinary|D180 Delight in the ordinary]] — the doctor-poet's practice and his two signature poems show the principle's noticing operation, attention making an ordinary wheelbarrow and some plums in an icebox carry "so much"; the pairing with the Daoist principle is my cross-tradition reading, and the contentment facet is named as not established by the opened sources.
