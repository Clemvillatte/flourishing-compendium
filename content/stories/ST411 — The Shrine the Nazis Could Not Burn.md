*Warsaw, Frankfurt, London, New York, 1907–1972 · Abraham Joshua Heschel, The Sabbath (1951); the biography from the Jewish Theological Seminary's timeline*

In The Sabbath (1951), Abraham Joshua Heschel calls the seventh day "the inspirer" and the weekdays "the inspired": rest gives activity its orientation. In October 1938 the Gestapo came for Heschel, a thirty-one-year-old rabbi and scholar living in a rented room in Frankfurt. The seminary's timeline records the scene plainly: "He had one hour to pack two suitcases," then "a hellacious three-day journey" by train to Warsaw "in which he had to stand the whole time," the passengers denied entry into Poland and "not enough food to sustain the overcrowded passengers." Six weeks before the German invasion he got out through London, and in March 1940 he arrived in the United States. His sister Esther was killed in the German bombing of Warsaw; his mother was murdered by Nazis who broke into her flat in the Warsaw Ghetto, where his sister Gittel also died; his sister Devorah was deported from Vienna to Auschwitz, where she perished. The JTS timeline places his own words over the escape: "I am a brand plucked from the fire, in which my people was burned to death."

In 1951 he published The Sabbath, a short book about a day. Its argument contrasts weekday preoccupation with things in space with the Sabbath's holiness in time, in the passages below as collected at LibQuotes:

> The meaning of the Sabbath is to celebrate time rather than space. Six days a week we live under the tyranny of things of space; on the Sabbath we try to become attuned to holiness in time. It is a day on which we are called upon to share in what is eternal in time, to turn from the results of creation to the mystery of creation; from the world of creation to the creation of the world.

And:

> The Sabbaths are our great cathedrals; and our Holy of Holies is a shrine that neither the Romans nor the Germans were able to burn.

The inversion the book builds on is this:

> The work on weekdays and the rest on the seventh day are correlated. The Sabbath is the inspirer, the other days the inspired.

Read alongside D039, that sentence offers an analogy for the principle. The principle says make rest the root of activity: not a refueling pause inserted between stretches of work, but the center the week travels from, the way the Daodejing's prince marches all day while staying near his baggage waggons. In Heschel's account the seventh day is not the week's recovery period but its source, the inspirer that the six days express. What the page records is that comparison, with the religious meaning of the day left intact.

**Principles:**
- [[D039 — Make rest the root of activity|D39 Make rest the root of activity]] — Heschel's Sabbath shows rest as the structural root of the week, not the leftover pause.
