Contemporary ACT · David Gillanders, Taking off your armour metaphor

Gillanders describes using this image with a client whose guardedness followed parental neglect. Her protective habits had a history; the metaphor acknowledges that the armour helped. In the situation he reports, she wanted greater closeness with her husband and found it difficult to allow it ([author's text](https://contextualscience.org/files/Taking%20off%20your%20armour%20metaphor.docx)).

The therapist imagines a knight who has worn armour so long that it feels like skin. He asks whether the battle is still being fought and what the armour now prevents. The spoken image closes with questions; the document then instructs the therapist to proceed compassionately and validate the fear. It reports no removal of defences or lasting change ([author's text](https://contextualscience.org/files/Taking%20off%20your%20armour%20metaphor.docx)).

> What is the armour really costing you?
>
> Gillanders, Taking off your armour metaphor ([author's text](https://contextualscience.org/files/Taking%20off%20your%20armour%20metaphor.docx)).

For an essay, the question allows protection and its costs to be considered together. It leaves room to respect what a habit once accomplished while asking whether that same response serves a relationship now.

An editorial application begins by asking whether the present relationship is safe. A small, freely chosen experiment might be expressing one preference to someone trustworthy, then noticing their response before deciding whether to repeat it. The dimensions are affect and overt behaviour. Keeping a boundary, seeking support or declining closeness can also be an appropriate decision; the image gives nobody a claim on another person's vulnerability.

**Principles:**
- [[P155 — Make workability your everyday criterion]]: consider what protection enables and costs relative to a freely chosen relationship.
- [[P156 — Cultivate psychological flexibility — the master skill]]: reconsider a habitual response when the circumstances change.
- [[D156 — Stay flexible — rigidity is death's companion]]: a modern comparison for a way of responding that has become difficult to revise.
