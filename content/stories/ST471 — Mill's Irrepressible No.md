England, 1826-1828 · John Stuart Mill, Autobiography (1873), chapter V · a memoir account, Project Gutenberg ebook #10378

In the autumn of 1826, twenty years old, John Stuart Mill put one question to himself: if every reform he lived for were granted that instant, would he be happy? The answer, as he reports it, was a distinct no, and it collapsed the life he had built on the question. His account of what followed, a recovery that included his reading of Wordsworth in 1828, is the story told here for P165. ([Mill, Autobiography ch. V](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10378/10378-0.txt))

Mill had been educated to a single theory of feeling: on his father's doctrine, feelings were associations, and education's job was to fasten pleasure to the useful and pain to the harmful. His training emphasized analysis, but he came to believe that analysis without complementary cultivation could weaken the feelings his education had tried to foster, and the crisis let him see that cost plainly ([Mill, Autobiography ch. V](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10378/10378-0.txt)):

> "the habit of analysis has a tendency to wear away the feelings"

> "to know that a feeling would make me happy if I had it, did not give me the feeling"

The question and its answer arrived in that frame ([chapter V](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10378/10378-0.txt)):

> "Suppose that all your objects in life were realized; that all the changes in institutions and opinions which you are looking forward to, could be completely effected at this very instant: would this be a great joy and happiness to you?" And an irrepressible self-consciousness distinctly answered, "No!"

> At this my heart sank within me: the whole foundation on which my life was constructed fell down.

What followed was "the dry, heavy dejection of the melancholy winter of 1826-7": he worked on "mechanically, by the mere force of habit." ([Mill, Autobiography ch. V](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10378/10378-0.txt))

The first relief came from an unlikely quarter: reading Marmontel's memoirs, he hit the scene where the boy consoles his family after his father's death ([Mill, Autobiography ch. V](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10378/10378-0.txt)):

> "A vivid conception of the scene and its feelings came over me, and I was moved to tears. From this moment my burden grew lighter. The oppression of the thought that all feeling was dead within me was gone. I was no longer hopeless: I was not a stock or a stone."

The recovery stayed gradual, and it consolidated in the autumn of 1828, when he took up Wordsworth's poems ([Mill, Autobiography ch. V](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10378/10378-0.txt)):

> "What made Wordsworth's poems a medicine for my state of mind, was that they expressed, not mere outward beauty, but states of feeling, and of thought coloured by feeling, under the excitement of beauty. They seemed to be the very culture of the feelings, which I was in quest of."

> "The result was that I gradually, but completely, emerged from my habitual depression, and was never again subject to it."

The experience rewired the creed ([Mill, Autobiography ch. V](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10378/10378-0.txt)):

> "The cultivation of the feelings became one of the cardinal points in my ethical and philosophical creed."

My reading: Mill's account offers an analogy for P165's openness to feeling ([Rogers, On Becoming a Person](https://www.panarchy.org/rogers/person.html)). His analytical training had lacked the complementary cultivation of feeling. Being moved by Marmontel reassured him that his capacity for feeling survived; Wordsworth then helped him cultivate it. The connection is receptivity: experiences of grief, sympathy and contemplative pleasure became resources for a fuller life. The memoir does not establish that Mill was employing Rogersian defensive mechanisms, or that analysis itself is a form of defensiveness. Read alongside P165, the story carries the arc from dried-up feeling to cultivated receptivity.
