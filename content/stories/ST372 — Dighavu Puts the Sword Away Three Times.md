Canonical Buddhist narrative · Mahavagga X.2.3-20

Prince Dighavu has a sword in his hand and the king responsible for his parents' deaths asleep in his lap. He remembers his father's counsel and puts the sword away, then faces the same urge again. His story gives B068 a difficult instance of examining an impulse before acting, with anger returning after the first decision. ([Mahavagga X.2.3-20, sleeping king episode](https://www.vipassana.com/canon/vinaya/mv10-2-3.html))

King Brahmadatta has conquered Dighavu's father's kingdom. The displaced parents live in disguise until they are discovered and condemned to death; Dighavu encounters them as they are being marched through the city. His father tells him, "For vengeance is not settled through vengeance. Vengeance is settled through non-vengeance." After their execution, Dighavu arranges their cremation and goes into the wilderness to weep. ([Mahavagga X.2.3-20, conquest, final counsel and cremation](https://www.vipassana.com/canon/vinaya/mv10-2-3.html))

He returns to the city and learns at an elephant stable beside the palace. The king hears him singing and playing the lute, summons him and takes him into his service. Dighavu gains his trust; on a hunting trip, he drives the king's chariot apart from the entourage. Tired, the king rests his head in the prince's lap and falls asleep. ([Mahavagga X.2.3-20, stable, royal service and hunt](https://www.vipassana.com/canon/vinaya/mv10-2-3.html))

Dighavu thinks of what the king has taken from his family and draws his sword to avenge them. Remembering his father, he thinks, "It would not be proper for me to transgress my father's words," and sheathes it. The text repeats the sequence a second and a third time. ([Mahavagga X.2.3-20, three sheathing episodes](https://www.vipassana.com/canon/vinaya/mv10-2-3.html))

The king wakes frightened by a dream in which Dighavu attacks him. The prince now reveals himself, seizes the king's head and draws his sword again; this is a renewed threat, not an account of anger disappearing. When the king begs for his life, Dighavu answers that it is the king who should grant him his. They agree to spare each other, clasp hands and swear to do one another no harm. ([Mahavagga X.2.3-20, awakening and mutual oath](https://www.vipassana.com/canon/vinaya/mv10-2-3.html))

Back at court, the ministers propose punishments for the prince, but Brahmadatta forbids them to harm him. Dighavu explains his father's counsel: killing the king would bring retaliation from the king's supporters, then further retaliation from his own. Their exchange of protection has interrupted that course. The king restores what he took from Dighavu's father and gives the prince his daughter in marriage. ([Mahavagga X.2.3-20, court, explanation and ending](https://www.vipassana.com/canon/vinaya/mv10-2-3.html))

The practical comparison with B068 concerns repeated reconsideration, followed by an agreement that protects both parties. The Vinaya presents the story as an example of forbearance and gentleness for quarrelling monks, who still reject the Buddha's appeal when he finishes. ([Mahavagga X.2.3-20, conclusion](https://www.vipassana.com/canon/vinaya/mv10-2-3.html); [Mv 10:2.2-20, surrounding monastic frame](https://www.dhammatalks.org/books/NobleWarrior/Section0018.html))

**Principles:**
- [[B068 — Find the gap between stimulus and response|B068 Find the gap between stimulus and response]]: remembering counsel repeatedly interrupts revenge, and the eventual agreement changes what each person can expect from the other.
