First-person account · William James, "What Makes a Life Significant," from Talks to Teachers (1899).

James tells his audience of a week a few summers earlier spent at the Assembly Grounds on Chautauqua Lake. The place was "a serious and studious picnic on a gigantic scale": a town of many thousands with a college, a chorus of seven hundred, athletics, kindergartens, and, he says, "no poverty, no drunkenness, no crime, no police." "I went in curiosity for a day. I stayed for a week" ([James](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/16287/pg16287.txt)).

Then, back in the outside world, he caught himself saying: "Ouf! what a relief! Now for something primordial and savage." Further: "This order is too tame, this culture too second-rate, this goodness too uninspiring." He treats this as a paradox and sets out to explain it. His first explanation is that Chautauqua lacked "strength and strenuousness, intensity and danger." The ideal "was so completely victorious already that no sign of any previous battle remained." That left him gloomy about a world heading toward "a mere Chautauqua Assembly on an enormous scale" ([James](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/16287/pg16287.txt)).

On the train to Buffalo, "the sight of a workman doing something on the dizzy edge of a sky-scaling iron construction brought me to my senses very suddenly." He saw that "I had been steeping myself in pure ancestral blindness, and looking at life with the eyes of a remote spectator." The heroism he missed was around him, in railway bridges, freight-trains and cattle-yards, in "virtue with horny hands and dirty skin." The scene turned him from wanting a better world to seeing the effort already in this one ([James](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/16287/pg16287.txt)).

The compendium reads this beside P183 and P175. Chautauqua has the ideals and little to do with them. The workman has the active part. James's lecture goes on to ask for both together.

Limits: this is James telling his own story to an audience, and nobody else in it speaks. The view of labourers is that of a professor on a train, and James's praise of "the unconscious natural man" romanticises hard and often dangerous work. His wish for "something primordial and savage," even if "as bad as an Armenian massacre," shows a spectator's distance from real violence; it is quoted only to flag that. The story supports a point about meaning, not about what any workman felt.

**Principles:**
- [[P183 — Make your life significant by fusing ideal with active virtue]]: the Assembly has ideals without struggle; the workman has the struggle.
- [[P175 — Seek vital engagement — meaning plus absorption]]: James's own flat reaction to a perfect setting.
