Real person, documented late career · Katsushika Hokusai (1760-1849), from the British Museum's catalogue entry and a curator's essay.

In 1834, at the age of 75 by Japanese counting, Hokusai signed the first volume of One Hundred Views of Mt. Fuji with a new name, Manji, written with a character the museum glosses as long life, and added a printed note. In Henry D. Smith II's translation it reads: "until the age of seventy, nothing that I drew was worthy of notice." At 73 he had "somewhat" understood the growth of plants and trees and the structure of birds, animals, insects and fish. At eighty he hoped to improve, at ninety to see further into the underlying principles of things, and at a hundred to reach "a divine state in my art." The last line of the plan: "at one hundred and ten, every dot and every stroke will be as though alive" ([British Museum catalogue, 1979,0305,0.454.1](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1979-0305-0-454-1)).

He closed it by asking readers to hold him to it: "Those of you who live long enough, bear witness that these words of mine prove not false." The catalogue entry, summarising Henry D. Smith II, says Hokusai regarded Fuji as "a powerful reservoir of immortality" that would help his quest to live past a hundred and "fathom ultimate artistic truths" ([British Museum catalogue](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1979-0305-0-454-1)).

Curator Tim Clark writes that the paintings he made at 88, 89 and 90 are signed with his age and sealed with a red seal bearing the character for "hundred": "Hokusai was literally willing himself to live ever longer." Hokusai died on 10 May 1849 in a humble rented dwelling in Edo, supported by his daughter Eijo (Ōi), herself a painter. His last words are recorded as: "If heaven will extend my life by ten more years...", and then, after a pause, "If heaven will afford me five more years of life, then I'll manage to become a true artist." Eijo wrote to his pupil at once to say that he had died, and added the time of the funeral ([Clark, British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/hokusai-old-master)).

The compendium sets this beside P116 and P114. Arrival never comes in the text. At 75 he is repeating the past as an unfinished apprenticeship, and at 89 he is still asking for time. The growth is shown as dated output, not as a feeling.

Limits: the colophon is also a publisher's promotional device, and the page gives no source for the last words, so they should be read as recorded tradition. The first major biography, by Iijima Kyoshin, appeared in 1893, 44 years after his death. The story does not show what the work felt like to him, or what the cost to his family was.

**Principles:**
- [[P116 — Pursue becoming, not arrival]]: the plan to reach 110 and the request for five more years leave "arrival" permanently ahead.
- [[P114 — Create as growth — creation is the evidence of growth]]: the dated late paintings are the evidence he offers for his own progress.
