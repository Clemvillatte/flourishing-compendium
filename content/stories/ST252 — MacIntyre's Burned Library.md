*Philosophical metaphor · United States, 1981 · [Alasdair MacIntyre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alasdair_MacIntyre)*

After Virtue opens with a thought experiment: imagine a catastrophe in which the natural sciences are blamed for society's ills, scientists are killed, books burned, and institutions destroyed. Later, people attempt to rebuild science from fragments: detached chapters, instruments without manuals, equations without derivations. They would produce something with the outward form of science and none of its coherence, and no one inside the wreckage would know what had been lost.

MacIntyre's claim is that this has already happened to moral discourse, and the book's project is to restore the context: virtue as a practice with internal goods, a tradition, and a narrative of a life, all of which require a community to exist. The moral point is that the fragments, the isolated virtues and values quoted in arguments, are not the thing, and the thing is relational all the way down.
