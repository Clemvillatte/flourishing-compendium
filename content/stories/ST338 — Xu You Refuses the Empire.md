*A tale from the first chapter of the Zhuangzi, in James Legge's translation*

In the opening tale of the Zhuangzi, Yao, the sage-king of the golden age, wants to hand the empire to Xu You, a hermit with no interest in ruling. Yao argues for the gift in two comparisons: torches still burning after the sun and moon have come forth, and farmers still watering the ground while the seasonal rains come down. Both are wasted effort next to what already works, and Yao says he would rather stop vainly occupying the place. It is a generous offer, made with humility.

Xu You declines, and his reason is not moral disgust at power. It is closer to arithmetic. His answer, in Legge's translation:

> 'You, Sir, govern the kingdom, and the kingdom is well governed. If I in these circumstances take your place, shall I not be doing so for the sake of the name? But the name is but the guest of the reality; shall I be playing the part of the guest? The tailor-bird makes its nest in the deep forest, but only uses a single branch; the mole drinks from the He, but only takes what fills its belly. Return and rest in being ruler - I will have nothing to do with the throne. Though the cook were not attending to his kitchen, the representative of the dead and the officer of prayer would not leave their cups and stands to take his place.'

A nest is one branch no matter how large the forest; a belly is one bellyful no matter how wide the river. Taking the throne would buy him a name, and the name, he says, is the guest of the reality, not the reality itself. The kitchen image makes the same point about roles rather than about goods: if the cook left his kitchen, the priests at the altar would not leave their rites to go cook. The compendium's use of the tale takes the branch-and-belly arithmetic as its center: the empire is exactly as big as the portion of it a person uses.
