*Warring States era · from the first chapter of the Zhuangzi, in James Legge's translation*

Yao, the sage-king of the golden age, wants to hand over the empire. His chosen successor is Xu You, a hermit with no interest in ruling, and Yao argues for the gift with real feeling: with Xu You on the throne the world would finally be governed as it deserves, and Yao himself could stop feeling like a torch burning beside the sun and the rain. It is the biggest prize anyone has ever offered anyone.

Xu You declines, and his reason is not moral disgust at power. It is closer to arithmetic. His answer, in Legge's translation:

> 'You, Sir, govern the kingdom, and the kingdom is well governed. If I in these circumstances take your place, shall I not be doing so for the sake of the name? But the name is but the guest of the reality; shall I be playing the part of the guest? The tailor-bird makes its nest in the deep forest, but only uses a single branch; the mole drinks from the He, but only takes what fills its belly. Return and rest in being ruler - I will have nothing to do with the throne. Though the cook were not attending to his kitchen, the representative of the dead and the officer of prayer would not leave their cups and stands to take his place.'

A nest is one branch no matter how large the forest; a belly is one bellyful no matter how wide the river. The empire would add nothing to the only portions of the world a person actually consumes. And the closing image completes the refusal without any heroics: if the cook leaves his kitchen, the priests will not abandon the altar to go cook. The world has its own keepers, and none of its posts needs his ambition to function.
