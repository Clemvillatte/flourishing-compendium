*Film narrative, cross-cultural · United States, 1946 · [It's a Wonderful Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/It%27s_a_Wonderful_Life)*

George Bailey, who has spent his life running the town's savings and loan instead of travelling, building bridges, and doing the great things he dreamed of, faces arrest over a misplaced deposit and stands on a bridge on Christmas Eve. An angel shows him the world without him: the town as Pottersville, the saloon-and-pawnshop dystopia owned by the slumlord Potter, his brother dead in childhood because George was not there to save him, his wife an old maid. George runs back to the bridge and prays to live, and the town, learning of his trouble, gathers in his living room and pays the debt.

The film's communitarian argument is the ledger. George's life, measured by his own dreams, is a record of sacrifices; measured by its effects, the angel shows, it is the thing that held the town together. The film's famous failure at release, followed by its rediscovery through television, is itself part of its story, and the final scene, the pile of money on the table, is the tradition's answer to Solon: you cannot count a life while it is being lived, because the units are other people.
