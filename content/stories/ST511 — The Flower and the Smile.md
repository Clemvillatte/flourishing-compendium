Traditional Chan narrative · the Flower Sermon, via the Wikipedia article on the story.

The Buddha gives a wordless sermon to his disciples by holding up a white flower, and no one in the audience understands it except Mahakasyapa, who smiles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower_Sermon)). That is the whole of the action: a flower held up, a room that does not understand, and one smile. The words that follow in the account name what the flower carried: "I possess the true Dharma eye, the marvelous mind of Nirvana, the true form of the formless, the subtle dharma gate that does not rest on words or letters but is a special transmission outside of the scriptures. This I entrust to Mahākāśyapa" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower_Sermon)).

The connection with D135 is offered as a cross-tradition illustration, with no historical link claimed between this Chan story and the Daoist chapters: the teaching is delivered with no words at all, and the reception is a smile rather than an answer. I read the scene as portraying teaching and recognition through a gesture and a smile, with the words that follow pointing at a gate that does not rest on words or letters. The story is told as an origin of Chan and Zen, the direct transmission of wisdom to Mahakasyapa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower_Sermon)), and it appears as Case 6 of the Gateless Barrier ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower_Sermon)). The account belongs to the tradition's own record rather than to the earliest texts: it appears to have been recorded by Chinese Chan Buddhists, and Wikipedia dates the earliest known version to 1036 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower_Sermon)).

Not carried: the page's everyday channel, example reaching where instruction cannot, with speaking as the weaker channel; the parable keeps to the transmission scene.

**Principles:**
- [[D135 — Teach without words]]: a teaching delivered as a held-up flower and received as a smile, with the entrusting words naming a gate outside the scriptures; the story is honestly marked as a Chan origin legend whose earliest known version Wikipedia dates to 1036.
