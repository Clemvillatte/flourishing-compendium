*San Francisco, 1959–1971 · Shunryū Suzuki and the book his students made from his talks*

Shunryū Suzuki was a Sōtō Zen priest, born in Japan in 1904, who came to the United States in 1959 and taught there until his death in 1971. He had been asked to lead the Japanese Sōtō congregation in San Francisco, and he meant to stay a year or two. In the introduction to his book, his student Richard Baker records why he stayed: "He stayed because he found that Americans have a beginner's mind, that they have few preconceptions about Zen, are quite open to it, and confidently believe that it can help their lives. He found they question Zen in a way that gives Zen life." The teacher of beginner's mind chose the students who already had it.

In those twelve years, with his students, he founded the San Francisco Zen Center and Tassajara, one of the first Buddhist monasteries outside Asia. The teaching he is remembered for fits in one line, the first line of the book his students assembled from his talks:

> "In the beginner's mind there are many possibilities, but in the expert's there are few."

The book, Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind, came out in 1970, a year before he died. It was a collective product: Marian Derby recorded the lectures in Los Altos and made the first draft, and Trudy Dixon edited it with Richard Baker. The polished sentence itself had a previous life. A transcript of a lecture Suzuki gave on November 10, 1965 reads, "In beginner's mind we have many possibilities, but in expert mind there is not much possibility"; Dixon's edit made it read better. When the finished book arrived, Suzuki picked up a copy, looked it over, and said to David Chadwick, who was there: "Good book. I didn't write it, but it looks like a good book."

Suzuki gives shoshin, beginner's mind, its practical warning in the same prologue. Repetition is what wears it out: "Suppose you recite the Prajna Paramita Sutra only once. It might be a very good recitation. But what would happen to you if you recited it twice, three times, four times, or more? You might easily lose your original attitude towards it." And the chapter titled "Nothing Special" extends the point to attainment itself: "If you continue this simple practice every day you will obtain some wonderful power. Before you attain it, it is something wonderful, but after you obtain it, it is nothing special. It is just you yourself, nothing special."

The compendium pairs the story with the Daoist instruction to unlearn daily as a cross-tradition analogy, not a claimed lineage: the same observation about expertise and freshness, made in a San Francisco zendo and in the Dao De Jing's lines about subtracting.
