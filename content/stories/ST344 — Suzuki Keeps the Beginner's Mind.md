*San Francisco, 1959–1971 · Shunryū Suzuki and the book his students made from his talks*

Shunryū Suzuki was a Sōtō Zen priest, born in Japan in 1904, who came to the United States in 1959 and taught there until his death in 1971. In those twelve years, with his students, he founded the San Francisco Zen Center and Tassajara, one of the first Buddhist monasteries outside Asia. The teaching he is remembered for fits in one line, the first line of the book his students assembled from his talks:

> "In the beginner's mind there are many possibilities, but in the expert's there are few."

The book, Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind, came out in 1970, a year before he died. It was a collective product: Marian Derby recorded the lectures in Los Altos and made the first draft, and Trudy Dixon edited it with Richard Baker. The polished sentence itself had a previous life. A transcript of a lecture Suzuki gave on November 10, 1965 reads, "In beginner's mind we have many possibilities, but in expert mind there is not much possibility"; Dixon's edit made it read better. When the finished book arrived, Suzuki picked up a copy, looked it over, and said to a student who was there: "Good book. I didn't write it, but it looks like a good book."

The prose around the famous line is where the teaching actually lives. Suzuki gives shoshin, beginner's mind, a practical warning label: repetition is what wears it out. "Suppose you recite the Prajna Paramita Sutra only once. It might be a very good recitation. But what would happen to you if you recited it twice, three times, four times, or more? You might easily lose your original attitude towards it." Practice improves and simultaneously dulls; expertise accumulates in the same years that freshness drains. His prescription is emptiness rather than effort: "If your mind is empty, it is always ready for anything; it is open to everything." And his test for the beginner's mind is the absence of a particular thought: "In the beginner's mind there is no thought, 'I have attained something.'"
