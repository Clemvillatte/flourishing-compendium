*Scientific/biographical narrative · United States, 2005 · [Sonja Lyubomirsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonja_Lyubomirsky)*

Lyubomirsky, Sheldon, and Schkade's research programme asks why well-being interventions, gratitude diaries, kindness acts, produce modest and inconsistent effects, and their answer is the person-activity fit: the same exercise that helps one person irritates another, and the effect size depends on the match between the activity and the person's motivation, their existing habits, and their culture. Their studies, including the often-cited 2005 Review of General Psychology paper, decompose well-being into a set point, circumstances, and intentional activity, with the intentional activity share, roughly 40 per cent, being the addressable portion.

The 40 per cent figure is frequently misquoted as a law, and the researchers have been careful to bound it, and the programme's real content is the interaction: the intervention is not the active ingredient, the fit is. The finding's clinical consequence is a shift from prescribing exercises to prescribing the matching of exercises, which is the field's version of the ancient doctrine of the mean.
