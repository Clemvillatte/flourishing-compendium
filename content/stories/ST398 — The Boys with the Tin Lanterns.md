Autobiographical essay with a parable · Robert Louis Stevenson, "The Lantern-Bearers" (1888), and William James's use of it.

Every autumn Stevenson and other boys came to a fisher-village on the east coast of Scotland. Toward the end of September, when the nights were black, each boy carried a tin bull's-eye lantern buckled to his waist on a cricket belt, with a buttoned top-coat over it. The lanterns smelled of blistered tin and never burned right. "their use was naught," he writes, yet "a boy with a bull’s-eye under his top-coat asked for nothing more" ([Stevenson, section I](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/614/pg614.txt)). When two boys met, the password was "Have you got your lantern?" and the answer "Yes!" Nobody was supposed to see the light.

The best of it was to walk alone in the black night with the slide shut, "not a ray escaping," "a mere pillar of darkness in the dark," and all the while, "deep down in the privacy of your fool’s heart, to know you had a bull’s-eye at your belt, and to exult and sing over the knowledge." Stevenson then imagines a realist writing it up: the talk merely silly and indecent, the boys cold and wet, all of which was true. Yet "ask the boys themselves, and they are discussing (as it is highly proper they should) the possibilities of existence." Seen from outside they are cold. Seen from inside they are "in the heaven of a recondite pleasure, the ground of which is an ill-smelling lantern" ([Stevenson, section II](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/614/pg614.txt)).

Stevenson's adult case is Dancer, a miser mocked by his neighbours and Harrow schoolboys, whose house is besieged. Why would any man keep up such a life? Stevenson answers that he must have had some joy that outsiders cannot see: "We see them on the street, and we can count their buttons; but heaven knows in what they pride themselves!" ([Stevenson, section II](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/614/pg614.txt)).

William James quotes the essay at length in Talks to Teachers, just after his own mountain-clearing scene. He calls it "an essay which I really think deserves to become immortal, both for the truth of its matter and the excellence of its form" ([James, Talks to Teachers](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/16287/pg16287.txt)).

The compendium uses this beside the clearing in P136. The settlers' clearing was ugly to James and meaningful to them. The lantern is invisible to everyone but the bearer, so the blindness is a matter of what an observer cannot know.

Limits: Stevenson is looking back on his own childhood, and his view of Dancer is a guess, since the miser left no account of himself. It is also an easy step from "there must be a hidden joy" to excusing real harm. The essay says nothing about how to find out what the hidden lantern is.

**Principles:**
- [[P136 — Recognize your blindness — others live in worlds you cannot see]]: the stenographer's record of the boys is accurate and misses the point.
