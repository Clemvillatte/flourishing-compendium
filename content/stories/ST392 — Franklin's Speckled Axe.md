Autobiographical memoir, primary text · Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography, Part II (ed. Pine, Project Gutenberg #20203)

Franklin's proposed daily timetable paired two questions: "What good shall I do this day?" and "What good have I done to-day?" His autobiography describes a written evening examination of faults, kept up with occasional interruptions. ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt))

The timetable belonged to his "bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection" ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt)). He listed thirteen virtues with precepts, from Temperance, "Eat not to dullness; drink not to elevation", to Humility, "Imitate Jesus and Socrates" ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt)). Conceiving that "daily examination would be necessary" (advice he attributed to Pythagoras's Golden Verses), he set up a method for it ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt)).

"I made a little book, in which I allotted a page for each of the virtues", ruled so that he "might mark, by a little black spot, every fault I found upon examination to have been committed respecting that virtue upon that day" ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt)). "I determined to give a week's strict attention to each of the virtues successively", "only marking every evening the faults of the day" ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt)); the timetable put the Morning Question at the day's start, taken with "take the resolution of the day", and the Evening Question at its end, followed by "Examination of the day" ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt)).

He reports how it went: "I enter'd upon the execution of this plan for self-examination, and continu'd it with occasional intermissions for some time. I was surpris'd to find myself so much fuller of faults than I had imagined; but I had the satisfaction of seeing them diminish" ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt)). "My scheme of Order gave me the most trouble", and writing in old age he admits, "In truth, I found myself incorrigible with respect to Order" ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt)). Repeated failures there nearly made him abandon the effort; he compares the temptation to a customer who, tired of turning the grindstone, settles for "I think I like a speckled ax best" ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt)). Something, he writes, that "pretended to be reason" kept suggesting that such exactness might be "a kind of foppery in morals" and that "a benevolent man should allow a few faults in himself, to keep his friends in countenance", an excuse he reports rather than endorses ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt)). His verdict: "tho' I never arrived at the perfection I had been so ambitious of obtaining, but fell far short of it, yet I was, by the endeavour, a better and a happier man than I otherwise should have been if I had not attempted it" ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt)).

Read beside B192, this illustrates the morning-intention/evening-review adaptation: an intention at the day's start, a written review at its end, one behaviour watched at a time, and an evening record that stings and encourages. MN 61's examination of actions before, during and after performance, its assessment of harm, and its responses after harmful conduct are not narrated in this account.
