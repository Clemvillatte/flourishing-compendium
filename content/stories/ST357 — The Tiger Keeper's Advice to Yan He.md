Daoist dialogue and animal-keeping analogies · Zhuangzi, chapter 4, in James Legge's 1891 translation, cross-checked against Robert Eno's.

Yan He is about to tutor the eldest son of Duke Ling of Wei and goes to ask Qu Boyu what to do. The young man, he says, is as bad by nature as a person can be: if he lets the prince go his way, the state is in danger, and if he insists on the right way, his own person is ([Legge, ch. 4 §4](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)). Qu Boyu does not tell him to resist or to give in. He tells him to stay close to the prince in body and in harmony of mind, and to watch for a danger in each: do not be drawn into his pursuits, and do not display how superior you are ([Legge, ch. 4 §4](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)).

Then come three animals. The mantis stretches out its arms to stop a carriage, not knowing it cannot, and Qu Boyu warns that boasting of one's own excellence and colliding with the prince invites the mantis's fate ([Legge, ch. 4 §4](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)). Tiger keepers do not give tigers living animals or whole carcasses, because killing and tearing would excite their rage. They wait until the hunger is satisfied:

> They watch till their hunger is appeased, (dealing with them) from their knowledge of their natural ferocity. ([Legge, ch. 4 §4](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf))

Eno's rendering puts the same lesson in one line: "it’s the trainers who confront them who are killed" ([Eno, §4.3](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/iuswrrest/api/core/bitstreams/5dc89a23-c6bf-4190-a1e4-6762dc5359cc/content)). The third image is a horse lover who keeps the animal's dung in baskets and its urine in jars, yet whose sudden swat at a gadfly sends the horse breaking its bit and bridle. Care that misreads the animal's reactions defeats itself ([Legge, ch. 4 §4](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)).

The passage ends there. It does not say whether Yan He took the post or what became of the prince.

The compendium reads the three images as a sequence for D069. First, do not answer force with a display of your own force; the mantis is the picture of that. Second, studying where the rage comes from and when it peaks is part of staying out of its way. Third, even good intentions can provoke, so watch the effect rather than your motive. The cataract swimmer joins a current already in motion; this dialogue covers what comes earlier, the discipline that keeps you from becoming the next thing the force hits.

Not carried: the passage gives no result, treats a tutor's access to power as a given, and does not say whether the prince can change. It also says nothing about when staying near danger stops being wise. A person in an abusive relationship is not helped by being told to "time" the abuser's rage.

**Principles:**
- [[D069 — Don't meet force with force]]: Qu Boyu's mantis and tiger-keeper images show someone who must deal with a dangerous temper by not provoking it and not mirroring it, while keeping his own judgment.
