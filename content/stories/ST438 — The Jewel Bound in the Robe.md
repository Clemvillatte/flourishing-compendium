Buddhist parable · the Lotus Sutra, chapter 8, in H. Kern's 1884 translation ([Lotus Sutra ch. 8, Kern](https://www.sacred-texts.com/bud/lotus/lot08.htm)).

The chapter's parable, voiced in the disciples' declaration, tells it briefly: "as if some man having come to a friend's house got drunk or fell asleep, and that friend bound a priceless gem within his garment, with the thought: Let this gem be his." ([Lotus Sutra ch. 8, Kern](https://www.sacred-texts.com/bud/lotus/lot08.htm)) After a while the man rises from his seat and travels further, to another country where he meets incessant difficulty and has great trouble finding food and clothing; by great exertion he is hardly able to obtain a bit of food, with which he is contented ([Lotus Sutra ch. 8, Kern](https://www.sacred-texts.com/bud/lotus/lot08.htm)). The stanzas describe him unaware:

> "The other man, unaware of it, goes forth and from that place travels to another town. There he is befallen with misfortune and, as a miserable beggar, seeks his food in affliction." ([Lotus Sutra ch. 8, Kern, stanza 38](https://www.sacred-texts.com/bud/lotus/lot08.htm))

He is content with what begging brings, and "as to that jewel, he has forgotten it; he has not the slightest remembrance of its having been tied in his upper robe." ([Lotus Sutra ch. 8, Kern, stanza 39](https://www.sacred-texts.com/bud/lotus/lot08.htm)) Later the old friend who bound the gem happens to see him again and reprimands him for struggling for food and clothing when a priceless gem, quite sufficient for his wishes, has been bound within his garment ([Lotus Sutra ch. 8, Kern](https://www.sacred-texts.com/bud/lotus/lot08.htm)). In the stanzas: "This friend properly reprimands him and shows him the jewel within his robe." ([Lotus Sutra ch. 8, Kern, stanza 40](https://www.sacred-texts.com/bud/lotus/lot08.htm)) The outcome follows:

> "At this sight the man feels extremely happy. The value of the jewel is such that he becomes a very rich man, of great power, and in possession of all that the five senses can enjoy." ([Lotus Sutra ch. 8, Kern, stanza 41](https://www.sacred-texts.com/bud/lotus/lot08.htm))

The chapter applies the parable to the disciples' own former aspiration, laid in them by the Tathâgata "in previous existences from time immemorial" without their perceiving it ([Lotus Sutra ch. 8, Kern](https://www.sacred-texts.com/bud/lotus/lot08.htm)).

For D065, the compendium reads the rediscovery as an image of returning attention to a neglected resource: the friend redirects the man toward something he already carries ([Lotus Sutra ch. 8, Kern](https://www.sacred-texts.com/bud/lotus/lot08.htm)). That is a cross-tradition analogy; the chapter's own interpretation is the disciples' forgotten aspiration, and the two are kept distinct. The parable does not narrate the man checking himself or changing a habit, so the principle's self-directed check remains a facet this story does not carry.

**Principles:**
- [[D065 — Use the light, return to the insight]]: a man meets hardship while a priceless gem hangs bound in his own garment, and the return happens when the old friend's reprimand redirects him to what he already carries; the parable supplies an image of reverted attention, with the chapter's own application (forgotten aspiration) kept distinct from this analogy.
