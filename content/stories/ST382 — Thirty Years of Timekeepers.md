England, 18th century · biographical account of John Harrison (1693-1776), from [Royal Museums Greenwich](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/harrisons-clocks-longitude-problem) and [MacTutor History of Mathematics](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/HistTopics/Longitude2/)

A clockmaker spent more than thirty years building four sea clocks, refused to send his own finished second machine to sea once he found a flaw in it, and ended with a watch small enough to hold. The prize was longitude at sea, and the 1714 Act put up sums up to £20,000 for a method accurate to half a degree ([MacTutor](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/HistTopics/Longitude2/)).

Harrison brought his first sea clock to London in 1735, "after testing the clock on the River Humber" ([RMG](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/harrisons-clocks-longitude-problem)). On the Lisbon trial of 1736 the payoff arrived early: "As they neared England, Harrison announced that a headland the officers had thought was the Start was in fact the Lizard. He was correct. This meant they were 60 miles off course and in danger." ([RMG](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/harrisons-clocks-longitude-problem)) The clock was right and the officers were wrong, which is the whole prize in one headland.

What follows is the part of the story that carries D018. The Board of Longitude recorded in 1737 that Harrison "proposes to make another machine of smaller dimensions within two years" ([MacTutor](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/HistTopics/Longitude2/)), and the two years became decades: "H2 never went to trial, because Harrison had discovered a fundamental flaw" in his own finished machine ([RMG](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/harrisons-clocks-longitude-problem)); "Harrison began work on his third attempt, H3, in 1740, and would continue to work on it for 19 years" ([RMG](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/harrisons-clocks-longitude-problem)); H4, begun in 1757 and finished two years later ([MacTutor](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/HistTopics/Longitude2/)), was a watch, and Harrison's own pride in it is on record: "I think I may make bold to say, that there is any other mechanical or mathematical thing in the World that is more beautiful or curious in texture than this my watch or time-keeper for the longitude" ([MacTutor](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/HistTopics/Longitude2/)).

Then the ending got the same care. On the 1761 Jamaica trial "H4 having only lost 5 seconds on the whole journey" ([MacTutor](https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/HistTopics/Longitude2/)), within the prize conditions, and the Board still required a second trial at Barbados in 1764 and further demonstration of the principles before recommending payment ([RMG](https://www.rmg.co.uk/stories/time/harrisons-clocks-longitude-problem)). The compendium's reading for D018: the sequence of four machines is pace rather than effort, a ripening that no funding and no impatience could hurry, and the story keeps the chapter's warning about endings in Harrison's own hands: he was the one who would not let a flawed machine out the door, and the one still proving his finished watch a decade after it worked.

**Principles:**
- [[D018 — Give things time|D018 Give things time]]: the four clocks are the principle's gradual-made-visible, thirty years of ripening that ends in a watch, with the endings warning carried by Harrison's own refusal to trial a machine he had found a flaw in and by the second trial demanded after the first succeeded.
