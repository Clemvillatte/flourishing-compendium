*Mythological narrative · Greece, antiquity · [Prometheus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prometheus)*

Prometheus, whose name means forethought, steals fire from the gods and gives it to humanity. The punishment is chained to a rock in the Caucasus, where an eagle eats his liver daily, and the liver regrows nightly, forever. Hesiod tells it as a cautionary tale about Zeus's power; Aeschylus, in Prometheus Bound, makes the Titan the defender of humanity against a tyrant, suffering knowingly, and the play's lost sequels reconciled them.

The myth's Nietzschean reading is explicit in the tradition: Prometheus is the figure of value creation, the one who takes from the gods what belongs to them and gives it to people, at permanent cost. The daily regeneration of the liver, the endlessness of the punishment, is the myth's precision about the cost, and the tradition's readings, from Shelley to the modern, treat the willingness to pay it as the definition of the creative act.
