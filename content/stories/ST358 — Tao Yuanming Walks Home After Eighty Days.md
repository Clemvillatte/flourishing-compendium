Real person, biographical account with a poem · Tao Yuanming (365-427), preface and "Let Me Return!" in David Knechtges's translation.

In the autumn of 405 Tao Yuanming took a small magistrate's post at Pengze, about thirty miles from home, and resigned after eighty days ([Knechtges, Brill 2020](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004438200/BP000012.xml?language=en)). His own preface says why he took it: his family was poor, the grain jar was empty, and the fields at Pengze could pay for wine. It also says why he left. A few days in he longed for home, because "My basic nature is that of spontaneity, and it is nothing that can be forced or tempered" ([Knechtges, preface](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004438200/BP000012.xml?language=en)). He had planned to stay another year, then the death of his younger sister in Wuchang sent him off at once.

The poem he wrote follows the journey: the boat, the wind in his robe, the first sight of the house, a servant and his young son at the gate, the garden paths almost overgrown but the pines and chrysanthemums still there ([Knechtges, ll. 1-32](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004438200/BP000012.xml?language=en)). In its middle, birds are the image of the return:

> Clouds aimlessly emerge from the peaks;
> Birds, weary of flying, know to return home;
> ([Knechtges, ll. 29-30](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004438200/BP000012.xml?language=en))

Later he sets spring and the farmers' work beside the end of his own life: "I like that all things have attained their season; I am moved that my life is approaching its end!" ([Knechtges, ll. 47-48](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004438200/BP000012.xml?language=en)). Knechtges reads the last couplet, "I shall follow changes of nature until my return is complete", as a second return, from the country home to death, which the Zhuangzi, he notes, treats as a return to the natural state of things ([Knechtges, discussion of ll. 59-60](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004438200/BP000012.xml?language=en)).

The famous "five pecks of rice" refusal to bow to an inspector comes from a later history, the Song shu. Tian Xiaofei, quoted by Knechtges, calls it "the least credible part in the biographical account" ([Knechtges](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004438200/BP000012.xml?language=en)). Use the preface, not the anecdote.

The compendium reads this as a human version of what Daodejing 16 shows with plants. D006 says things go out and come back to their root, and that the coming back is stillness, not loss. Tao's homecoming is that shape: a person who goes out for the stipend, returns to what he calls his nature, and sees his own end as the same movement.

Limits: his return is one homecoming, not a repeating cycle, and he does not narrate renewal for himself. Knechtges also observes that Tao may not have been as poor as he claims.

**Principles:**
- [[D006 — Move by return (fu) — everything renews in cycles]]: Tao goes back to his nature, sees spring and his own ending as parts of one movement, and calls the final end a return.
