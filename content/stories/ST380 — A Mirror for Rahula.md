Buddhist canonical account · MN 61, the Ambalatthika-rahulovada Sutta, in Thanissaro Bhikkhu's translation at Access to Insight.

The Buddha's son Rahula, seven years old according to the Commentary ([MN 61, Thanissaro](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.061.than.html)), receives instruction that opens with a household object. The Buddha asks him:

> "What do you think, Rahula: What is a mirror for?"
> "For reflection, sir."
> "In the same way, Rahula, bodily actions, verbal actions, & mental actions are to be done with repeated reflection." ([MN 61, Thanissaro](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.061.than.html))

The examination runs through three moments of the same action. Before acting: "Whenever you want to do a bodily action, you should reflect on it: 'This bodily action I want to do — would it lead to self-affliction, to the affliction of others, or to both? Would it be an unskillful bodily action, with painful consequences, painful results?'" ([MN 61, Thanissaro](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.061.than.html)). The same question is asked while doing the action, with an instruction to give it up if it is leading to affliction, and again after doing it ([MN 61, Thanissaro](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.061.than.html)). A harmful action is to be confessed to the Teacher or a knowledgeable companion, followed by restraint in the future; a harmful mental action is to be met with distress, shame and disgust ([MN 61, Thanissaro](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.061.than.html)). A skillful action gets a different close: "you should stay mentally refreshed & joyful, training day & night in skillful mental qualities" ([MN 61, Thanissaro](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.061.than.html)).

For B192, the compendium reads the mirror exchange as the review practice's founding scene. The image a child can hold is the practice's whole shape: an action, like a face, is something you look at, and the looking happens before, during and after. The sutta does not prescribe a morning intention and an evening review; it attaches the examination to each action as it arises, and the principle's daily format is an adaptation of that. The review it teaches includes the emotional half the principle's plain words keep: acknowledgment of harm without collapse, and an allowance of refreshment and joy when conduct was skillful.

**Principles:**
- [[B192 — Begin the day with intention; end it with review]]: the Buddha's instruction to his son runs the examination before, during and after each action by body, speech and mind, with confession and restraint for harm and refreshment and joy for skillful conduct; the daily morning-and-evening format adapts this action-by-action looking.
