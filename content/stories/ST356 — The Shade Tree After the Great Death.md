Zen biographical account · Hakuin Ekaku (1686–1769), from his autobiographical Tale of My Childhood, translated in Norman Waddell's Hakuin's Precious Mirror Cave ([Waddell, introduction](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf)). Waddell dates Hakuin's initial satori under Shōju to age twenty-three, and notes that these writings contain an unusually large amount of personal history for Zen literature ([Waddell, introduction](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf)).

Hakuin recounts that he traveled over the pass at Mount Tomikura to see the old teacher Shōju Rōjin at Iiyama, who asked how he saw Chao-chou's Mu, then pushed the end of his nose with his fingers ([Hakuin's account, ch. 1](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf)). In Hakuin's words, "He had crushed my pride and arrogance as easily as if he were smashing an egg on a large rock." ([same passage](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf)) The training that follows includes Shōju once picking him up and tossing him off the temple veranda "as if I were a kitten" ([same passage](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf)). Shōju then assigns him a koan, with a lineage story attached ([same passage](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf)):

> "Long ago, after Hsi-keng Rōshi had once died the Great Death, he bored into this story for four long years, and he eventually emerged as a great shade tree without equal in all of China." ([Shōju, quoted in Hakuin's account](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf))

Hakuin gnaws at the koan from the front and from the sides, forgetting food and sleep, and reports: "The tremendous sense of joy I had felt on my previous enlightenment was now all transformed into a boundless agony." ([same passage](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf)) The breakthrough arrives in an unpromising shape: a local madman strikes him about the head with a broom, he topples over unconscious, and on coming to, he finds he has bored through koans "right to their roots and smashed them to smithereens" ([same passage](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf)). Shōju praises the attainment, then warns against stopping there ([same passage](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf)):

> "But don’t be satisfied with this minor attainment. The farther you proceed into enlightenment, the harder you must strive. The greater the understanding you achieve, the more effort you must exert. Do not cease striving until the day you die." ([Shōju, quoted in the same passage](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf))

For B133, the important turn is Shōju's refusal to make realization a finishing point. Hakuin reports a breakthrough; his teacher immediately demands further practice ([Hakuin's account](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf)). The compendium reads the earlier shade-tree image as a figure for what may grow after awakening, an interpretation rather than an outcome the episode itself narrates.

**Principles:**
- [[B133 — Let the self-construct fall away (the Great Death)]]: the account narrates humbled pride, ordeal and a reported breakthrough, and its closing instruction is that the practice continues after realization ([Hakuin's account](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf)); the shade tree stands for what the compendium reads as the point of that continuation.
