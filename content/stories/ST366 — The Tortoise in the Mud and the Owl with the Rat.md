Real person, anecdotes from a philosophical text · Zhuangzi, chapter 17, in Legge's translation and Giles's.

Zhuangzi is fishing in the river Pu when the king of Chu sends two high officials with a message: he wants to entrust him with the whole of his territory. Zhuangzi keeps holding his rod and does not look round. He says he has heard that Chu keeps a sacred tortoise shell, from a creature dead 3,000 years, in a hamper covered with a cloth in the ancestral temple ([Legge, ch. 17 §11](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)).

Then he asks them a question: "Was it better for the tortoise to die, and leave its shell to be thus honoured? Or would it have been better for it to live, and keep on dragging its tail through the mud?" They answer for themselves: it would have been better to live. His reply: "Go your ways. I will keep on drawing my tail after me through the mud" ([Legge, ch. 17 §11](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)). Giles has the same exchange and renders the last line, "Begone! I too will wag my tail in the mud" ([Giles, ch. XVII](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/59709/pg59709.txt)).

The next anecdote reverses the scene. Zhuangzi's friend and rival Huizi is minister in Liang. Someone tells him Zhuangzi has come to take his post, so he searches the kingdom for three days and three nights. Zhuangzi comes to him anyway and tells of a bird called the Young Phoenix, which flies from the south sea to the north and eats and drinks only the finest things. An owl that had got a rotten rat looked up as the phoenix passed and screamed at it. "Do you wish now, in your possession of the kingdom of Liang, to frighten me with a similar scream?" ([Legge, ch. 17 §12](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)).

The compendium puts the two together for D079 and D157. The king offers what the crowd measures success by, and Zhuangzi weighs it against the life it would use up. Huizi, who holds the same office, guards it so hard that he panics at a visit.

Limits: the passages report one man's choice and say nothing about what happened afterward. The anecdote about Huizi is also a jab at a friend, and it would be easy to read Zhuangzi's calm as a boast. The text treats high office as dead flesh without saying that every duty is that.

**Principles:**
- [[D079 — Question conventional measures of success]]: Zhuangzi's tortoise question makes the officials judge a prized relic by the life it cost.
- [[D157 — Don't gamble what matters for what doesn't]]: the stake is a living creature's freedom, set against an honour it cannot enjoy.
