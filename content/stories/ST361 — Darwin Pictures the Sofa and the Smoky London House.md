Real person, private document · Charles Darwin, 1838, on whether to marry; the notes are in the Cambridge University Library.

In the spring and summer of 1838 Charles Darwin, then 29 and back from the Beagle voyage, took a pencil and wrote down what a marriage might do to his work. One note lists where he could live and what it would cost: no travel, limited means, a Cambridge professorship to make the best of, or "poor man" on the outskirts of London ([Darwin Correspondence Project, first note](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/tags/about-darwin/family-life/darwin-marriage)). A second note, which the editors date conjecturally to July, is headed "This is the Question," with the words circled and columns for "Marry" and "Not Marry."

The "Not Marry" column is a tally of freedoms and losses: the clubs, the conversation of clever men, no obligation to visit relatives, but also "fatness & idleness" ([Darwin Correspondence Project, second note](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/tags/about-darwin/family-life/darwin-marriage)). A tally alone would not have settled it. The turn comes in the "Marry" column, where he stops adding and imagines:

> Only picture to yourself a nice soft wife on a sofa with good fire, & books & music perhaps- Compare this vision with the dingy reality of Grt. Marlbro' St. ([Darwin Correspondence Project](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/tags/about-darwin/family-life/darwin-marriage))

He sets a scene beside the room he actually lives in. Then comes the verdict, "Marry-Mary-Marry Q.E.D.," and the rehearsal does not stop there. On the verso he asks when, soon or late, and runs the other side's scenes: the trouble of furnishing a house, the lost French and trips abroad, "horrid poverty." He answers himself in the second person: "Never mind my boy- Cheer up," and ends with "trust to chance-keep a sharp look out" ([Darwin Correspondence Project](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/tags/about-darwin/family-life/darwin-marriage)).

The editors note that, as far as the notes show, he had no definite prospect in mind then. On 11 November Emma Wedgwood accepted him, and he told Charles Lyell the next day, "I determined, when last at Maer, to try my chance" ([Darwin Correspondence Project, note 12](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/tags/about-darwin/family-life/darwin-marriage)). They married on 29 January 1839. In his Autobiography he wrote of her, "She has been my wise adviser and cheerful comforter throughout life, which without her would have been during a very long period a miserable one from ill-health" ([Darwin, Autobiography, ed. Barlow, p. 97](https://darwin-online.org.uk/converted/published/1958_Autobiography_F1497/1958_Autobiography_F1497.html)).

The compendium uses the notes for P039 as a small case of Dewey's idea. Darwin starts out with a list, which is a verdict by tally, but what moves him is a rehearsal: two rooms, two evenings, compared as lived. Limits: the notes are one-sided. They treat a wife mostly as company and order for the man's work, in the idiom of 1838, and Emma's deliberation is not recorded here. The later happiness does not show that this method caused it.

**Principles:**
- [[P039 — Deliberate by rehearsal, not by verdict]]: the pictured sofa against the dingy rooms is a working rehearsal inside a list.
