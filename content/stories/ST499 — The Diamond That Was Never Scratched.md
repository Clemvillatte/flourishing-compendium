United States, 1878 · thought experiment in an essay from Charles Sanders Peirce's "How to Make Our Ideas Clear", the second paper of the Illustrations of the Logic of Science series in Popular Science Monthly, with the text at peirce.org and the Wikisource transcription

In January 1878 the Popular Science Monthly printed the second paper of Peirce's series Illustrations of the Logic of Science, titled "HOW TO MAKE OUR IDEAS CLEAR" ([Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Popular_Science_Monthly/Volume_12/January_1878/Illustrations_of_the_Logic_of_Science_II)). In it Peirce proposed a rule for getting an idea truly clear. A belief, he had argued, is a habit, and "what a thing means is simply what habits it involves" ([Peirce 1878, Section II](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p119.html)). The rule: "Consider what effects, that might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then, our conception of these effects is the whole of our conception of the object" ([Peirce 1878, Section II](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p119.html)).

The maxim asks about conceivable effects, not only observed ones. A habit's identity, Peirce wrote, "depends on how it might lead us to act" even in circumstances that "might possibly occur, no matter how improbable they may be" ([Peirce 1878, Section II](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p119.html)). His test case was almost embarrassingly small: "what we mean by calling a thing hard". The whole conception lies in its effects, for "There is absolutely no difference between a hard thing and a soft thing so long as they are not brought to the test" ([Peirce 1878, Section III](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p119.html)). Then the question: "Suppose, then, that a diamond could be crystallized in the midst of a cushion of soft cotton, and should remain there until it was finally burned up. Would it be false to say that that diamond was soft?" Peirce's answer is deliberately deflating. Nothing would be false in such a way of speaking, only clumsy; the facts would be the same facts, arranged badly ([Peirce 1878, Section III](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p119.html)).

Earlier in the essay, Peirce described the Catholic position on the sacrament: that the elements are literally flesh and blood, though they keep all the sensible qualities of wafer-cakes and diluted wine. His ruling on the dispute: "to talk of something as having all the sensible characters of wine, yet being in reality blood, is senseless jargon", and "It is foolish for Catholics and Protestants to fancy themselves in disagreement about the elements of the sacrament, if they agree in regard to all their sensible effects, here and hereafter" ([Peirce 1878, Section II](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p119.html)). He dropped the theology as soon as he had used it as a logical example.

For P012, the diamond tests what "hard" means by asking what difference it could make under possible conditions, including circumstances never actually encountered. The maxim asks about conceivable practical differences; agreement on every such effect leaves no difference of meaning under the test. The principle's ethical application, the worked example of the good heart, is not carried here.

**Principles:**
- [[P012 — An idea's meaning is its practical effects|P012 An idea's meaning is its practical effects]]: Peirce's own two illustrations of the maxim, the diamond that was never scratched and the wine that is called blood, show the test doing its work; a conception is cashed out in the effects its truth would make conceivable, including effects under circumstances that never arise.
