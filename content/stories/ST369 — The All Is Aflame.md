Canonical Buddhist discourse with a narrative frame · Saṃyutta Nikāya 35.28, Thanissaro Bhikkhu translation; the carrier's introduction identifies the audience as monks who formerly worshiped fire

In SN 35:28 the Buddha tells a thousand monks near Gayā that sense experience itself is burning with the fire of passion, the fire of aversion and the fire of delusion, and the discourse ends by reporting their release ([SN 35:28, Thanissaro](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN35_28.html)). The carrier's introduction identifies the audience as monks "who formerly worshiped fire" ([dhammatalks.org](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN35_28.html)).

The frame is one sentence: "the Blessed One was staying near Gayā at Gayā Head with 1,000 monks. There he addressed the monks" ([SN 35:28, Thanissaro](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN35_28.html)). The diagnosis is one line: "Monks, the All is aflame." ([SN 35:28, Thanissaro](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN35_28.html)) The catalogue then moves through the listeners' own sensing: the eye aflame, forms aflame, eye-consciousness aflame, eye-contact aflame, and whatever arises dependent on eye-contact and is "experienced as pleasure, pain or neither-pleasure-nor-pain" is aflame too ([SN 35:28, Thanissaro](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN35_28.html)). Then the fuel is named:

> Aflame with what? Aflame with the fire of passion, the fire of aversion, the fire of delusion. Aflame, I tell you, with birth, aging & death, with sorrows, lamentations, pains, distresses, & despairs. ([SN 35:28, Thanissaro](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN35_28.html))

The same three fires are repeated at each remaining sense door: ear and sounds, nose and aromas, tongue and flavors, body and tactile sensations, intellect and ideas ([SN 35:28, Thanissaro](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN35_28.html)).

The turn is narrated as a sequence. "Seeing thus, the instructed disciple of the noble ones grows disenchanted with the eye, disenchanted with forms, disenchanted with eye-consciousness, disenchanted with eye-contact." ([SN 35:28, Thanissaro](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN35_28.html)) The sequence extends likewise to each of the other senses ([SN 35:28, Thanissaro](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN35_28.html)). The path out is spelled out in the discourse's own steps:

> Disenchanted, he becomes dispassionate. Through dispassion, he is released. With release, there is the knowledge, 'Released.' He discerns that 'Birth is ended, the holy life fulfilled, the task done. There is nothing further for this world.' ([SN 35:28, Thanissaro](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN35_28.html))

The outcome closes the frame: "Gratified, the monks delighted in the Blessed One's words. And while this explanation was being given, the minds of the 1,000 monks, through lack of clinging/sustenance, were released from effluents" ([SN 35:28, Thanissaro](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN35_28.html)).

The compendium reads the discourse as B016's three-root teaching in narrative form ([SN 35:28, Thanissaro](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN35_28.html)): the roots (passion, aversion, delusion) are named as fires running through experience rather than as vices attached to bad people, and the release is reported to follow the discourse's own sequence of disenchantment and dispassion. The story does not show an individual identifying which root is active in a particular everyday situation; that naming step is what the principle adds for a reader.

**Principles:**
- [[B016 — Know the three root poisons|B016 Know the three root poisons]] — the discourse names passion, aversion and delusion as the fires burning all experience and locates them in the senses rather than in moral character, carrying the principle's three-root diagnosis, its non-moral use, and its consequence sequence. ([SN 35:28, Thanissaro](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN35_28.html))
