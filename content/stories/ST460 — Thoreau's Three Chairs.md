Autobiographical narrative · Thoreau at Walden Pond; the cabin and the village, in his own words

The three chairs are the book's own summary of the arrangement: "I had three chairs in my house; one for solitude, two for friendship, three for society." Thoreau adds: "It is surprising how many great men and women a small house will contain. I have had twenty-five or thirty souls, with their bodies, at once under my roof, and yet we often parted without being aware that we had come very near to one another." ([Walden](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-h/205-h.htm))

Thoreau opens the Visitors chapter with the other side of the scale: "I think that I love society as much as most, and am ready enough to fasten myself like a bloodsucker for the time to any full-blooded man that comes in my way. I am naturally no hermit, but might possibly sit out the sturdiest frequenter of the bar-room, if my business called me thither." ([Walden](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-h/205-h.htm)) He adds: "I had more visitors while I lived in the woods than at any other period in my life; I mean that I had some. I met several there under more favorable circumstances than I could any where else." ([Walden](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-h/205-h.htm)) And he names the arrangement's geography: "In this respect, my company was winnowed by my mere distance from town. I had withdrawn so far within the great ocean of solitude, into which the rivers of society empty, that for the most part, so far as my needs were concerned, only the finest sediment was deposited around me." ([Walden](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-h/205-h.htm))

Thoreau records the forenoon: "After hoeing, or perhaps reading and writing, in the forenoon, I usually bathed again in the pond, swimming across one of its coves for a stint, and washed the dust of labor from my person, or smoothed out the last wrinkle which study had made, and for the afternoon was absolutely free." ([Walden](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-h/205-h.htm)) Then the stroll: "Every day or two I strolled to the village to hear some of the gossip which is incessantly going on there ... which, taken in homœopathic doses, was really as refreshing in its way as the rustle of leaves and the peeping of frogs." He adds: "As I walked in the woods to see the birds and squirrels, so I walked in the village to see the men and boys; instead of the wind among the pines I heard the carts rattle." ([Walden](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-h/205-h.htm))

The railroad is the route he walks to get there. "The Fitchburg Railroad touches the pond about a hundred rods south of where I dwell. I usually go to the village along its causeway, and am, as it were, related to society by this link." ([Walden](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-h/205-h.htm))

**Principles:**
- [[P134 — Grow in solitude and community together|P134 Grow in solitude and community together]]: Thoreau's cabin accommodates time alone and visitors, while his village walks show regular movement between solitude and company; the page illustrates the practical balance and does not claim to demonstrate Dewey's account of personhood or what either phase produced. ([Walden](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-h/205-h.htm))
