Traditional Zen story, "No Water, No Moon", no. 29 of the [ashidakim.com koan collection](https://ashidakim.com/zenkoans/29nowaternomoon.html) (its sources: the thirteenth-century Shasekishū of Mujū and Japanese anecdote books of about 1900)

A nun who could not "attain the fruits of meditation for a long time" was set free by a broken pail ([case 29](https://ashidakim.com/zenkoans/29nowaternomoon.html)). Chiyono studied Zen under Bukko of Engaku, and the story reports the long study without result, then the moonlit night, an old pail bound with bamboo, and the moment the bamboo gave way.

The narrative is a few sentences and the poem she wrote in commemoration ([case 29](https://ashidakim.com/zenkoans/29nowaternomoon.html)): "At last one moonlit night she was carrying water in an old pail bound with bamboo. The bamboo broke and the bottom fell out of the pail, and at that moment Chiyono was set free!" The poem puts the weight on what came before the break:

> In this way and that I tried to save the old pail
> Since the bamboo strip was weakening and about to break
> Until at last the bottom fell out.
> No more water in the pail!
> No more moon in the water!
> ([case 29](https://ashidakim.com/zenkoans/29nowaternomoon.html))

The compendium's reading for B133: the pail is the self as something maintained, an effort rather than a thing, and the poem's first lines are that effort seen from inside, the repair work of a container that was always weakening. The moon in the pail is what the maintained self gets to keep: an image, carried in the water one is forever hauling. When the bottom fell out, the water went and the moon in the water went with it, and the story names what was lost without naming anything that remained. The limit of the story is marked on the principle's own authority: Yamada Mumon Roshi writes that "it is easy to die the Great Death but difficult to regain one's life after that", and that "returning eko, going back into the world to work for living beings after attaining realization, is a very arduous undertaking" ([Tricycle, "Hakuin's Tears of Awakening"](https://tricycle.org/article/hakuin-ekaku-awakening/)). This story stops at the release; that return half is carried by [ST356](https://github.com/Clemvillatte/flourishing-compendium/blob/main/content/stories/ST356%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Shade%20Tree%20After%20the%20Great%20Death.md), the account of Hakuin's teacher refusing to let realization be a finishing point.

**Principles:**
- [[B133 — Let the self-construct fall away (the Great Death)|B133 Let the self-construct fall away (the Great Death)]]: the nun's release arrives when the pail she laboured to save breaks, the maintenance effort and the moon-image going out with the water; the return half of the principle is named as not carried.
