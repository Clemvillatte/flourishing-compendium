Buddhist sutta · Majjhima Nikaya 20, Vitakkasanthana Sutta, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu

The discourse addresses a monk intent on the heightened mind, in whom thoughts imbued with desire, aversion or delusion arise. The Buddha's teaching on relaxing such thoughts offers a ladder of five moves, each an operation on the thought rather than a debate with it, and each rung ends the same way: the thoughts are abandoned and subside, and the monk steadies his mind right within. ([MN 20, Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.020.than.html))

The opening names the situation plainly: "There is the case where evil, unskillful thoughts — imbued with desire, aversion, or delusion — arise in a monk while he is referring to and attending to a particular theme." The first move is to attend to another theme, apart from that one, connected with what is skillful, and the sutta gives it a carpenter's picture: a small peg to knock out, drive out and pull out a large one. ([MN 20](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.020.than.html))

If the thoughts still arise, the next rung is offered. The monk scrutinizes their drawbacks, and the sutta puts the reflection in his own mouth: "Truly, these thoughts of mine are unskillful, these thoughts of mine are blameworthy, these thoughts of mine result in stress." If they still arise, he pays them no mind and no attention. If they still arise, he attends to the relaxing of thought-fabrication with regard to those thoughts. And if they still arise, "with his teeth clenched and his tongue pressed against the roof of his mouth — he should beat down, constrain, and crush his mind with his awareness." ([MN 20](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.020.than.html))

The walking-man simile belongs to the fourth method in the sutta's own arrangement: "Just as the thought would occur to a man walking quickly, 'Why am I walking quickly? Why don't I walk slowly?' So he walks slowly. The thought occurs to him, 'Why am I walking slowly? Why don't I stand?' So he stands": through sitting and lying down. "In this way, giving up the grosser posture, he takes up the more refined one." ([MN 20](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.020.than.html))

The outcome is mastery, and the wording is the point: "He is then called a monk with mastery over the ways of thought sequences. He thinks whatever thought he wants to, and doesn't think whatever thought he doesn't." This page compares MN 20 with B074 as an illustration of working with unwanted thoughts: the cited instruction evaluates their unskillfulness and offers ways to abandon them; it does not establish whether a thought is factually true or false. ([MN 20](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.020.than.html))

**Principles:**
- [[B074 — Thoughts are not facts|B074 Thoughts are not facts]]: MN 20's ladder evaluates unwanted thoughts for unskillfulness and offers ways to abandon them; an illustration of working with thoughts as workable events, without assessing their truth or falsity.
