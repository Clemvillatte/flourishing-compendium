Ecological account · the wolves, elk, willows, beavers and streams of Yellowstone National Park, via a decade-long experiment reported in Proceedings of the Royal Society B, a National Geographic Education page, and the International Wolf Center's 2024 review of the science.

The experimental finding comes first, because it is what was actually measured. Marshall, Hobbs and Cooper reported in 2013 the results of a decade-long experiment in Yellowstone: "moderating browsing alone was not sufficient to restore riparian zones along small streams", and "restoration of willow communities depended on removing browsing and restoring hydrological conditions that prevailed before the removal of wolves" ([Marshall et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3574379/)). Their summary line carries the history: "The 70-year absence of predators from the ecosystem changed the disturbance regime in a way that was not reversed by predator reintroduction" ([Marshall et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3574379/)).

The popular account runs the other way. The International Wolf Center's review states it as the popular narrative: 41 wolves reintroduced from 1995-1997 caused a dramatic recovery through a trophic cascade, fewer elk eating less vegetation letting aspen and willow thrive, "More plants meant more songbirds, beavers, and other species. And more beavers and vegetation meant less stream bank erosion and the recovery of wetland and riparian species" ([International Wolf Center](https://wolf.org/wolf-info/international-wolf-magazine/do-wolves-really-change-rivers-2/)). National Geographic Education tells it at full strength: wolves reintroduced in 1995 "resulting in a trophic cascade through the entire ecosystem", elk doubled with their predator gone, the elk's hooves eroding the riverbanks so "the rivers and streams clouded with soil", and after the wolves' return, "the return of the beaver dams creates new habitats for fish, amphibians, reptiles, and even otters" ([NatGeo Education](https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/wolves-yellowstone/)). The review's own conclusion holds both halves: "recovery of aspen and willow communities in Yellowstone since wolf reintroduction has not been as strong or widespread as claimed. Still, there has been some recovery, suggesting a trophic cascade has unfolded" ([International Wolf Center](https://wolf.org/wolf-info/international-wolf-magazine/do-wolves-really-change-rivers-2/)).

The connection with B015 is offered as my comparison, not a scientific conclusion: elk, willow, beaver, stream and wolf appear in each other's business, and I use the experiment's finding as an illustration of interdependence, since moderating browsing alone was not sufficient and restoration of willow communities depended on removing browsing and restoring hydrological conditions ([Marshall et al. 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3574379/)). The draft makes no claim that the popular cascade account is established, and no claim about unanimity among scientists.

Not carried: the page's practice of listing what had to be true for a thing to be here; the elk and willows carry that lesson for themselves.

**Principles:**
- [[B015 — Nothing exists alone (interbeing)]]: the Yellowstone web told with the experimental finding first, the popular cascade attributed as popular, and the interbeing reading labelled as a comparison rather than a finding.
