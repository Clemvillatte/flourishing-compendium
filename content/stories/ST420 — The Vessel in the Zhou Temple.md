Literary-historical account, from the Shuoyuan · Confucius tests the temple vessel with water; the connection to D029 is this page's, not the anecdote's.

Confucius walks into the Zhou temple and sees a tilted vessel. He asks the keeper what it is; the keeper answers that it is said to be an ornament for the right side of a seat. Confucius has heard of the type and states what he knows: "I have heard that this vessel, when full, overturns; when empty, tilts; and only when half-full stands upright. Is there such a thing?" The keeper says yes, and rather than leave the matter as a report, Confucius has it tested. In the anecdote, Zilu brings the water, and the vessel does what was said of it: "When filled, the vessel overturned; when half-full, it stood upright; and when empty, it tilted." Confucius' response is the sigh the passage gives him: "Alas! How can there be anything that is full yet does not overturn!" (Shuoyuan, [Jing Shen, Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/shuo-yuan/jing-shen/zh); Chinese verified verbatim, English renderings editorial).

The sigh turns the demonstration into a question, and the question comes from Zilu: "May I ask if there is a way to maintain fullness without downfall?" The answer is short and is the story's hinge: "The way to hold onto fullness is to draw from it and reduce it" (Shuoyuan, [Jing Shen, Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/shuo-yuan/jing-shen/zh); English editorial). The passage then gives the method its shape: to be high yet able to descend, full yet able to remain empty, wealthy yet frugal, wise yet able to appear foolish, brave yet able to seem timid, which it calls reduction without reaching the extreme (Shuoyuan, [Jing Shen, Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/shuo-yuan/jing-shen/zh); Chinese verified verbatim, 高而能下，滿而能虛，富而能儉，貴而能卑，智而能愚，勇而能怯，辯而能訥，博而能淺，明而能闇；是謂損而不極).

Read beside D029, the anecdote makes excess visible: in the story, the vessel stands upright at the middle and overturns when full. Zilu's question adds a practical angle, and the passage answers it: preserving fullness requires drawing something away, rather than continually adding more. The Chinese line the vessel obeys is three clauses, 滿則覆，虛則欹，中則正 — full it overturns, empty it tilts, at the middle it stands upright (Shuoyuan, [Jing Shen, Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/shuo-yuan/jing-shen/zh); Chinese verified verbatim, gloss editorial).

**Principles:**
- [[D029 — Stop before the cup overflows]]: The temple vessel stands upright at half and overturns when full in the anecdote, and the passage's teaching answers Zilu's question about holding fullness with drawing from it and reducing it.
