*Therapeutic metaphor · 2005 · retold from Steven C. Hayes, Get Out of Your Mind and Into Your Life*

You find a baby tiger on your doorstep. He is small, and he is hungry, and it seems cruel to leave him there, so you take him in until a shelter can be found. The next morning he is growling with hunger, so you feed him — and the feeding works. He is calm again. He is also bigger.

Days pass like this. Every feeding settles him and grows him, and now the growls are frightening, and the safest-seeming move is to feed him sooner. From inside the loop, feeding the tiger is not a mistake; it is the only thing that has ever worked.

The tiger stands for the relief-seeking that can grow around a painful feeling: the avoidance that soothes the anxiety today and makes the anxiety larger tomorrow. The metaphor is not a claim that soothing yourself is wrong, or that the feeling is a dangerous animal. It is a description of one specific cycle — immediate relief that reinforces avoidance — and an invitation to notice when relief has stopped being information about what helps.

Retold from Hayes (2005, pp. 36–37), as presented in Stoddard & Afari, *The Big Book of ACT Metaphors* (2014), pp. 18–19.
