Traditional Daoist parable · from the Huainanzi, chapter 18, "In the World of Man" (2nd century BCE), via the English rendering printed in the Wikipedia article, attributed there to Claude Larre et al.

A man near the border loses his horse for no reason, and everyone feels sorry for him; his father asks, "Who knows if that won't bring you good luck?" Months later the horse returns with a group of noble barbarian horses, and everyone congratulates him; his father asks, "Who knows if that won't bring you bad luck?" The house is now rich in horses, the son rides with joy, falls, and breaks his leg; everyone feels sorry for him, and his father asks again, "Who knows if that won't bring you good luck?" A year later the barbarians invade, the adult men string up their bows and go to battle, and nine out of ten border residents are killed, except the son, because of his broken leg; father and son both survive ([Wikipedia, rendering attributed to Larre et al.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_old_man_lost_his_horse)). The parable opens with a line that frames it: "Good luck and bad luck create each other and it is difficult to foresee their change" ([Wikipedia, rendering attributed to Larre et al.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_old_man_lost_his_horse)).

The connection with D110 is the father's three replies doing the holding. The neighbours arrive with the verdict ready, first sorry, then glad, then sorry again, and each time he declines to let the verdict settle. I read the repeated questions as keeping judgment provisional: the father holds each event lighter than the reaction around it, and the rendering makes no bet on a good outcome. The closing line names why: bad luck brings good luck and good luck brings bad luck, "without end and nobody can estimate it" ([Wikipedia, rendering attributed to Larre et al.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_old_man_lost_his_horse)). The story survives as the chengyu Sai Weng Shi Ma, "the old man lost his horse, but it all turned out for the best," which the tradition uses to mean, how could one know it was not good fortune ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_old_man_lost_his_horse)).

Not carried: the page's psychological-parallel content, affective forecasting and stress appraisal, and its mid-story example belong to the principle page; the parable carries the refusal to issue a verdict from the middle of the chain, and stops there.
