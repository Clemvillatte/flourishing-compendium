Chicago, 1896–1903 · John Dewey's Laboratory School; the model-farm episode is recorded in Mayhew and Edwards' history of the school, quoted in Robert B. Westbrook's UNESCO profile of Dewey

In January 1896 the Laboratory School of the University of Chicago opened its doors with sixteen children and two teachers; by 1903 it was instructing 140 students with twenty-three teachers and ten graduate assistants, and was already known as the Dewey School ([Westbrook 1993](https://cyc-net.org/cyc-online/cyconline-apr2010-dewey.html)). At the centre of its curriculum was what Dewey termed the occupation: "a mode of activity on the part of the child which reproduces, or runs parallel to, some of work carried on in social life" ([Dewey 1899, p. 92](https://cyc-net.org/cyc-online/cyconline-apr2010-dewey.html)). The youngest children, four and five years old, cooked, sewed and did carpentry. Dewey reported: "the child comes to school to do; to cook, to sew, to work with wood and tools in simple constructive acts; within and about these acts cluster the studies 'writing, reading, arithmetic, etc'" ([Dewey 1896, p. 245](https://cyc-net.org/cyc-online/cyconline-apr2010-dewey.html)). Mistakes were built into the design: the method introduced children to experimental problem-solving "in which mistakes were an important part of learning," and Dewey insisted that "(even at the expense of experimentation and error) the mind is not really freed" ([Dewey 1903, p. 237](https://cyc-net.org/cyc-online/cyconline-apr2010-dewey.html)).

The model-farm episode shows the design working. When their sand-table farm had to be divided into several fields for wheat, corn, oats, and for the house and the barn, the children took a one-foot ruler as their unit and met what halves and fourths are. The record notes that "the divisions made, though not accurate, were near enough to allow them to mark off their farm" ([Mayhew & Edwards, pp. 83-84](https://cyc-net.org/cyc-online/cyconline-apr2010-dewey.html)). Building the farm-house needed four corner posts and six or seven slats all of the same height. The children "frequently forgot to keep the left-hand edge of the ruler on the left-hand side of the slat, so the measurements had to be repeated two or three times before they were correct. What they did to one side of the house, they also did to the other and naturally worked more rapidly and more accurately as the work was repeated" ([Mayhew & Edwards, pp. 83-84](https://cyc-net.org/cyc-online/cyconline-apr2010-dewey.html)). As they learned the half-foot, the quarter-foot and the inch, "finer work was naturally expected of them and obtained" ([Mayhew & Edwards](https://cyc-net.org/cyc-online/cyconline-apr2010-dewey.html)).

The compendium reads the farm as a working model of existence as both precarious and stable. The first measurements were the precarious: approximate, error-prone, near enough. The stable was not given to the children in advance; it grew out of repetition, out of the two or three wrong trials, until the ruler became habit. The school's wager was to put the precarious first — real materials, real error, first-hand experience — and let the stable be earned rather than imposed.

**Principles:**
- [[P190 — Existence is both precarious and stable|P190. Precarious and stable]] — the compendium reads the farm as a working model of the principle: stability (the accurate measurement, the habit) was produced out of precarious first attempts, not substituted for them.
- [[P038 — Make habit your flywheel|P38. Habit as flywheel]] — the repeated measurements show habit forming in the open: the same act done two or three times wrongly, then more rapidly and more accurately without being told.
