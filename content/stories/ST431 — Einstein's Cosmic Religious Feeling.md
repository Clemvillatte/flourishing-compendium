Biographical account with primary text · Albert Einstein, "Religion and Science" (1930), the 1929 Spinoza telegram, and Autobiographical Notes (quoted via Wikipedia)

Einstein's own account of his childhood religion ends abruptly: "... I came—though the child of entirely irreligious (Jewish) parents—to a deep religiousness, which, however, reached an abrupt end at the age of twelve. Through the reading of popular scientific books I soon reached the conviction that much in the stories of the Bible could not be true" ([Autobiographical Notes, quoted in Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_and_philosophical_views_of_Albert_Einstein)). The container broke; the question is what, if anything, was inside it.

The world kept asking him. On 24 April 1929, Rabbi Herbert S. Goldstein cabled him to ask, in effect, where he stood, and Einstein answered in words that made headlines: "I believe in Spinoza's God, who reveals himself in the harmony of all that exists, not in a God who concerns himself with the fate and the doings of mankind" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_and_philosophical_views_of_Albert_Einstein)). A year later he published the essay "Religion and Science" (1930), where he sorted religion into three stages and kept only the third, the one he named directly: "I shall call it cosmic religious feeling", a kind of feeling that "knows no dogma and no God conceived in man's image" ([Religion and Science](https://merrell-wolff.org/sites/default/files/Religion%20and%20Science.pdf)). What the individual feels, in his description, is "the futility of human desires and aims and the sublimity and marvelous order which reveal themselves both in nature and in the world of thought", and he "wants to experience the universe as a single significant whole" ([Religion and Science](https://merrell-wolff.org/sites/default/files/Religion%20and%20Science.pdf)).

The essay then makes the claim that gave his position its edge: "I maintain that the cosmic religious feeling is the strongest and noblest motive for scientific research", and "it is the most important function of art and science to awaken this feeling and keep it alive in those who are receptive to it" ([Religion and Science](https://merrell-wolff.org/sites/default/files/Religion%20and%20Science.pdf)). The feeling needs no doctrine to be had; science, of all things, is one of its keepers.

Read beside P187, Einstein is one documented case of the principle's core distinction: the boy lost the Bible stories and kept the awe, which then found a new, unlikely container — theoretical physics. This is Dewey's point lived out, the experience not requiring the doctrine, though Einstein's formulation is his own, not Dewey's, and it pleased neither side: believers rejected his impersonal God, and atheists rejected his refusal of the label. The draft does not claim everyone leaving a tradition keeps the goods; it claims one man did, and said so in print. ([P187](https://github.com/Clemvillatte/flourishing-compendium/blob/main/content/principles/P187%20%E2%80%94%20Separate%20the%20religious%20quality%20of%20experience%20from%20religion.md))

**Principles:**
- [[P187 — Separate the religious quality of experience from religion|P187 Separate the religious quality of experience from religion]]: Einstein's arc carries the experience-vs-container distinction — childhood religiousness ending at twelve, the cosmic religious feeling of the 1930 essay surviving without dogma or an anthropomorphic God, and science named as its cultivator; Dewey's own proposals and the everyone-keeps-the-goods generalisation are not claimed. ([Religion and Science](https://merrell-wolff.org/sites/default/files/Religion%20and%20Science.pdf))
