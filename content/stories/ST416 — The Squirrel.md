The mountains, some years before 1907 · William James, Pragmatism (1907), Lecture II, "What Pragmatism Means" · a personal anecdote inside a lecture, Project Gutenberg raw text

A camping party splits in two over a question with no visible stakes, and the story of that split is the cleanest narrative version of P012's rule that an idea's meaning is its practical effects. James came back from a walk to find the quarrel in full cry, and settled it in one move by asking what the disputed words would practically mean ([Pragmatism](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5116/pg5116.txt)):

> "Some years ago, being with a camping party in the mountains, I returned from a solitary ramble to find everyone engaged in a ferocious metaphysical dispute. The corpus of the dispute was a squirrel--a live squirrel supposed to be clinging to one side of a tree-trunk"

> "The resultant metaphysical problem now is this: DOES THE MAN GO ROUND THE SQUIRREL OR NOT? He goes round the tree, sure enough, and the squirrel is on the tree; but does he go round the squirrel?"

The squirrel keeps the tree between itself and the man, so the man never passes from its belly side to its back side. James's settlement ([Pragmatism](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5116/pg5116.txt)):

> "Which party is right," I said, "depends on what you PRACTICALLY MEAN by 'going round' the squirrel."

> "Make the distinction, and there is no occasion for any farther dispute. You are both right and both wrong according as you conceive the verb 'to go round' in one practical fashion or the other."

He then names the distinction as the method itself ([Pragmatism](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5116/pg5116.txt)):

> "I tell this trivial anecdote because it is a peculiarly simple example of what I wish now to speak of as THE PRAGMATIC METHOD."

> "The pragmatic method in such cases is to try to interpret each notion by tracing its respective practical consequences. What difference would it practically make to anyone if this notion rather than that notion were true? If no practical difference whatever can be traced, then the alternatives mean practically the same thing, and all dispute is idle."

My reading: P012's test asks what conceivable practical differences follow from a conception's truth, and the anecdote shows the test doing its work on a single verb. Before the distinction, the campers had two beliefs that appeased the same doubt with different words, which is the exact state the principle calls an unclear conception. James cashes "going round" out twice: passing the squirrel's north, east, south and west, or staying first in front, then at his right, then behind, then at his left. Both cashes are complete, so both answers are right for their own question, and the quarrel has nothing left to feed on. Note what the story does not do: no one acts on either meaning. The effects are named as positions a walker would occupy, which fits the principle's point that the net is wider than immediate behaviour. The story does not carry Peirce's deeper grounding of the maxim in belief as a habit of action; it carries the maxim itself, at work on one stubborn word.

**Principles:**
- [[P012 — An idea's meaning is its practical effects|12. An idea's meaning is its practical effects]] — the camping-party quarrel ends when "going round the squirrel" is cashed out as two complete sets of practical differences, showing a dispute without a named difference to be a dispute about nothing
