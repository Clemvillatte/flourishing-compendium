2004 · experimental narrative · Masuda, Hayes, Sackett and Twohig

Eight female undergraduates took part in two small experiments. In the defusion training, they first said the word milk and noticed its associations. They then repeated it rapidly for thirty seconds. Participants described its meaning receding and its sound becoming more prominent; the procedure was subsequently applied to a word representing a personally troubling thought ([Method, pp. 479-480](https://contextualscience.org/sites/default/files/305%20Defusion%20word%20repetition%20BRAT%202004.pdf)).

Ratings of discomfort and believability were lower after the defusion condition than after the comparison conditions. The researchers carefully limited what this could establish ([Results and General discussion, pp. 480-484](https://contextualscience.org/sites/default/files/305%20Defusion%20word%20repetition%20BRAT%202004.pdf)).

> they provide no evidence that such procedures are better than any existing clinical intervention.
>
> Masuda and colleagues, p. 484 ([primary paper](https://contextualscience.org/sites/default/files/305%20Defusion%20word%20repetition%20BRAT%202004.pdf)).

An editorial application is to notice an ordinary, neutral word as a sound, then return to a chosen activity. Compare whether the brief change in attention helped the return; retain it only if useful. Cognition and attention are the dimensions being examined. There is no need to rehearse a painful self-description to understand the image.

The story supports curiosity about how words affect us. This limited laboratory result does not establish clinical usefulness or lasting benefit, decide whether a thought is true, or justify replacing care for someone in distress.

**Principles:**
- [[P159 — Defuse — see thoughts as thoughts]]: the spoken form stays recognisable while the person's immediate experience of it changes.
