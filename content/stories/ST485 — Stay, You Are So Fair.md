Germany, 1808-1832 · Johann Wolfgang von Goethe's Faust, the wager in Part I and the final scene of Part II · a tragedy, Project Gutenberg raw texts

Goethe spent sixty years on Faust, and the plot he built is P151's claim, that a person is never finished being formed, staged as a wager with the devil. The old scholar Faust bets his soul on a condition: he loses only if he ever wants a moment to stop. The bet is the principle's test, written as poetry ([Faust, Part I, Taylor translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/14460/pg14460.txt)):

> "Whenever to the passing hour / I cry: O stay! thou art so fair! / To chain me down I give thee power / To the black bottom of despair!"

Faust then lives out the bet. At the end of Part II, old and blind, planning land for a free people, he speaks the line the wager waited for, in the German text ([Faust, Part II](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2230/pg2230.txt)):

> "Zum Augenblicke dürft' ich sagen: / Verweile doch, du bist so schön!"

To the moment I may say: stay, you are so fair. That is my translation; the German is quoted word-for-word. He falls dead on the words. But his speech just before it carries the other side of the principle ([Faust, Part II](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2230/pg2230.txt)):

> "Nur der verdient sich Freiheit wie das Leben, / Der täglich sie erobern muß."

Only he deserves freedom as he deserves life, who must win it anew each day. That is again my translation. And the angels who carry his soul off answer the wager ([Faust, Part II](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2230/pg2230.txt)):

> "Gerettet ist das edle Glied / Der Geisterwelt vom Bösen, / Wer immer strebend sich bemüht, / Den können wir erlösen."

Saved is the noble member of the world of spirits from evil: whoever strives with all his might, him can we redeem. My translation, as marked. And the angels' speech continues ([Faust, Part II](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2230/pg2230.txt)):

> "Und hat an ihm die Liebe gar / Von oben teilgenommen, / Begegnet ihm die selige Schar / Mit herzlichem Willkommen."

And if love from above has taken part in him, the blessed host meets him with heartfelt welcome. Again my translation.

My reading: the play agrees with the principle's plain words that an identity is a snapshot and not a destination. Faust's wager makes settling the act that costs him everything: the moment he tells the passing hour to stay, time is over for him. What the ending weighs is his unceasing striving, the creed that freedom and life must be won anew daily, which is the principle's fit-checked-not-assumed in verse form. The play does not grant redemption to striving alone: the angels' speech continues with love from above taking part in him, and I have quoted that continuation rather than cutting it. The story does not carry Simondon's theory of individuation as operation, which the principle page supplies; it carries the same idea in the shape of a man who is lost at rest and weighed, at the end, by his motion.

**Principles:**
- [[P151 — Keep individuating — never settle into a final form|151. Keep individuating: never settle into a final form]] — Faust's wager makes the wish to stop the moment the forfeit, and the ending weighs his ceaseless striving, with love from above, rather than any finished man
