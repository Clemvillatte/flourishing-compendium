Historical exchange · 1929; documented in Gerald Holton, Einstein's Third Paradise (Dædalus, 2003), with Einstein's 1930 essay Religion and Science

A Boston cardinal called Einstein's relativity "befogged speculation producing universal doubt about God and His Creation" ([Holton 2003](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/essay-Einsteins-Third-Paradise.pdf)), and a New York rabbi wired Einstein a paid question: "Do you believe in God? Stop. Answer paid 50 words." ([Holton 2003](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/essay-Einsteins-Third-Paradise.pdf)) Einstein answered in twenty-five German words that he believed in Spinoza's God, "Who reveals Himself in the lawful harmony of the world, not in a God Who concerns Himself with the fate and the doings of mankind." ([Holton 2003](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/essay-Einsteins-Third-Paradise.pdf)) Beside P187, the exchange shows religious feeling without an anthropomorphic God: the reply rejects a God concerned with human fate, and Einstein's later essay describes the feeling behind his work. ([Einstein 1930](https://sacred-texts.com/aor/einstein/einsci.htm))

The charge came from Cardinal O'Connell of Boston, who warned of "the ghastly apparition of atheism." ([Holton 2003](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/essay-Einsteins-Third-Paradise.pdf)) Alarmed, Rabbi Herbert S. Goldstein sent the telegram; the reply, for which Holton notes Einstein "needed but twenty-five (German) words", stated his beliefs succinctly ([Holton 2003](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/essay-Einsteins-Third-Paradise.pdf)). Goldstein read the reply as evidence that Einstein was not an atheist, and declared that "Einstein's theory, if carried to its logical conclusion, would bring to mankind a scientific formula for monotheism." ([Holton 2003](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/essay-Einsteins-Third-Paradise.pdf)) Holton adds that Einstein wisely remained silent on that point ([Holton 2003](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/essay-Einsteins-Third-Paradise.pdf)).

In his 1930 essay, Einstein describes a third stage of religious experience he called cosmic religious feeling, in which "The individual feels the futility of human desires and aims and the sublimity and marvelous order which reveal themselves both in nature and in the world of thought." ([Einstein 1930](https://sacred-texts.com/aor/einstein/einsci.htm)) Einstein writes that "there is no anthropomorphic conception of God corresponding to it." ([Einstein 1930](https://sacred-texts.com/aor/einstein/einsci.htm)) He called it "the strongest and noblest motive for scientific research." ([Einstein 1930](https://sacred-texts.com/aor/einstein/einsci.htm))

The compendium's reading for P187: the reply rejects a God concerned with human fate, while the later essay describes the awe at nature's order as the strongest motive of research; Goldstein's response shows a different interpretation of the telegram, and Einstein's reported silence establishes no further rejection. The page does not carry Dewey's positive program of cultivating the quality in ordinary experience, and it makes no claim about the truth of theism or about religion generally.

- [[P187 — Separate the religious quality of experience from religion|P187 Separate the religious quality from religion]]: the reply rejects a God concerned with human fate while keeping the awe at nature's order, which Einstein's later essay names cosmic religious feeling; Goldstein's response shows a different interpretation of the telegram.
