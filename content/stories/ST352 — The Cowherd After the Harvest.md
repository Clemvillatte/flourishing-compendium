Canonical retrospective account with embedded similes · MN 19, Two Sorts of Thinking, translated by Thanissaro Bhikkhu.

In MN 19, the Buddha describes training his thinking before awakening through two pictures of a cowherd: one guarding ripening crops, the other watching after the harvest ([MN 19](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html)). The teaching connects what the mind repeatedly pursues with the direction it comes to take ([MN 19](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html)).

The account begins with a division: thoughts of sensuality, ill will and harmfulness on one side; renunciation, non-ill will and harmlessness on the other ([MN 19, opening account](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html)). When harmful thinking arises, he considers its cost to himself and others, and abandons it ([MN 19](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html)).

The first cowherd must keep the cattle out of ripening crops, tapping and checking them because their wandering could bring punishment or a fine ([MN 19, first cowherd simile](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html#cowherd1)). This is the image for recognizing the danger in harmful qualities and keeping them in check ([MN 19](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html)).

Beneficial thinking carries no such danger, but the account gives it a bodily limit: prolonged thinking can tire the body and disturb concentration ([MN 19](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html)). He therefore settles and concentrates the mind; the second cowherd watches when the crops have been gathered, resting in the shade or open air and simply keeping the cattle in mind ([MN 19, second cowherd simile](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html#cowherd2)). The account continues through four jhanas and three knowledges: recollection of past lives, beings' passing and reappearance according to kamma, and the ending of the mental fermentations, culminating in release ([MN 19, continuation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html#tevijja)).

> "Whatever a monk keeps pursuing with his thinking & pondering, that becomes the inclination of his awareness." ([MN 19, repeated-thinking passage](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html#habit))

For an essay about habit, the useful movement is from repeated engagement to an inclination that has been cultivated. The harvest image also gives the compendium a way to distinguish care from constant interference. B088's "votes" are the compendium's analogy for inclination; MN 19 does not count individual thoughts as equal increments ([MN 19, repeated-thinking passage](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html#habit)). This reading concerns thoughts repeatedly pursued, not a judgment that each intrusive thought has been chosen. It offers neither a clinical method nor a complete account of karma, and the canonical recollection should be presented as a textual account rather than an independently verified record of the Buddha's mental life.

**Principles:**
- [[B088 — Every repetition is a vote (habit as karma)]]: the account explicitly connects repeated thinking with the mind's inclination; its two cowherds distinguish corrective effort from continued watchfulness.
