*Tham Luang cave, Chiang Rai, Thailand · June–July 2018 · the Wild Boars youth football team*

On 23 June 2018, twelve boys of the Wild Boars football team, aged 11 to 16, walked into the Tham Luang cave system in northern Thailand after practice, with their 25-year-old assistant coach. Monsoon floodwaters cut off the way out. The full group would not be out until 18 days later.

The coach, Ekapol Chanthawong, had been orphaned young: he was the only member of his family to survive an epidemic in his village when he was 10 years old, and two years later he went to live at a Buddhist temple, where he trained to become a monk and studied meditation. He spent about ten years there before leaving to care for a sick grandmother and taking the assistant-coach job. When the cave flooded, that decade came with him.

The reporting after the rescue records what he did with it. He gave his own share of the team's meager food rations to the boys and was suffering malnutrition by the end of the ordeal. He taught them how to collect runoff from the cave walls instead of drinking the floodwaters, and how to conserve their energy while they waited for rescuers. And he taught them meditation. Ten days in, when two British divers found the group, they were sitting quietly in the dark, meditating. A Thai government spokesperson described the approach afterward, noting the coach had the boys lie down and not move too much, "and of course, by meditation, they stay conscious all the time, so their minds will not be wandering around." The mother of one of the boys, watching the widely shared video of the moment they were found, put it more simply: "Look at how calm they were sitting there waiting. No one was crying or anything. It was astonishing."

The compendium's use of the story is about the resource the coach had that required no equipment, no light, and no air beyond what was already in the chamber. The boys' own breath and attention were the only tools that survived the flood with them, and for two weeks those tools fed them.
