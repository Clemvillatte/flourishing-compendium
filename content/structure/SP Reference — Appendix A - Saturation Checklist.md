Method, stated so the word "exhaustive" is bounded and auditable.

Corpus: the complete Ethics in the Elwes translation (all five parts, all scholia, both appendices, and the full definitions of the affects), the complete Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect, the Theological-Political Treatise and the Political Treatise read for their practical teaching, and the practical letters of the Correspondence (the Blyenbergh exchange on evil, Letter 58 to Schuller on the drunkard, and the Oldenburg letters).

Extraction method: the Ethics' practical load was mapped in a structured reading pass against the Elwes translation, with the load-bearing propositions, scholia, corollaries, and appendices checked directly against the text and dispositioned to retained principles, scope exclusions, or named judgment calls. Part I's practical load is concentrated in the Appendix (teleology) and the necessity propositions; Parts III and IV were walked in full, with the affect definitions audited item by item; Part V was walked proposition by proposition for its remedies. The Emendation was walked section by section. The two Treatises were walked chapter by chapter for instruction-bearing passages. Judgment calls are named here: the notes to IV P44-P45 (avarice, ambition, and lust as species of madness; derision and contempt never good) are retained as companions to the cheerfulness and moderation principles rather than as separate entries; the drunkenness example of Letter 58 is retained as its own principle because it carries a distinct instruction about the limits of insight; the Political Treatise's right-as-power analysis is retained under the institutions principle rather than duplicated in a separate sovereignty entry; the third kind of knowledge is treated as one principle with its practice directions rather than split into three; the demonstrations of Parts I-II are used only where they carry practical weight, and the excluded technical material (optics, Hebrew grammar, scripture scholarship, the demonstrations of Part I beyond necessity and teleology) is excluded on the stated scope grounds.

Corrections made in the verification pass: the opening maxim's 'not to mock, lament, or detest' formulation is in Political Treatise I.4 (the Ethics III Preface carries the lines-and-planes method); the drunkard example is the scholium to Ethics III, P2, with Letter 58 carrying the conscious-of-desires, ignorant-of-causes statement; the desire-cannot-be-excessive claim is IV P61 (not P44, which says love and desire may be excessive); the free person's avoidance of hatred is grounded in the proof of P70 (there is no corollary to P67); Part IV's appendix contains thirty-two items (not thirteen); and V P7's strength-of-reason's-affects claim carries its condition (when time is taken into account). The P63 corollary's 'pleasure which cannot be excessive' reference and P73's 'more free in a State' proposition were verified verbatim against the text.

Verification ledger (Ethics passages checked verbatim against the Elwes translation in this edition's pass, with destination principles): III, P2 scholium, the drunkard and the mind-body identity (6, 26, 28); III, P43, hatred destroyed by love (30, 66); IV, P41, pleasure in itself good (61); IV, P42, mirth cannot be excessive (61); IV, P43, localized stimulation may be excessive (62); IV, P44 with note, love and desire may be excessive, and avarice, ambition, and lust as species of madness (25, 62, 63); IV, P61, desire springing from reason cannot be excessive (63); IV, P63 with corollary, the fear-led deed is not reason-led (65); IV, P67, wisdom a meditation on life (68); IV, P70 with proof, the free person avoids the ignorant's favors (37, 69); IV, P72, the free person never acts fraudulently (67); IV, P73 with note, more free in a State, hatred overcome with love (80, 30, 66); the Appendix to Part IV, thirty-two items (72, 66); V, P7, reason's affects stronger when time is taken into account (55); V, P10, ordering and connecting images (53, 54); V, P42 with scholium, the wise person's portrait (29, 61). Passages cited in principles but not listed here were retained from the structured reading pass without a verbatim check in this edition; the corrections recorded above are every case where the check changed a citation.

Deduplication pass performed: the understand-not-condemn attitude is taught once (principle 1) and cross-referenced where it recurs (principles 5 and 29); necessity is taught once (3) and cross-referenced in the determinism principles (27-28); the affect-versus-affect rule is taught once (50) and referenced by every remedy in Layer V; the free person's portrait is distributed as principles 68-73 with the appendix list consolidated into one audit principle (72); eternity is taught once in its practical form (82-83) with the love-of-God cluster kept separate because it carries distinct instructions; the political principles are consolidated to seven (74-81) to avoid chapter-by-chapter duplication of the Treatises.

Deliberate exclusions, with reasons: the technical metaphysics of Part I beyond necessity, teleology, and the one-substance frame (retained only where practical); Part II's epistemology beyond the four levels, common notions, and the words-versus-ideas warning; the scripture scholarship and theology of the Theological-Political Treatise (retained only the freedom-to-philosophize, piety-as-practice, obedience, and unity principles); the Political Treatise's constitutional details of aristocracy and monarchy (retained the general power analysis and the democracy principle); the Hebrew grammar and the correspondence beyond the practical letters; the posthumous reception of Spinozism (German Idealism, Deleuze, contemporary neuro-Spinozism), which appears only as philosophy parallels inside entries, clearly labeled.

Limits, stated plainly: saturation is claimed as an editorial judgment within the declared corpus and practical scope, after a proposition-by-proposition walk of the Ethics and section-by-section walks of the other works, a closing pass that merged near-duplicates, and a reverse-check of the entries against their cited passages, prioritizing the passages the drafting flagged as uncertain, with the corrections listed below. It is not a claim of Spinoza scholarship beyond that scope; the Elwes translation was the working text, and a future edition could strengthen the pass with a systematic collation against the Gebhardt edition.

Stopping rule: the sweeps were closed when further material produced only duplicates of retained principles, material excluded on the stated scope grounds, or doctrine without a distinct practical instruction.
