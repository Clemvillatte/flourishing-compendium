> **Principle 56** — Recognise self and social media: curated selves distort self-perception

The core of this principle holds up in experiments: comparing yourself with other people's posts can lower how you rate yourself. In Vogel and colleagues' second study, "state self-esteem and relative self-evaluations were lower when the target person's profile contained upward comparison information" than when it contained downward comparison information ([Vogel et al. 2014, APA](https://psycnet.apa.org/doiLanding?doi=10.1037/ppm0000047)). In four studies (N = 798), "more frequent and more extreme upward comparisons resulted in immediate declines in self-evaluations", and people with low self-esteem were more likely to make them ([Midgley et al. 2021, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32790470/)).

The wider claim is shakier. Fardouly's experiment had 112 women browse Facebook, a magazine site or a neutral site for ten minutes. The Facebook group reported a more negative mood, but appearance discrepancies rose only for women high in appearance comparison tendency ([Fardouly et al. 2015, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25615425/)). Across 387 adolescents followed for three weeks, "the majority of adolescents (88%) experienced no or very small effects of SMU on self-esteem", while 4% had positive and 8% negative effects ([Valkenburg et al. 2021](https://academic.oup.com/joc/article/71/1/56/6124731)). The effect is person-specific, not universal.

### Reading the evidence

These studies measure comparison and self-evaluation. The principle's phrase "curated selves" is the compendium's framing. The studies I opened do not test whether posts are edited, and the experiments cover mostly young adults and teenagers.

**Plain words**
A feed shows other people's best moments next to your whole day. For some people, and mainly when they compare upward, that lowers how they see themselves. Notice whether it happens to you.

**For example**
After ten minutes on a feed, rate your mood from 1 to 10 and note whether you compared yourself with anyone. Do this for a week and see whether a pattern appears. If it does, change what you follow.

## Psychology parallels

- **Social media and self-comparison**, exposure to curated presentations reduces self-esteem and increases negative comparison (Vogel et al., 2014; Fardouly et al., 2015)
- **Passive social media use is particularly harmful**, passive scrolling (not posting) is associated with worse well-being (Verduyn et al., 2015; Kross et al., 2013)

## Philosophy parallels

- **Appearance vs reality** (Plato, Republic)

## Practice

- Recognise social media selves as curated. Do not compare your actual self to others' edited presentations. Reduce passive scrolling. Use social media actively (connect, create) rather than passively (scroll, compare).
