> **Principle 154** — Care for the community of life

Cobb argues that relations to the natural world belong within an account of the common good. In his reading of Whitehead, human experience takes shape through relations with other people, animals, plants and the wider world. Ecological concern therefore includes both human dependence and the importance of other creatures in their own right ([“The Common Good in a Postmodern World,” section II](https://www.religion-online.org/article/the-common-good-in-a-postmodern-world/)).

> "The well being of the other creatures has intrinsic importance and is also important for us." (Cobb, 2003, section II.)

### Include more than human advantage

This is Cobb's philosophical and ethical argument, not a scientific measurement of intrinsic value. The compendium applies it by asking whose interests a decision leaves out, including nonhuman lives. Calling the biosphere a stakeholder is a practical metaphor here, not a claim that an ecosystem has a single voice or legally defined standing.

(Cobb and Griffin, Process Theology, 1976: secularized; Whitehead's value realism.)

### Plain words

Before comparing options, name the people and other living things you intend to consider. What do you know about the effects on them, and where would you need advice? Including an interest in the discussion does not mean you have already resolved the conflict.

### For example

Imagine a building proposal near a wetland. A proposed exercise is to ask what ecological information the decision requires before treating the site as empty space. No particular ecological impact or planning outcome is assumed.
