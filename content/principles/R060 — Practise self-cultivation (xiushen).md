> **Principle 322** — Practise self-cultivation (xiushen)

Make the cultivation of your own character your primary project. The Confucian classics teach a cascade: whoever would order the state well must first regulate the family; whoever would regulate the family must first cultivate the person; whoever would cultivate the person must first rectify the heart. The work that improves the world begins as work on oneself.

(The Great Learning; Confucius, The Analects 1.4, 12.1; [[The doctrine of the mean|The Doctrine of the Mean]], ch. 1.)

### The cascade

The Great Learning runs the chain from the world back to the heart:

> "The ancients who wished to establish illustrious virtue throughout the empire, first ordered well their own states. Wishing to order well their states, they first regulated their families. Wishing to regulate their families, they first cultivated their persons. Wishing to cultivate their persons, they first rectified their hearts." — The Great Learning, in Legge's translation, excerpted in [Quackenbos, "Illustrated History of Ancient Literature" (Project Gutenberg)](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/67687/67687-h/67687-h.htm)

The text then draws the moral for rulers: "from the ambition and perverseness of one man, the whole state may be led to rebellious disorder: such is the nature of influence" (The Great Learning, same source).

### The Master's answer

Asked directly what the superior man amounts to, Confucius kept giving the same answer in widening circles:

> "The cultivation of himself in reverential carefulness." ... "He cultivates himself so as to give rest to others." ... "He cultivates himself so as to give rest to all the people:— even Yao and Shun were still solicitous about this." — Confucius, Analects XIV.45, Legge's translation ([Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3330/pg3330-images.html))

The practice is concrete: on perfect virtue, the Master said, "To subdue one's self and return to propriety, is perfect virtue" (Analects XII.1), and Tsang reported, "I daily examine myself on three points" (Analects I.4).

**Plain words** Pick one habit of yours that leaks onto others: the temper, the phone at the table, the sarcasm under stress. Give it the Tsang treatment: three questions, honestly answered, at the end of the day. The family dinner and the team meeting are downstream of that work.

*For example:* A coach spends the off-season on their own temper, on the theory that the team's discipline is downstream of it. The Great Learning's cascade is that theory in miniature: person first, then family, then team, then state.
