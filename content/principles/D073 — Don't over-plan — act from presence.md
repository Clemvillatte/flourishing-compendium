> **Principle 73** — Don't over-plan: act from presence

The principle draws on two passages. Zhuangzi 6 describes the True Men of old, who did not seek their ends like heroes and did not lay plans to attain them ([Legge, Zhuangzi 6](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)). DDJ 15 describes ancient masters through images of caution and reserve, including people wading through a winter stream, then pictures muddy water gradually clearing when left still ([Legge, DDJ 15](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt)).

> The True men of old did not reject (the views of) the few; they did not seek to accomplish (their ends) like heroes (before others); they did not lay plans to attain those ends. (Zhuangzi 6, [Legge](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf))

> Who can (make) the muddy water (clear)? Let it be still, and it will gradually become clear. (DDJ 15, [Legge](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

Applied to planning, these images suggest leaving room for what has not yet become clear. Prepare what you can, then reconsider your approach when events differ from your expectations. This is a practical reading of the passages.

(Zhuangzi 6; DDJ 15.)

**Plain words**
A plan includes guesses about what you have not met yet. Use those guesses to prepare, then compare them with what actually happens. Watch for the temptation to defend the guess instead of revising it. Treat the plan the way you would treat a weather forecast: useful for choosing shoes, useless as an argument with the rain.

**For example**
Imagine preparing an opening line and three points for a difficult conversation. The other person raises a concern you had not expected. Ask about it before deciding which of your prepared points still matter.
