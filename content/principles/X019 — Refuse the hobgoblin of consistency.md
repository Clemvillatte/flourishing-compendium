> **Principle 19** — Refuse the hobgoblin of consistency

Emerson's line is from "Self-Reliance": "A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds, adored by little statesmen and philosophers and divines. With consistency a great soul has simply nothing to do." He continues: "Speak what you think now in hard words, and to-morrow speak what to-morrow thinks in hard words again, though it contradict every thing you said to-day" ([Self-Reliance, University of Washington text](https://depts.washington.edu/lsearlec/TEXTS/EMERSON/SELFREL.HTM)). The word is "foolish", and his concern in the passage is fear of being misunderstood: "Is it so bad, then, to be misunderstood?" The text says nothing about weighing evidence. Applying it to belief revision is an interpretation, which the Wikipedia essay on the passage also gives ([Wikipedia project essay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Emerson_and_Wilde_on_consistency)).

The psychological side is easier to support. Kunda's review found "considerable evidence that people are more likely to arrive at conclusions that they want to arrive at", limited by their ability "to construct seemingly reasonable justifications" ([Kunda 1990, Psychological Bulletin](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2270237/)).

(Emerson, Self-Reliance.)

### Scope

Kunda studied motivated reasoning in general, not specifically identity-protecting consistency. I did not open Nyhan's work, and I did not test the monthly "I no longer believe" exercise.

**Plain words**
Emerson mocks clinging to consistency for its own sake, and psychology shows we are often motivated to protect conclusions we like. Changing your mind in the open is a way to resist both.

**For example**
Once a month, write "I no longer believe..." and "I now believe...", each with one sentence on what changed your mind.
