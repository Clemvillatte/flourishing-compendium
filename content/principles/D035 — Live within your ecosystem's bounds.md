> **Principle 35** — Live within your ecosystem's bounds

Chapter 80 of the Daodejing is the source image, and it is deliberately small: a little state with a small population, where the tools of long distance sit unused:

> "Though they had boats and carriages, they should have no occasion to ride in them; though they had buff coats and sharp weapons, they should have no occasion to don or use them." (Daodejing 80, [Legge](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

The contentment it prescribes is local, built from the coarse and near:

> "They should think their (coarse) food sweet; their (plain) clothes beautiful; their (poor) dwellings places of rest; and their common (simple) ways sources of enjoyment." (Daodejing 80, [Legge](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

The chapter ends with a neighbouring state within sight, the fowls and dogs audible across the boundary, and no intercourse between the two communities to the end of their lives: the ideal carries an isolation this principle does not adopt. The present-day application is to ask which everyday needs can be met nearby, and which distant dependencies are worth their standing costs.

(DDJ 80.)

**Plain words**
List your regular journeys and deliveries: groceries, work, friends, movement. For each, check whether a nearby version exists, and what breaks if the distant one becomes unavailable. The choice is which standing costs to keep, not whether to have any.

**For example**
Two people with the same jobs and friends may live where one walks to all of them and the other commutes an hour each way; comparing their weeks is a way of pricing that difference.
