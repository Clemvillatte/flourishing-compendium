> **Principle 35** — Live within your ecosystem's bounds

Chapter 80 of the Daodejing is the source image, and it is deliberately small: a little state with a small population, where the tools of long distance sit unused:

> "Though they had boats and carriages, they should have no occasion to ride in them." (Daodejing 80, [Legge](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

The contentment it prescribes is local, built from the coarse and near:

> "They should think their (coarse) food sweet; their (plain) clothes beautiful; their (poor) dwellings places of rest; and their common (simple) ways sources of enjoyment." (Daodejing 80, [Legge](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

The chapter ends with a neighbouring state within sight, the fowls and dogs audible across the boundary, and still no dependence on it. Read as a design principle rather than a transportation policy: every need you place far away becomes a standing errand, and a standing errand is a fragile thing. A life assembled from what is near simply has fewer of them. The compendium's version keeps the useful half of the utopia and drops the isolation: neighbours in sight, the distant optional.

(DDJ 80.)

**Plain words**
Everything your week needs has a distance attached: groceries, friends, work, movement. Each distance is a standing cost, paid in travel and in what breaks when one link fails. Choosing the close version where one exists lowers the number of things that have to go right for an ordinary week to work.

**For example**
The person whose groceries, friends, gym and work are all within a walk has a sturdier week than the one commuting across a city for each.
