> **Principle 77** — Climb the subtlety ladder

Sutra 1.17 lists four forms that concentration takes when it is conscious of an object: "[Concentration becomes] conscious [of its object] by assuming forms either of deliberation [upon coarse objects] or of reflection [upon subtile objects] or of joy or of the sense-of-personality" (trans. Woods, p. 40).

### How the commentary orders the four

"Deliberation (vitarka) is the mind-stuff's coarse direct-experience (abhoga) when directed to its supporting [object]. Reflection (vicara) is the subtile [direct-experience]. Joy is happiness. The sense-of-personality is a feeling (samvid) which pertains to one self" (p. 40). Each form is the one before it with something taken away: "The second, [that is, reflection,] which has deliberation subtracted [from it]"; "the third, [that is, joy,] which has reflection subtracted from it"; and the fourth, the sense-of-personality, "has this [joy] subtracted from it" (p. 40). The commentary adds that "all these kinds of concentrations have an object upon which they rest" (p. 40).

### Where the principle adds to the text

The text gives a ladder of four forms that grow less gross by subtraction, with the coarse object at the bottom and the subtle at the top. It does not say that the objects themselves differ in subtlety as a rule for choosing what to attend to. Breath and bodily sensation are the principle's examples, and "do not celebrate; refine" is its practical advice. I did not read sutras 1.42-1.44, which discuss the related balanced states, so I make no claim about them here.

**Plain words** Woods' commentary describes four kinds of conscious concentration, each with less in it than the one before: first reasoning about a coarse object, then a subtler look at it, then joy, then only the sense of being oneself. The advice to move from the easy coarse object to a finer one is the principle's way of using this ladder.

*For example:* Imagined example: counting breaths becomes easy, so you drop the count and follow the finer sensation of air at the nostrils. That is a modern use of the idea of moving to finer objects.

(Yoga Sutra 1.17 with Vyasa's Bhasya, trans. James Haughton Woods, The Yoga-System of Patanjali (1914), p. 40, Internet Archive, opened 2026-10-21. Woods renders vitarka as deliberation and vicara as reflection; the principle's "reasoning" and "beyond reasoning" are other translators' words.)
