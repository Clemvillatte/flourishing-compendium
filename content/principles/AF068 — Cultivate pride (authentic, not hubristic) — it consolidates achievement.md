> **Principle 68** — Cultivate pride (authentic, not hubristic): it consolidates achievement

Tracy and Robins distinguish two facets of pride, [[Authentic pride]] and [[Hubristic pride]], in a model that links each to different explanations of success. The two-facet passage:

> "Specifically, authentic pride ("I'm proud of what I did") may result from attributions to internal, unstable, controllable causes ("I won because I practiced"), whereas hubristic pride ("I'm proud of who I am") may result from attributions to internal, stable, uncontrollable causes ("I won because I'm always great")." (Tracy & Robins, 2007 — [The Nature of Pride](http://www.gruberpeplab.com/3131/6.2_TracyRobins_2007_PrideIntro.pdf) — "Theoretical and Historical Perspectives Pointing to Two Facets of Pride")

In a questionnaire study of 936 undergraduates, authentic pride correlated with measures of self-control, while hubristic pride correlated with impulsivity and aggression ([Carver & Johnson, 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3137237/)). A longitudinal study of 1,653 students reported bidirectional associations between authentic pride and prestige, and between hubristic pride and dominance ([Witkower et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8892063/)).

**Plain words**
When you feel proud, ask what you are taking credit for. Are you describing a particular achievement and the effort involved, or making a broad claim about yourself? The compendium suggests starting with the particular achievement.

**For example**
Imagine a graduate who says, "I'm a genius." They try describing the accomplishment more specifically: "I worked through a difficult course and finished it."

## Practice

- When you achieve something, allow authentic pride: "I worked hard and did this." Do not inflate it into a global self-evaluation ("I am superior") or dismiss it ("it was nothing").
