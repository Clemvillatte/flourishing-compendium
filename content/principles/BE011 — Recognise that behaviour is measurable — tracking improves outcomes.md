> **Principle 11** — Recognise that behaviour is measurable: tracking improves outcomes

Harkin and colleagues pooled 138 randomised studies (N = 19,951) that prompted participants to monitor goal progress against a control. The prompts raised how often people monitored (d+ = 1.98) and "promoted goal attainment (d+ = 0.40, 95% CI [0.32, 0.48])". Effects on attainment were larger "when the outcomes were reported or made public, and when the information was physically recorded" (Harkin et al. 2016, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26479070/)). The studies focused mainly on personal health goals such as weight, smoking, diet and blood pressure, and tracking a behaviour moved the behaviour, while tracking an outcome moved the outcome ([APA press release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2015/10/progress-goals)).

That is a moderate average effect from interventions that prompted monitoring. It does not show that tracking works for every person or every goal.

**Plain words**
Keeping a record of what you do tends to help you do it, and the evidence is stronger when you write it down than when you rely on memory. Across 138 trials the effect was moderate. Track the thing you want to change (the walks, not just the weight), write it down, and consider telling someone.

*For example:* Each evening you tick a box if you walked that day, and on Sunday you read the week back. This is an imagined routine that applies Harkin's finding that physically recording progress, and reporting it, gave larger effects.

## Practice

- Track behaviours: log, count, measure. Use a habit tracker, journal, or app. Do not rely on memory or feeling; use data. Measurement is a behaviour-change technique.
