> **Principle 24** — Root your work in the people it serves

Work in the Nahua and Oaxacan traditions was and is embedded in obligations to neighbours. Britannica notes that among the Nahua, "Groups of three or four men may cultivate corn, beans, and squash collectively", and that in villages each barrio "recruits compulsory labour to work village common lands in addition to private farming" ([Britannica, Nahua](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Nahua)).

(The milpa's relational structure; the tequio communal work tradition.)

### Beneficiaries you can name

The Oaxaca state government's planning document says tequio is the work each person contributes to resolve a community problem, for maintaining streets, schools, government buildings and festivals, or in disasters, and adds that neighbours also give each other tequio when building houses, working the land, sowing and harvesting (my translation of [Gobierno de Oaxaca, interculturality plan](https://www.oaxaca.gob.mx/planeacion/wp-content/uploads/sites/29/2024/12/PE_INTERCULTURALIDAD-PUEBLOS-Y-COMUNIDADES-INDIGENAS-Y-AFROMEXICANAS.pdf)). The work therefore has named recipients and a visible result.

I did not find a source in this search for the claim that milpa work is offered to the gods, nor a study showing that knowing the beneficiaries preserves meaning in work. That second step is the compendium's modern reading.

**Plain words**
Work feels different when you know who it is for. Where you can, link what you do to a person or a group you can name.

**For example**
For one week, keep a note by your desk. At the end of each day, write the name of one person your work helped or will help. If you can't, find one person to ask what they use from it.
