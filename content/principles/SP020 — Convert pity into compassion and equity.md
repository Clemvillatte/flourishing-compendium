> **Principle 20** — Convert pity into compassion and equity

Spinoza defines pity as a pain:

> "Pity is pain accompanied by the idea of evil, which has befallen someone else whom we conceive to be like ourselves." (Ethics III, Definitions of the Affects, XVIII, [Elwes](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800))

His verdict on it for the rational person is harsh:

> "Pity, in a man who lives under the guidance of reason, is in itself bad and useless." (Ethics IV, P50, [Elwes](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800))

The proof's reasoning is that pity is a pain, and a pain is in itself bad; the good effect that may follow, freeing the pitied person from misery, is an action that reason alone can certify as good. The note adds two cautions about pity's reliability: a person easily moved by another's tears "often does something which he afterwards regrets", partly because "we are easily deceived by false tears". The concession runs the other way as well: someone moved to help others neither by reason nor by compassion is "rightly styled inhuman".

Spinoza's alternative is helping under the guidance of reason; the proof allows that pity may lead someone to relieve another's misery, and his objection concerns pity itself and whether it reliably guides the action. The rational person "desires for others the good which he seeks for himself" (IV. xxxvii.), and the title's word for that stance, equity, is the compendium's label for it.

**Plain words**
Another person's misfortune can make you sad and move you to help. Spinoza's warning about false tears concerns acting before understanding the situation. For a practical application, ask what assistance is needed and whether you understand the circumstances well enough to offer it.

*For example:* A friend loses her job. Ask whether she wants company or help with an application, and respond to what she says.
