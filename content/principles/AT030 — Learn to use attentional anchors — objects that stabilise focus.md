> **Principle 30** — Learn to use attentional anchors: objects that stabilise focus

Lutz and colleagues describe focused attention meditation as "the voluntary focusing of attention on a chosen object". They say the practice develops three skills: monitoring distractions without losing the intended focus, disengaging from a distracting object, and promptly redirecting focus to the chosen object ([Lutz et al. 2008, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2693206/)). A methods review adds that the usual object is breath sensations, with attention shifted back "without self-judgment" ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4627495/)).

Hasenkamp and colleagues modelled the experience as a four-phase cycle: wandering, noticing the wandering, shifting attention, and holding it. Imaging showed default-mode regions active during wandering and salience-network regions during the noticing, with the pattern shaped by lifetime experience ([Hasenkamp et al. 2012, summary](https://github.com/buddhist-uni/buddhist-uni.github.io/blob/main/_content/articles/mind-wandering-and-attention-during_hasenkamp-wendy-et-al.md)). The wandering is thus part of the practice, and the return is where the training happens.

### Scope

These sources concern breath-focused meditation in trained practitioners. Direct support for objects other than the breath, such as feet on the floor in a meeting, was not found, and Bregman 1990 or Holmes 2018 on mantra is cited at second hand. One secondary source says expertise reduces the effort of sustaining focus, citing Lutz et al.; that claim was not read in the paper itself.

**Plain words**
A mind drifts within minutes of being left alone, like a boat in a current. Attentional anchors are the buoy the mooring line runs to, the fixed point the boat keeps swinging back toward. The craft is not never drifting, since no sailor achieves that, but the quickness of the return. The quality being trained is the return itself, calm and free of scolding, since a buoy that insulted the boat would be a strange aid to navigation.

**For example**
One anchor, such as the feeling of breath at the nostrils, serves; each time wandering is noticed, attention returns to it once.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Anchors in focused attention meditation|Anchors in focused attention meditation]]**, breath and body sensations serve as stable anchors for returning attention (Lutz et al., 2008; Tang et al., 2015)
- **Mantra and verbal anchors**, repetition of a word or phrase stabilises attention and reduces mind-wandering (Bregman, 1990; Holmes, 2018)

## Philosophy parallels

- **Shamatha with support** (Buddhist meditation)
- **The breath as anchor** (Buddhism, Anapanasati Sutta)
- **[[Wu wei|Wu wei as returning to the root]]** (Daoism, Daodejing 16)

## Practice

- Choose a stable anchor (breath is most common). When you notice attention has wandered, return to the anchor. Use this in meditation and during work (return to the task as anchor).
