> **Principle 26** — Study the drunkard, and keep your seat

Spinoza's scholium on the mind's supposed freedom runs through the examples:

> "a drunken man believes that he utters from the free decision of his mind words which, when he is sober, he would willingly have withheld" (Ethics II, P35 scholium, [Elwes](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800))

The diagnosis is general: "men believe themselves to be free, simply because they are conscious of their actions, and unconscious of the causes whereby those actions are determined". The drunkard's speech sounds fine to the drunkard; the wine is doing the talking. The practical consequence Spinoza draws in Part IV is that insight is a weak counter at the moment of appetite:

> "An emotion can only be controlled or destroyed by another emotion contrary thereto, and with more power for controlling emotion." (Ethics IV, P7, [Elwes](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800))

Knowledge of what is good, at the moment of temptation, needs a stronger contrary affect or an arrangement already in place to back it. That is the moral of the remedies: do not rely on insight alone in the moment; rely on the environment and the stronger affect prepared in advance.

**Plain words**
Consciousness is not self-knowledge. You feel the wanting and you miss the causes, so from inside, the impulse always looks like a choice. The drunkard test: everyone at the table can see what the third glass is doing to the speech except the person speaking. The same blindness applies to hunger, fatigue, infatuation, and moral certainty. The remedy is a stronger contrary feeling or an arrangement made while sober, secured before the moment arrives.

*For example:* The person certain they are arguing from principle, while everyone in the room can see the fatigue driving it. Keep your seat: collect the outside view before trusting the inside one. A decision made after the second drink gets re-made sober.
