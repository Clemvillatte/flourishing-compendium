> **Principle 26** — Study the drunkard, and keep your seat

In the letter to G. H. Schuller, Spinoza gives his compact statement of the human predicament: people are conscious of their desires and ignorant of the causes that determine them; the drunkard, in the scholium's example, believes his speech is free while the wine is doing the talking. Knowledge of what is good, at the moment of appetite, is a weak force by itself. The practical use is the moral of the remedies, stated in advance: do not rely on insight alone at the moment of temptation; rely on arrangement, on the environment and the stronger affect prepared in advance.

(Correspondence, Letter 58 to Schuller; Ethics III, P2 scholium, the drunkard passage.)

**Plain words** The Schuller letter: people are conscious of their desires and ignorant of the causes that determine them. The drunkard believes his speech is free and graceful — everyone at the table has other data. Study the drunkard, and keep your seat: consciousness is not self-knowledge.

*For example:* The person absolutely certain they're arguing from principle — and everyone in the room can see the hunger or the fatigue driving it. The drunkard's speech always sounds fine from inside the drunkard. That's the whole point of getting data from outside.
