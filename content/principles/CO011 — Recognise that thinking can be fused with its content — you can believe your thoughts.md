> **Principle 11** — Recognise that thinking can be fused with its content: you can believe your thoughts

The Buddhist root of defusion is the Anattalakkhana Sutta's instruction to see consciousness as it actually is: not mine, not my self, not what I am ([SN 22.59, Thanissaro](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn22/sn22.059.than.html)). The ACT skill runs the same move in clinical language: fusion treats the thought as the world; defusion sees it as an event.

> Any consciousness whatsoever that is past, future, or present; internal or external; blatant or subtle; common or sublime; far or near: every consciousness is to be seen as it actually is with right discernment as: "This is not mine. This is not my self. This is not what I am." (Anattalakkhana Sutta, SN 22.59, [Thanissaro](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn22/sn22.059.than.html))

The distinction the page keeps is between the thought's content and your relationship to it: "I'll fail" and "I'm having the thought that I'll fail" describe the same content from two positions. The sutta's version of the second position is the discernment above.

**Plain words**
Cognitive fusion: being so caught in a thought that you treat it as reality rather than a mental event. Defusion: seeing thoughts as thoughts — mental events passing through. "I'm having the thought that I'll fail" is a different room than "I'll fail."

**For example**
The performer who believes the thought "I'll choke" is a prophecy — versus the one who hears it as a mental event, like a passing radio. Same thought, two relationships: one obeys it, one observes it.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Cognitive defusion|Cognitive defusion in ACT]]**, stepping back from thoughts and seeing them as mental events reduces their behavioural impact (Hayes et al., 2011; Gillanders et al., 2014)
- **Defusion techniques reduce distress and believability of negative thoughts**, even brief defusion exercises reduce the impact of self-critical thoughts (Masuda et al., 2004; Deacon et al., 2011)

## Practice

- When you notice "I'm a failure," try adding "I'm having the thought that I'm a failure." This small linguistic shift creates distance between you and the thought.
