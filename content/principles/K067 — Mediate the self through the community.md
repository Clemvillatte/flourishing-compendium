> **Principle 67** — Mediate the self through the community

Tanabe's logic of species (shu no ronri) inserts a third term between the person and humanity: the "species," a sociopolitical identity, between the "individual" (the person) and the "genus" (humanity). As the Stanford Encyclopedia summarizes, the logic attends to "the irrational element of the specific through which the individual and the universal must be mediated," a reappraisal of "the logical and ethical role that ethnic specificity plays in mediating the particular individual and universal humanity" ([SEP, Kyoto School](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kyoto-school/)). A review of Morisato's study puts the scheme compactly: "The logic of species is Tanabe's tripartite dialectical conception of no-self, which Morisato represents in terms of the 'individual' (e.g., person), 'species' (e.g., nationality), and 'genus' (e.g., humanity)" ([Coughlin, review of Morisato](https://blogs.dickinson.edu/buddhistethics/files/2023/11/Coughlin_Review_of_Morisato_Tanabe_Hajime_and_the_Kyoto_School.pdf)). Tanabe later corrected the oppositional reading: "a species does not stand over against another species at all; but their relation must remain a matter of differences rather than one of opposition."

The logic's political history is a live warning, kept here: the SEP records that Tanabe "irrationally proposed that the 'relative absolute' of the Japanese nation-state could serve as a kind of 'supreme archetype' for other nations," while his own logic runs the other way, that "the community of the human race is to be made up of a community of nations that have found a way to transcend their specificity without transcending time and culture."

My reading: the individual is constituted in and through the communities that mediate them (family, nation, craft); the self that ignores the mediation is abstract. Practically, name the three communities that constitute you, and give each one deliberate service in the season ahead.

**Plain words** You did not assemble yourself from scratch. The language you think in, the craft you practise, the town that raised you — communities did that work, and you are their output before you are anything else. Tanabe's point is that the universal never touches you directly; it arrives through these mediators. Two practical consequences follow: serving the communities that made you is repaying a real debt, and the help you can actually give anyone is usually routed through some concrete community or other. The dark twin of the idea is nationalism — my community as the supreme one. Tanabe's correction: communities relate by difference, not opposition, and humanity works as a community of communities.

For example: A craftsperson realises her skill is a gift from her teachers and community, and teaching an apprentice feels less like sacrifice and more like repayment.
