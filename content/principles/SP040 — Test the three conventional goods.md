> **Principle 40** — Test the three conventional goods

Spinoza starts the Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect by sorting what people pursue into three headings. In Elwes's translation they are "Riches, Fame, and the Pleasures of Sense: with these three the mind is so absorbed that it has little power to reflect on any different good" ([Elwes, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1016/1016-h/1016-h.htm)). He then says why he resolved to test them: seeing that the three are "only a hindrance, so long as they are sought as ends not as means; if they be sought as means, they will be under restraint, and, far from being hindrances, will further not a little the end for which they are sought" (same translation).

The test is therefore a demotion, as the seed says, not a rejection. Pleasure gets particular criticism. One translation says that "after the enjoyment of this pleasure there ensues a profound depression" ([Spinoza, Complete Works, 2002](https://homepages.uc.edu/~martinj/Spinoza_&_Hobbes/English/Spinoza%20-%20Complete%20Works%20(2002).pdf)). Michael Della Rocca summarises that such pursuits, riches and fame especially, can lead to endless competition for more ([Della Rocca, Spinoza](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781134456376_A25022964/preview-9781134456376_A25022964.pdf)).

(Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect, sections 4-7.)

### Scope

Spinoza writes as a narrator describing his own conversion, and the passage is a philosophical argument, not a finding about modern money, fame or pleasure. I did not open the sentence behind the seed's claim that fame requires "living according to others' understanding", so it is not given here as a quotation.

**Plain words**
Spinoza does not tell you to drop money, reputation or pleasure. He says they cause trouble when they become the aim, and they are fine as means to something else.

**For example**
For each of the three, write what it is for. If you cannot name an end beyond itself, look at how much of your week it is taking.
