> **Principle 39** — Learn to limit alcohol: it disrupts sleep and worsens mood

The two halves of the title each have a literature. For sleep, a review of studies on alcohol and the nocturnal sleep of healthy volunteers found that at all doses alcohol shortens sleep onset latency, consolidates the first half of the night, and increases sleep disruption in the second half; the first REM period was significantly delayed at all doses, and total-night REM was reduced in most studies at moderate and high doses ([Ebrahim et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347102/)). For mood, a pooled analysis of the alcohol use disorder and major depression literature found each roughly doubles the odds of the other, with the more plausible causal direction, in the authors' reading, running from the alcohol use disorder to the major depression ([Boden & Fergusson, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21382111/)).

> "At all dosages, alcohol causes a reduction in sleep onset latency, a more consolidated first half sleep and an increase in sleep disruption in the second half of sleep." (Abstract; Ebrahim et al., 2013, Alcohol Clin Exp Res 37(4): 539-49, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347102/))

> "The analysis revealed that the presence of either disorder doubled the risks of the second disorder, with pooled AORs ranging from 2.00 to 2.09." (Abstract, Results; Boden & Fergusson, 2011, Addiction 106(5): 906-14, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21382111/))

**Plain words**
Falling asleep quickly is only part of sleeping well. The review found more disruption in the second half of the night, so a nightcap's sedating effect does not by itself establish better sleep ([Ebrahim et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347102/)). The epidemiology concerns diagnosable alcohol problems and diagnosable depression, each roughly doubling the odds of the other, with the authors reading the drinking as the likelier driver ([Boden & Fergusson, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21382111/)).

**For example**
(Imagined) Someone who has been using a nightcap plans a week without one and notes how the nights differ, rather than assuming one bad night proves anything.
