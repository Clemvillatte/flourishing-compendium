> **Principle 39** — Learn to limit alcohol: it disrupts sleep and worsens mood

The two halves of the title each have a literature. For sleep, a review of studies on alcohol and the nocturnal sleep of healthy volunteers found that at all doses alcohol shortens sleep onset latency, consolidates the first half of the night, and increases sleep disruption in the second half; the first REM period was significantly delayed at all doses, and total-night REM was reduced in most studies at moderate and high doses ([Ebrahim et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347102/)). For mood, a pooled analysis of the alcohol use disorder and major depression literature found each roughly doubles the odds of the other, with the more plausible causal direction running from the drinking to the depression ([Boden & Fergusson, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21382111/)).

> "At all dosages, alcohol causes a reduction in sleep onset latency, a more consolidated first half sleep and an increase in sleep disruption in the second half of sleep." (Abstract; Ebrahim et al., 2013, Alcohol Clin Exp Res 37(4): 539-49, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347102/))

> "The analysis revealed that the presence of either disorder doubled the risks of the second disorder, with pooled AORs ranging from 2.00 to 2.09." (Abstract, Results; Boden & Fergusson, 2011, Addiction 106(5): 906-14, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21382111/))

**Plain words**
Two findings, one per half of the title. In the sleep lab, people fall asleep faster after alcohol, then sleep more broken in the small hours, with the dreaming part of the night delayed and shortened ([Ebrahim et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347102/)). In the epidemiology, diagnosable alcohol problems and diagnosable depression travel together, each roughly doubling the odds of the other, and the evidence points at the drinking as the likelier driver ([Boden & Fergusson, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21382111/)). Note the scope: the sleep review covered healthy volunteers on test nights, and the doubled odds concern disorders, not the odd glass of wine.

**For example**
(Imagined) Two wines at dinner make falling asleep quicker; the same night produces the 3 a.m. wake-up the sleep review describes as second-half disruption ([Ebrahim et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23347102/)). The drink did its sedating in the first half of the night.

## Practice

- Limit alcohol: if you drink, do so moderately and not before bed. Notice the effect on sleep and next-day anxiety. Reduce or eliminate if it worsens mood or sleep.
