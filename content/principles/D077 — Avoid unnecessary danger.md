> **Principle 77** — Avoid unnecessary danger

Chapter 50 opens with a plain accounting: some people are on the side of life, some are already on the side of death, and a full three in ten are trying so hard to stay alive that they walk straight into the place of death (DDJ 50.3). The effort itself — the over-vigilance, the unnecessary battle, the spectacular risk taken to prove something — is what kills them.

The skilled one's method is almost anticlimactic: he travels where the rhino has nothing to thrust at, the tiger no place to claw, the weapon no place to enter. There is no place of death in him, not because he is invincible but because he never put himself where the horns live (DDJ 50.4).

> "I have heard that he who is skilful in managing the life entrusted to him for a time travels on the land without having to shun rhinoceros or tiger, and enters a host without having to avoid buff coat or sharp weapon... And for what reason? Because there is in him no place of death." (Daodejing 50.4, James Legge translation, [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

**Plain words** Three in ten people walk toward the thing they most want to avoid. That is the chapter's count, and the mechanism is effort, not bad luck: the tighter you grip at staying safe or staying alive, the more you put yourself in the way of the harm you were running from. The alternative is not courage or cowardice — it is simply not standing where the horns are. If a route exists that passes no rhino, take it. Nothing else needs to be true.

*For example:* Two runners both want to finish a race. One pushes through a known sharp corner at full speed because pulling back feels like losing; the other slows for three seconds and finishes. Same race, same goal — one of them kept a place for death in the plan.
