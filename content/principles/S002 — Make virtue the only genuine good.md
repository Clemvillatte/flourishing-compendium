> **Principle 124** — Make virtue the only genuine good

Wealth, health, reputation, and pleasure are "preferred indifferents": they have value but are not good in the strict sense. Only virtue, the excellent condition of the rational soul, is genuinely good. Only vice is genuinely bad. Everything else is indifferent. Orient your life around this hierarchy: virtue above all, then preferred indifferents, never confusing the two.

(Ench. 1; Disc. I.1, III.19; Med. III.6, VI.13.)

### What "good" means here

The Stoics use "good" strictly: what benefits you unconditionally. Tested that way, wealth, health, reputation and pleasure fail: each can be had by a bad person, used badly, or lost while you act well. Hence "preferred indifferents": worth having, never worth trading virtue for. Epictetus draws the line at the will: "OF things some are in our power, and others are not"; "not in our power are the body, property, reputation, offices" (Ench. 1). The test runs by independence from the will: "Tell me then what things are indifferent? The things which are independent of the will" (Disc. I.1).

### The only thing that can injure you

> "For nothing outside the sphere of the moral purpose can hamper or injure the moral purpose; it alone can hamper or injure itself." — Epictetus, Discourses III.19 (Long & Oldfather trans., Delphi Complete Works)

An illness takes your health; a cowardly choice takes your character, and only you can hand that over. Only vice is bad: the one injury that comes from inside.

### The practical reading

Health and money matter conditionally: they help a life already well-directed and magnify one that isn't. The discipline is keeping the ordering straight when a preferred indifferent starts demanding a bad act: that trade gives up the only unconditional thing for a conditional one.

**Plain words**
"Good" is their technical term for what can only improve you: your own judgement and character. Nothing outside the will injures it; only your use of things can. Health, money and reputation are worth pursuing and never worth trading character for: the moment getting one requires acting badly, the trade hands over the one thing that cannot be replaced for something that can.

*For example:* You can prefer the bigger flat. But if getting it means shading the truth with your landlord, the Stoic says you've swapped a good for an indifferent.
