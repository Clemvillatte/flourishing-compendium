> **Principle 19** — Recognise framing effects: how something is presented changes how you evaluate it

The same decision problem, stated two ways, produces different choices. In Tversky and Kahneman's example, a disease is expected to kill 600 people, and the options are a program that saves 200 for sure or a gamble with a one-third chance of saving everyone. Stated as lives saved, most people take the sure thing; stated as lives lost, most people gamble. The two versions are, in their words, "effectively identical" — the outcomes are described by lives saved in one and lives lost in the other, and that difference "is accompanied by a pronounced shift from risk aversion to risk taking" (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981). The principles governing the perception of decision problems, they wrote, "produce predictable shifts of preference when the same problem is framed in different ways" (p. 453).

The effect holds where decisions matter most. When physicians and patients chose between surgery and radiation for lung cancer, preferences "varied markedly when their probable outcomes were described in terms of mortality or survival": the surgery option, which entails a risk of death during treatment, was "relatively less attractive when the statistics of treatment outcomes were described in terms of mortality rather than in terms of survival" (McNeil, Pauker, Sox, & Tversky, 1982, as described in Kahneman & Tversky, 1984). The numbers were the same numbers; what changed was which ones the reader counted.

Later research had to sort out what "framing" even names: Levin, Schneider, and Gaeth argued that the literature's mixed findings arose because "different studies have employed different operational definitions of framing" (Levin et al., 1998). But the core observation survived the sorting — whether information is stated in positive or negative terms changes judgments and decisions. A frame is not a neutral container that carries the facts; it tells you what to count.

**Plain words** Framing effects: the same numbers, stated one way or the other, produce different decisions — lives saved or lives lost, gains or losses. The frame is not a container for the facts; it is a selector that tells you what to count. Ask for the other frame before you decide.

*For example:* The treatment choice that shifts when the statistics arrive as "90 will survive" rather than "10 will die" — same data, different decisions, documented for physicians and patients alike. The person who asks for both frames before deciding has stopped letting whichever frame arrived first do the deciding.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Framing effects|Framing effects]]**, logically equivalent descriptions lead to different choices (Tversky & Kahneman, 1981; Kahneman & Tversky, 1984)
- **[[Prospect theory|Prospect theory]]**, losses loom larger than gains; people are risk-averse in gains and risk-seeking in losses (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Perspectivism|Perspectivism]]** (Nietzsche, The Gay Science)

## Practice

- When making a decision, deliberately reframe it. Describe it in terms of both gains and losses, both survival and mortality. If your preference flips, the framing is driving the choice, not the substance.
