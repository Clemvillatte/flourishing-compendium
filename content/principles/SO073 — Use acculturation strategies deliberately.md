> **Principle 73** — Use acculturation strategies deliberately

Use acculturation strategies — Berry's integration, assimilation, separation, and marginalization (1997) — deliberately, especially in migration and cultural transition. Berry's framework starts from two questions: whether a person wishes to maintain the heritage culture, and whether they seek interaction with the wider society. Describing the integration option, he writes:

> When there is an interest in both maintaining one's heritage culture while in daily interactions with other groups, integration is the option. In this case, there is some degree of cultural integrity maintained, and at the same time seeking, as a member of an ethnocultural group, to participate as an integral part of the larger social network. (Berry, 2005, p. 705)

On outcomes, his review reports a consistent pattern:

> For both forms of adaptation, those who pursue and accomplish integration appear to be better adapted, and those who are marginalized are least well adapted. And again, the assimilation and separation strategies are associated with intermediate adaptation outcomes. This generalization is remarkably consistent, and parallels the generalization made above regarding acculturative stress. (Berry, 2005, p. 709)

The pattern carries a condition about the receiving society:

> For acculturative stress, there is a clear picture that the pursuit of integration is least stressful (at least where it is accommodated by the larger society), but marginalization is the most stressful; in between are the assimilation and separation strategies, sometimes one, sometimes the other being the less stressful. (Berry, 2005, p. 708)

**Plain words** Two questions sit under the menu: keep the heritage culture, and join the wider society. Yes to both is integration; no to both is marginalization; the other two answers are assimilation and separation. The research links integration with better adaptation where the receiving society accommodates it. Knowing the menu is a first move, and circumstances still shape what is available to choose.

*For example (imagined):* A migrant family keeps the mother tongue at home and uses the local language at work and school, treating both languages as parts of one arrangement.
