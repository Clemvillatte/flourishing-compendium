> **Principle 28** — Ground everything in non-harm

Sutra 2.30 opens the list of restraints with non-injury, and the commentary says what follows from that order:

> Abstinence from injury means the abstinence from malice towards all living creatures in every way and at all times. And the other abstentions and observances are rooted in it.

(Yoga Sutras 2.30, Vyasa's commentary, trans. James Haughton Woods, *The Yoga-System of Patanjali*, Harvard Oriental Series 17, 1914, pp. 178-179, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/yogasystemofpata00wooduoft).)

The other restraints are read in its light. On truthfulness, the commentary says speech should be "used for the service of all; not for the ruin of creatures", and that if it is "only for the ruin of creatures, it would not be an abstinence from falsehood; it would be nothing less than wrong." It tells the yogin to "consider [first] what is good for all creatures and [then] speak with abstinence-from-falsehood." Sutra 2.35 gives the effect: "As soon as he is grounded in abstinence from injury, his presence begets a suspension of enmity."

So the page's claim that truthfulness is bounded by non-injury has direct support. Two other parts do not: the text does not state a rule that whichever value reduces harm wins when two collide, and it does not mention including your own long-term welfare in the sum. Both are this page's own extensions. Woods' footnote adds only that a harmless, beneficial untrue remark is to be counted as true.

**Plain words**

The commentary puts non-injury first and reads the rest through it, so even honesty is judged by whether it serves or ruins people. If honesty and kindness pull apart, look for the true thing that does its work without the damage.

**For example**

A manager has to tell a colleague that her report missed the mark. She says what is wrong and what she saw working, and leaves out the sarcastic aside she'd been planning. The facts are the same, and the colleague can use them.
