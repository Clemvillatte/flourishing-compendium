> **Principle 6** — Understand that neurotransmitters are not simple cause-and-effect: complexity matters

The "chemical imbalance" story, depression as a serotonin shortage that pills top up, is the popular version of the serotonin theory. A 2022 systematic umbrella review of the evidence concluded:

> "The main areas of serotonin research provide no consistent evidence of there being an association between serotonin and depression, and no support for the hypothesis that depression is caused by lowered serotonin activity or concentrations." (Moncrieff et al., 2022, Molecular Psychiatry 28(8):3243-3256, [UCL Discovery record](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10153006/))

The review covered six research areas (serotonin and its metabolite 5-HIAA in body fluids, receptors, the serotonin transporter, tryptophan depletion, SERT gene associations and gene-stress interactions) in 17 studies. In the metabolite studies, "Two meta-analyses of overlapping studies examining the serotonin metabolite, 5-HIAA, showed no association with depression" (Moncrieff et al., 2022). The abstract adds a wrinkle both ways: "Some evidence was consistent with the possibility that long-term antidepressant use reduces serotonin concentration." The review's conclusion is itself contested. A published response in the same journal argues that "We present reasons for why this conclusion is overstated, including methodological weaknesses in the review process, selective reporting of data, over-simplification, and errors in the interpretation of neuropsychopharmacological findings" (Jauhar et al., 2023, Molecular Psychiatry 28(8), [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10618084/)). The disagreement is live.

Efficacy is a separate question from mechanism, and it has its own large evidence base. A network meta-analysis of 522 double-blind trials of the acute treatment of adults with major depressive disorder found: "In terms of efficacy, all antidepressants were more effective than placebo, with ORs ranging between 2·13 (95% credible interval [CrI] 1·89–2·41) for amitriptyline and 1·37 (1·16–1·63) for reboxetine" (Cipriani et al., 2018, The Lancet 391(10128):1357-1366, [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5889788/)). The finding covers the 21 studied drugs, on average, for that population and time frame, not antidepressants in general or any individual's response. The same paper is candid about quality: "46 (9%) of 522 trials were rated as high risk of bias, 380 (73%) trials as moderate, and 96 (18%) as low; and the certainty of evidence was moderate to very low."

**Plain words** Two findings sit side by side and both belong in one head. First, the umbrella review found no support for the low-serotonin story, and a published critique argues that finding is itself overstated; what survives is the disagreement. Second, the studied drugs outperformed placebo in the trials, for the acute treatment of adults with major depressive disorder. So the pill can help without the popular story being true. That is this page's reason for caution about simple chemical stories, not a settled account of what depression is.

**For example:** Imagined: someone says "my serotonin is low" and takes an SSRI that helps. The review says the self-diagnosis rests on a theory the evidence it examined does not support; the trials say the studied drugs outperformed placebo for the studied condition. Both can be true at once, which is the point of holding the complexity.

## Psychology parallels

- **Serotonin hypothesis disputed (Moncrieff et al., 2022; Jauhar et al., 2023)**, an umbrella review of 17 studies found no consistent evidence of an association; a published critique argues the review's conclusion is overstated
- **Antidepressants outperform placebo (Cipriani et al., 2018)**, 522 double-blind trials of acute treatment in adults with major depressive disorder, all 21 studied antidepressants more effective than placebo; certainty of evidence rated moderate to very low

## Practice

- Do not accept the "chemical imbalance" simplification. If you take or are considering antidepressants, discuss with your prescriber what the evidence does and does not support for your situation, and how antidepressants fit with other tools (therapy, lifestyle, meaning). Do not stop or start without professional guidance.
