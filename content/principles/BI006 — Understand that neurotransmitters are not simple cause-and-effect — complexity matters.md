> **Principle 6** — Understand that neurotransmitters are not simple cause-and-effect: complexity matters

The "chemical imbalance" story, depression as a serotonin shortage that pills top up, is the popular version of the serotonin theory. A 2022 systematic umbrella review of the evidence concluded:

> "The main areas of serotonin research provide no consistent evidence of there being an association between serotonin and depression, and no support for the hypothesis that depression is caused by lowered serotonin activity or concentrations." (Moncrieff et al., 2022, Molecular Psychiatry 28(8):3243-3256, [UCL Discovery record](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10153006/))

The review covered six research areas (serotonin and its metabolite 5-HIAA in body fluids, receptors, the serotonin transporter, tryptophan depletion, SERT gene associations and gene-stress interactions) across 17 studies. In the metabolite studies, "Two meta-analyses of overlapping studies examining the serotonin metabolite, 5-HIAA, showed no association with depression" (Moncrieff et al., 2022). The abstract adds a wrinkle both ways: "Some evidence was consistent with the possibility that long-term antidepressant use reduces serotonin concentration." The review's conclusion has been debated in published rebuttals and defences; the mechanism question is not closed.

Efficacy is a separate question from mechanism, and it has its own large evidence base. A network meta-analysis of 522 double-blind trials found: "In terms of efficacy, all antidepressants were more effective than placebo, with ORs ranging between 2·13 (95% credible interval [CrI] 1·89–2·41) for amitriptyline and 1·37 (1·16–1·63) for reboxetine" (Cipriani et al., 2018, The Lancet 391(10128):1357-1366, [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5889788/)). The same paper is candid about quality: "46 (9%) of 522 trials were rated as high risk of bias, 380 (73%) trials as moderate, and 96 (18%) as low; and the certainty of evidence was moderate to very low."

**Plain words** Two findings sit side by side and both belong in one head. First, the low-serotonin story of depression does not hold up in the review of the evidence. Second, antidepressants do outperform placebo in trials. So the pill can help without the popular story being true. Neurotransmitters work in networks, with receptors, feedback and plasticity; "more or less of one chemical" is a poor sketch of how they matter. Complexity is not a reason to stop treatment; it is a reason to drop the bathtub picture.

**For example:** Imagined: someone says "my serotonin is low" and takes an SSRI that helps. The review says the self-diagnosis rests on a theory the evidence does not support; the trials say the help can be real. Both can be true at once, which is the point of holding the complexity.

## Psychology parallels

- **Serotonin hypothesis not supported (Moncrieff et al., 2022)**, umbrella review of 17 studies across six research areas: no consistent evidence of an association between serotonin and depression
- **Antidepressants outperform placebo (Cipriani et al., 2018)**, 522 double-blind trials, all 21 antidepressants more effective than placebo; certainty of evidence rated moderate to very low

## Practice

- Do not accept the "chemical imbalance" simplification. If you take or are considering antidepressants, understand: they work for many people, but the mechanism is complex, and they are one tool among many (therapy, lifestyle, meaning). Do not stop or start without professional guidance.
