> **Principle 6** — Understand dispassion as release, not numbness

The sutra is short: "Passionlessness is the consciousness of being master on the part of one who has rid himself of thirst for either seen or revealed objects" (Yoga Sutra 1.15, trans. Woods). Vairagya here means mastery over thirst, so it is not a rule against having things. The commentary describes a mind that, "even when in contact with objects", is aware of their "inadequateness", and has "nothing to be rejected or received" (Vyasa on 1.15, in Woods).

### What it is not

Vacaspati Misra heads off the reading that dispassion is just numbness: "Passionlessness is not merely riddance from thirst" (Woods, p. 36). The commentary says it is "essentially the absence of immediate-experience of objects whether supernal or not, even when in contact with them" (p. 37). Mastery comes in stages: effort, discrimination, a single remaining sense of faint desire, and last, "the Consciousness of Being Master" (p. 37).

### A higher stage

Sutra 1.16 goes further: passionlessness is "highest when discernment of the Self results in thirstlessness for qualities" (Woods, p. 37). The commentary's own picture is more demanding than a loose grip: it ties the highest passionlessness to Isolation, so the principle's gentler version describes an early stage.

**Plain words** Dispassion is not a lack of feeling. It is the freedom of not being run by what you want. You can still enjoy things, and you can still lose them without losing yourself. The commentary treats this as a skill built in stages, starting with noticing what pulls you.

*For example:* Imagined example: you love your house, and a job takes you to another city. You can sell it with sadness and without panic, because your sense of being all right was never tied to owning it.

(Yoga Sutras 1.15-1.16 with Vyasa's Bhasya and Vacaspati Misra's gloss, trans. James Haughton Woods, The Yoga-System of Patanjali (Harvard Oriental Series 17, 1914), pp. 36-38, Internet Archive, opened 2026-10-21. The old parenthetical rendering "seen and heard" is from a modern translation not re-opened.)
