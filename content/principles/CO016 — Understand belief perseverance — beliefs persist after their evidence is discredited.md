> **Principle 16** — Understand belief perseverance: beliefs persist after their evidence is discredited

Ross, Lepper and Hubbard ran the classic test. In two experiments, people first received false feedback that they had succeeded or failed at a task, and were then "thoroughly debriefed concerning the predetermined and random nature of this outcome manipulation". Even so, "both actors and observers showed substantial perseverance of initial impressions concerning the actors' performance and abilities". A "process" debriefing, "in which explicit discussion of the perseverance process was provided, generally proved sufficient to eliminate erroneous self-perceptions" (Ross et al. 1975, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1185517/)).

Classroom work found the same pattern: high-school students recognised that their film instruction had been good or useless, yet "continued to draw unwarranted inferences--in line with their initial outcomes--about their personal capacities" (Lepper et al. 1986, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3701590/)).

The title's wider claim about fact-checking needs care. In five experiments with more than 10,100 subjects on 52 political issues, Wood and Porter found "no corrections capable of triggering backfire" and concluded that "by and large, citizens heed factual information, even when such information challenges their ideological commitments" (Wood & Porter 2019, [Political Behavior](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11109-018-9443-y.pdf)). So corrections often work, and the lab results show that leftover impressions can linger after evidence is withdrawn.

**Plain words**
In the lab, people kept an impression of themselves after being told it was based on made-up feedback. Telling them the feedback was fake was not enough. Walking them through how such impressions persist did help. In politics, large studies find that corrections mostly do improve accuracy, so do not assume that facts never work. If you are correcting a belief, explain how the false impression formed as well as what is true.

*For example:* A colleague was told early on that a new hire was weak, and later learned the report was mistaken, yet still treats that hire as weak. Sit down with them and go through how the first impression formed, as well as what the new facts are. This is an imagined case of the "process debriefing" Ross and colleagues tested.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Belief perseverance effect|Belief perseverance effect]]**, impressions persisted after complete debriefing, and a process debriefing generally eliminated them (Ross et al., 1975); Anderson et al., 1980
- **The backfire effect is contested**, five experiments (N > 10,100) found no corrections that triggered backfire (Wood & Porter, 2019); Nyhan & Reifler, 2010

## Philosophy parallels

- **Paradigm shifts are slow** (Kuhn, The Structure of Scientific Revolutions)
- **Conversion as transformation, not argument** (James, The Varieties of Religious Experience)

## Practice

- When correcting a false belief (yours or someone else's), explain how the false impression formed as well as what is true. This is the "process" debriefing that Ross and colleagues found generally sufficient to remove erroneous self-perceptions.
