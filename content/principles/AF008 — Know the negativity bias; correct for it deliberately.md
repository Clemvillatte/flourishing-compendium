> **Principle 8** — Know the negativity bias; correct for it deliberately

The review's thesis covers everyday events, major life events such as trauma, close relationships, social networks, interpersonal interactions and learning processes, concluding that bad emotions, bad parents and bad feedback have more impact than good ones ([Baumeister et al., 2001](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2026-01/vohs-et-al-2001-bad-stronger-good.pdf)). The meaning of the thesis: "bad things will produce larger, more consistent, more multifaceted, or more lasting effects than good things" ([Baumeister et al., 2001](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2026-01/vohs-et-al-2001-bad-stronger-good.pdf)). The authors' evolutionary explanation is offered with an explicit caution — they call it "admittedly speculative" — and their closing note is that "many lives can be happy by virtue of having far more good than bad events" ([Baumeister et al., 2001](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2026-01/vohs-et-al-2001-bad-stronger-good.pdf)). The exercise below invites observation of the balance.

> The greater power of bad events over good ones is found in everyday events, major life events (e.g., trauma), close relationship outcomes, social network patterns, interpersonal interactions, and learning processes. Bad emotions, bad parents, and bad feedback have more impact than good ones, and bad information is processed more thoroughly than good.
>
> Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Finkenauer and Vohs, "Bad Is Stronger Than Good," Review of General Psychology 5(4), 323–370, [paper PDF](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2026-01/vohs-et-al-2001-bad-stronger-good.pdf).

> Even though a bad event may have a stronger impact than a comparable good event, many lives can be happy by virtue of having far more good than bad events.
>
> Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Finkenauer and Vohs (2001), same paper, [paper PDF](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2026-01/vohs-et-al-2001-bad-stronger-good.pdf).

(Baumeister et al., 2001, Review of General Psychology 5(4): 323–370.)

### Plain words

Counting pleasant and unpleasant events answers a different question from noticing their impact; the review concerns their relative impact ([Baumeister et al., 2001](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2026-01/vohs-et-al-2001-bad-stronger-good.pdf)). For reflection, list both kinds of event from one day and note which occupied your attention, without expecting a particular pattern.

For example: after a review with nine strengths and one critique, write down all ten as they were stated, and note which ones you remembered on the way home. (An invented illustration.)
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Negativity bias|Negativity bias]]**, negative events elicit stronger and faster reactions than positive events (Baumeister et al., 2001; Rozin & Royzman, 2001)
- **[[Positivity offset|Positivity offset]]**, mild positive affect exists even in neutral conditions, but negative events dominate once they occur (Cacioppo et al., 1999)

## Practice

- When something goes wrong, deliberately note 2–3 things that went right in the same period. This is not toxic positivity; it is a calibration exercise.
