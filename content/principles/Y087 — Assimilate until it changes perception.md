> **Principle 87** — Assimilate until it changes perception

Vedanta lays out learning in three steps, and the third is the one that changes how you see. The root text is Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 2.4.5, where Yajnavalkya tells Maitreyi that "The Self, my dear Maitreyi, should be realized: should be heard of, reflected on and meditated upon." Shankara's commentary on that verse, in Swami Madhavananda's translation, reads:

> It should first be heard of from a teacher and from the scriptures, then reflected on through reasoning, and then steadfastly meditated upon.

(Shankara on Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 2.4.5, trans. Swami Madhavananda, as quoted at [Advaita Vision](https://www.advaita-vision.org/nididhyasana-as-shankara-explains-1-2/).)

He continues that realization comes "when these means, viz. hearing, reflection and meditation, have been gone through", and not "by hearing alone". The third step, nididhyasana, is steady meditation on what has already been heard and reasoned out.

That the practice must be long and unbroken is a separate point from Patanjali. Sutra 1.14 says practice "becomes confirmed when it has been cultivated for a long time and uninterruptedly and with earnest attention" (trans. James Haughton Woods, *The Yoga-System of Patanjali*, Harvard Oriental Series 17, 1914, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/yogasystemofpata00wooduoft)). Linking that sutra to nididhyasana is this page's own bridge, since the two texts belong to different schools.

The monthly rhythm (one truth for a month, revisited daily) and the image of a language no longer being translated are also the page's own. I found neither in the sources opened.

**Plain words**

Understanding something and seeing by it are different. The tradition asks you to keep returning to a truth you already follow until it is simply how you look at things. A month on one idea is a modest, testable way to try that.

**For example**

A man knows, in the abstract, that his dread before meetings is mostly noise. Each morning for a month he spends five minutes with that one sentence. By the third week he catches the dread arriving and recognises it before it takes hold.
