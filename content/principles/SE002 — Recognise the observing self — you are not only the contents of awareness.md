> **Principle 2** — Recognise the observing self: you are not only the contents of awareness

There is a distinction between the contents of experience (thoughts, feelings, sensations) and the awareness that observes them. The observing self — the capacity to be aware of experience without being identical to it — is a foundation of psychological flexibility and wisdom.

**Plain words** There's a distinction between the contents of experience — thoughts, feelings, sensations — and the awareness that observes them. The observing self: the capacity to be aware without being the weather. You are not only the contents; something is watching them.

*For example:* The panic attack that says "you ARE the panic" — and the moment of noticing "there is panic occurring" — a distinction as small as grammar and as large as freedom. The contents raged; the observer was never in danger.

### The observing self

Arthur Deikman, who gave the concept its clearest modern formulation:

> "Emotions, thoughts, impulses, images, and sensations are the contents of consciousness: we witness them; we are aware of their existence. Likewise, the body, the self-image, and the self‑concept are all constructs that we observe. But our core sense of personal existence ‑- the "I" ‑- is located in awareness itself, not in its content." (Arthur Deikman, [The Observing Self](https://www.deikman.com/observing.html))

The asymmetry that makes it easy to miss:

> "In contrast, we cannot observe the observing self; we must experience it directly. It has no defining qualities, no boundaries, no dimensions." (Arthur Deikman, [The Observing Self](https://www.deikman.com/observing.html))

And why the distinction matters clinically:

> "Yet, at the heart of psychopathology lies a fundamental confusion between the self as object and the self of pure subjectivity." (Arthur Deikman, [The Observing Self](https://www.deikman.com/observing.html))

In a later paper he sharpened the point against the infinite-regress objection:

> "The core `I' of subjectivity is different from any content because it turns out to be that which witnesses — not that which is observed. The 'I' can be experienced, but it cannot be `seen'. `I' is the observer, the experiencer, prior to all conscious content." (Arthur Deikman, ["I = Awareness"](https://www.deikman.com/awareness.html))

Acceptance and commitment therapy builds the same distinction into a core process, calling it self-as-context: the perspective from which thoughts and feelings are noticed as passing events rather than instructions or identities. The practical exercise is the one in the Practice section below, and the panic example above is it in miniature: re-describing "I am panicking" as "there is panic occurring" does not change the weather, but it relocates you from inside the storm to the place where the storm is being watched.

## Practice

- Practise noticing: "there is a thought," "there is a feeling," "there is a sensation." This small shift creates a gap between you and the contents of experience. You are the space in which experience occurs, not the experience itself.
