> **Principle 2** — Recognise the observing self: you are not only the contents of awareness

There is a distinction between the contents of experience (thoughts, feelings, sensations) and the awareness that observes them. The observing self — the capacity to be aware of experience without being identical to it — is a foundation of psychological flexibility and wisdom.

**Plain words** There is a difference between a storm and the window you watch it from. Thoughts, feelings and sensations arrive, change and pass; the noticing of them does not come and go in the same way. Saying "there is panic" instead of "I am panic" leaves the storm untouched, but it moves you to the window. That shift, small as grammar, is what the observing self refers to.

*For example:* The panic attack that says "you ARE the panic" — and the moment of noticing "there is panic occurring" — a distinction as small as grammar and as large as freedom. The contents raged; the observer was never in danger.

### The observing self

Arthur Deikman, in the opening chapter of The Observing Self:

> "Emotions, thoughts, impulses, images, and sensations are the contents of consciousness: we witness them; we are aware of their existence. Likewise, the body, the self-image, and the self‑concept are all constructs that we observe. But our core sense of personal existence ‑- the "I" ‑- is located in awareness itself, not in its content." (Arthur Deikman, ["An Invitation", opening chapter of The Observing Self](https://www.deikman.com/observing.html))

The asymmetry that makes it easy to miss:

> "In contrast, we cannot observe the observing self; we must experience it directly. It has no defining qualities, no boundaries, no dimensions." (Arthur Deikman, ["An Invitation", opening chapter of The Observing Self](https://www.deikman.com/observing.html))

And why the distinction matters clinically:

> "Yet, at the heart of psychopathology lies a fundamental confusion between the self as object and the self of pure subjectivity." (Arthur Deikman, ["An Invitation", opening chapter of The Observing Self](https://www.deikman.com/observing.html))

In a later paper he sharpened the point against the infinite-regress objection:

> "The core `I' of subjectivity is different from any content because it turns out to be that which witnesses — not that which is observed. The 'I' can be experienced, but it cannot be `seen'. `I' is the observer, the experiencer, prior to all conscious content." (Arthur Deikman, ["I = Awareness", Journal of Consciousness Studies](https://www.deikman.com/awareness.html))

The exercise in the Practice section below is the distinction put to work: noticing "there is a thought" gives the content room to be one event among others, rather than the whole of what is happening.

## Practice

- Practise noticing: "there is a thought," "there is a feeling," "there is a sensation." This small shift creates a gap between you and the contents of experience. You are the space in which experience occurs, not the experience itself.
