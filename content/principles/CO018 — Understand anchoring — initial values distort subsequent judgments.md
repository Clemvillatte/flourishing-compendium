> **Principle 18** — Understand anchoring: initial values distort subsequent judgments

When making numerical estimates or judgments, the first number or frame you encounter anchors your estimate, even when it is arbitrary or irrelevant. This affects negotiations, pricing, probability estimates, and self-assessment.

**Plain words** Anchoring: the first number or frame you encounter anchors your estimate, even when arbitrary. Negotiations, pricing, probability estimates — all pulled by the first figure on the table. Know your anchors; set some deliberately.

*For example:* The "was $200, now $80" that makes the shirt feel like a find — the $200 was never a price, it was an anchor. And the salary negotiation where whoever names a number first drags the range: anchors aren't influences on judgment; they ARE the judgment's starting point.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Anchoring effect|Anchoring effect]]**, initial values bias subsequent numerical judgments (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974)
- **Anchoring is robust across many domains**, including self-evaluation and negotiation (Epley & Gilovich, 2006; Furnham & Boo, 2011)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[First impressions bias|First impressions bias]]** (Buddhism, Right View)
- **[[The primacy of the given|The primacy of the given]]** (Husserl, Ideas)

## Practice

- Before making an estimate or judgment, generate your own initial answer before being exposed to others' numbers or frames. In negotiations, be aware of who set the anchor.
