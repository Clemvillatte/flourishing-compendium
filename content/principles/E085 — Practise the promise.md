> **Principle 477** — Practise the promise

For Ricoeur the promise is the clearest case of selfhood, not the whole of it. "Keeping one's word expresses a self-constancy which cannot be inscribed, as character was, within the dimension of something in general but solely within the dimension of who?" (Oneself as Another, p. 123, as quoted in Truc, 2008). Character lets others recognise you by habit. A promise holds even when habit and desire move: "even if my desire were to change, even if I were to change my opinion or my inclination: 'I will hold firm'" (as quoted in Halsema).

### Being counted on

He defines the result by what others can do with it: "Self-constancy is for each person that manner of conducting himself or herself so that others can count on that person. Because someone is counting on me, I am accountable for my actions before another." The answer to another who needs me is "Here I am!", "a statement of self-constancy" (Oneself as Another, p. 165, as quoted in Dierckxsens, 2017).

### Marcel's version

Marcel, whom the principle cites, gives the same act a first-person angle. "When I commit myself, I grant in principle that the commitment will not again be put into question." He calls this "a rudimentary form of creative fidelity", and adds the paradox: "to commit myself I must know myself, but the fact is I really only know myself when I have committed myself" (Marcel 1964, pp. 162-163, quoted in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy).

**Plain words** Character is the part of you others can predict: same habits, same reactions. A promise is different in kind. It is a bet on yourself that holds while your inclinations move, and Ricoeur treats it as the clearest showing of selfhood for exactly that reason. The other side is social: because people count on you, you become someone they can plan around.

*For example:* you promised to help a friend move on Saturday, then a better offer comes in. Keeping the promise tells your friend, and you, that your word does not depend on your mood.

(Ricoeur, Oneself as Another, trans. Blamey (1992), pp. 123, 165, via Truc, Halsema and Dierckxsens; Marcel via the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy; all.)
