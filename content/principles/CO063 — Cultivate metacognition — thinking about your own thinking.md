> **Principle 63** — Cultivate metacognition: thinking about your own thinking

Flavell introduced the term in 1979 and gave it a structure. His model says that "the monitoring of a wide variety of cognitive enterprises occurs through the actions of and interactions among four classes of phenomena: (a) metacognitive knowledge, (b) metacognitive experiences, (c) goals (or tasks), and (d) actions (or strategies)" ([Flavell, 1979, American Psychologist 34](https://jwilson.coe.uga.edu/EMAT7050/Students/Wilson/Flavell%20(1979).pdf)).

### The founding observation

His starting point was a memory study with children. Both age groups studied until they said they were ready. "The older subjects studied for a while, said they were ready, and usually were, that is, they showed perfect recall. The younger children studied for a while, said they were ready, and usually were not." Young children, he concluded, "do relatively little monitoring of their own memory, comprehension, and other cognitive enterprises."

### What counts as metacognition

Metacognitive experiences are "any conscious cognitive or affective experiences that accompany and pertain to any intellectual enterprise", such as "the sudden feeling that you do not understand something another person just said." Metacognitive knowledge concerns what affects "the course and outcome of cognitive enterprises", across person, task and strategy variables (as summarised in an [Oxford handbook chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61768/chapter/553589652)).

### A caution from Flavell

Monitoring can backfire. He names "the feckless obsessive, paralyzed by incessant critical evaluation of his own judgments and decisions", yet still holds there is "far too little ... cognitive monitoring in this world" (same chapter). Fleming and Dolan (2012) add that metacognitive accuracy "is dissociable from task performance and varies across individuals" ([abstract](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:ae68a055-a477-4355-b444-915b47ba6938)). Being good at a task does not mean you are good at judging how well you did it.

"Trainable" and "the OS above the OS" are the compendium's wording; the sources opened here do not show that metacognition can be trained.

(Flavell, 1979.)

**Plain words** Metacognition is noticing how your own thinking is going: whether you understand, whether you are confused, whether the way you are working is working. Flavell's early evidence was simple. Younger children said they were ready and were not, and older ones judged better. The skill is not the same as being good at the task itself, and too much self-checking can freeze you.

*For example:* You finish a page and ask whether you could explain it to someone else. If you could not, that feeling is a metacognitive experience, and what you do next, such as re-reading with a question in mind, is the strategy it points to.

## Practice

- Practise asking: "do I actually understand this, or does it just feel familiar?" "Am I sure, or do I just feel sure?" Test yourself rather than assuming understanding.
