> **Principle 118** — Engineer flow: match challenge to skill

The claim that flow needs a match between challenge and skill is Csikszentmihalyi's. His book has not been checked for this page; the check here is a meta-analysis of the research that followed.

### What the evidence says

Fong, Zaleski and Leach (2015) pooled 28 studies. Flow, they write, is "characterized by simultaneous perception of high challenge and skill". Their finding: "the relationship between challenge–skill balance and flow was moderate, and smaller with intrinsic motivation." Compared with other proposed antecedents, challenge–skill balance "was a robust contributor to flow along with clear goals and sense of control" ([abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17439760.2014.967799)).

### Where it is weaker

The same abstract reports "weaker correlations when individuals were from an individualistic culture, in work or education contexts, using experience sampling method, and self-reporting state flow vs. trait." A later paper reads this as a questioning of how robust the balance is, moderated by "age, culture, and the activity domain", and suggests high challenge may not always be needed ([Where do people experience flow](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/23311908.2025.2538737)). So "match challenge to skill" is a well-supported rule of thumb, not a precise law, and the principle's own job example is a work setting, one of those with weaker correlations.

### What is the compendium's

"Raise skill, not always lower challenge" and "boredom is a skills surplus" are practical readings. They fit the model (if challenge sits below skill, one fix is to raise challenge), but the meta-analysis does not test them.

(Csikszentmihalyi, Flow, 1990; Fong et al., 2015.)

**Plain words** People are more likely to report flow when a task is hard enough to stretch them and they have the skill to meet it. Too easy and attention drifts. Too hard and anxiety takes over. You can adjust either side: make the task harder, or build the skill. The link is real but moderate, so treat it as a place to start.

*For example:* Imagine a task you could do in your sleep. Raising the difficulty, say by setting a tighter time or a harder case, is one way to bring the challenge level back up toward your skill.
