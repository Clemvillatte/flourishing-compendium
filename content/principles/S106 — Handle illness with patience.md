> **Principle 228** — Handle illness with patience

Illness is a dispreferred indifferent. The Stoic does not seek illness but does not let it destroy their peace. Epictetus, who was physically disabled, taught that illness does not touch the character. Face illness with patience and dignity.

(Ench. IX; Med. VII.33, VII.64, IX.41; Seneca, Letters 78.)

Epictetus gives the doctrine its sharpest form: "Sickness is an impediment to the body, but not to the will unless itself pleases. Lameness is an impediment to the leg, but not to the will; and say this to yourself with regard to everything that happens. For you will find it to be an impediment to something else, but not truly to yourself" ([Enchiridion IX, Thomas Wentworth Higginson translation, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/45109/pg45109.txt), opened). The body can be slowed, but the disposition cannot be made worse by the sickness.

Marcus repeats the point from the pain side: "the pain which is intolerable carries us off; but that which lasts a long time is tolerable; and the mind maintains its own tranquillity by retiring into itself" ([Meditations VII.33, George Long translation, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt), opened). The same book adds: "In every pain let this thought be present, that there is no dishonor in it" (VII.64, same source).

Marcus also records Epicurus' bedside practice: "Epicurus says, In my sickness my conversation was not about my bodily sufferings, nor, says he, did I talk on such subjects to those who visited me; but I continued to discourse on the nature of things as before" (IX.41, same source). A Stoic emperor quotes the rival school approvingly; the technique is the point, not the school. Seneca's Letter 78 is the classic Stoic treatment of illness as something to be endured rather than feared; the body leans on the compendium's own S023 record of that opening, not a fresh quote.

**Plain words** Being sick is unwelcome, but it is only a change to what the body can do. The sick person still has full control over how they think, speak and treat the people around them, and nothing about a bed, a fever or a limp can take that away. Patience in illness is not pretending you are well; it is refusing to let a sick body talk you into a sour disposition.

*For example (imagined):* A hospital stay spent talking kindly with the nurses rather than raging at the delay.
