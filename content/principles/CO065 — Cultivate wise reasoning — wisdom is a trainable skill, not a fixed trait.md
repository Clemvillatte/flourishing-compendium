> **Principle 65** — Cultivate wise reasoning: wisdom is a trainable skill, not a fixed trait

Wise reasoning, in Grossmann and Kross's operationalisation, is a set of moves: recognising the limits of one's knowledge, considering other perspectives, recognising compromise and future change. Their paper on Solomon's paradox reports, verbatim:

> "In three experiments (N = 693), participants displayed wiser reasoning (i.e., recognizing the limits of their knowledge and the importance of compromise and future change, considering other people's perspectives) about another person's problems compared with their own. Across Studies 2 and 3, instructing individuals to self-distance (rather than self-immerse) eliminated this asymmetry." (Grossmann and Kross, 2014, Psychological Science 25(8), 1571-1580, [author-hosted full text](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/emotion-selfcontrol-psych/wp-content/uploads/sites/1322/2024/07/grossman.pdf))

They frame the paradox itself as "a common habit of mind: People reason more wisely about other people's social problems than about their own." The intervention is the remarkable part: reasoning in the third person, as if about someone else, eliminated the asymmetry, with effects comparable for younger and older adults, contrary to the adage "with age comes wisdom." Note what this is: evidence that a change of perspective changes wise reasoning about one's own conflicts. The principle title's broader claim, that wisdom is trainable rather than a fixed trait, is supported here in that specific sense; the general training literature is inherited below.

**Plain words** People reason more wisely about other people's problems than their own, and the gap closes when you reason about your own problem from the outside, as if it were someone else's. The practical move: when stuck on your own conflict, ask how you would advise a friend in the same spot, and answer in those terms.

*For example:* After the layoff, instead of the bitter monologue, the advice-to-a-friend move: what don't I know here, how would this look from the CEO's desk, what is true in both stories? It is the same event either way; the perspective is what differs.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Wise reasoning|Wise reasoning (Grossmann)]]**, wisdom involves intellectual humility, contextualism, recognition of change, and integration of perspectives (Grossmann et al., 2013; Grossmann, 2017)
- **Ego-centrism reduces wise reasoning**, self-focus and emotional involvement reduce wise reasoning; distanced self-talk increases it (Grossmann & Kross, 2014)
- **Wise reasoning predicts well-being**, wise reasoning is associated with better relationship outcomes, longevity, and life satisfaction (Grossmann et al., 2013)

## Practice

- When facing a difficult situation, practise wise reasoning: (1) acknowledge the limits of your knowledge, (2) consider the context and other perspectives, (3) recognise that things change, (4) look for ways to integrate different views. Use third-person self-talk to reduce ego-centrism.
