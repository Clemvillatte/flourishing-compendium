> **Principle 65** — Cultivate wise reasoning: wisdom is a trainable skill, not a fixed trait

Wise reasoning — the capacity to recognise the limits of one's knowledge, consider context and multiple perspectives, recognise change and uncertainty, and integrate different viewpoints — is not a fixed trait. It can be measured, trained, and improved.

**Plain words** Wise reasoning — recognising the limits of your knowledge, considering context and multiple perspectives, integrating viewpoints — is not a fixed trait or a product of IQ. It's a mode you can practise, and it practises better in the third person and after setbacks. Wisdom is a skill with a workout schedule.

*For example:* The person who after the layoff runs the wise-reasoning moves — "what don't I know here? how would this look from the CEO's desk? what's true in both stories?" — versus the one who runs the bitter monologue. Same event; one of them got wiser by Thursday.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Wise reasoning|Wise reasoning (Grossmann)]]**, wisdom involves intellectual humility, contextualism, recognition of change, and integration of perspectives (Grossmann et al., 2013; Grossmann, 2017)
- **Ego-centrism reduces wise reasoning**, self-focus and emotional involvement reduce wise reasoning; distanced self-talk increases it (Grossmann & Kross, 2014)
- **Wise reasoning predicts well-being**, wise reasoning is associated with better relationship outcomes, longevity, and life satisfaction (Grossmann et al., 2013)

- **[[Phronesis|Phronesis (practical wisdom)]]** (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI)

## Practice

- When facing a difficult situation, practise wise reasoning: (1) acknowledge the limits of your knowledge, (2) consider the context and other perspectives, (3) recognise that things change, (4) look for ways to integrate different views. Use third-person self-talk to reduce ego-centrism.
