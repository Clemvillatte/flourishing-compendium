> **Principle 128** — Don't cling even to the teaching (dharma-grasping)

MN 22 gives the teaching's use in two similes. The water-snake section describes those who "study the Dhamma both for attacking others and for defending themselves in debate" and so "don't reach the goal for which (people) study the Dhamma" ([MN 22, Thanissaro](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN22.html)). The raft simile then states the teaching's purpose directly.

> "Monks, I will teach you the Dhamma compared to a raft, for the purpose of crossing over, not for the purpose of holding onto."
>
> (Alagaddūpama Sutta, MN 22, Thanissaro, the raft simile)

In the simile, a man crosses a great expanse of water on a raft he has built, and then asks whether he should hoist it on his head and carry it wherever he goes. The monks' answer is no: dragging it to dry land or sinking it in the water is what should be done with the raft. The discourse closes the simile with the sentence this principle restates:

> "In the same way, monks, I have taught the Dhamma compared to a raft, for the purpose of crossing over, not for the purpose of holding onto. Understanding the Dhamma as taught compared to a raft, you should let go even of Dhammas, to say nothing of non-Dhammas."
>
> (Alagaddūpama Sutta, MN 22, Thanissaro)

Dhammapada 97 praises "the man who is without blind faith, who knows the Uncreated" ([Dhp 97, Buddharakkhita](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/dhp/dhp.07.budd.html)); the verse pairs freedom from credulity with knowledge rather than allegiance. The compendium's application of the raft simile: notice when a practice or belief has stopped being something you use and become something you carry.

**Plain words**
A teaching is equipment, like the raft in the sutta: you build it, cross with it, and leave it at the shore. Test your relation to any practice or belief you hold by asking what it is doing for you now. If the answer is mostly winning arguments, decorating a self-image, or furnishing an identity, the grasping has replaced the crossing. The sutta's own words for the failure are studying the Dhamma "for attacking others and for defending themselves in debate".

*For example:* Imagine someone who brings up their meditation retreat in every conversation and cannot let a remark about Buddhism pass uncorrected. MN 22's water-snake passage names the pattern: the study has become a device for attack and defense.
