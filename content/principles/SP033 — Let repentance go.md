> **Principle 33** — Let repentance go

Spinoza's claim is in Ethics IV, Proposition 54: "Repentance is not a virtue, or does not arise from reason; but he who repents of an action is doubly wretched or infirm." His proof uses the definition of repentance as "pain accompanied by the idea of some action, which we believe" we did by free decision. The person "allows himself to be overcome, first, by evil desires; secondly, by pain" ([Ethics, Part IV, tr. Elwes](https://sacred-texts.com/phi/spinoza/ethics/eth04.htm); [definition in the Elwes text](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/spinoza/1675/ethics.pdf)). The two defeats are the bad desire and the pain that follows. The text does not say "sorry for having been sorry".

The scholium qualifies the proposition: "As men seldom live under the guidance of reason", humility and repentance, like hope and fear, "bring more good than harm; hence, as we must sin, we had better sin in that direction" (same Elwes text). The Stanford Encyclopedia notes that Spinoza treats these traditional virtues as, "if not genuine virtues themselves, at least means to virtue" ([SEP, Spinoza's Psychological Theory](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/spinoza-psychological/)).

(Ethics IV, P54; the analysis of repentance as doubled sorrow.)

### Scope

The advice to extract one lesson and move on is the compendium's addition. I did not find it in the passages I opened. I did not open Spinoza's other remarks on remorse in Part III or any research on self-criticism.

**Plain words**
For Spinoza, repentance adds pain to a mistake without helping you act better. He still thinks it can help people who are not yet guided by reason.

**For example**
After a mistake, write down what happened, what you would do differently, and one repair you can make. Then act on the repair rather than replaying the event.
