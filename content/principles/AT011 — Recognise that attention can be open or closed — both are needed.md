> **Principle 11** — Recognise that attention can be open or closed: both are needed

Attention can be narrowly focused (closed, concentrated) or broadly open (receptive, inclusive). Each has costs and benefits. Narrow focus enables concentration and deep engagement but can miss context and novelty. Open awareness enables creativity, context-sensitivity, and flexibility but can be scattered.

### The Buddhist pairing

The oldest meditation traditions hold both modes, and Thanissaro's reading of the Pali discourses is that samatha and vipassana were never rival techniques but "complementary ways of relating to the present moment":

> "Samatha provides a sense of ease in the present; vipassana, a clear-eyed view of events as they actually occur, in and of themselves." — Thanissaro Bhikkhu, "One Tool Among Many" ([Access to Insight](https://accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/thanissaro/onetool.html))

On that reading, "the proper path is one in which vipassana and samatha are brought into balance, each supporting and acting as a check on the other" (Thanissaro).

### The Daoist pairing

The Daoist grammar of pairs is the same shape: "difficulty and ease produce the one (the idea of) the other" (Daodejing 2, [Legge trans.](https://classics.mit.edu/Lao/taote.mb.txt)). Read as a pair, the two attention modes define each other, and neither is meant to stand alone.

**Plain words** A camera has one lens and two habits: the tight shot that finds the detail, and the wide shot that finds the room. Attention is the same instrument. The skill is knowing which shot the moment calls for, and switching without pride.

*For example:* The surgeon narrows for the incision, then broadens to check the room, the patient, the team. Wide open during the incision is how you miss things; narrowed all day is how you burn out.

## Practice

- Match attentional mode to task. Use narrow focus for detailed, analytical work. Use open awareness for creative, integrative, or contextual thinking. Do not use one mode exclusively.
