> **Principle 373** — Practise the recognition of difference

> The thesis is that our identity is partly shaped by recognition or its absence, often by the misrecognition of others, and so a person or group of people can suffer real damage, real distortion, if the people or society around them mirror back to them a confining or demeaning or contemptible picture of themselves. Nonrecognition or misrecognition can inflict harm, can be a form of oppression, imprisoning someone in a false, distorted, and reduced mode of being.

(Taylor, "The Politics of Recognition," in Multiculturalism: Examining the Politics of Recognition, ed. Amy Gutmann, Princeton University Press, 1994, p. 25; first published 1992. Text read in [a PDF copy of the essay](https://web.ics.purdue.edu/~drkelly/TaylorPoliticsOfRecognition1994.pdf). The same page is cited in the [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/recog_sp/).)

Taylor does not simply say that all differences should be celebrated. He proposes a "presumption of equal worth," which he says is "by no means unproblematic, and involves something like an act of faith." The claim is "that all human cultures that have animated whole societies over some considerable stretch of time have something important to say to all human beings." He also warns that a demand for favourable judgments of every culture "is paradoxically – perhaps one should say tragically – homogenizing," because it "implies that we already have the standards to make such judgments" (as quoted in a [Canadian thesis](https://www.collectionscanada.gc.ca/obj/thesescanada/vol2/002/MR59229.PDF?is_thesis=1&oclc_number=759667747), page not given there).

For the psychology, Phinney and Chavira followed 18 adolescents from three ethnic groups from age 16 to 19 and found that "self-esteem and ethnic identity were significantly related to each other at each time period" (Journal of Adolescence 15(3), 1992, abstract; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1447413/)). The sample was small and exploratory. A later study found a moderate link of r = .40 between ethnic identity and self-esteem even after accounting for shared method effects ([PMC4240512](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4240512/), abstract and results as shown). These show association, and say nothing about whether recognition of cultural difference causes well-being. The Phinney (1992) paper the entry cites is the Multigroup Ethnic Identity Measure, which was not opened. Kymlicka and Honneth were not opened either.

**Plain words** Taylor argues that how others see your group affects how you see yourself, and that being shown a demeaning picture of yourself does real harm. His remedy is a starting presumption that other cultures have something worth learning from, not a requirement to praise everything.

*For example (imagined):* A workplace calendar built around one tradition's holidays is revised to include the festivals that people in the room keep. The change costs little, and it tells everyone that they belong in the picture.
