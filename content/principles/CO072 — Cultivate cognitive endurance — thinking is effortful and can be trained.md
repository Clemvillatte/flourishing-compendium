> **Principle 72** — Cultivate cognitive endurance: thinking is effortful and can be trained

Treat a difficult reading session as a task to organise, not a verdict on your intelligence. That is a practical invitation; the sources below do not establish a general "endurance muscle."

### Three sources, different claims

Aristotle distinguishes intellectual virtue, which grows mainly through teaching, from moral virtue, which comes through habit:

> "intellectual virtue in the main owes both its birth and its growth to teaching (for which reason it requires experience and time)" ([Nicomachean Ethics II.1, Ross translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.ii.html)).

This is a philosophical account, not a test of cognitive endurance.

Ericsson and colleagues studied specialist expertise and [[Deliberate practice]]. Their violinists' retrospective practice estimates were associated with performance; the authors explicitly limit the finding to a relationship, not proof of causation ([1993, p. 380](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/1993-ericsson.pdf)).

A two-week randomised mindfulness trial reported improved GRE reading comprehension and working memory, with less mind wandering. It tested mindfulness, not whether effortful reading builds endurance ([Mrazek et al., 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23538911/)). These sources do not establish the full claim in this entry's inherited title.

### Plain words

Instead of asking, "How long can I force myself to sit here?", try, "What am I trying to understand?" Choose a paragraph, explain it in your own words, and note what remains unclear. That is a way to organise a session, not a promise that persistence will expand your capacity.

### For example

Imagine returning to one difficult argument tomorrow with a written question. The example proposes a next step; it does not explain why reading felt hard.

## Sources

- [Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.1 (W.D. Ross translation), Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.ii.html) — intellectual virtue and teaching distinguished from moral habituation
- [Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer, "The Role of Deliberate Practice in the Acquisition of Expert Performance," Psychological Review 100 (1993) (PDF)](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/1993-ericsson.pdf) — p. 380, explicit limit of the correlational musician comparison
- [Mrazek et al., "Mindfulness Training Improves Working Memory Capacity and GRE Performance While Reducing Mind Wandering," Psychological Science 24 (2013) (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23538911/) — abstract of a two-week randomised mindfulness trial, not an endurance-practice trial

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Deliberate practice|Deliberate practice builds cognitive stamina]]**: this broader claim is not established by Ericsson et al.'s (1993) specialist musician comparison.
- **Mindfulness and task performance**: the specific intervention and outcomes reported by Mrazek et al. (2013).

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[The examined life|The examined life]]**: a related compendium theme, not empirical evidence for this title.
- **[[Right effort|Right effort]]**: a related practice theme, not an equivalent of the tested intervention.
- **[[The unity of the virtues|Virtue as habituated capacity]]**: compare Aristotle II.1 on moral virtue; do not substitute it for evidence about cognition.
