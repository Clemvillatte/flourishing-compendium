> **Principle 72** — Cultivate cognitive endurance: thinking is effortful and can be trained

Aristotle's model of excellence is training, and he argues it from the stone:

> "the stone which by nature moves downwards cannot be habituated to move upwards, not even if one tries to train it by throwing it up ten thousand times; [...] Neither by nature, then, nor contrary to nature do the virtues arise in us; rather we are adapted by nature to receive them, and are made perfect by habit." (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.1, [Ross's translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.ii.html))

The stone marks the boundary. Whatever behaves one way by nature cannot be trained into another, so whatever does respond to training was never fixed by nature in the first place. Aristotle's case is philosophical; the empirical picture on one terrain, expertise, comes from Ericsson's Berlin academy study, where the gap between the best and the good violinists was already wide by age 18: "At this age, the best young violinists had accumulated an average of 7,410 hr of practice, which is reliably different from 5,301 hr, the average number of hours accumulated by the good violinists" ([Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer 1993](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/1993-ericsson.pdf)). And the practice that marked the difference was of a particular kind: "We claim that deliberate practice requires effort and is not inherently enjoyable" ([ibid.](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/1993-ericsson.pdf)). The principle carries that logic to thinking itself: reading difficult material, working through a problem without surrender. The direct evidence that cognition moves with training comes from a two-week randomized trial: "Mindfulness training improved both GRE reading-comprehension scores and working memory capacity while simultaneously reducing the occurrence of distracting thoughts during completion of the GRE and the measure of working memory" ([Mrazek et al. 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23538911/)). The regimen that builds it is [[Deliberate practice]].

**Plain words**
Thinking hard for a long stretch runs on talent and mileage. The part you can affect is the mileage: hours spent at the edge of what you can follow. Build it the way you build running: a little more each week than feels easy.

*For example:* The person who "can't read books anymore" after a decade of feeds — and the month of gradual retraining: ten pages, then twenty, then an hour. The capacity wasn't lost; it was detrained. The attention is a muscle and the feed was the couch.

## Psychology parallels

- **Cognitive endurance / need for cognition**, people who enjoy effortful thinking show better reasoning and learning (Cacioppo & Petty, 1982)
- **[[Deliberate practice|Deliberate practice builds cognitive stamina]]**, sustained engagement with difficult material improves capacity (Ericsson et al., 1993)
- **Note: ego depletion (willpower as muscle) failed replication**, but the subjective experience of mental fatigue and the benefit of practice are real (Hagger et al., 2016; Inzlicht et al., 2015)

## Practice

- Practise sustained thinking: read a difficult text for 30 minutes without distraction, work through a problem without giving up, hold a complex idea in mind. Build tolerance for cognitive effort.
