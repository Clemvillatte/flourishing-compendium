> **Principle 21** — Treat truth as something you stand in, not only something you hold

Scholars usually translate the Nahuatl word neltiliztli as "truth," but James Maffie notes that Nahua philosophers "did not understand truth in terms of correspondence (or coherence)". They understood it as "well-grounded stability, well-foundedness, and well-rootedness", citing Leon-Portilla's reading (Maffie, "Aztec Philosophy," Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, section 4b). The word applied widely. The Nahuas "characterized persons, things, activities, and utterances equally and without equivocation in terms of neltiliztli". That which is well-rooted is "genuine, true, authentic, and well-balanced" (section 4b).

Maffie ties this to how a life goes. To be wise, people "must become neltiliztli, i.e. well-rooted, authentic, true" (section 3b). He writes that Nahua thinkers saw humans as "creatures yearning for rootedness", and that many "give their hearts to what appears to be well-rooted and authenthic [sic] but is not", so their hearts become "scattered, unbalanced, and lost". He quotes Nezahualcoyotl: "If you give your heart to each and everything, you lead it nowhere: you destroy your heart" (section 3b). Maffie presents this as his own reconstruction of Nahua thought, and I read it that way.

**Plain words**
In this view a belief or a person is true when it has roots. Ask of each conviction whether it grew from your own experience or was borrowed. Then check whether how you spend your days fits what you say you value.

**For example**
Someone says family matters most, yet every calendar slot goes to work. Their words have little root. Moving a weekly dinner into the calendar and keeping it begins to supply one.
