> **Principle 55** — Learn to work with self and others' expectations: navigate social definitions

The construct behind this principle is Cooley's looking-glass self: the self as seen in the imagined minds of others.

> "A self-idea of this sort seems to have three principal elements: the imagination of our appearance to the other person; the imagination of his judgement of that appearance; and some sort of self-feeling, such as pride or mortification." (Cooley, Human Nature and the Social Order, 1902, pp. 151-53)

Cooley adds a note that does the principle's work for it: "The thing that moves us to pride or shame is not the mere mechanical reflection of ourselves, but an imputed sentiment, the imagined effect of this reflection upon another's mind." The mirror is imagined, which is why the reflection can be examined rather than merely absorbed. The compendium's application: others will hand you definitions of yourself all your life, and the skill is processing incoming definitions instead of swallowing them whole; being defined by others is unavoidable, being determined by them is not. That navigation framing is the page's application; Cooley describes the process, not the resistance to it.

**Plain words**
Cooley's three beats: I imagine how I appear to you, I imagine your judgment of that, and I feel something, pride or mortification. The second beat is the load-bearing one, and it is imagined. That is the gap the principle works in: since the judgment is an imputation, it can be taken out, looked at, and kept or handed back.

*For example:* Imagine the whole family calls her "the flaky one" because she was late twice at nineteen. At forty, she is the most organised person at her firm, and still flinches at the nickname. At some point the label has to be handed back: "that was one decade, not a diagnosis."

## Practice

- Notice which others' definitions you have internalised. Ask: "is this my view of myself, or someone else's view that I have absorbed?" Retain what fits; release what does not.
