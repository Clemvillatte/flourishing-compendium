> **Principle 24** — Learn to use chaining: link behaviours into sequences

In applied behaviour analysis, chaining teaches the component steps of a task in sequence, using prompts and differential reinforcement. In forward chaining, the first step is taught first. Later steps are added while the learner continues to perform the steps already taught.

> "Response chaining involves breaking a task into its component parts via a task analysis and then sequentially teaching each individual component to mastery levels via prompting and differential reinforcement." (Slocum & Tiger, 2011, p. 793)

Fogg's recipe cards describe a related habit-design approach: an existing routine acts as a reminder for a small new behaviour. This differs from teaching a task's component steps through prompting and reinforcement.

**Plain words** Write out the actions involved in a familiar task. Learning the first action gives you a place to begin; you can then practise it together with the next action. A difficult sequence can be taught a little at a time.

*For example* Imagined: an instructor first teaches a learner to place a toothbrush on the counter. Applying toothpaste is added next, with both steps practised together.
