> **Principle 79** — Do not demonise pleasure

Aristotle's discussion of pleasure in Book X argues that pleasure is not the Good, but that it perfects activity. ([Nicomachean Ethics X.3-4, trans. H. Rackham](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0054:book=10)) The compendium reads this as a refusal to treat pleasure as an enemy of the good.

(NE VII.11-15, X.1-5.)

> "But the pleasure perfects the activity, not as the fixed disposition does, by being already present in the agent, but as a supervening perfection, like the bloom of health in the young and vigorous." (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.4, trans. H. Rackham, [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0054:book=10))

The argument against identifying pleasure with the Good is comparative: "if pleasure combined with something else is better than pleasure alone, it is not the Good, for the Good is not rendered more desirable by the addition of anything to it." ([NE X.2, Rackham](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0054:book=10)) The chapter's conclusion is measured: "It seems therefore that pleasure is not the Good, and that not every pleasure is desirable, but also that there are certain pleasures, superior in respect of their specific quality or their source, that are desirable in themselves." ([NE X.3, Rackham](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0054:book=10))

Pleasures also differ with their activities: "pleasures correspond to the activities to which they belong; it is therefore that pleasure, or those pleasures, by which the activity, or the activities, of the perfect and supremely happy man are perfected, that must be pronounced human in the fullest sense." ([NE X.5, Rackham](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0054:book=10))

**Plain words**
Pleasure isn't the good, but it isn't the enemy either. ([NE X.3, Rackham](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0054:book=10)) Aristotle says it perfects activity the way a bloom accompanies good health. ([NE X.4, Rackham](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0054:book=10)) So the question isn't whether to enjoy things, it's which activities are worth enjoying.

*For example:* Enjoying a long meal with people you like, after a day of work you're proud of, belongs to a good life. Pleasure doesn't need an apology.
