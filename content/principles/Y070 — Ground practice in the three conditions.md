> **Principle 70** — Ground practice in the three conditions

Sutra 1.14 names the three:

> But this [practice] becomes confirmed when it has been cultivated for a long time and uninterruptedly and with earnest attention.

(Yoga Sutra 1.14, trans. Woods, [p. 35](https://archive.org/details/yogasystemofpata00wooduoft).)

Vyasa unpacks the third condition as a list: practice "carried out with self-castigation and with continence and with knowledge and with faith, in a word, with earnest attention, becomes confirmed" (p. 35). Confirmation has a technical sense: the practice's object is "not likely to have its object suddenly overpowered by an emergent subliminal-impression" (p. 35). The gloss adds the warning that follows: if a man "should fail to persevere... in the course of time he might be overrun [reading abhibhuyeta]. Therefore one must not fail to persevere" (p. 35).

### What the principle adds

Reading the three as separate design requirements to audit, and diagnosing most failures as one missing condition rather than a wrong practice, is the principle's practical reading. The text supplies the three qualifications, the sense of confirmation, and the perseverance warning.

**Plain words** The three conditions are duration, continuity and quality of attention: a long time, no interruptions, and something done with care rather than while distracted. The first two show up as schedule; the third shows up as how you actually attend while you practice. A practice that runs long and unbroken but without the earnest attention is doing less than it appears to.

*For example:* Imagined: the gym-goer with long attendance and real devotion, but a pattern of month-long interruptions. Each restart gives up the compounding the first condition exists for. The one who does less, but never stops, ends up ahead.

(Yoga Sutra 1.13-1.14 with Vyasa's Bhasya and Vacaspati Misra's Tattva-vaicaradi, trans. James Haughton Woods, The Yoga-System of Patanjali (1914), p. 35.)
