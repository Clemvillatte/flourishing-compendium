> **Principle 62** — Let go of what exceeds your grasp

Chapter 29 opens with a warning about trying to acquire and control a kingdom:

> "If any one should wish to get the kingdom for himself, and to effect this by what he does, I see that he will not succeed. The kingdom is a spirit-like thing, and cannot be got by active doing. He who would so win it destroys it; he who would hold it in his grasp loses it." (DDJ 29, Legge trans., [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

The following verse describes reversals of position and fortune:

> "The course and nature of things is such that / What was in front is now behind; / What warmed anon we freezing find. / Strength is of weakness oft the spoil; / The store in ruins mocks our toil." (DDJ 29)

Its conclusion is the sage's practice:

> "Hence the sage puts away excessive effort, extravagance, and easy indulgence." (DDJ 29 §2)

Read for ordinary life rather than statecraft, the chapter's point is about grip. The kingdom is the extreme case of something that cannot be got by active doing, and the verse generalises the risk: positions reverse, strength spoils, stores fall apart. The sage's response is subtractive. He does not find a better grip; he puts away the excessive effort itself.

(DDJ 29.)

**Plain words** Read as advice for everyday life, the chapter suggests noticing when your efforts start damaging what you want to preserve. You might want a friendship to last, yet put it under pressure by demanding constant reassurance. You can explain what you need and listen to the reply, while leaving the other person room to respond freely.

*For example:* Imagine leading a volunteer group. You propose a change and ask for objections before deciding how to proceed. When someone disagrees, discuss their concern instead of demanding a show of loyalty.
