> **Principle 3** — Practise measurement humility

Self-report measures of well-being hold up better than the usual warnings suggest. In his chapter for the Handbook of Well-Being, Richard Lucas writes: "Fortunately, existing research suggests that SWB measures typically have desirable psychometric properties, including relatively high levels of reliability, convergent, discriminant, and construct validity" ([Lucas 2018](https://www.handbookofwellbeing.com/chapters/05-reevaluating-the-strengths-and-weaknesses-of-self-report.html)). Humility still applies, in specific ways.

### The mood effect is weaker than assumed

Schwarz and Clore (1983) found that people in induced good moods reported higher life satisfaction. Lucas reports that nine replications with samples five to ten times larger found effects "typically not significant", with meta-analytic estimates "less than one-tenth the size" of the originals. He concludes: "These studies suggest that mood has only weak effects on life satisfaction judgments." A sample of almost one million Americans found "no evidence that weather systematically influenced satisfaction judgments" (same chapter; [Lucas and Lawless 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23607534/)). So the sunny-Friday example is not a safe illustration.

### Where caution remains

The same chapter lists other concerns: people "may misremember their affective experiences" or "may be unwilling to provide an honest response", and repeated questioning "can change respondent's interpretation of the questions". Diener, Oishi and Tay describe the validity of the measures "and their potential biases" ([Nature Human Behaviour 2018](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41562-018-0307-6)).

### Triangulating

Lucas stresses showing that "different measures of the same construct cohere even when assessed using different methods of assessment", because correlations "can be inflated by shared method variance".

**Plain words** Well-being scales mostly work, and a single score on a single day is still only one reading. Compare methods, and watch change over time.

*For example:* This is an imagined case. A person rates her life satisfaction at 6 in March and 7 in June. She also notes sleep and contact with friends, and treats the one-point change as a prompt to ask what shifted.
