> **Principle 46** — Learn to use the body to regulate emotion: posture, breath, movement

Behavioural emotion regulation works on the body's inputs: how fast you breathe, how you sit.

A systematic review of slow-breathing studies found the practice promotes autonomic changes that increase heart rate variability and respiratory sinus arrhythmia (Zaccaro et al., 2018). Heart-rate-variability biofeedback trains the same loop deliberately: participants breathe slowly and try to maximise respiratory sinus arrhythmia, and most studies find the largest effects around six breaths per minute, with the most supported possible mechanism a strengthening of homeostasis in the baroreceptors (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014).

> "Psychological/behavioral outputs related to the abovementioned changes are increased comfort, relaxation, pleasantness, vigor and alertness, and reduced symptoms of arousal, anxiety, depression, anger, and confusion." (Zaccaro et al., 2018, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 12:353, Abstract, [full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353/full))

Posture carries the same direction under stress. In a randomised trial, participants assigned to sit upright through a stressor speech task reported "higher self-esteem, more arousal, better mood, and lower fear, compared to slumped participants" (Nair et al., 2015, Health Psychology 34(6):632-641, [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25222091/)).

The replication record for related ideas is mixed. In Ranehill et al.'s conceptual replication with a substantially larger sample, the authors "found no significant effect of power posing on hormonal levels or in any of the three behavioral tasks", though the study also reported a significant effect on self-reported feelings of power (Ranehill et al., 2015, Psychological Science 26(5):653-656, [full-text PDF](https://www.zne.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:e5fc4c3c-d50e-4aa9-96ab-4389e426d20d/%20Psychological%20Science-2015-Ranehill-0956797614553946.pdf)). Across 17 direct replications of the original facial-feedback study, the rating difference came out at "0.03 units with a 95% confidence interval ranging from −0.11 to 0.16", against the original 0.82 units (Wagenmakers et al., 2016, Perspectives on Psychological Science 11(6):917-928, [journal PDF](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/1745691616674458)).

**Plain words** Changing your breathing pace or how you sit gives you something concrete to try when you feel wound up. Notice your response. A study reporting better mood in one group does not promise the same response for everyone, or establish an effect on hormones.

**For example:** Before a difficult phone call, sit upright and take about ten slow breaths over two minutes, then make the call.

## Psychology parallels

- **Breath and emotion regulation**, slow breathing promotes autonomic changes that increase heart rate variability, with links to [[Parasympathetic activation|parasympathetic]] activity (Zaccaro et al., 2018; Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014)
- **Posture and mood**, participants randomly assigned to sit upright during a stress task reported higher self-esteem, better mood, and lower fear than slumped participants (Nair et al., 2015)
- **Replication limits**, no significant effect of power posing on hormonal levels or behavioral tasks (Ranehill et al., 2015); a 17-lab replication put the facial-feedback rating difference at 0.03 units, 95% CI −0.11 to 0.16 (Wagenmakers et al., 2016)

## Practice

- Use the body to regulate: slow breathing at about six breaths per minute, upright seated posture.
