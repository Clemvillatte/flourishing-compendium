> **Principle 450** — Practise measure

In The Rebel, measure is not a middle path chosen for comfort. It comes out of the rebel's own "no". A rebel means, among other things, that "there is a limit beyond which you shall not go" (Part One, trans. Bower). In Part Five, "Thought at the Meridian", Camus argues that this limit works as a standard: "every thought, every action that goes beyond a certain point negates itself", so "there is, in fact, a measure by which to judge events and men" (Part Five, "Rebellion and Murder").

### Measure as tension

The section "Moderation and Excess" is the clearest statement of the idea. "Moderation is not the opposite of rebellion. Rebellion in itself is moderation." It is no soft compromise: "Moderation, on the one hand, is nothing but pure tension" and "a perpetual conflict, continually created and mastered by the intelligence". It "does not triumph either in the impossible or in the abyss. It finds its equilibrium through them." He also says that "excess will always keep its place in the heart of man", so measure never abolishes excess; it holds it in check.

### Where he finds it

Camus ties measure to Greek thought. Heraclitus, he says, "set a limit to this perpetual process", which "was symbolized by Nemesis, the goddess of moderation and the implacable enemy of the immoderate". Camus calls it a Mediterranean tradition against German ideology (Part Five, "Thought at the Meridian").

**Plain words** Measure means holding two refusals at once. You do not say yes to everything as it is, and you do not say no to everything. You keep your protest and your limits together, even though that is tiring. Camus says it is tense because both sides keep pulling. For him this is a source of strength, not a half-measure.

*For example:* Imagined example: a friend wants to rage against an employer that treated them badly. Measure is saying the harm was real and that you will not lie about colleagues or burn the references that might help you next year.

(Camus, The Rebel (1951), Part One and Part Five, "Moderation and Excess" and "Thought at the Meridian", trans. Anthony Bower; text opened 2026-10-21.)
