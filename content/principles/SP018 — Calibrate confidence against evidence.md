> **Principle 18** — Calibrate confidence against evidence

In Elwes's translation, the definitions read: "Hope is an inconstant pleasure, arising from the idea of something past or future, whereof we to a certain extent doubt the issue", and fear is the matching pain. "Confidence is pleasure arising from the idea of something past or future, wherefrom all cause of doubt has been removed", and despair is the matching pain ([Ethics, Part III, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3800/3800-h/3800-h.htm)). So hope and fear carry doubt; confidence and despair are what remain once doubt is gone, one pleasant and one painful.

Spinoza adds that "there is no hope unmingled with fear, and no fear unmingled with hope", and that confidence springs from hope, and despair from fear, "when all cause for doubt as to the issue of an event has been removed" (same text).

(Ethics III, definitions of Confidence and Despair.)

### What this supports

The text supports a narrower point than the original entry's: feeling sure of an outcome, good or bad, means the doubt has stopped, whether or not the evidence has. Advice to state confidence as a probability with a reason is a practical addition; Spinoza does not give it in the passages opened, and a calibration study is cited at second hand. Ethics IV on hope and fear was not opened.

**Plain words**
Spinoza files the four feelings in two pairs separated by one thing: doubt. Hope and fear are pleasures and pains that still carry a question mark, which is why they keep trading places. Confidence and despair are what remains once the question mark is spent, and each quietly claims a certainty. Before settling into either, find what paid for the certainty; feelings issue no receipts.

**For example**
Before an uncertain event such as a job decision, write down what you expect to happen and one reason. After the outcome, compare it with what you wrote.
