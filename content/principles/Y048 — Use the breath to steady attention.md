> **Principle 48** — Use the breath to steady attention

Sutra 1.34 offers the breath as one means of steadying the mind: "Or [he gains stability] by expulsion and retention of breath" (trans. Woods, p. 72). The "or" matters. The commentary says it signals a choice among further means, after the cultivation of friendliness and the other feelings in 1.33.

### What the commentary says

"Expulsion is the ejection of the abdominal air through the apertures of the nose by a special kind of effort." Retention is "restraint of the breath", defined as keeping the expelled breath outside and "not allowing it to enter suddenly" (Woods, p. 72). The result: "by these two, the expulsion and retention of wind, his body becomes light and his central organ gains the stable state" (p. 72).

### Where the principle goes further

The sutra names expulsion and retention. It does not mention extending the exhalation or counting breaths. Those are the principle's practical gloss, and a reasonable one, since a measured breath is easy to follow. Note too that the text describes a "special kind of effort, described in books of Yoga". It is a trained technique, and a beginner should not treat the line as a casual instruction.

**Plain words** One of Patanjali's listed ways to steady the mind is to work with the breath, by pushing it out and holding it out. The commentary says the body grows light and the mind stable. Counting slow, long exhalations is a gentler modern adaptation, offered in the spirit of the sutra but not found in it.

*For example:* Imagined example: scattered before a meeting, you breathe out slowly for a count of six, five times, then turn to the first agenda item. The count is your own addition.

(Yoga Sutra 1.34 with Vyasa's Bhasya and Vacaspati Misra's gloss, trans. James Haughton Woods, The Yoga-System of Patanjali (1914), p. 72, Internet Archive, opened 2026-10-21.)
