> **Principle 28** — Mattering matters

Two related ideas sit under the word "mattering", and the original page blended them.

### Interpersonal mattering (Rosenberg and McCullough)

Rosenberg and McCullough (1981) introduced the concept to capture the feeling that other people notice you, regard you as important and depend on you. [Flett's 2022 review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/07342829211057640) reports that in four adolescent samples "mattering predicted depression after first controlling for self-esteem". The review adds that later work found mattering predicting depression beyond social support in adults (Taylor and Turner 2001), and that mattering "typically predicts unique variance in key outcomes beyond other predictor variables". The idea of being missed ("noted absence") was added later by Rosenberg in 1985, according to a [2026 systematic review of adolescent studies](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10826-026-03372-4).

### Existential mattering (George and Park)

George and Park (2016) use the word for something broader, one of three parts of meaning in life, in which "their existence is of significance, importance, and value to the world" (quoted in [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10615806.2021.1994556), citing p. 206). This is about the world at large, not about particular people.

### How strong is the evidence?

The adolescent systematic review found mostly weak-to-moderate links, with depression correlations from -0.02 to -0.42. It notes that "methodological limitations restrict the strength of these conclusions" and that causality needs longitudinal and experimental designs. Read "predicts" as an association.

**Plain words** Mattering is the feeling that particular people notice you, value you and rely on you. It adds something beyond self-esteem and support in several studies, though the evidence is mostly correlational. George and Park use the same word for feeling that your life counts in the wider world.

*For example:* This is an imagined case. A neighbour asks you to the street meeting because they want your view. Their asking, more than the meeting, is what tells you that you count.
