> **Principle 301** — Practise hau

Hau has more than one meaning in the opened sources. [Te Aka Māori Dictionary](https://maoridictionary.co.nz/word/38822) has one entry for "vital essence, vitality - of a person, place or object", and another for "wind, breeze, breath" ([second entry](https://maoridictionary.co.nz/word/924)). The older Williams dictionary entry gives "vitality of man, vital essence of land, etc." ([online dictionary](https://www.dictionary.maori.nz/hau)).

Hau is also known from Marcel Mauss's The Gift, which draws on an account by Tamati Ranaipiri given to Elsdon Best. In the translation Mauss used, it reads: "Hau is not the wind. Not at all." Mauss reads hau as the spirit of a gift that obliges a return ([Mauss, The Gift, excerpt](https://web.mit.edu/allanmc/www/mauss1.pdf)). That reading is disputed. Raymond Firth objected that Mauss turned the hau of the object into the hau of the giver ([Hau journal](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdf/10.14318/hau6.2.017)), and a later article that went back to the Māori-language text finds Best "both mistranslated and misinterpreted" Ranapiri ([Journal of the Polynesian Society](https://thepolynesiansociety.org/jps/index.php/JPS/article/view/374)). That last piece is drawn from its abstract only.

The seed's instructions, "do not take the hau of others; do not let your own hau be depleted", are not stated in the opened sources, so the page treats them as a modern application and not as a tikanga rule. No opened source frames hau as something a person can run short of.

For the psychology parallel, Ryan and Frederick (1997) studied subjective vitality, "a positive feeling of aliveness and energy". They report associations with psychological well-being and physical symptoms, and lower vitality in people with chronic pain than in matched controls ([abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9327588/)). That is a measured personal feeling. Hau, in the sources above, is broader, and applies to places and objects too.

**Plain words** Hau is the vital essence of a person, place or object, and also the word for wind and breath. Its use in gift exchange is a famous but contested story. Subjective vitality is a related Western idea about personal energy, not a translation.

*For example:* This is an imagined case. A person notices that some friendships leave them feeling more alive and some obligations leave them flat, and plans their week with that in mind.
