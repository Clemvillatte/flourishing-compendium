> **Principle 61** — Take obstacles as information, not verdicts

The Yoga Sutras treat obstacles as a fixed part of the path. Sutra 1.30 lists nine, and the very next sutras name their companions and a response:

> Pain and despondency and unsteadiness of the body and inspiration and expiration are the accompaniments of the distractions. To check them [let there be] practice upon a single entity.

(Yoga Sutras 1.31-1.32, trans. James Haughton Woods, *The Yoga-System of Patanjali*, Harvard Oriental Series 17, 1914, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/yogasystemofpata00wooduoft).)

After that the text goes on with a run of "or" options: friendliness towards happiness, compassion towards pain, and joy towards merit (1.33), the breath (1.34), and objects such as an "undistressed and luminous" sense-activity (1.36). So the structure is as the principle says: obstacles are expected, and a layer of supports follows.

The sutras do not speak of obstacles as information or feedback, and they say nothing about what obstacles mean for one's worth. That reading, and the suggestion of logging time, trigger and type for a monthly review, are this page's own. The text does list the accompaniments of obstacles (pain, despondency, unsteady body, irregular breath), which could serve as a checklist for what to log.

**Plain words**

The sutras assume that practice runs into trouble, and they give an ordered set of things to try. A plateau is therefore part of the path. Treating it as data to review is a modern way to use that.

**For example**

A man keeps a short note for a month: day, time, what he was doing before sitting, what went wrong. In the fourth week he sees that every bad sit followed a late-night email, and he moves the sitting to before breakfast.
