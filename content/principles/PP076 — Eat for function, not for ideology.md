> **Principle 76** — Eat for function, not for ideology

The strongest direct evidence concerns dietary patterns. A meta-analysis of 16 randomised trials (45,826 participants) found that dietary interventions "significantly reduced depressive symptoms (g = 0.162)", although "the majority" of the samples had nonclinical depression ([Firth et al. 2019, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30720698/)). That is a small effect.

The SMILES trial was a 12-week single-blind trial in 67 adults with moderate to severe depression, comparing dietitian-led dietary support with social support. Remission was reached by 32.3% of the diet group and 8.0% of controls, and the effect size was d = -1.16 ([Jacka et al. 2017, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28137247/)). The authors later called it "an important, albeit preliminary, first step", and acknowledged expectation bias as a key limitation ([Jacka et al. reply, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30591059/)). A secondary summary notes the diet group also received a fish oil supplement ([Think Through Nutrition](https://thinkthroughnutrition.org/smiles-trial)).

(Sarris et al., 2015; Lopresti et al., 2013.)

### Scope

I did not open Sarris et al. 2015 or Lopresti et al. 2013, which the seed cites, so I cannot confirm the claim that patterns beat single nutrients or supplements. These trials tested diet as a treatment for depression or low mood, not as a way to feel better in general, and none addressed ideology.

**Plain words**
Trials suggest that improving the overall pattern of what you eat can modestly lift low mood. The strongest result comes from one small study, so treat it as promising, not settled.

**For example**
For four weeks, add one plant-based meal a day and note your mood each evening, as in the tracking principle.
