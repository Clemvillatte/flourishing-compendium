> **Principle 29** — Walk calmly; do not run in the streets

In Chapter 22 of Book VI, a father counsels his son on conduct. In the Dibble and Anderson translation, as excerpted by [Children and Youth in History](https://cyh.rrchnm.org/primary-sources/442.html), he says: "peacefully, quietly, tranquilly, deliberately you are to go, to take the road, to travel. Do not throw your feet much, nor raise your feet high, nor go jumping, lest it be said of you... fool, shameless." He also warns: "Neither are you to travel very slowly, nor to drag your feet." Elsewhere in the same speech, "when you are summoned" you are "to arise quickly", and a messenger is "to run, to be swift". So the counsel is for steadiness and fit to the occasion. It does not ask for a slow pace, and I did not find the words "do not run in the streets" in the excerpt.

The seed's "80 percent pace" is the compendium's idea, and the research points the other way. [Michalak et al. 2009](https://www.biomotionlab.ca/Abstracts/MichalakTrojepap09.pdf) found that sad and depressed walkers show reduced walking speed, arm swing and head movement, and a slumped posture. In [an everyday-life study](https://d-nb.info/1232059730/34), higher walking speed and more vertical movement predicted more positive mood afterwards.

(Florentine Codex, Book VI, the counsel on public bearing.)

### Scope

I did not open Book VI directly. The mood studies are small, and the seed's breathing-rate claim was not tested.

**Plain words**
The elders ask for a calm, deliberate walk without hopping or dragging, and speed when speed is needed. Research suggests an upright, lively gait lifts mood more than a slow one.

**For example**
For one week, walk the same route with your head up and an even stride, neither dragging nor rushing. Rate your mood from 1 to 10 before and after each walk.
