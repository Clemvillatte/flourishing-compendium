> **Principle 12** — Recognise that affect is socially and culturally shaped; do not universalise it

Jeanne Tsai separates actual affect, "the affective states that people actually feel", from ideal affect, "the affective states that people ideally want to feel". Her 2007 review compares American and East Asian cultures, "which differ in their valuation of high-arousal positive affective states (e.g., excitement, enthusiasm) and low-arousal positive affective states (e.g., calm, peacefulness)" ([Tsai, 2007](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1745-6916.2007.00043.x)). The paper's framework treats both "cultural and temperamental factors" as shaping affect and behaviour.

### Expression and well-being

How much holding back an emotion costs may also differ by group. One study of Chinese Americans and Mexican Americans found that suppression of positive emotions "was related to lower hedonic and eudaimonic well-being among Mexican Americans but not among Chinese Americans" ([Zamboanga et al., 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8460117/)). A later study across two culturally distinct regions found habitual suppression of positive emotions "consistently" predicted lower well-being in both ([Yu, Haase and Chang, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10751279/)). So the evidence on cultural differences in the costs of suppression is mixed.

### Scope

These sources support the claim about ideal affect and about varied results on suppression. They do not cover how emotion labels differ across languages, or which feelings count as acceptable in a given culture. Those parts of the principle are the compendium's.

**Plain words** People in different cultures tend to prize different feelings, such as excitement or calm. Excitement can read as confidence in one setting and as instability in another. Be cautious about treating your own affect norms as universal.

*For example:* Imagine a team member whose enthusiasm comes across as confident in one workplace and unserious in another. The manager who knows this reads the person's affect in its local setting before judging it.

## Psychology parallels

- **Ideal affect differs across cultures**, European Americans idealise high-arousal positive affect; East Asians idealise low-arousal positive affect (Tsai, 2007)
- **Emotion norms vary; the costs of suppression are not uniform across groups** (Zamboanga et al., 2015; Soto et al., 2011)

## Practice

- When you judge someone's (or your own) emotional expression as "too much" or "too little," check whether you are applying your own culture's affective ideal.
