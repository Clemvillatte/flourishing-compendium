> **Principle 12** — Recognise that affect is socially and culturally shaped; do not universalise it

The emotions you feel, the labels you use, the expressions you show, and which feelings you consider "acceptable" are shaped by culture. High-arousal positive affect (excitement, enthusiasm) is idealised in some cultures; low-arousal positive affect (calm, serenity) in others. Treating your own culture's affective norms as universal is a common source of misjudgement.

**Plain words** The emotions you feel, the labels you use, the expressions you show, and which feelings are "acceptable" are shaped by culture. Do not universalise your affect rules onto others. Excitement reads as competence in one room and instability in another.

*For example:* The team member whose enthusiasm (high arousal, positive) plays as confident in one culture and unserious in another — and the manager who learns to read affect through the local lens before grading it. The feeling was fine; the rubric was regional.
## Psychology parallels

- **Ideal affect differs across cultures**, European Americans idealise high-arousal positive affect; East Asians idealise low-arousal positive affect (Tsai, 2007)
- **Emotion norms vary; suppression is not uniformly maladaptive across cultures**, expressive suppression is associated with worse outcomes in Western samples but not necessarily in East Asian samples (Butler & Gross, 2009; Soto et al., 2011)

## Practice

- When you judge someone's (or your own) emotional expression as "too much" or "too little," check whether you are applying your own culture's affective ideal.
