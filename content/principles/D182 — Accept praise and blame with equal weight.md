> **Principle 182** — Accept praise and blame with equal weight

Daodejing 13 opens with the pairing this principle is named from:

> "Favour and disgrace would seem equally to be feared; honour and great calamity, to be regarded as personal conditions (of the same kind)." (DDJ 13, Legge trans., [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

The chapter explains the fear of favour:

> "The getting that (favour) leads to the apprehension (of losing it), and the losing it leads to the fear of (still greater calamity):--this is what is meant by saying that favour and disgrace would seem equally to be feared." (DDJ 13)

Zhuangzi 12 carries the same weight on the other side, in Zi-gong's description of the man complete in spirit (the passage is his report; Confucius's reply that follows criticises the man for regulating what is internal without what is external):

> "Though all the world should praise them, they would (only) get what they think should be loftily disregarded; and though all the world should blame them, they would but lose (what they think) fortuitous and not to be received - the world's blame and praise can do them neither benefit nor injury." (Zhuangzi 12, Legge trans., [ETH Zurich PDF](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf))

The compendium brings the two passages together: DDJ 13 treats favour and disgrace as a single category because each leaves you exposed to what comes next, and Zi-gong's description in Zhuangzi 12 gives the person for whom both have lost their purchase.

(DDJ 13; Zhuangzi 12.)

**Plain words** DDJ 13's reasoning is that getting favour produces the apprehension of losing it, so the praised and the blamed position share a structure: both wait on someone else's next move. Zhuangzi 12 sets the other pole, a person whom the world's blame and praise can neither benefit nor injure. A practical reading: when a verdict arrives, notice whether you are treating it as information about the work or as news about yourself. The chapters do not promise indifference; they describe where each verdict is allowed to land.

*For example:* Imagine finishing a piece of work that is reviewed both admiringly and harshly by different readers. You could sort each review into what it says about the work and what it says about the reviewer, and take only the first kind forward.
