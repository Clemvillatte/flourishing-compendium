> **Principle 168** — Put yourself last: and be preserved

Daodejing 7 grounds the paradox in heaven and earth, then states it for the sage:

> "The reason why heaven and earth are able to endure and continue thus long is because they do not live of, or for, themselves. This is how they are able to continue and endure. Therefore the sage puts his own person last, and yet it is found in the foremost place; he treats his person as if it were foreign to him, and yet that person is preserved. Is it not because he has no personal and private ends, that therefore such ends are realised?" (DDJ 7, Legge trans., [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

Chapter 13 adds the stakes, the body itself as what makes calamity possible:

> "What makes me liable to great calamity is my having the body (which I call myself); if I had not the body, what great calamity could come to me?" (DDJ 13)

(DDJ 7, 13.)

**Plain words** The chapter's question carries its own reading: is it not because the sage has no personal and private ends that such ends are realised? The paradox is stated, and the chapter does not argue us out of it. A practical reading: treating your own position as the thing being guarded keeps that position as the load-bearing concern of everything you do; the alternative the chapter points to is absorption in something beyond that position. What it does not supply is a guarantee of outcomes, only its own paradox to test.

*For example:* Imagine performing a piece you care about. You could practise attending to the material rather than to how you are coming across, and notice what changes in the performance.
