> **Principle 57** — Recognise the peak-end rule in memory: you remember peaks and endings

Your memory of an experience is disproportionately shaped by its most intense moment (peak) and its final moment (end), not by its average. This affects how you remember events, relationships, and experiences.

**Plain words** Your memory of an experience is shaped by its most intense moment and its ending — not its average. The average of the holiday doesn't exist in memory; the peak and the last day do. Design peaks and endings on purpose.

*For example:* The restaurant meal where everything was fine but the ending was the botched bill — remembered as "a bad night" — versus the one with an average meal and a perfect dessert. The peak-end rule is why endings get outsized budgets in every well-run service.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Peak-end rule|Peak-end rule]]**, retrospective evaluation is shaped by peak and end (Kahneman et al., 1993; Redelmeier & Kahneman, 1996)
- **[[Duration neglect|Duration neglect]]**, the length of an experience has little effect on its remembered quality (Kahneman et al., 1993)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[The whole in the part|The whole in the part]]** (Whitehead, Process and Reality)
- **[[Ending as completion|Ending as completion]]** (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics I)

## Practice

- When designing experiences, invest in the peak and the ending. When evaluating a past experience, remember that your memory is biased toward the peak and end, not the average.
