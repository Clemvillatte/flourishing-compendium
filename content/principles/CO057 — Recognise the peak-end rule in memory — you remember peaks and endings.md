> **Principle 57** — Recognise the peak-end rule in memory: you remember peaks and endings

Redelmeier and Kahneman recorded pain minute by minute in patients having a colonoscopy (n = 154) or lithotripsy (n = 133), then asked them afterwards how bad the whole procedure had been. Their judgments of total pain "were strongly correlated with the peak intensity of pain" and "with the intensity of pain recorded during the last 3 min of the procedure" ([Redelmeier & Kahneman, 1996, Pain 66(1), abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8857625/)). Length hardly registered: "lengthy procedures were not remembered as particularly aversive."

The authors suggest that "patients' memories of painful medical procedures largely reflect the intensity of pain at the worst part and at the final part of the experience." The study is about painful procedures. Applying the same rule to holidays, meals or relationships is the compendium's extension and has not been checked here.

**Plain words** Memory does not keep an average of how an experience felt. It keeps the worst stretch and the last few minutes and builds its verdict from those, so a long experience and a short one can be remembered as equally bad. If you plan something for other people, the ending is worth designing. If you are judging your own past, remember that the verdict rests on two moments.

*For example:* In the colonoscopy group, the patients' own total-pain ratings tracked the worst moment and the final three minutes, and the length of the procedure made little difference. When you rate a trip or a job as awful, ask which moment was the peak and what happened at the end.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Peak-end rule|Peak-end rule]]**, retrospective evaluation is shaped by peak and end (Kahneman et al., 1993; Redelmeier & Kahneman, 1996)
- **[[Duration neglect|Duration neglect]]**, the length of an experience has little effect on its remembered quality (Kahneman et al., 1993)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[The whole in the part|The whole in the part]]** (Whitehead, Process and Reality)
- **[[Ending as completion|Ending as completion]]** (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics I)

## Practice

- When designing experiences, invest in the peak and the ending. When evaluating a past experience, remember that your memory is biased toward the peak and end, not the average.
