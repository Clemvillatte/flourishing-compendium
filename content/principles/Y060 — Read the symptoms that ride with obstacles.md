> **Principle 60** — Read the symptoms that ride with obstacles

Sutra 1.30 names nine obstacles to the practice, and 1.31 says what goes with them:

> Pain and despondency and unsteadiness of the body and inspiration and expiration are the accompaniments of the distractions.

(Yoga Sutras 1.31, trans. James Haughton Woods, *The Yoga-System of Patanjali*, Harvard Oriental Series 17, 1914, p. 65, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/yogasystemofpata00wooduoft).)

Vyasa's commentary defines despondency as "agitation of mind due to an impediment [to the fulfilment] of a desire" and unsteadiness of the body as "that which makes it unsteady [and] makes it tremble". The breathing meant is involuntary: it is breath "without volitional action", unlike the controlled breathing of practice. He then says: "These occur in one whose mind-stuff is distracted. These do not occur in one whose mind-stuff is concentrated."

So the text gives the symptoms as marks of a distracted mind, and a steady mind lacks them. That fits a practical rule: a tight chest or ragged breath at the start of a sitting tells you the mind is distracted today. The text does not say the body knows first, and it does not offer the sitting as a test of success. Those claims, and the idea that the signs deserve a flag, are this page's reading.

**Plain words**

When you sit down and your breath is short, your body is shaky, or a low mood has come over you, the old text counts that as a sign of a distracted mind. That does not mean the practice has failed. It tells you what kind of day it is. Take it as information, adjust, and carry on.

**For example**

Someone keeps waking at 4 a.m. with shallow breathing while telling themselves nothing is wrong. Reading that as the sutra's distraction signs, they check what has been going on in their week and find a conflict they had been ignoring.
