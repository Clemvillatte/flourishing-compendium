> **Principle 31** — Learn to increase friction for undesired behaviours: make it hard


The strongest test of "make it harder" comes from food, alcohol and tobacco. A Cochrane review of randomised trials (Hollands et al. 2019, CD012573) found that "exposure to food placed farther away resulted in a moderate reduction in its consumption: SMD -0.60 (95% CI -0.84 to -0.36)" across 15 comparisons from 12 studies (n = 1098). The certainty was "low". Only one small study (n = 41) looked at what people chose, and its certainty was "very low" ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31482606/)). The review's authors call for tests "in real-world settings", with other products, and "over sustained time periods" (same abstract). The full text also says that the effect was larger in studies at high risk of bias ([discussion](https://www.cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD012573.pub3/full/de)).

Wood and Neal's model of habit explains why changing the setting can work: habits "emerge from the gradual learning of associations between responses and the features of performance contexts", and the model has implications for "the self-regulation of habit cuing" ([Psychological Review 114(4):843-863, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17907866/)). Friction changes the cue or the path, and the habit has less to run on.

A common practice tip, keeping the phone in another room, rests partly on the "brain drain" claim that a phone's mere presence reduces cognitive capacity. That result did not replicate in an attempt that used the same tasks ([FLoRA replication atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1086/691462/)). Moving the phone can still make scrolling harder. The cognitive-capacity claim should not be cited as the reason.

**Plain words** Make the thing you want less of a little harder to reach. Put it further away, add a step, remove the shortcut. The evidence is best for food, with modest certainty, and it does not show that friction fixes every habit.

*For example:* This is an imagined case. A reader who scrolls late at night charges her phone in the kitchen. The phone is still hers, and each use costs a walk.

## Psychology parallels

- **Friction and undesired behaviour**, placing food farther away reduced its consumption in a Cochrane meta-analysis of 12 studies (Hollands et al., 2019; low certainty); Thaler & Sunstein (2008) is unopened
- **Habit disruption**, habits are learned context-response associations, so changing cues bears on habit change (Wood & Neal, 2007); Gardner (2015) is unopened

## Practice

- Increase friction for undesired behaviours: remove junk food from the house, log out of social media, delete the app, put the phone in another room. Make the undesired behaviour the path of most resistance.
