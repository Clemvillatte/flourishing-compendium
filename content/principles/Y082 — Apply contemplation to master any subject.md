> **Principle 82** — Apply contemplation to master any subject

Woods translates samyama as "constraint", and sutra 3.4 defines it: "The three in one are constraint" (p. 205). The commentary says: "When having a single object the three means are called constraint", where the three are fixed attention, contemplation and concentration (p. 205). Sutra 3.5 gives the result: "As a result of mastering this constraint, there follows the shining forth of insight" (p. 206).

### Progress by stages

Sutra 3.6 says "Its application is by stages". The commentary warns that by "overleaping the next stage without having first mastered the lower stage, [the yogin] does not gain constraint in the highest stages" (p. 206). The principle's advice to work on one area at a time has support here, though the text speaks of stages of meditation, not areas of life.

### The catalogue of applications

From sutra 3.16, the commentary says, "the field-of-operation for the constraint and the supernormal powers indicative of the mastery over objects will be described" (p. 233). The first is "As a result of constraint upon the three mutations there follows the knowledge of the past and of the future" (3.16, p. 232). The later sutras in the series name other objects, which are not read in full here, so the page does not claim to list them.

### What is not in the text

The sutras present these as supernormal powers (vibhuti) that follow from mastering constraint, which is a larger claim than learning faster. The principle's version, that sustained, absorbed attention to a subject yields knowledge that scattered study cannot, and its rule of one domain per quarter, are modern adaptations of the idea.

**Plain words** The text names a bundle: fix your attention, turn the thing over in thought, and let the mind rest absorbed in it, all on one object. That bundle, kept up, is said to end in the object showing itself plainly. The stages matter, and skipping ahead is said to gain nothing at the top. What a modern reader can take is the study habit: one text, one season, worked through all three gears. The supernormal powers the catalogue promises are the sutras' claim, not this page's.

*For example:* you give one subject, say a single philosophical text, a full season of sustained reading and reflection. This is a modern use of the idea, not a power the sutras describe.

(Yoga Sutras 3.4-3.6 and 3.16 with Vyasa's Bhasya and Vacaspati Misra's gloss, trans. James Haughton Woods, The Yoga-System of Patanjali (1914), pp. 205-206, 232-233, Internet Archive. Sutras 3.17-3.29 not read.)
