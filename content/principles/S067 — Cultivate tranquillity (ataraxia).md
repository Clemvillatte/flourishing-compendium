> **Principle 189** — Cultivate tranquillity (ataraxia)

Seneca defines the state he is after. It is a mind that keeps a steady course, likes its own company and is not thrown by what comes.

> "What we are seeking, then, is how the mind may always pursue a steady, unruffled course, may be pleased with itself, and look with pleasure upon its surroundings, and experience no interruption of this joy, but abide in a peaceful condition without being ever either elated or depressed: this will be 'peace of mind.'" (Seneca, De Tranquillitate Animi 2, Stewart translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/64576/64576-0.txt).)

Marcus Aurelius pins the word down further: "By tranquillity I understand a decent orderly disposition and carriage, free from all confusion and tumultuousness." (Meditations IV.3, Casaubon translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2680/pg2680.txt).) He means order, which is different from numbness.

### How it arrives

Epictetus gives the sequence. Asked what virtue produces, he answers in one line: "What is the product of virtue? Tranquillity." (Discourses I.4, Long translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/10661/pg10661.txt).) Calm follows the practice of [[The dichotomy of control|the dichotomy of control]], which sorts what is yours to decide from what is not. Seneca's own text is a manual for that work, written for a friend who knows the right principles and still wavers.

**Plain words** Being calm in this sense means your mood stops swinging with every piece of news. You can still feel things. The calm comes from having decided in advance what you will and will not fight over, so a bad day has less to grab. Chasing the calm directly usually fails, because the chase is itself a worry.

*For example:* A nurse who stays steady in an emergency isn't cold. She's calm enough to help.
