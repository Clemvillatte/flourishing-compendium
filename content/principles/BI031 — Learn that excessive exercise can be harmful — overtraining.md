> **Principle 31** — Learn that excessive exercise can be harmful: overtraining

The joint consensus statement of the European College of Sport Science and the American College of Sports Medicine says successful training must "avoid the combination of excessive overload plus inadequate recovery". Short-term performance dips ("functional overreaching") "will eventually lead to an improvement in performance after recovery", whereas when "athletes do not sufficiently respect the balance between training and recovery, nonfunctional overreaching (NFOR) can occur", and the more serious overtraining syndrome shows "prolonged maladaptation". The statement adds that there is "no scientific evidence to either confirm or refute" the idea that overtraining syndrome's fatigue, performance decline and mood disturbances are more severe than those of NFOR (Meeusen et al. 2013, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23247672/)).

For ordinary exercisers, a cross-sectional study of 1,237,194 US adults found that people who exercised had 1.49 fewer days of poor mental health a month than matched non-exercisers, with the largest associations at durations of 45 minutes and three to five sessions a week. It concluded that "more exercise was not always better" (Chekroud et al. 2018, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30099000/)). A cross-sectional design cannot show that more exercise caused the drop.

**Plain words**
Hard training works when it is followed by enough recovery, and trouble comes from piling load on top of load without it. In a very large US survey, moderate amounts of exercise went with the best self-reported mental health, and more was not always better. The U-shaped curve in the title is a reasonable summary and not a measured law, and the evidence on immune suppression did not appear in the sources checked here.

*For example:* After a hard week you swap one session for an easy walk and a long sleep, and you note your mood and energy over the next three days. This is an imagined adjustment that applies the "overload plus recovery" principle, and the consensus statement does not offer a ready formula for how much rest any one person needs.

## Psychology parallels

- **Overreaching and overtraining**, training needs overload followed by adequate recovery; excessive overload with inadequate recovery can lead to nonfunctional overreaching and overtraining syndrome (Meeusen et al., 2013)
- **Exercise volume and mental health**, in a cross-sectional survey of 1.2 million adults, the largest associations were at about 45 minutes and three to five times a week, and more was not always better (Chekroud et al., 2018)

## Practice

- Do not over-exercise: moderate dose is optimal. If you notice fatigue, injury, mood decline, or obsession with exercise, this may be overtraining. Balance exercise with rest.
