> **Principle 44** — Treat the WEIRD critique as a standing corrective

The critique comes from a 2010 review by Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine and Ara Norenzayan, who questioned the broad claims about human psychology that behavioral scientists routinely publish from narrow Western samples (Henrich et al. 2010, author manuscript, long abstract). Their comparison of experimental results across populations found substantial variation, including in visual perception and in experimental measures of fairness.

> The findings suggest that members of Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic societies, including young children, are among the least representative populations one could find for generalizing about humans. (Henrich et al. 2010, [author manuscript, long abstract](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~ara/Manuscripts/Weird_People_BBS_Henrichetal.pdf))

The authors' own summary keeps the corrective proportionate: generalizing from industrialized populations to humans broadly is, on their review of the comparative evidence, problematic without supportive empirical evidence (§2.6). The critique does not invalidate psychology; it makes cultural generality an empirical question rather than an assumption.

**Plain words**
Suppose a study tests students at one university. Before using its result to advise someone of a different age or background, look for research involving comparable people. Finding a pattern among those students is a reason to investigate further; it does not settle how everyone behaves.

*For example:* In the Dictator Game, one participant divides money and the recipient cannot reject the allocation. In the Phase II comparison across 15 societies (Figure 3A), the U.S. adult, non-student sample made the highest mean offer, nearly twice the means for the Hadza and the Tsimane (§2.2). The comparison cautions against treating one sample's pattern as a universal standard.
