> **Principle 8** — Treat good and evil as relations, not properties

In the Preface to Part IV, Spinoza writes that the terms good and bad indicate no positive quality in things regarded in themselves, but are merely modes of thinking formed from comparing things one with another, so that one and the same thing can be at the same time good, bad, and indifferent: music is good for him that is melancholy, bad for him that mourns, and neither for him that is deaf. ([Ethics IV, Preface, Elwes tr.](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800)) His working definitions follow the same pattern: good is what we certainly know to be a means of approaching more nearly the type of human nature we have set before ourselves, and bad what hinders that approach. ([Ethics IV, Preface, Elwes tr.](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800))

> "As for the terms good and bad, they indicate no positive quality in things regarded in themselves, but are merely modes of thinking, or notions which we form from the comparison of things one with another. Thus one and the same thing can be at the same time good, bad, and indifferent. For instance, music is good for him that is melancholy, bad for him that mourns; for him that is deaf, it is neither good nor bad." (Ethics IV, Preface, Elwes tr., [Gutenberg #3800](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800))

**Plain words** When you call something bad, the sentence has a missing end: bad for what, for whom, over what timescale. The label lives in the comparison, not in the thing. This is not relativism as Spinoza sets it up, because the definitions fix a standard, the type of human nature we set before ourselves, even while the applications differ from person to person.

**For example (imagined):** A 5 a.m. routine transforms one person's semester and wrecks another's health. The routine was never the good; the fit was. The best-diet, best-method, best-schedule arguments quiet down once the question becomes: best for which person, doing what?
