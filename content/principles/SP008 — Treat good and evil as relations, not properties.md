> **Principle 8** — Treat good and evil as relations, not properties

Spinoza refuses to place good and evil in things themselves: by good he will mean what is useful to our power of living, and by evil what hinders it, and the same thing can be good to one person, bad to another, and indifferent to a third. This is not relativism, because the standard is fixed (power of living) even though the applications vary. Practically, when you call something bad, complete the sentence: bad for what, for whom, over what timescale. Most moral vertigo dissolves when the missing end of the sentence is supplied.

(Ethics IV, Preface.)

**Plain words** Good and evil aren't in things: by good Spinoza means what's useful to your power of living, by evil what hinders it — and the same thing can be good for one, bad for another, indifferent to a third. Relations, not properties. Stop arguing about the thing; ask for whom.

*For example:* The 5 a.m. routine that transforms one person and hospitalises another — the routine was never the good; the fit was. The endless "what's the best diet/method/schedule" arguments dissolve once the question becomes "best for which machine?"
