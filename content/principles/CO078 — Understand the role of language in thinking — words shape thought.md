> **Principle 78** — Understand the role of language in thinking: words shape thought

The studies I opened support a modest version of this principle: language shapes habits of thought without fixing them. Fuhrman and colleagues compared English and Mandarin speakers on how they arrange time in space. Mandarin uses vertical metaphors for time more often than English does, and the results fit: "only Mandarin speakers showed a vertical top-to-bottom pattern for time", even "in a non-linguistic task". Bilinguals "were more likely to arrange time vertically when they were tested in Mandarin than when they were tested in English" (Fuhrman et al., 2011, abstract, [Cognitive Science 35](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21884222/)).

For feelings, two lines of work bear on the principle. In an imaging study, Lieberman and colleagues found that "affect labeling, relative to other forms of encoding, diminished the response of the amygdala and other limbic regions to negative emotional images" (Lieberman et al., 2007, abstract, [Psychological Science 18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576282/)). A second line concerns emotional granularity, the ability to "create instances of emotion that are precise and context-specific", which "is associated with positive mental health outcomes" (Hoemann et al., 2021, abstract, [Frontiers in Psychology 12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34393942/)). That association is correlational. In the same study, granularity rose with the number of experience-sampling prompts people answered, which points toward practice without settling cause and effect.

**Plain words**
Words do not decide what you can feel or think, but they sharpen what you notice and give you something to work with. If you can say "drained" instead of "stressed", or "wistful" instead of "fine", you have more to go on. Learn the finer words and use them.

**For example**
Someone who says "stressed" about everything tries one precise word each evening for a week, such as "restless", "drained" or "frazzled". The aim is a clearer sense of what the day actually did to them.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Linguistic relativity|Linguistic relativity (Sapir-Whorf, weak version)]]**, language influences (but does not determine) thought (Boroditsky, 2001; Lupyan, 2012)
- **Emotion vocabulary shapes emotional experience**, people with richer emotion vocabularies regulate emotions better (Kashdan et al., 2015; Barrett, 2017)
- **[[Cognitive defusion|Cognitive defusion via language]]**, changing how you relate to words changes their impact (Hayes et al., 2011)

## Philosophy parallels

- **Language as the house of being** (Heidegger, Letter on Humanism)
- **The limits of my language are the limits of my world** (Wittgenstein, Tractatus)
- **In the beginning was the Word** (John, Gospel)

## Practice

- Learn new words for your experience. When you notice a vague feeling, try to name it precisely. The word creates the handle; the handle creates the leverage.
