> **Principle 133** — Don't dominate: the world can't be controlled

Laozi's argument runs through a kingdom, and starts with a prediction:

> "The kingdom is a spirit-like thing, and cannot be got by active doing. He who would so win it destroys it; he who would hold it in his grasp loses it." (Dao De Jing 29, [Legge's translation](https://www.sacred-texts.com/tao/taote.htm))

The kingdom is the example; Laozi's warning extends past kingdoms. Applied to whatever runs on another person's will, a family, a team, a friendship, a class, the grip replaces their own motion with yours, and managing the grip becomes a job in its own right. The chapter closes with: "Hence the sage puts away excessive effort, extravagance, and easy indulgence" ([DDJ 29, Legge](https://www.sacred-texts.com/tao/taote.htm)). A narrower parallel comes from parenting research: "parental autonomy support was linked positively with child well-being, r = 0.30, 95% CI [0.26, 0.33], whereas parental psychological control was positively linked with child ill-being, r = 0.26, 95% CI [0.23, 0.28]" ([Bradshaw et al., American Psychologist 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39052356/)), with both patterns reported across regions and cultures. Influence travels through the other person's own will; the compendium's account is [[Autonomy-supportive leadership]].

**Plain words**
You can hold people or you can move them, and the grip and the motion do not go together. When you push someone into doing what you want, watch what happens to the wanting: it is now yours rather than theirs, and the doing drags. The alternative is to spend your effort where it compounds: explain what you want, make the choice easy to make, and leave the choosing theirs. Giving someone a choice also means accepting that they may refuse.

*For example:* The parent who tries to control their adult child's choices gets compliance at best — the influence they wanted required letting go.
