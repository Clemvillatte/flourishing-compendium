> **Principle 33** — Let truth change its face

In his 1981 internalist account, Hilary Putnam argues that what counts as an object is a question within a theory or description. He allows for more than one true description without claiming that minds simply invent the world ([Reason, Truth and History, preface and pp. 49-50](https://archive.org/download/hilary-putnam-reason-truth-and-history-1981/Hilary%20Putnam%20-%20Reason%2C%20Truth%20and%20History%20%281981%29_djvu.txt)):

> If one must use metaphorical language, then let the metaphor be this: the mind and the world jointly make up the mind and the world.

He also [distinguishes truth from what presently seems justified](https://archive.org/download/hilary-putnam-reason-truth-and-history-1981/Hilary%20Putnam%20-%20Reason%2C%20Truth%20and%20History%20%281981%29_djvu.txt). His example is a flat earth: changing what people rationally accepted does not mean the earth changed shape (p. 55). The title's invitation to reconsider descriptions should not erase that distinction.

**Plain words**
You may disagree with someone because you have answered different questions about the same situation. Finding that difference can save an argument, but it does not settle whether either answer is right. A description still has to answer to experience. Changing your way of looking is permission to examine the evidence again, not permission to call any convenient account true.

For example: One neighbour maps a garden by species, another by what is flowering this month. Compare both maps against the plants. Retain each for the question it answers, and correct a misplaced plant. This imagined exercise applies the distinction between alternative descriptions and factual mistakes; it does not test Putnam's theory.
