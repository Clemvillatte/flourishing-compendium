> **Principle 37** — Use epistemic diversity as a resource

The famous claim for this principle is weaker than it sounds. Hong and Page built a model of problem-solving agents and found that "a team of randomly selected agents outperforms a team comprised of the best-performing agents", because the best performers "necessarily become similar in the space of problem solvers" ([Hong and Page 2004, PNAS](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15534225/)). It is a result about simulated agents on a ring of 2,000 random numbers, and a replication confirmed it only when "the solution landscape is fully random" ([Wallrich, ReScience replication](https://eprints.bbk.ac.uk/id/eprint/51352/1/article%20(17).pdf)).

The result is also disputed. Thompson argued that the paper "contains a theorem that has neither mathematical content nor real-world applications" ([Notices of the AMS](https://www.ams.org/notices/201409/rnoti-p1024.pdf)). A defender replied that her criticisms are "incorrect, misleading, or irrelevant" while granting that her point about randomisation has value ([Diversity, Ability, and Democracy](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/08913811.2017.1288455)). A further modelling paper found that on smoother landscapes "it is groups of the best performing that do better", while a larger pool of heuristics favours diversity again ([Expertise versus Diversity across Epistemic Landscape, PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/archive/GRIWOC)).



### What to take from it

Evidence from real groups is better placed elsewhere: group performance in [Woolley et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20929725/) tracked social sensitivity and balanced turn-taking more than member intelligence. I did not open Page 2007, Phillips et al. 2004 or Reynolds and Lewis 2017, so I make no claim from them.

**Plain words**
Different ways of thinking can help a group find what it would otherwise miss, but the case is conditional, not automatic. It helps when the problem is hard and everyone gets heard.

**For example**
Before finalising a plan, ask the person whose training differs most from yours to name the likeliest way it fails. Write down what they say before replying.

- In team composition, prioritize epistemic diversity (different expertise, backgrounds, cognitive styles) alongside demographic diversity. In knowledge work, actively seek out perspectives that differ from your own (see principle 36).
