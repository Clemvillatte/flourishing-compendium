> **Principle 16** — Cultivate emodiversity: the range and evenness of your emotional life

The study introduces "emodiversity: the variety and relative abundance of the emotions that humans experience" ([Quoidbach et al., 2014](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/quoidbach%20et%20al%202014_9105d828-db78-49eb-b434-23f53cdba042.pdf)). Two cross-sectional studies across more than 37,000 respondents found that "emodiversity is an independent predictor of mental and physical health... over and above mean levels of positive and negative emotion," with results that "remained robust after controlling for gender, age, and the 5 main dimensions of personality" ([Quoidbach et al., 2014](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/quoidbach%20et%20al%202014_9105d828-db78-49eb-b434-23f53cdba042.pdf)). The paper adapted the Shannon biodiversity index "to quantify emodiversity, or the richness (how many specific emotions are experienced) and evenness (the extent to which specific emotions are experienced in the same proportion) in the human emotional ecosystem" ([Quoidbach et al., 2014](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/quoidbach%20et%20al%202014_9105d828-db78-49eb-b434-23f53cdba042.pdf)). The authors' summary: "Emodiversity is a practically important and previously unidentified metric for assessing the health of the human emotional ecosystem" ([Quoidbach et al., 2014](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/quoidbach%20et%20al%202014_9105d828-db78-49eb-b434-23f53cdba042.pdf)). The exercise below inventories the range.

> These results remained robust after controlling for gender, age, and the 5 main dimensions of personality.
>
> Quoidbach, Gruber, Mikolajczak, Kogan, Kotsou and Norton (2014), "Emodiversity and the emotional ecosystem," Journal of Experimental Psychology: General 143(6), 2057-2066, [paper PDF](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/quoidbach%20et%20al%202014_9105d828-db78-49eb-b434-23f53cdba042.pdf).

### Plain words

The reported finding is an association in two cross-sectional studies, rather than a test of whether deliberately increasing emotional variety improves health ([Quoidbach et al., 2014](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/quoidbach%20et%20al%202014_9105d828-db78-49eb-b434-23f53cdba042.pdf)). For reflection, list the emotions you recall from a week without treating the length of the list as a health score.

For example: one week contains interest, pride, nostalgia, mild frustration and contentment; another contains a single note repeated. Listing the distinct emotions of the week is the exercise. (An invented illustration.)
## Psychology parallels

- **Emodiversity predicts lower depression, anxiety, and better well-being**, independently of mean positive and negative affect (Quoidbach et al., 2014)
- **Effect size is comparable to other well-established predictors**, emodiversity explains variance similar to mean affect itself

## Practice

- Expand your emotional vocabulary and seek experiences that elicit different emotions — awe, contentment, determination, tenderness — not just the familiar few.
