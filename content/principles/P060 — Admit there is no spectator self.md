> **Principle 60** — Admit there is no spectator self

You are always a participant, never a pure observer: your looking changes the scene, your interests shape what shows up. Drop the fantasy of the detached judge; own your involvement.

James gives the interest half. In The Principles of Psychology, attention is not a neutral camera: "My experience is what I agree to attend to. Only those items which I notice shape my mind—without selective interest, experience is an utter chaos." What shows up in experience is already shaped by what the creature cares about. He also names the observer's blind spot, the "psychologist's fallacy": "the confusion of his own standpoint with that of the mental fact about which he is making his report". The one reporting stands outside the mental state, and forgets that he stands there. Dewey gives the knowing half: the tradition modeled knowing on vision, where the object "makes a difference to the eye and to the person having an optical apparatus, but none to the thing seen", and so arrived at "a spectator theory of knowledge". The experimental reversal he records is the whole of it.

(James, The Principles of Psychology, 1890; Dewey, The Quest for Certainty, 1929.)

> "From knowing as an outside beholding to knowing as an active participant in the drama of an on-moving world is the historical transition whose record we have been following." (Dewey, The Quest for Certainty, ch. IX, [full text at Internet Archive](https://archive.org/download/questforcertaint032529mbp/questforcertaint032529mbp_djvu.txt))

The compendium's extension: the statement's "your looking changes the scene" goes beyond James's claim that interest shapes what shows up in experience; the scene-changing claim belongs to the observer-effect parallel, not to the two opened texts.

**Plain words**
The anthropologist changes the village by being in it. Before you trust your own summary of a situation, name your stake in it: who benefits, what you want to see, what your presence is doing to the room.

*For example:* The compendium's hypothetical: the manager "quietly observing" the team meeting, while the team performs for the manager. There is no observation deck; there is only the field.
