> **Principle 20** — Accept suffering as a teacher, without seeking it

The Nahua tradition holds that the earth is a place of trial, and the songs return to the theme: the heart is forged on earth, and the gods' gift of life includes its difficulty. The counsel is neither hedonism nor masochism but the middle the tradition keeps finding: difficulty is expected, used, and not worshipped. Practically, when hardship arrives, ask what is being forged, and do not add to it by treating it as an anomaly.

> "Thou wert sent here on earth, a place of weariness, a place of pain, a place of affliction, a place of torment; a place where misery, where torment emerge." — the midwife's address to the newborn, Florentine Codex Book VI ([full text](https://archive.org/stream/volume-11-book-11-earthly-things-florentine-codex-general-history-of-the-things-of-new-spain/Volume%206,%20Book%206%20-%20Rhetoric%20and%20Moral%20Philosophy%20-%20%5BFlorentine%20Codex%20General%20History%20of%20the%20Things%20of%20New%20Spain%5D_djvu.txt))

(The Cantares's earth-as-trial passages; the huehuetlatolli's counsel on endurance.)

**Plain words** The tradition's line is blunt: you were sent to a hard place on purpose. Pain is not a bug in the design, and it is not a punishment you deserved — it is the forge. The counsel cuts both ways: do not chase suffering (no martyr badges), and do not treat it as an anomaly (no "why me" spirals). When it comes, the working question is what it is making of you.

*For example:* Someone who treats their hard year as either a catastrophe or a badge — and finally treats it as a course: expected, brutal, and with a syllabus. They don't miss it when it ends, but they don't waste it either.
