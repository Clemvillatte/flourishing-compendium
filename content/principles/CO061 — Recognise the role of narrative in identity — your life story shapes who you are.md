> **Principle 61** — Recognise the role of narrative in identity: your life story shapes who you are

> Narrative identity is the internalized, evolving story of the self that each person crafts to provide his or her life with a sense of purpose and unity.

(Adler, "Living into the story: agency and coherence in a longitudinal study of narrative identity development and mental health over the course of psychotherapy," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 102(2), 2012, abstract; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21910554/). McAdams and McLean's 2013 definition, quoted second-hand in other papers, is "a person's internalized and evolving life story, integrating the reconstructed past and imagined future to provide life with some degree of unity and purpose" (their original article was not opened).)

Adler's study followed 47 adults who wrote narratives before and after every session of psychotherapy, nearly 600 stories in total. Agency in the stories, though not coherence, increased over time, and increases in agency were related to better mental health. Lagged models showed that "changes in the theme of agency occurred prior to the associated improvements in mental health." That timing suggests the story may be doing some of the work, though the study is observational and small.

A review of the field (Adler, Lodi-Smith, Philippe and Houle, Personality and Social Psychology Review 20(2), 2016; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25968138/)) reports "the incremental validity of narrative identity as a cross-sectional indicator and prospective predictor of well-being," compared with other individual difference and situational variables. It describes the case as "strongest for motivational, affective, and integrative meaning themes," alongside dispositional personality traits.

The entry's specific "redemption" and "contamination" claims (McAdams et al., 2001) were not opened. Those parallels, the Ricoeur, MacIntyre and White and Epston links, and the compendium's advice that "the arc can" change are not tested in these sources.

**Plain words** People carry an inner story about their lives, and the way it is told, especially how much it stresses agency, goes along with how well they are doing. In one therapy study, stories became more agentic before mental health improved. That shows association and timing, not proof that changing the story causes the change.

*For example (imagined):* After a job loss, someone tells it first as something that happened to them. Months later, the same facts appear in a version that includes what they did next.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Narrative identity|Narrative identity]]**, life stories are a core component of identity and predict well-being (McAdams, 2001; McAdams & McLean, 2013)
- **Redemptive narratives predict well-being**, life stories with themes of growth and redemption are associated with higher well-being (McAdams et al., 2001; Adler, 2012)
- **Contamination sequences (good turns bad) predict lower well-being**, narratives where good things turn bad are associated with depression (McAdams et al., 2001)

## Practice

- Notice the narrative arc of your life story. Is it a story of growth, redemption, contamination, or stagnation? The facts may not change, but the arc can. Consider how you tell the story of difficult experiences.
