> **Principle 49** — Cultivate mindfulness of thoughts: observing thinking without being consumed by it

Two lines of research sit behind this principle. The first is why suppression is the wrong tool. In Wegner's white-bear experiment, people told not to think of a bear were "unable to suppress the thought as instructed," and afterwards they thought about it more than people who had been asked to think about it from the start, which suggests that suppression may produce "the very obsession or preoccupation that it is directed against" ([Wegner et al., 1987, J Pers Soc Psychol 53(1), abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3612492/)). A later meta-analysis found only "a small to moderate rebound effect" that varied with the target thought and how it was measured ([Abramowitz et al., 2001, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11434226/)), so the effect is real but not uniform.

The second is what to do instead. [Fresco et al. (2007)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17697849/) define decentering as "the ability to observe one's thoughts and feelings as temporary, objective events in the mind, as opposed to reflections of the self that are necessarily true," and report that it related negatively to depression symptoms. Teasdale and colleagues describe a "cognitive set in which negative thoughts/feelings are experienced as mental events, rather than as the self." In residually depressed patients, its accessibility predicted relapse, and where mindfulness-based cognitive therapy reduced relapse in recovered patients, it also raised it. They suggest the therapies "may reduce relapse by changing relationships to negative thoughts rather than by changing belief in thought content" ([Teasdale et al., 2002, J Consult Clin Psychol 70(2), abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11952186/)).

**Plain words** Fighting a thought tends to keep it close. What the research supports is a change of relationship: you see the thought as an event passing through the mind, not as a fact about you. You do not have to believe it, argue with it or push it out.

*For example:* Mid-meditation, a to-do list shows up. Instead of battling it, you note "planning" and go back to the breath. The thought is allowed to be there without being obeyed.

## Practice

- Practise observing thoughts arise and pass without following them. When you notice you have been swept away, gently return to observing. This is not suppression; it is non-engagement.
