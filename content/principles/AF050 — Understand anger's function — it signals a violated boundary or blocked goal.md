> **Principle 50** — Understand anger's function: it signals a violated boundary or blocked goal

Anger is a mobilising response to perceived injustice, violation, or obstruction. What it carries is information about value: something mattered enough to defend. Aristotle's discussion of good temper already treats the emotion as usable material rather than noise:

> "The man who is angry at the right things and with the right people, and, further, as he ought, when he ought, and as long as he ought, is praised." (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.5, [Ross, MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.4.iv.html))

The signal is fine; the calibration is the virtue. The failures Aristotle and the affect literature converge on are anger that is disproportionate, misdirected, or expressed destructively. His next sentences add the other half of the principle's point: people who never get angry at what deserves anger are thought to be fools and unlikely to defend themselves. Read the message, then choose the channel.

**Plain words**
Anger is a smoke alarm: it tells you something is burning, not what to do about the fire. The alarm going off is not the failure; ignoring it, or smashing the wall with a hammer, is. First ask what exactly was crossed or blocked. Then decide whether to say something now, later, or never, and in what tone. The feeling hands you the topic and the energy; the channel is still your choice.

*For example (imagined):* The flash of anger at the meeting reads "boundary violated", and the reading is accurate. The choice is between the sarcastic strike in the room (discharge, no repair) and the boundary stated plainly the next morning (repair).
## Psychology parallels

- **Anger has a functional signal value**, it mobilises response to violation and is associated with [[Approach versus avoidance motivation|approach motivation]] (Carver & Harmon-Jones, 2009)
- **Venting anger does not reduce it; it can amplify it**, catharsis theory is not supported; expressing anger can increase anger and aggression (Bushman, 2002)
- **[[Rumination|Anger rumination maintains aggression]]**, repeatedly revisiting anger and its causes predicts aggressive behaviour (Bushman, 2002; Denson et al., 2011)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Righteous anger vs wrath|Righteous anger vs wrath]]** (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV)
- **Ira furor brevis est (anger is brief madness)** (Horace, Epistles)

## Practice

- When angry, extract the message ("a boundary was crossed," "a goal was blocked"). Then decide whether the action it urges (confront, assert, protect) is proportionate and wise. Do not vent to "release" it; venting amplifies.
