> **Principle 55** — Retract like the tortoise when needed

The Gita's tortoise appears in its description of the steady sage. Verse 2.58 says:

> One who is able to withdraw the senses from their objects, just as a tortoise withdraws its limbs into its shell, is established in divine wisdom.

(Bhagavad Gita 2.58, trans. Swami Mukundananda, [holy-bhagavad-gita.org](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/2/verse/58/).)

The verse names the ability to withdraw as a mark of wisdom. It does not say the tortoise comes out again when it is safe, so the point about timing, and the idea of re-extending, is this page's own reading. The next verse, 2.59, adds a limit: "Aspirants may restrain the senses from their objects of enjoyment, but the taste for the sense objects remains. However, even this taste ceases for those who realize the Supreme" ([BG 2.59](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/2/verse/59/)). So withdrawal alone is not the point. Mukundananda's comment on that verse compares feeding the senses to pouring clarified butter on a fire: it "may be smothered for a moment, but then it flares up with redoubled intensity."

In this passage the tortoise image is about control of the senses as part of wisdom, with the further warning that suppression leaves the appetite. The practice suggestions are all the page's own: naming a personal withdrawal signal such as rising irritation or compulsive checking, and practising early retraction before the state solidifies.

**Plain words**

The Gita says a steady person can pull the senses back from their objects. It also says that pulling back is not enough if the wanting stays. Whether and when to come out again is something you decide, and the verse doesn't say.

**For example**

A man finds that scrolling in the evening is what winds him up. He puts the phone in another room for a week. By the weekend he notices he still reaches for it, and takes this to mean the wanting hasn't gone.
