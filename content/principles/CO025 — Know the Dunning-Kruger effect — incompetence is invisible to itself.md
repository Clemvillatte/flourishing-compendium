> **Principle 25** — Know the Dunning-Kruger effect: incompetence is invisible to itself

Kruger and Dunning's four studies gave this principle its evidence. Participants scoring in the bottom quartile on tests of humor, grammar, and logic grossly overestimated their performance and ability: their scores put them in the 12th percentile, and they estimated themselves to be in the 62nd. (Kruger & Dunning, 1999.)

> "Not only do these people reach erroneous conclusions and make unfortunate choices, but their incompetence robs them of the metacognitive ability to realize it." (Kruger & Dunning, 1999, [Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 77(6):1121-1134](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/sasi/wp-content/uploads/sites/275/2015/11/krugerdunning99.pdf))

Several analyses linked the miscalibration to deficits in metacognitive skill, and the abstract adds a paradox the compendium keeps: improving participants' skills, and thus their metacognitive competence, helped them recognize the limitations of their abilities. What the four studies do not show is the popular confidence curve, the peak-and-crash-and-recovery meme; what they show is bottom-quartile overestimation in these tests, linked to metacognitive skill deficits. (Kruger & Dunning, 1999.)

**Plain words**
In the studies behind this page, the people who scored lowest guessed themselves near the top, and the authors' explanation was double: the skill that produces good answers is also the skill that recognises bad ones. The practical reading is the page's practice: in areas where you feel confident, ask someone more experienced, because the feeling is not the evidence.

*For example:* Three weeks into a new field is a common place for confident takes; the studies suggest the confidence and the competence are not yet connected. The compendium's exercise is to collect feedback before the takes.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Dunning-Kruger effect|Dunning-Kruger effect]]**, low performers overestimate their relative standing (Kruger & Dunning, 1999)
- **The effect is about metacognitive calibration, not stupidity**, it reflects the difficulty of knowing what you do not know (Kruger & Dunning, 1999; Dunning, 2011)
- **Note: some statistical critiques argue the effect is partly a statistical artifact**, though the core phenomenon (miscalibration) is supported (Gignac & Zajenkowski, 2020)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Socratic ignorance|Socratic ignorance]]** (Plato, Apology)
- **[[The more you know, the more you know you don't know|The more you know, the more you know you don't know]]** (attributed to Einstein)
- **[[Humility|Humility as epistemic virtue]]** (Aquinas, Summa Theologica)

## Practice

- In areas where you feel confident, seek feedback from more experienced others. The feeling of confidence is not a reliable indicator of competence.
