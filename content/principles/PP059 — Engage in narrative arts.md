> **Principle 59** — Engage in narrative arts

The claim is that fiction works as a simulator of social life. The evidence for a large, quick effect has weakened, and a small positive link remains.

Kidd and Castano reported in five experiments that reading literary fiction "temporarily enhances ToM" (theory of mind) compared with nonfiction, popular fiction or nothing ([Science, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24091705/)). A replication by three research groups with 792 participants found "no significant advantage in RMET scores for literary fiction compared to any of the other conditions", while lifetime exposure to fiction still predicted scores ([Panero et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27762576/)).

Two preregistered meta-analyses later found that experiments on reading fiction gave "significant small-sized cognitive benefits", g = 0.14, for empathy and mentalizing, and that lifetime fiction exposure correlated at r = .16 ([Cognitive effects and correlates of reading fiction](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38602788/)). An earlier meta-analysis of reading habits found correlations with empathy of r = .070 and with theory of mind of r = .211 ([Mumper and Gerrig, manuscript version](https://psycnet.apa.org/manuscript/2016-59834-001.pdf)).

**Plain words**
Stories may help you imagine other minds, but the effect is small, and part of it may be that people who read fiction were already good at it. A single reading session is unlikely to change you.

**For example**
After a novel or film, write three sentences about what one character wanted and what they were afraid of. Then ask whether anyone you know has wanted or feared the same.
