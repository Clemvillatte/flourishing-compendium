> **Principle 17** — Recognise the availability heuristic: vividness is mistaken for frequency

You judge the likelihood of events by how easily examples come to mind. Vivid, recent, emotionally charged events are more available and thus judged more common, regardless of actual frequency. This drives fear of plane crashes over car crashes, shark attacks over drownings.

**Plain words** The availability heuristic: you judge likelihood by how easily examples come to mind — vivid, recent, emotionally charged events feel common regardless of actual frequency. After the shark story on the news, the ocean feels deadly; the drive to the beach was the actual risk.

*For example:* The parent who won't let the child walk to school (abduction stories are vivid) and drives them daily (crashes are boring) — the availability of the fear has nothing to do with its frequency. Run the numbers; the numbers are boring and true.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Availability heuristic|Availability heuristic]]**, ease of recall is used as a proxy for frequency (Tversky & Kahneman, 1973, 1974)
- **Media amplifies availability bias**, sensational coverage makes rare events feel common (Combs & Slovic, 1979)

## Philosophy parallels

- **Vividness vs truth** (Descartes, Meditations)
- **The distinction between appearance and reality** (Plato, Republic)

## Practice

- When you feel something is common or dangerous, ask: "is this feeling based on data or on how easily I can recall examples?" Check actual base rates.
