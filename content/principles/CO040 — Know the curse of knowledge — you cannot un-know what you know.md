> **Principle 40** — Know the curse of knowledge: you cannot un-know what you know
The economic analyses discussed by Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber assume that better-informed agents can reproduce less-informed agents' judgments by setting aside information the latter lack. They tested that assumption in the laboratory. They first collected earnings estimates for eight real companies from 51 students. Two months later they had a second group trade assets that paid a dividend equal to those estimates; the traders knew the companies' actual earnings, and to price the asset they had to predict the estimates of people who lacked that knowledge. The informed traders did not fully ignore what they knew when estimating the others' estimates. (Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber, 1989, Journal of Political Economy 97(5):1232-1254.)

> "Better-informed agents should ignore their additional information when forecasting the forecasts of less-informed agents." (Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber, 1989, [Journal of Political Economy 97(5):1235](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/CurseknowledgeEconSet.pdf))

Their formal statement: the better-informed forecaster overestimates the scope of the less-informed agent's information, landing between the correct forecast and a full projection of their own knowledge. In their markets, trading cut the effect by roughly half without removing it. The authors suggested underwriting as a possible analogue: informed experts price goods for a less-informed public. ([Camerer et al., 1989](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/CurseknowledgeEconSet.pdf))

**Plain words**
Imagine seeing the solution to a problem, then being asked to predict a classmate's answer before they have seen it. Your prediction needs to reflect the information available to the classmate. For the compendium's exercise, list what they have been told before predicting their answer.

*For example:*
Imagine introducing a software system to a newcomer. You refer to its pipeline before checking whether they know that term. As a compendium exercise, ask them to describe the next step in their own words, then explain any missing term.

## Practice

- When explaining something, ask: "what does this person actually know?" Do not assume. Test understanding rather than assuming it.
