> **Principle 40** — Know the curse of knowledge: you cannot un-know what you know
Economists assumed the reverse of this principle: in models of asymmetric information, a better-informed agent can reproduce the judgment of a less-informed one, since it can ignore what it knows. Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber tested that assumption in the laboratory. They first collected earnings estimates for eight real companies from 51 students. Two months later they had a second group trade assets that paid a dividend equal to those estimates; the traders knew the companies' actual earnings, and to price the asset they had to predict the estimates of people who lacked that knowledge. The informed traders did not fully ignore what they knew when estimating the others' estimates. (Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber, 1989, Journal of Political Economy 97(5):1232-1254.)

> "Better-informed agents should ignore their additional information when forecasting the forecasts of less-informed agents." (Camerer, Loewenstein & Weber, 1989, [Journal of Political Economy 97(5):1235](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/CurseknowledgeEconSet.pdf))

Their formal statement: the better-informed forecaster overestimates the scope of the less-informed agent's information, landing between the correct forecast and a full projection of their own knowledge. In their markets, trading cut the effect by roughly half without removing it. They noted the closest natural analogue is underwriting, where experts price goods for a less-informed public. ([Camerer et al., 1989, pp. 1235, 1250-1251](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/CurseknowledgeEconSet.pdf))

**Plain words**
Once you know the answer, it is hard to remember what guessing felt like. If you have ever given directions using a landmark only you can see, you have met the problem: the landmark is invisible to them and present to you, so you keep giving directions from it. The compendium's exercise is to describe the ground the other person is standing on before you build on it: ask what they already have, and start there.

*For example:*
An expert explains a new system by saying "just use the pipeline." The newcomer has never seen a pipeline. Ask what the person in front of you already has, and name the next step in their terms.

## Practice

- When explaining something, ask: "what does this person actually know?" Do not assume. Test understanding rather than assuming it.
