> **Principle 59** — Practice generosity

Strength of character (fortitudo), Spinoza says in the note to III, P59, divides into two: courage, which aims at the agent's own good, and "highmindedness (generositas)", which aims at others'. The definition, in Elwes's rendering of the term:

> "By highmindedness I mean the desire whereby every man endeavours, solely under the dictates of reason, to aid other men and to unite them to himself in friendship." (Ethics III, P59 note, [Elwes](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800))

The grounding is the note to IV, P18: "to man there is nothing more useful than man--nothing, I repeat, more excellent for preserving their being can be wished for by men, than that all should so in all points agree, that the minds and bodies of all should form, as it were, one single mind and one single body". Aided others are allies in your own perseverance, which is why the strong man "should desire for others the good which he seeks for himself" (IV, P73 note). The page's exercise drawn from the account: treat helpfulness as a capacity to train, one deliberate act of aid per day, however small, with the reason attached.

**Plain words**
Generosity here is a desire, and the desire's source is reason rather than pity or mood. The case for aiding others is arithmetic in Spinoza's system: of external things, nothing is more useful to you than another person in agreement with your nature, so joining others in friendship serves your own perseverance as much as theirs. Warmth can come along; the account does not rest on it.

*For example:* Imagined: a colleague explains a broken process to a new hire, unprompted, because he wants the team to work and knows the fix. Whether he also felt warm about it does not change the act's standing in the account.
