> **Principle 44** — Establish a steady, comfortable seat

Patanjali defines posture in the shortest sutra of the second book:

> Stable-and-easy posture.

(Yoga Sutras 2.46, trans. James Haughton Woods, *The Yoga-System of Patanjali*, Harvard Oriental Series 17, 1914, pp. 191-192, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/yogasystemofpata00wooduoft).)

Vyasa's commentary says: "Stable means motionless. That posture which is easy, which brings ease is the one intended by the sutra." He lists many traditional postures, including the lotus, and then says the right one is the arrangement "in which one finds entire (sidhyati) stability and ease". So the test is the two qualities. The shape matters less than whether you can hold it.

The next sutra says how to reach it: "By relaxation of effort or by a [mental] state-of-balance with reference to Ananta." The commentary explains that "when efforts cease the posture is completed, so that there is no agitation of the body." That supports the practical rule on this page that a position needing constant upkeep is not yet your posture. The rule is this page's phrasing of that commentary.

**Plain words**

The sutra asks two questions of a seat: can you stay still in it, and is it comfortable? A deep or impressive shape has no standing of its own. If you are fighting your body to hold a position, keep adjusting until the effort drops away. Then it is a posture.

**For example**

Someone gives up the full lotus that their knees object to and sits on a chair with a firm cushion. Twenty minutes later they have not shifted once, and the sutra's two qualities are present.
