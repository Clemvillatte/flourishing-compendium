> **Principle 63** — Trust plainness over attainment

The life of Gampopa records the pattern. Each time Gampopa brought Milarepa a new vision or meditation experience, the answer came back in the same shape:

> "This is neither good nor bad. Make further effort to actualize it. Do not be attracted by such visions, perfect the power of the inner heat." (Milarepa, on Gampopa's vision of the five Dhyani Buddhas, in Geshe Ngawang Dhargyey's life of Gampopa)

The account's summary is explicit: "whenever Milarepa heard reports on the various stages of experiences of Gampopa, he always said, 'It is neither good nor bad. Meditate more.' He fully explained to his disciple what his experiences meant, but never praised him." The reason is given too: "If a guru praises too much and gives too much encouragement... the disciple will get carried away, which will be a great obstacle. He will not improve and will become attached to his various experiences and be overcome by them." And the parting warning, as the account reports it, repeats the caution for powers: Milarepa gave Gampopa "a great deal of advice, warning him about pride, specifically because he had so many miraculous powers", telling him not to be overwhelmed by them, "these could become great obstacles for him". ([Study Buddhism, The Life of Gampopa](https://studybuddhism.com/en/tibetan-buddhism/spiritual-teachers/gampopa/the-life-of-gampopa))

**Plain words**
The teaching's shape: experiences and powers are read as indications, not trophies. Milarepa's repeated response, neither good nor bad, meditate more, keeps the practitioner from stopping to admire what arose. In the compendium's application, when something impressive happens in practice, mention it to one grounded person and return to the practice.

For example: After a striking experience on retreat you mention it to a friend; the conversation moves on to what you will practice next, and you go back to the cushion.
