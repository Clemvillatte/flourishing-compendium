> **Principle 30** — Use pre-mortems: imagine failure before it happens

Before starting a project or making a decision, imagine that it has already failed. Ask: "what went wrong?" This surfaces risks that optimistic planning misses and reduces overconfidence.

**Plain words** Pre-mortems: before starting, imagine the project has already failed — "it's a year later and it failed; what went wrong?" This surfaces risks that optimistic planning misses and reduces overconfidence. The imagination of failure is cheap; the discovery after is not.

*For example:* The team that spends twenty minutes writing the failure story before the launch — and finds three concrete risks (the vendor, the single point of contact, the holiday timing) that six weeks of optimistic planning never surfaced. The funeral was fictional; the fixes were real.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Pre-mortem technique|Pre-mortem technique]]**, imagining future failure improves identification of risks and reduces overconfidence (Klein, 2007; Mitchell et al., 1989)
- **Prospective hindsight improves risk detection**, imagining an event has already occurred increases the ability to identify reasons for it (Mitchell et al., 1989)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Premeditatio malorum|Premeditatio malorum]]** (Stoics, Seneca, Letters)
- **[[Negative visualisation|Negative visualisation]]** (Stoics, Epictetus, Discourses)
- **[[Memento mori|Memento mori]]** (Stoics, Marcus Aurelius, Meditations)

## Practice

- Before committing to a plan, spend 5 minutes writing: "it is six months from now, and this has failed. What went wrong?" Use the answers to strengthen the plan.
