> **Principle 119** — The self is a chariot: parts without an essence

In the Milindapañha, King Milinda asks the monk Nāgasena his name. Nāgasena answers that "Nāgasena" is "only a generally understood term, a designation in common use. For there is no permanent individuality (no soul) involved in the matter" (the bracketed "no soul" is the translator's).

### The chariot test

Nāgasena then asks how the king travelled. By carriage, says Milinda. So what is the carriage?

> "Is it the pole that is the chariot?" ... "Is it the wheels, or the framework, or the ropes, or the yoke, or the spokes of the wheels, or the goad, that are the chariot?" ... "Then is it all these parts of it that are the chariot?" ... "But is there anything outside them that is the chariot?"
> — T. W. Rhys Davids, trans., The Questions of King Milinda (SBE 35, pp. 43-44)

To each the king says no, and Nāgasena concludes, "Chariot is a mere empty sound." Milinda replies that the word applies "on account of its having all these things". Nāgasena agrees and turns it on himself, citing a verse by the nun Vajirā:

> "Just as it is by the condition precedent / Of the co-existence of its various parts / That the word 'chariot' is used, / Just so is it that when the Skandhas / Are there we talk of a 'being.'"

### Two older texts

The verse comes from the Vajira Sutta (SN 5.10). In Thanissaro's translation: "Just as when, with an assemblage of parts, there's the word, chariot, even so when aggregates are present, there's the convention of living being."

The Anatta-lakkhana Sutta (SN 22.59) has no chariot in it. It supplies a different test, applied to each of the five aggregates: "If form were the self, this form would not lend itself to dis-ease. It would be possible [to say] with regard to form, 'Let this form be thus. Let this form not be thus.'"

(Milindapañha, chariot simile; SN 5.10; SN 22.59.)

**Plain words** A chariot is wheels, axle, pole and frame arranged to carry someone. Take them apart and no single piece is the chariot, and nothing extra turns up either. The word still works. The suttas say "I" works the same way: body, feeling, perception, fabrications and consciousness, with no separate owner standing behind them. The simile's target is that extra thing, not the everyday use of the word.

*For example:* Take the word "team": players, a coach, a ground, a fixture list. Nothing on that list is the team and nothing is missing from it, yet "the team lost on Saturday" is true.
