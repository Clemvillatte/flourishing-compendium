> **Principle 113** — Make your household a school of virtue

Aristotle does not say in so many words that a household is a school of virtue. What he does say, in [NE VIII.10-12 (Ross, MIT Classics)](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.8.viii.html), is that each household relationship has its own form of justice and friendship, and that these can go well or badly. A father's care for his sons "bears the form of monarchy, since the father cares for his children", and it goes wrong when it turns "tyrannical", as among the Persians, who "use their sons as slaves". The bond between brothers "is like timocracy; for they are equal, except in so far as they differ in age". Parents "love their children as being a part of themselves".

His account also contains a hierarchy that this principle does not endorse. He describes a husband's rule as "aristocratic" and says a master's rule over slaves "seems to be a correct form of government", adding that "the slave is a living tool". He also allows that "qua man" one can be a friend to a slave. The household question remains worth asking without accepting those hierarchies.

(NE VIII.10-12.)

**Plain words** Aristotle asks what fairness and affection require within different family relationships. A contemporary way to use that question is to look at ordinary household decisions: who receives care, whose needs get heard, and how responsibilities are shared. His descriptions of marriage and slavery also need to be read critically; this principle does not endorse them.

For example: dividing the last slice of cake between two children is a small exercise in fairness, and how you do it shows them what fair looks like.
