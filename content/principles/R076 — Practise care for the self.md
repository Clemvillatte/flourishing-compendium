> **Principle 338** — Practise care for the self

I did not find Noddings (2002) or Tronto (2013) discussing care for the self, and I did not open either book, so the attribution stays unverified. The "empty cup" line is a common saying, and I found no source for it. The psychology sources give more solid ground, with one caution.

Neff (2003) defines self-compassion as "being open to and moved by one's own suffering, experiencing feelings of caring and kindness toward oneself, taking an understanding, nonjudgmental attitude toward one's inadequacies and failures, and recognizing that one's experience is part of the common human experience" ([Self-Compassion, Stress, and Coping, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2914331/)). Its three parts are self-kindness, common humanity and mindfulness. That is a way of treating yourself in hard moments. It is not the same as taking a rest day, though the two can go together.

The caution comes from Maslach and Leiter (2016). They define burnout as "a psychological syndrome emerging as a prolonged response to chronic interpersonal stressors on the job" and describe it as person-job imbalances in six areas: workload, control, reward, community, fairness and values ([World Psychiatry, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4911781/)). Their psychiatry section lists "too much work, long working hours, chronic staff shortages" and lack of management support. Burnout is therefore not only a self-care problem, which echoes the political side of care. I did not find evidence that individual self-care alone prevents burnout.

**Plain words** Caring for yourself is part of caring well. Neff's self-compassion describes how to treat yourself when things go wrong. Burnout research warns that overloaded systems cannot be fixed by self-care alone.

*For example:* This is an imagined case. A carer takes the offered respite weekend. She also asks the agency to share the night shifts, because rest alone will not fix the load.
