> **Principle 354** — Practise listening as a moral act

Buber's essay "Elements of the Interhuman" (in The Knowledge of Man) is about dialogue in general, and the passages cited here do not single out listening. It says "genuine conversation, and therefore every actual fulfilment of relation between men, means acceptance of otherness", and describes a speaker who, while wanting to influence the other, "unreservedly accepts and confirms him in his being this man" ([quoted at Jenny Connected](https://jennymackness.wordpress.com/tag/martin-buber/)). It also says "the primary word I-Thou can only be spoken with the whole being" ([Buber text excerpt](https://faculty.salisbury.edu/~jdhatley/101BuberText.htm)). So "listening is the primary act of the I-Thou relation" is this page's reading, not a quotation in the cited passages.

Rogers and Farson (1957) name the listening attitude directly: "Until we can demonstrate a spirit which genuinely respects the potential worth of the individual, which considers his sights and trusts his capacity for self-direction, we cannot begin to be effective listeners". Their model of the listener says "I'm not trying to change you or evaluate you. I just want to understand you" ([Rogers and Farson, PDF](http://www.ask-force.org/web/Discourse/Rogers-Farson-ActiveListening-1957.pdf)). This matches the original entry's idea of listening as respect and not as a technique.

For therapeutic empathy, Elliott et al. (2011) pooled 59 samples with 3,599 clients and found empathy a "moderately strong predictor of therapy outcome", mean weighted r = .31. Client and observer ratings of empathy predicted outcomes better than therapist ratings, and the relation was strongest for less experienced therapists ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21401273/)). The data are correlational and cover therapy, not everyday friendship.

**Plain words** Listening is usually taught as a way to gather information. Here it is the respect itself. Buber says real dialogue means accepting the other person as the one he is; Rogers and Farson say you cannot start to listen effectively until you truly respect someone's worth and trust them to steer their own life. The therapy numbers add a caution: feeling understood goes together with doing better in treatment, but the studies only correlate.

*For example:* A friend says "I'm not going to say anything for a while, just tell me all of it". The speaker hears their own answer partway through.
