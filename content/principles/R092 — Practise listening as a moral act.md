> **Principle 354** — Practise listening as a moral act

Buber's essay "Elements of the Interhuman" (in The Knowledge of Man) is about dialogue in general, and the passages I found do not single out listening. It says "genuine conversation, and therefore every actual fulfilment of relation between men, means acceptance of otherness", and describes a speaker who, while wanting to influence the other, "unreservedly accepts and confirms him in his being this man" ([quoted at Jenny Connected](https://jennymackness.wordpress.com/tag/martin-buber/)). It also says "the primary word I-Thou can only be spoken with the whole being" ([Buber text excerpt](https://faculty.salisbury.edu/~jdhatley/101BuberText.htm)). So "listening is the primary act of the I-Thou relation" is the page's reading, not a quotation I found.

Rogers and Farson (1957) name the listening attitude directly: "Until we can demonstrate a spirit which genuinely respects the potential worth of the individual, which considers his sights and trusts his capacity for self-direction, we cannot begin to be effective listeners". Their model of the listener says "I'm not trying to change you or evaluate you. I just want to understand you" ([Rogers and Farson, PDF](http://www.ask-force.org/web/Discourse/Rogers-Farson-ActiveListening-1957.pdf)). This matches the seed's idea of listening as respect and not as a technique.

For therapeutic empathy, Elliott et al. (2011) pooled 59 samples with 3,599 clients and found empathy a "moderately strong predictor of therapy outcome", mean weighted r = .31. Client and observer ratings of empathy predicted outcomes better than therapist ratings, and the relation was strongest for less experienced therapists ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21401273/)). The data are correlational and cover therapy, not everyday friendship.

**Plain words** Listening can be an act of respect, not only a way to collect information. Buber speaks of accepting the other as they are, Rogers of trusting their capacity for self-direction, and therapy research links felt empathy with better outcomes.

*For example:* This is an imagined case. A friend says "I'm not going to say anything for a while, just tell me all of it". The speaker hears their own answer partway through.
