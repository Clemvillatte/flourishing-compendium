> **Principle 321** — Practise the Mencian sprouts

Nurture the four moral sentiments that are natural to human beings. Mencius names them and their virtues: "The feeling of commiseration is the principle of benevolence. The feeling of shame and dislike is the principle of righteousness. The feeling of modesty and complaisance is the principle of propriety. The feeling of approving and disapproving is the principle of knowledge." (Mencius, The Mencius 2A6, tr. Legge, https://www.sacred-texts.com/book/mencius/chapter-6.) They come as standard equipment: "Men have these four principles just as they have their four limbs." (Ibid.) Their universality is argued from a thought experiment in the same passage: "if men suddenly see a child about to fall into a well, they will without exception experience a feeling of alarm and distress" — not to win the favour of the child's parents or the praise of neighbours.

They are beginnings rather than finished growth, and the chapter's instruction is development: "let them know to give them all their development and completion, and the issue will be like that of fire which has begun to burn, or that of a spring which has begun to find vent." Those who deny the possibility, Mencius adds, "play the thief with themselves." (Ibid.) The work of the principle is cultivation: notice the feeling, act on it, and let practice turn a reaction into a character.

(Mencius, The Mencius 2A6.)

**Plain words** You already flinch when someone gets hurt; you wince at your own bad behaviour; you feel the pull to yield and the sense that something is just wrong. Mencius's claim is that these starts come standard in everyone — his instruction is to grow them. The flinch is raw material; acting on it, repeatedly, is what turns it into kindness.

*For example:* A person at the scene of an accident feels the flinch toward the injured — that flinch, on Mencius's account, is the sprout. The person who kneels down and helps is just the sprout, watered.
