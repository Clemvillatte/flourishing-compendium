> **Principle 69** — Learn to think in probabilities: certainty is usually an illusion

The strongest evidence that probability thinking can be learned comes from a forecasting tournament. Mellers and colleagues recruited volunteers to give probabilities for geopolitical events over two years. They report that "probability training corrected cognitive biases, encouraged forecasters to use reference classes, and provided forecasters with heuristics, such as averaging when multiple estimates were available," and that training, teaming and tracking "improved both calibration and resolution" ([Mellers et al., 2014, Psychological Science 25(5), abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24659192/)). Calibration means that events given 70% happen about 70% of the time, and the percentage format is what allows that check.

Two limits apply. A later reanalysis of the same data found that once extraneous variables were controlled, they "substantially eliminated, reduced, and, in some cases, even reversed the effects of the experimental manipulations of teaming and training" ([Hauenstein et al., Psychological Science, abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/09567976241266481)). The result is contested. And the tournament measures accuracy, not feelings. The compendium's further claim that probability thinking softens the sting of being wrong is not tested in these sources.

**Plain words** Give a number instead of a verdict. "Seventy percent" can be scored later and corrected, while "definitely" can only be right or embarrassing. In a large forecasting tournament, people trained in this way were scored on their accuracy, and the training was reported to help, although that finding has been challenged since. Keep a record of your numbers so you can see how often your 70% was right.

*For example:* Before a launch, write "70% this ships by the end of the month" and note the date. When the month ends, check it. After enough of these you can see whether your 70% claims come true about seven times in ten.

## Practice

- When making judgments, express them as probabilities ("I am 70% confident") rather than certainties. Track your calibration over time. Being wrong at 70% is expected 30% of the time.
