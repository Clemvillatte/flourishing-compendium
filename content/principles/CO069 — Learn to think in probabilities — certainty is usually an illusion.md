> **Principle 69** — Learn to think in probabilities: certainty is usually an illusion

Most real-world judgments are probabilistic, not certain. Thinking in probabilities (rather than certainties) improves decision-making, calibration, and learning. It also reduces the emotional impact of being wrong.

**Plain words** Most real-world judgments are probabilistic, not certain — thinking in probabilities improves decisions, calibration, and learning, and softens the emotional impact of being wrong. Certainty is usually an illusion. Trade your "definitely" for percentages.

*For example:* The forecaster who says "70% chance this launch lands" — and can be scored, calibrated, improved — versus the one who says "it'll definitely work" and learns nothing either way. The percentage isn't weakness; it's a target you can aim at.
## Psychology parallels

- **Probabilistic reasoning and forecasting**, thinking in probabilities improves forecasting accuracy (Tetlock & Gardner, Superforecasting, 2015)
- **Calibration training improves judgment**, feedback on probability estimates improves calibration (Tetlock, 2005; Mellers et al., 2014)

## Practice

- When making judgments, express them as probabilities ("I am 70% confident") rather than certainties. Track your calibration over time. Being wrong at 70% is expected 30% of the time.
