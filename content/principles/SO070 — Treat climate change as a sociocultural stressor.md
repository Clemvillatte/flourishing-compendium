> **Principle 70** — Treat climate change as a sociocultural stressor

Berry and colleagues' public-health framework treats climate change as a mental-health stressor that can operate directly, through trauma in severe weather events, and indirectly, through effects on physical health and on community wellbeing, where eroded physical environments damage social ones. ([Berry et al., 2010](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00038-009-0112-0))

The scale is documented: in a ten-country survey of 10,000 people aged 16 to 25, more than 45% said their feelings about climate change negatively affected their daily life and functioning, and distress was correlated with perceived inadequate government response. ([Hickman et al., 2021](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanplh/article/PIIS2542-5196(21)00278-3/fulltext))

> "Different aspects of climate change may affect mental health through direct and indirect pathways, leading to serious mental health problems, possibly including increased suicide mortality." ([Berry et al., 2010, abstract: Conclusions](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00038-009-0112-0))

**Plain words** The stress reaches people by more than one road: through the disaster itself, through heat that stops work and cuts income, and through the slow damage to the places and groups people share. The survey numbers say this is already daily life for many young people rather than a forecast. Because the stressor is shared, the response is partly shared too: what governments do or fail to do is part of the exposure.

**For example (imagined):** A heatwave cuts a crew's working hours, pay falls with the hours, and the lost income shows up at home as worry and short temper. Berry and colleagues trace exactly that chain, from heat-limited work through income loss to distress.
