> **Principle 16** — Distinguish realistic from unrealistic optimism

The working half of optimism is effort. Scheier and Carver's self-regulation model ties expectancies to behaviour: "If expectancies are favorable, the result is renewed effort. If expectancies are sufficiently unfavorable, the result is reduced effort, or even complete disengagement from further attempts" (p. 222). In their 1985 longitudinal study, subjects higher in optimism were less likely to report being bothered by the development of physical symptoms over the assessment period, even after controlling for initial symptom levels (partial r(138) = -.18, p < .05).

> "By a number of metrics and across a variety of domains, people have been found to assign higher probabilities to their attainment of desirable outcomes than either objective criteria or logical analysis warrants." (Armor and Taylor, 2002, p. 334)

Armor and Taylor's review adds the other half of the ledger: those same biased expectations tend to track past performance, and can raise what people attain. The compendium's reading of the two literatures together is that a positive expectation earns its keep where effort can move the outcome, and quietly retires you where only the evidence can.

(Scheier and Carver, 1985; Schwarzer, 1994;Armor and Taylor, 2002.)

**Plain words** Both kinds of optimist expect good things. The realistic one has checked what she controls and priced what she does not before committing her effort. The unrealistic one skips the checking and expects the wanting to be enough. If being wrong would send you back to the plan, the expectation was doing its job.

*For example:* A founder who says "we can fix the cash problem if we change the pricing" is realistic. One who ignores the bank statement is not.
