> **Principle 95** — Collect the mind (samadhi as gathering)

In MN 118, concentrating the mind is one part of a sequence that also includes becoming sensitive to it, gladdening it and releasing it. Concentration belongs to a larger contemplative training, rather than a claim that one browser tab guarantees clear thinking (Ānāpānasati Sutta, steps 9-12, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, [text](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN118.html)).

> "I will breathe in concentrating the mind." (The practitioner's training intention in MN 118, step 11, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, [text](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN118.html))

For comparison, Lutz and colleagues describe focused-attention practice as involving the recognition of distraction, disengagement from it and return to a chosen object. That is a scientific framework for investigating practices, not a demonstration that every period of concentration improves every kind of judgment ([2008, Box 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2693206/)).

### Plain words

The compendium suggests being clear about what you intend to attend to. When you notice that attention has moved elsewhere, check whether the new task is necessary now; if not, return to the one you chose. You need not count every distraction as failure or confuse having fewer materials open with understanding them.

### For example

Imagine revising a paragraph with both the draft and its reference text open. Read the relevant passage, make the correction, and notice when you have drifted into unrelated browsing. Returning to the paragraph serves the task; closing the necessary reference would not.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Focused-attention meditation|Focused-attention meditation]]**: Lutz and colleagues distinguish maintaining a focus from monitoring the stream of experience, and frame both as subjects for further research ([2008, abstract and Box 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2693206/)).
- **[[Attention residue|Attention residue costs of fragmentation]]**: two experiments examined difficulty disengaging from a prior task and poorer subsequent-task performance; they did not establish an ideal browser-tab count ([Leroy, 2009, abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749597809000399)).
