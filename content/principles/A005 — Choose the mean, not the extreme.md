> **Principle 5** — Choose the mean, not the extreme

Aristotle defines moral virtue in one sentence: "Virtue, then, is a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e. the mean relative to us, this being determined by a rational principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it." Two things follow. First, the mean is not arithmetic: he illustrates that "if ten is many and two is few, six is the intermediate" taken in terms of the object, but "the intermediate relatively to us is not to be taken so" — six pounds of food may be too much for a beginner and too little for Milo. Second, the mean is defined by fit: to feel passions "at the right times, with reference to the right objects, towards the right people, with the right motive, and in the right way, is what is both intermediate and best, and this is characteristic of virtue." That is the doctrine: [[Virtue|Virtue finds the intermediate]] between excess and deficiency: courage between cowardice and recklessness, generosity between miserliness and prodigality. And he bounds the doctrine at once: "not every action nor every passion admits of a mean; for some have names that already imply badness... adultery, theft, murder" — with these, "one must always be wrong." The compendium's application: seek the fitting, not the average; the mean shifts with the situation, the person, and the point — and some things have no mean to seek.

(NE II.2, II.6.)

**Plain words** Too much and too little both miss. Courage sits between cowardice and recklessness, generosity between stinginess and waste. But the middle isn't an average you can calculate. It shifts with the situation, the person, and the point. The target is whatever fits.

*For example:* Being honest with a grieving friend doesn't mean listing every flaw in the eulogy. The fitting amount of truth depends on who's in the room — and at some moments, for some people, the fitting amount is very little.
