> **Principle 68** — Yield in conflict to remain whole

In chapter 22, the sage holds to humility and refrains from self-display and self-assertion ([Legge, DDJ 22](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt)). Chapter 68 describes a skilled warrior without martial display and a fighter who avoids rage ([Legge, DDJ 68](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt)).

> It is because he is thus free from striving that therefore no one in the world is able to strive with him. (DDJ 22, [Legge](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

> He who in (Tao's) wars has skill
> Assumes no martial port;
> He who fights with most good will
> To rage makes no resort. (DDJ 68, [Legge](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

Both passages associate strength with restraint ([Legge, DDJ 22 and 68](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt)). Applied to an ordinary disagreement, yielding can mean giving up a demand to dominate the exchange while continuing to address the issue. This is a practical reading of the verses.

(DDJ 22, 68.)

**Plain words**
Imagine two people trying to pass through a doorway at once. You can step back and let the other person through, even if you reached it first. In an argument, ask whether insisting on every point serves the matter you are trying to settle. Concede what you can honestly concede, and keep any boundary that matters.

**For example**
If a colleague misstates your work in a meeting, correct the relevant fact without attacking their motives. Decide whether the remaining disagreement needs discussion with the group or a separate conversation.
