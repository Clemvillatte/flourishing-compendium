> **Principle 56** — Use memory deliberately: encoding and retrieval matter

> When the final test was given after 5 min, repeated studying improved recall relative to repeated testing. However, on the delayed tests, prior testing produced substantially greater retention than studying, even though repeated studying increased students' confidence in their ability to remember the material.

(Roediger and Karpicke, "Test-enhanced learning: taking memory tests improves long-term retention," Psychological Science 17(3), 2006, abstract; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/).)

In those experiments, students studied prose passages and either took one or three free-recall tests without feedback or restudied the same number of times. The final retention test came 5 minutes, 2 days or 1 week later. The cramming result is part of the finding: restudying wins at 5 minutes, and testing wins after a delay, while restudying feels more reassuring.

Spacing has its own evidence. Cepeda and colleagues' meta-analysis found 839 assessments of distributed practice in 317 experiments and concluded that the gap between study sessions that gives the best retention grows as the delay before the final test grows (Psychological Bulletin 132(3), 2006, abstract; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719566/)). A classroom meta-analysis of 22 reports found a moderate benefit of distributed over massed practice, d = 0.54 (Mawson and Kang, 2025; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40564553/)). For quizzing in classrooms, another meta-analysis found a medium effect (g = 0.499) ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33683913/)).

A scheduling study with 533 students concluded that students should "practice recalling concepts to an initial criterion of 3 correct recalls and then to relearn them 3 times at widely spaced intervals" (Rawson and Dunlosky, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General 140(3), 2011, abstract; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21707204/)).

The entry's claim about encoding strategies cites Craik and Lockhart (1972), which was not opened. The art-of-memory and Aristotle parallels in the philosophy section were also not opened.

**Plain words** Trying to recall something works better for lasting memory than reading it again, though rereading feels better. Spreading practice out over days beats cramming. The sensible method is to close the book, write what you remember, check, and come back after a gap.

*For example (imagined):* A student finishes a chapter, closes the book, and writes down everything they can recall. They then check the gaps, and repeat the exercise two days later.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Encoding strategies|Encoding strategies]]**, elaboration, organisation, and connecting to prior knowledge improve memory (Craik & Lockhart, 1972)
- **[[The testing effect / retrieval practice|Retrieval practice / testing effect]]**, actively recalling information strengthens memory more than rereading (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006)
- **[[Spaced repetition|Spaced repetition]]**, distributing practice over time improves retention relative to massed practice (Cepeda et al., 2006)

## Practice

- When learning, use active recall (test yourself), spaced repetition (review over increasing intervals), and elaboration (connect to what you know). Do not just reread.
