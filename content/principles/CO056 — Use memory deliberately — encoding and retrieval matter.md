> **Principle 56** — Use memory deliberately: encoding and retrieval matter

Memory is not just something that happens to you; it is something you can influence. How you encode (elaborate, connect, organise) and how you retrieve (test, explain, space) determine what you remember.

**Plain words** Memory is something you can influence: encode elaborately (connect, organise, explain) and retrieve actively (test yourself, space the reviews). The difference between "I'm bad at names" and "I remember people" is mostly method, not capacity.

*For example:* The student who re-reads the chapter four times (feels like studying, isn't) — versus the one who closes the book and writes what they remember (retrieval practice, brutal and effective). Same hours; one of them learns.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Encoding strategies|Encoding strategies]]**, elaboration, organisation, and connecting to prior knowledge improve memory (Craik & Lockhart, 1972)
- **[[The testing effect / retrieval practice|Retrieval practice / testing effect]]**, actively recalling information strengthens memory more than rereading (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006)
- **[[Spaced repetition|Spaced repetition]]**, distributing practice over time improves retention relative to massed practice (Cepeda et al., 2006)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Ars memoriae (art of memory)|Ars memoriae (art of memory)]]** (Cicero, De Oratore)
- **[[Virtue|Virtue as habituated memory]]** (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II)

## Practice

- When learning, use active recall (test yourself), spaced repetition (review over increasing intervals), and elaboration (connect to what you know). Do not just reread.
