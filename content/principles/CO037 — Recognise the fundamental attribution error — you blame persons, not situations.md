> **Principle 37** — Recognise the fundamental attribution error: you blame persons, not situations

> The actor-observer hypothesis (E. E. Jones & R. E. Nisbett, 1971) states that people tend to explain their own behavior with situation causes and other people's behavior with person causes. Widely known in psychology, this asymmetry has been described as robust, firmly established, and pervasive. However, a meta-analysis on 173 published studies revealed average effect sizes from d = -0.016 to d = 0.095.

(Malle, "The actor-observer asymmetry in attribution: a (surprising) meta-analysis," Psychological Bulletin 132(6), 2006, abstract; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17073526/).)

The entry packs two claims into one. The first, that observers over-read a person's behaviour as a sign of who they are, has strong experimental support. In the classic essay study by Jones and Harris (1967), readers were told that a pro-Castro or anti-Castro essay was either freely chosen or assigned. A modern account of it says that "when the reader was told that the position had been assigned, readers continued to estimate the authors true attitude to be consistent with the essay's position, albeit more weakly" (Walker, Smith and Vul, "The 'Fundamental Attribution Error' is rational in an uncertain world," 2015, [MIT-hosted PDF](https://www.mit.edu/~k2smith/pdf/WalkerSmithVul_2015_FAE.pdf)). That paper's title argues the bias may be rational under uncertainty, so the interpretation is contested even where the effect is not.

The second claim, that people explain their own behaviour by situation, is the actor-observer asymmetry quoted above. Malle's meta-analysis concluded that "across valence, no actor-observer asymmetry exists," so the "you excuse yourself" half of the entry is on weaker ground than the "you blame the other person" half.

On culture, Miyamoto and Kitayama found that when behaviour was still diagnostic of the actor's attitude, "both Americans and Japanese show an equally strong CB" (correspondence bias), but when it was minimally diagnostic, "Americans showed a strong CB. But Japanese did not show any CB" (Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 83(5), 2002, abstract; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12416925/)). This is narrower than "weaker in East Asian cultures." Ross (1977) and Nisbett et al. (2001) were not opened.

**Plain words** People tend to read another person's behaviour as showing their character, even when circumstances are pushing them, as the essay studies show. The commonly repeated second half, that we always excuse ourselves by pointing to the situation, did not hold up in a large meta-analysis. The useful habit survives: before judging someone's character, ask what their situation might be doing.

*For example (imagined):* A colleague's curt email reads as rudeness. Before replying, the reader asks what the sender's week might look like, and answers more gently.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Fundamental attribution error|Fundamental attribution error]]**, others' behaviour is attributed to disposition, own behaviour to situation (Ross, 1977; Jones & Harris, 1967)
- **The error is weaker in East Asian cultures**, holistic cognition increases attention to situational context (Nisbett et al., 2001; Miyamoto & Kitayama, 2002)

## Practice

- When someone behaves badly, before judging their character, ask: "what situation might be producing this behaviour?" Do not assume the behaviour reveals the person.
