> **Principle 73** — Build rest into the system

Recovery research treats rest as part of how work is sustained. Zijlstra and colleagues propose that recovery is "the continuous process of harmonizing the 'actual state' with the state that is 'required' at that moment", drawing on the Effort-Recovery model and Conservation of Resources theory ([Zijlstra, Cropley and Rydstedt 2014](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/smi.2604)). Sonnentag's "recovery paradox" is that sustained workload disrupts the psychological detachment from work during nonwork hours that restores depleted resources ([Springer article citing Sonnentag 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41542-026-00275-0)).

The evidence on what rest does is correlational. A meta-analysis of 91 samples (N = 38,124) found detachment associated with less exhaustion, higher life satisfaction, better sleep and task performance, with small to medium effects ([Wendsche and Lohmann-Haislah 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28133454/)). A later meta-analysis of 316 samples found that relaxation and mastery experiences predicted job and personal outcomes, while detachment reduced negative personal outcomes but did not seem to benefit job outcomes ([Recovery Experiences meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10869-022-09821-3)). So the benefits depend on what the rest consists of.

(Sonnentag, 2018; Zijlstra et al., 2014.)

### Scope

These studies concern recovery from paid work. I found no evidence here on whether scheduled rest keeps wellbeing practices from becoming stressful, which is the seed's claim. The evidence is correlational. I did not open Sonnentag 2018 directly.

**Plain words**
Mentally leaving work, relaxing and doing something that builds skill are linked with feeling better. Rest that is planned in tends to be rest you get.

**For example**
Protect one evening a week with no tasks, and note afterwards whether you were thinking about work.
