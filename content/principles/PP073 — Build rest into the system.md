> **Principle 73** — Build rest into the system

Rest needn't wait until the work is done. Sonnentag describes a “recovery paradox”: high work stress can make detaching, exercising and sleeping well harder just when recovery is needed ([2018 review, abstract, p. 169](https://www.helsinki.fi/assets/drupal/2024-04/Sonnentag.pdf)). Zijlstra and colleagues likewise treat recovery as an ongoing adjustment of one's state, rather than a single completed event ([2014 conceptual paper, abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/smi.2604)).

### What the studies support

A meta-analysis of 91 samples involving 38,124 employees found detachment from work associated with less exhaustion, better sleep and greater life satisfaction. These are correlations, with substantial variation between studies, not proof that a particular rest schedule produces those gains ([Wendsche and Lohmann-Haislah, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28133454/)).

A separate meta-analysis of experimental and quasi-experimental micro-break studies found small increases in self-reported vigour and reductions in self-reported fatigue, but no statistically significant overall performance gain. Its breaks lasted no more than ten minutes; it does not establish an ideal duration for everyone ([Albulescu et al., 2022, abstract and introduction](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)).

(Sonnentag, 2018; Zijlstra et al., 2014.)

### Plain words

An empty slot on the calendar is only a chance to rest, not evidence that you have recovered. Notice how you feel afterwards rather than turning rest into another target to complete.

### For example

Optional application (Affect; Overt behaviour): reserve one short break away from the task. Compare your fatigue before and after it; try a different activity or timing next time if it did not help. Repeat what leaves you more restored. Applying work-recovery research to flourishing practices is a practical analogy, not a tested programme.
