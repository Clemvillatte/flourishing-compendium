> **Principle 53** — Learn to manage perfectionism: "good enough" enables action

The psychology here is more specific than the blanket claim that perfectionism causes procrastination. A review of the student literature states the consensus: "perfectionism in fact has two higher-order dimensions; (1) perfectionistic strivings, i.e., setting high standards and expecting no less than perfection from yourself, and (2) perfectionistic concerns, i.e., being highly self-critical and overly concerned about others' perception of you, and having a hard time enjoying your achievements" ([Rozental et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8965624/)). The meta-analysis behind that picture reported a weak negative correlation between perfectionistic strivings and procrastination (r = −0.22) and a weak positive correlation between perfectionistic concerns and procrastination (r = 0.23) ([Sirois et al., 2017, as reported in Rozental et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8965624/)). The correlations are small, and high standards alone were linked to somewhat less delay ([Sirois et al., 2017, as reported in Rozental et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8965624/)).

A neighbouring literature studies a different pair of constructs, this one about choosing ([Schwartz et al., 2002](https://bschwartz.domains.swarthmore.edu/maximizing.pdf)). Schwartz and colleagues drew the line between two postures:

> "Maximizers desire the best possible result; satisficers desire a result that is good enough to meet some criterion." (Schwartz et al., 2002, Study 1 Discussion, [JPSP 83(5), author PDF](https://bschwartz.domains.swarthmore.edu/maximizing.pdf))

Of the maximizer's stance the paper says: "the question the maximizer is asking him- or herself is not 'is this a good outcome?' but 'is this the best outcome?'" ([Schwartz et al., 2002, introduction](https://bschwartz.domains.swarthmore.edu/maximizing.pdf)). Across seven samples, "maximizers reported significantly less life satisfaction, happiness, optimism, and self-esteem, and significantly more regret and depression, than did satisficers" ([Schwartz et al., 2002, Study 1 Discussion](https://bschwartz.domains.swarthmore.edu/maximizing.pdf)). Perfectionism dimensions and maximizing are related topics, not interchangeable constructs; the two literatures are reported here side by side without equating them.

**Plain words** The studies separate two things people call perfectionism, and the correlations are small, so these are tendencies, not types: striving for high standards went with somewhat less procrastination, and worrying about mistakes and others' judgment went with somewhat more ([Sirois et al., 2017, as reported in Rozental et al., 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8965624/)). The Schwartz studies are about a different pair of constructs, this time about choosing: maximizers seek the best option, satisficers stop at good enough, and across seven samples maximizers reported more regret and less satisfaction ([Schwartz et al., 2002](https://bschwartz.domains.swarthmore.edu/maximizing.pdf)).

*For example (compendium exercise):* For a stalled task, write down what "done" means before starting: the criteria that make it acceptable. Ship at that threshold and revise after feedback.
