> **Principle 53** — Learn to manage perfectionism: "good enough" enables action

The psychology here is more specific than "perfectionism causes procrastination". Perfectionism has two dimensions, and they pull in opposite directions. A review of the student literature states the consensus: "perfectionism in fact has two higher-order dimensions; (1) perfectionistic strivings, i.e., setting high standards and expecting no less than perfection from yourself, and (2) perfectionistic concerns, i.e., being highly self-critical and overly concerned about others' perception of you, and having a hard time enjoying your achievements" (Rozental et al., 2022). The meta-analysis behind that picture found that "perfectionistic strivings had a weak negative correlation with procrastination, r = −0.22, while perfectionistic concerns had a weak positive correlation with procrastination, r = 0.23" (Sirois et al., 2017, as reported there). High standards alone were linked to somewhat less delay; the self-critical, exposure-fearing side to more.

The satisficing literature gives the replacement standard. Schwartz and colleagues drew the line between the two postures:

> "Maximizers desire the best possible result; satisficers desire a result that is good enough to meet some criterion." (Schwartz et al., 2002, JPSP 83(5), author PDF)

The maximizer's question keeps the search open and the task unstarted: "the question the maximizer is asking him- or herself is not 'is this a good outcome?' but 'is this the best outcome?'". Across seven samples, "maximizers reported significantly less life satisfaction, happiness, optimism, and self-esteem, and significantly more regret and depression, than did satisficers".

**Plain words** There are two versions of high standards. One says "make it excellent" and starts work. The other says "only the best will do, and everyone will see it if it isn't" and cannot start, because the finish line keeps moving. The delay lives in the second one. The fix is to set the finish line before starting: write down the criteria that count as done, meet them, ship, and revise from real feedback. Ambition can stay; the open-ended search cannot.

*For example (imagined):* A writer stuck rewriting one paragraph for weeks writes a definition of done ("every section drafted, intro included"), produces the ugly full draft in two days, and revises it once someone has actually read it.

## Practice

- For a stalled task, write down what "done" means before starting: the criteria that make it acceptable. Ship at that threshold and revise after feedback. If the standard cannot be stated, the standard is the problem.
