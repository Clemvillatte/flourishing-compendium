> **Principle 35** — Recognise the representativeness heuristic: similarity is mistaken for probability

Tversky and Kahneman describe the heuristic in these terms: "people typically rely on the representativeness heuristic, in which probabilities are evaluated by the degree to which A is representative of B, that is, by the degree to which A resembles B" ([Tversky & Kahneman, 1974, Science 185, p. 1124](https://stanford.edu/~jlmcc/Presentations/tversky_kahneman_1974.pdf)). Their illustration is Steve, described as shy, tidy and detail-minded. Asked whether he is more likely a farmer or a librarian, people rank the jobs by how well Steve fits each stereotype.

The error is that fit and likelihood are separate questions. As the same paper puts it, "the fact that there are many more farmers than librarians in the population should enter into any reasonable estimate of the probability that Steve is a librarian rather than a farmer" (p. 1124). The stereotype ignores that fact (see [[CO020 — Know the base rate neglect — you ignore background frequencies|base-rate neglect]]), because the number of farmers does nothing to Steve's resemblance to a librarian.

**Plain words** Steve sounds like a librarian, so you say he probably is one. Sounding like a librarian is a fact about the description, though. How many librarians exist compared with farmers is a fact about the world, and it belongs in your answer too. Before trusting a good fit, ask how common the thing is.

*For example:* Imagine a hiring panel that meets a candidate who looks like the team's picture of an engineer and rates her highly on that basis alone. Before the interview, someone could ask how often people with this profile succeed in the job, using past hires as evidence, and let that figure move the rating.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Representativeness heuristic|Representativeness heuristic]]**, similarity judgments substitute for probability judgments (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974; Kahneman & Tversky, 1973)
- **[[Conjunction fallacy|Conjunction fallacy]]**, judging a conjunction (Linda is a feminist and a bank teller) as more probable than a component (Linda is a bank teller) (Tversky & Kahneman, 1983)

## Philosophy parallels

- **The typical vs the actual** (Plato, Republic)
- **Appearance vs reality** (Kant, Critique of Pure Reason)

## Practice

- When judging probability, ask: "am I judging by how typical this seems or by actual frequencies?" Deliberately check the base rate.
