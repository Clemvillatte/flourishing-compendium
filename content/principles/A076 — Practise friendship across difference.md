> **Principle 76** — Practise friendship across difference

Aristotle notes that friendships can form between people of different ranks and capacities, but they require adaptation. The friendship of a parent and child, a teacher and student, an elder and younger person has its own form. Do not demand sameness; adapt to the difference.

(NE VIII.7, VIII.8, VIII.11.)

### Aristotle's case

> "Some mothers put their infants out to nurse, and though knowing and loving them, do not ask to be loved by them in return, if it be impossible to have this as well, but are content if they see them prospering; they retain their own love for them even though the children, not knowing them, cannot render them any part of what is due to a mother." — Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VIII.8, 1159a (Ross trans., [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0054%3Abekker+page%3D1159a))

That is the shape of the thing: love that does not invoice the other for what they cannot yet, or ever, pay back.

### What adaptation looks like

The gap is the material, not the obstacle. A friendship across rank or age runs on an exchange neither could have with a peer: what the elder has seen, what the younger is about to see. These friendships die when one side demands the symmetry of equals, same interests, same dues, same standing.

**Plain words**
Cross-gap friendships ask two things a same-age friendship does not: accept an exchange that is lopsided on purpose, and give up the ledger. The grandmother is not shortchanged because the teenager brings energy and appetite where she brings memory; that asymmetry is the trade. The practice is to ask what this person can give that a peer cannot, and what you can give that they cannot get elsewhere, and to be content when the two columns refuse to match.

*For example:* A grandmother and teenager who bond over cooking get along because each lets the other be what they are.
