> **Principle 56** — Convert passive joy into active

In Part III's definitions, we act insofar as an effect can be understood through our nature alone; we are passive insofar as we are only a partial cause. III, P1 connects the distinction with ideas: "In so far as it has adequate ideas it is necessarily active, and in so far as it has inadequate ideas, it is necessarily passive." So a joy is active or passive by the ideas it springs from, not by its object. V, P4's note gives the conversion:

> "all appetites or desires are only passions, in so far as they spring from inadequate ideas; the same results are accredited to virtue, when they are aroused or generated by adequate ideas." (Ethics V, P4 note, [Elwes](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800))

The note's own example: the desire that others live after one's own fashion is "ambition" in a man not guided by reason, and "piety", an activity or virtue, in a man who lives by the dictates of reason. One line of stability belongs to the reason-based side: "an emotion which springs from reason is necessarily referred to the common properties of things... which we always regard as present... Wherefore an emotion of this kind always remains the same" (V, P7 proof). The page's exercise drawn from the account: for a joy that arrived with inadequate ideas attached, examine those ideas; the desire itself can stay, and become an activity.

**Plain words**
The same desire can come with a confused understanding or a clear one. Spinoza connects passivity with inadequate ideas and activity with adequate ideas. For this page's exercise, examine why you are pleased and which parts of your explanation you actually understand.

*For example:* Authored examination drawn from the account: someone pleased by praise for a presentation lists which claims they actually understand and checks one they had merely repeated.
