> **Principle 73** — Recognise self and culture conflict: bicultural and marginalised selves

> Bicultural Identity Integration (BII), or the degree to which a bicultural individual perceives his/her two cultural identities as "compatible" versus "oppositional"

(Benet-Martínez and Haritatos, "Bicultural Identity Integration (BII): Components and Psychosocial Antecedents," Journal of Personality 73(4), 2005, 1015-1049, abstract; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15958143/).)

In a sample of Chinese American biculturals, the authors found that "variations in BII do not define a uniform phenomenon," and instead cover two separate constructs: perceived distance (versus overlap) and perceived conflict (versus harmony) between a person's two cultural identities. So conflict is something some bicultural people perceive more than others, and the entry's claim that straddling cultures always means conflicting definitions of self goes beyond this study.

The second strand concerns discrimination. A meta-analysis of 134 samples found that "perceived discrimination has a significant negative effect on both mental and physical health" (Pascoe and Smart Richman, Psychological Bulletin 135(4), 2009, abstract; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19586161/)). That paper is about health outcomes. It does not address self-esteem or identity, which is what the entry's parallel says.

That being bicultural is "a strength" or "a skill set" is the entry's position, and neither abstract states it.

**Plain words** People who live between cultures do not all feel pulled apart. How much conflict they feel varies, and so does the stress that comes with discrimination. Support that fits the person's situation is more useful than treating the situation as a flaw.

*For example:* A daughter is called too direct at her family's table and too deferential at work. Her difficulty comes from two sets of norms, and she is not failing at either.

## Psychology parallels

- **Bicultural identity integration**, bicultural individuals can integrate multiple cultural identities (Benet-Martínez & Haritatos, 2005; Huynh et al., 2011)
- **Discrimination and self**, experiences of discrimination affect self-esteem and identity (Major & O'Brien, 2005; Pascoe & Smart Richman, 2009)
- **Bicultural identity can be a strength**, bicultural individuals can develop integrative, flexible identities (Benet-Martínez et al., 2002; Cheng et al., 2006)

## Practice

- If you are bicultural or navigating multiple cultural selves, work on integration rather than choosing one. Seek others who share this experience. Recognise bicultural identity as a strength, not a problem.
