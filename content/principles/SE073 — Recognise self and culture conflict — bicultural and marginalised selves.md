> **Principle 73** — Recognise self and culture conflict: bicultural and marginalised selves

People who straddle multiple cultures (bicultural, immigrant, marginalised) face specific self-challenges: navigating conflicting cultural definitions of self, experiencing discrimination, and integrating multiple identities. This is not pathology; it is a specific challenge requiring specific support.

**Plain words** Living between cultures — immigrant, bicultural, marginalised — means holding two or more definitions of a good self that genuinely conflict. That's not confusion to be fixed; it's a real skill set, with real costs. Bicultural people aren't torn between worlds. They're fluent in more than one.

*For example:* The daughter who is "too direct" in the family's culture and "too deferential" in her workplace's isn't failing at either. She's operating in two languages of respect, and both audiences are grading her by only one. The exhaustion is real; so is the competence.
## Psychology parallels

- **Bicultural identity integration**, bicultural individuals can integrate multiple cultural identities (Benet-Martínez & Haritatos, 2005; Huynh et al., 2011)
- **Discrimination and self**, experiences of discrimination affect self-esteem and identity (Major & O'Brien, 2005; Pascoe & Smart Richman, 2009)
- **Bicultural identity can be a strength**, bicultural individuals can develop integrative, flexible identities (Benet-Martínez et al., 2002; Cheng et al., 2006)

## Practice

- If you are bicultural or navigating multiple cultural selves, work on integration rather than choosing one. Seek others who share this experience. Recognise bicultural identity as a strength, not a problem.
