> **Principle 74** — Use bicultural identity integration as a strength

Benet-Martínez and colleagues found that Chinese American biculturals who perceived their two cultural identities as compatible responded in culturally congruent ways to cultural cues, while those who perceived them as oppositional showed a reverse priming effect. ([Benet-Martínez et al., 2002](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ653127)) A later review concludes that integrating bicultural identities is central to psychological adjustment in immigration-based and globalization-based acculturation. ([Chen, 2015](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1111/ajsp.12088)) The creativity link is context-dependent: among 177 Chinese Americans, greater bicultural identity blendedness predicted domain-general creativity in bicultural but not monocultural contexts, mediated by ideational fluency. ([Saad et al., 2013](https://www.academia.edu/9065434/Saad_Damian_Benet_Martinez_Moons_and_Robins_2013_))

> "Results found that Chinese American biculturals who perceived their cultural identities as compatible responded in culturally congruent ways to cultural cues, while those who perceived their cultural identities as oppositional exhibited a reverse priming effect." ([Benet-Martínez et al., 2002, abstract as carried by ERIC EJ653127](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ653127))

**Plain words** The question this principle turns on is whether your two cultures feel like they live in the same house or in two separate flats. When they share the house, a cue from either culture calls out a matching response, and the switching itself looks like a skill rather than a strain. One research caveat matters: the creativity payoff appeared when both cultures were active at once, not in settings where only one was.

**For example (imagined):** At a family wedding, the grandchild of immigrants moves between the elders' language and the friends' banter without missing a step. The same person is direct in one room and indirect in the next, and the two rooms do not fight inside them.
