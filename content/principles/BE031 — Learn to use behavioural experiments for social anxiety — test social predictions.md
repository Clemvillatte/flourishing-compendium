> **Principle 31** — Learn to use behavioural experiments for social anxiety: test social predictions

Social anxiety runs on predictions about being judged, rejected, or humiliated, and the behavioural experiment treats those predictions as testable claims. The Oxford Guide to Behavioural Experiments gives the operational definition:

> "Behavioural experiments are planned experiential activities, based on experimentation or observation, which are undertaken by patients in or between cognitive therapy sessions. Their design is derived directly from a cognitive formulation of the problem, and their primary purpose is to obtain new information which may help to: test the validity of the patients' existing beliefs about themselves, others, and the world" ([Bennett-Levy et al., Oxford Guide to Behavioural Experiments in Cognitive Therapy, 2004](http://cabct.hr/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Oxford-Guide-to-Behavioural-Experiments-in-Cognitive-Therapy-Bennett-Levy-J-Butler-G-Fennell-M-Hackman-A-Mueller-M-Westbrook-D-compressed.pdf))

In the cognitive therapy of social anxiety disorder, the experiment has a specific shape. The treatment component list describes "behavioural experiments to test negative beliefs by dropping safety behaviours and focusing attention externally in social situations or purposefully displaying feared behaviours or signs of anxiety (decatastrophising)" ([Warnock-Parkes et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7411446/)). An early exercise makes the mechanism visible: the patient has two conversations with the same stranger, one while monitoring their own performance and using safety behaviours such as preparing what to say, one while focusing externally and dropping them, then compares how the two actually went.

**Plain words** Social anxiety hands you a verdict before the event: you will go red, they will judge, it will be humiliating. The experiment turns the verdict back into a bet with terms. Write down what you predict and what would count as it coming true; then enter the situation with your safety behaviours switched off and your attention on the other person; then write down what actually happened. The exercise can be run while nervous. What it needs is the written prediction and the written outcome.

*For example* Imagined: someone predicts that pausing mid-sentence will read as stupidity, writes the prediction with a confidence rating, then deliberately pauses twice in a conversation and records what the other person actually did. The follow-up experiment is planned from what the record shows.

## Practice

- Test social predictions: when you predict "I will say something stupid and they will laugh," deliberately enter the situation and observe. Use video feedback (record and watch yourself) to calibrate your self-perception.
