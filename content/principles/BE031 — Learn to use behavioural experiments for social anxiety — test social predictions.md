> **Principle 31** — Learn to use behavioural experiments for social anxiety: test social predictions

Social anxiety runs on predictions about being judged, rejected, or humiliated, and the behavioural experiment treats those predictions as testable claims. The Oxford Guide to Behavioural Experiments gives the operational definition:

> "Behavioural experiments are planned experiential activities, based on experimentation or observation, which are undertaken by patients in or between cognitive therapy sessions." ([Bennett-Levy et al., Oxford Guide to Behavioural Experiments in Cognitive Therapy, 2004, ch. 1, p. 8](http://cabct.hr/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/Oxford-Guide-to-Behavioural-Experiments-in-Cognitive-Therapy-Bennett-Levy-J-Butler-G-Fennell-M-Hackman-A-Mueller-M-Westbrook-D-compressed.pdf))

Their design, the guide continues, comes from a cognitive formulation of the problem, and the purpose is new information that may help test existing beliefs and construct or test more adaptive ones. In the cognitive therapy of social anxiety disorder, the experiment has a specific shape. The treatment component list describes "Behavioural experiments to test negative beliefs by dropping safety behaviours and focusing attention externally in social situations or purposefully displaying feared behaviours or signs of anxiety (decatastrophising)" ([Warnock-Parkes et al., 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7411446/)). An early exercise makes the mechanism visible: the patient has two conversations with the same stranger, one while monitoring their own performance and using safety behaviours such as preparing what to say, one while focusing externally and dropping them, then compares how the two actually went.

**Plain words** Before a social situation, write down a specific prediction and decide what observable response would count as it happening. Afterwards, compare the prediction with what occurred. In therapy, the experiment may also involve dropping a safety behaviour and directing attention towards the conversation.

*For example* Imagined: someone predicts that pausing mid-sentence will make the other person roll their eyes or end the conversation. They plan an experiment with their therapist, then record whether either response occurs.

## Practice

- Test social predictions: when you predict "I will say something stupid and they will laugh," deliberately enter the situation and observe. Use video feedback (record and watch yourself) to calibrate your self-perception.
