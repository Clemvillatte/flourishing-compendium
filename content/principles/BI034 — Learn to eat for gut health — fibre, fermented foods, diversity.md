> **Principle 34** — Learn to eat for gut health: fibre, fermented foods, diversity

The strongest trial evidence concerns the fermented side of the principle. In the Stanford trial, 36 healthy adults were randomly assigned to a 10-week diet high in either fermented foods or fibre ([Stanford Medicine](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2021/07/fermented-food-diet-increases-microbiome-diversity-lowers-inflammation.html)). The fermented group, eating yogurt, kefir, fermented cottage cheese, kimchi and other fermented vegetables, vegetable brine drinks and kombucha, saw overall microbial diversity rise, with stronger effects from larger servings, while 19 inflammatory proteins fell and four types of immune cells showed less activation. In the high-fibre group, by contrast, none of those 19 proteins decreased, and the diversity of their gut microbes remained stable on average ([Stanford Medicine](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2021/07/fermented-food-diet-increases-microbiome-diversity-lowers-inflammation.html)).

The plant-diversity rule comes from the American Gut Project: participants who reported eating more than 30 different plant types in an average week had more diverse gut microbiomes than those eating 10 or fewer, in a citizen-science dataset of thousands of stool samples ([McDonald et al. 2018](https://msystems.asm.org/content/3/3/e00031-18)). The mood side of the principle stays hedged: the review literature describes the microbiota-gut-brain axis as a real communication system, through immune pathways, tryptophan metabolism and the vagus nerve, with the gut microbiota implicated in psychiatric and neurological conditions while mechanisms and human studies continue ([Cryan et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31460832/)).

> "Eating foods such as yogurt, kefir, fermented cottage cheese, kimchi and other fermented vegetables, vegetable brine drinks, and kombucha tea led to an increase in overall microbial diversity, with stronger effects from larger servings." (Stanford Medicine, on Wastyk et al. 2021, [Cell](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2021/07/fermented-food-diet-increases-microbiome-diversity-lowers-inflammation.html))

**Plain words**
The trial and the survey data agree with the rule of thumb, with one surprise: over ten weeks in healthy adults, the fermented foods did more measurable work than the fibre. The diversity habit survives, because about thirty different plants a week is the number the American Gut Project attached to more diverse microbiomes ([McDonald et al. 2018](https://msystems.asm.org/content/3/3/e00031-18)), and the mood claim stays what the review literature says it is: a real communication axis under active study, not a settled lever ([Cryan et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31460832/)).

*For example:* An imagined case: someone who takes a probiotic capsule daily but eats the same ten plants every week swaps the capsule habit for a shopping habit: kimchi and yogurt in the fridge, thirty different plants on the list. The opened trials concern foods and whole diets; the capsule was not what they tested.

## Psychology parallels

- **Gut microbiome and mental health**, microbiome composition is associated with depression and anxiety (Cryan et al., 2019; Chevalier et al., 2020)
- **Fibre and fermented foods support gut health**, these foods promote a diverse, healthy microbiome (Cryan et al., 2019; Mörkl et al., 2018)
- **Note: probiotic evidence for mood is mixed**, and the specific causal pathways are still being established (Nikolova et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2019)

## Practice

- Eat for gut health: fermented foods daily (yogurt, kefir, kimchi, other fermented vegetables) and aim for dietary diversity — about thirty different plant types a week is the American Gut Project's marker of more diverse microbiomes ([McDonald et al. 2018](https://msystems.asm.org/content/3/3/e00031-18)). Fibre stays part of the pattern; the mood link is under study, not settled ([Cryan et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31460832/)).
