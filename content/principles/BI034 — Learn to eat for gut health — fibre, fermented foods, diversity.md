> **Principle 34** — Learn to eat for gut health: fibre, fermented foods, diversity

In a Stanford trial, 36 healthy adults were randomly assigned to a 10-week diet high in either fermented foods or fibre ([Stanford Medicine](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2021/07/fermented-food-diet-increases-microbiome-diversity-lowers-inflammation.html)). The fermented group, eating yogurt, kefir, fermented cottage cheese, kimchi and other fermented vegetables, vegetable brine drinks and kombucha, saw overall microbial diversity rise, with stronger effects from larger servings, while 19 inflammatory proteins fell and four types of immune cells showed less activation. In the high-fibre group, by contrast, none of those 19 proteins decreased, and the diversity of their gut microbes remained stable on average ([Stanford Medicine](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2021/07/fermented-food-diet-increases-microbiome-diversity-lowers-inflammation.html)).

The plant-diversity association comes from the American Gut Project: in a citizen-science dataset of thousands of stool samples, participants who reported eating more than 30 different plant types in an average week carried gut communities with more short-chain-fatty-acid fermenting microbes, and fewer genes that confer antibiotic resistance, than participants eating 10 or fewer ([McDonald et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5954204/)). The mood side of the principle stays hedged: the review literature describes the microbiota-gut-brain axis as a real communication system, through the immune system, tryptophan metabolism and the vagus nerve, with research on psychiatric and neurological disorders still developing ([Cryan et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31460832/)).

> "Eating foods such as yogurt, kefir, fermented cottage cheese, kimchi and other fermented vegetables, vegetable brine drinks, and kombucha tea led to an increase in overall microbial diversity, with stronger effects from larger servings." (Stanford Medicine, on Wastyk et al. 2021, [Cell](https://med.stanford.edu/news/all-news/2021/07/fermented-food-diet-increases-microbiome-diversity-lowers-inflammation.html))

**Plain words**
The diet trial measured gut microbes and inflammatory markers, not depression. Its results do not establish that eating yogurt treats a mood disorder. The plant-food findings were observational, so they do not establish what changing your own diet would do.

*For example:* An imagined shopper adds a vegetable they rarely buy to their usual shopping list and tries yogurt alongside their usual foods.

## Psychology parallels

- **Gut microbiome and mental health**, microbiome composition is associated with depression and anxiety (Cryan et al., 2019; Chevalier et al., 2020)
- **Fibre and fermented foods support gut health**, these foods promote a diverse, healthy microbiome (Cryan et al., 2019; Mörkl et al., 2018)
- **Note: probiotic evidence for mood is mixed**, and the specific causal pathways are still being established (Nikolova et al., 2019; Liu et al., 2019)

## Practice

- Eat for gut health: fermented foods daily (yogurt, kefir, kimchi, other fermented vegetables) and aim for dietary variety; in the American Gut Project's data, more than 30 different plant types a week marked more short-chain-fatty-acid fermenters and fewer antibiotic-resistance genes than 10 or fewer ([McDonald et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5954204/)). Fibre stays part of the pattern; the mood link is under study, not settled ([Cryan et al. 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31460832/)).
