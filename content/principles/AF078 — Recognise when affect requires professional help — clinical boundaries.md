> **Principle 78** — Recognise when affect requires professional help: clinical boundaries

The American Psychiatric Association describes depression as follows:

> "Depression (major depressive disorder) is a common and serious mental disorder that negatively affects how you feel, think, act, and perceive the world." ([APA, What Is Depression?](https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/depression/what-is-depression))

On how the diagnosis is made:

> "To diagnose depression, a healthcare professional will conduct a thorough diagnostic evaluation that includes a comprehensive interview to discuss your symptoms in addition to your personal, medical and family histories." ([APA, same page](https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/depression/what-is-depression))

The same page describes depression as among the most treatable of mental disorders and names seeing your family physician or psychiatrist as a first step if you are experiencing symptoms. The page concerns depression specifically; the boundary-reading here is extended to other persistent, impairing patterns as a matter of prudence, not as a diagnostic claim.

**Plain words** There is a wrong order people get stuck in: they wait until they are sure it is bad enough, and being sure is exactly what the problem takes away. The right order runs the other way: describe what is happening, the weeks of low mood, the work getting hard, and let someone whose job it is sort out what to call it. Self-directed practices have a place, but next to that conversation, not instead of it.

*For example:* Your mood has been low and work has become difficult. Describe those changes when arranging an evaluation, rather than deciding the diagnosis yourself.

## Practice

- If your affective pattern is persistent, impairing, or dangerous, seek professional evaluation. Self-directed practices are complements, not substitutes, for treatment.
