> **Principle 15** — Study the commanding self as your specific opponent

> And I do not acquit myself. Indeed, the soul is a persistent enjoiner of evil, except those upon which my Lord has mercy. Indeed, my Lord is Forgiving and Merciful.

(Qur'an 12:53, Sahih International translation, as printed in [Qur'an Codex, "Stations of the Soul"](https://www.qurancodex.com/en/atlas/nefs-mertebeleri). The Arabic text is confirmed at [Quran.com 12:53](https://quran.com/12:53), whose own English rendering differs in wording: "for indeed the soul is ever inclined to evil, except those shown mercy by my Lord.")

This verse is the root of the term nafs al-ammāra. The same page explains that ammāra is an intensive form from the root meaning "to command," which signals a self that commands evil "persistently, continuously, intensely." It also lists the Qur'an's three named states of the self: the commanding self (12:53), the self-reproaching self (75:2) and the soul at peace (89:27), and notes that Sufi writers extended these to a seven-stage scheme.

The verse is spoken by Joseph, and it presents the commanding self as a standing tendency of the human soul that mercy can overcome. The entry's account of the self as a "specific, adaptive negotiator" with disguises such as piety, victimhood, fatigue and charm is its own development, and it was not found in the sources opened. Al-Ghazali's Ihyā' takes the verse as the root of the term: in Book 21 he writes that when the soul stops opposing the appetites and submits to them, it is called "the soul that commands to evil" (al-nafs al-ammāra), citing Joseph's words at Quran 12:53.

> "But if the soul leaves its opposition and becomes submissive and obedient to the demands of the fleshly appetites and the invitations of Satan, it is called 'the soul that commands to evil.'" (al-Ghazali, Ihyā' ʿulūm al-dīn, Book 21, The Marvels of the Heart, Skellie trans., [Internet Archive](https://ia801500.us.archive.org/20/items/GhazaliTheMarvelsOfTheHeartSkellie_201512/TheMarvelsOfTheHeart.pdf))

**Plain words** The Sufi tradition takes a verse from the Qur'an and names a part of the self that keeps urging a person toward what they know is not good. Naming it makes it something to watch, instead of a general sense of weakness. How it behaves, and in what costumes, is for each person to observe.

*For example:* Someone notices that the voice saying "I deserve this treat" is loudest right after they have done something admirable, and stops being fooled by the timing.
