> **Principle 72** — Learn by doing, not only by reading

The wheelwright can't put his knack into words, and books are the dregs of the ancients. Balance study with direct, hands-on experience.

(Zhuangzi 13.)

### The wheelwright's argument

The story places the duke's reading above the wheelwright's work, and the work gets the better of the exchange:

> "You can't put it into words, and yet there's a knack to it somehow. I can't teach it to my son, and he can't learn it from me." (Zhuangzi 13, tr. Burton Watson, [Wikiquote](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Zhuangzi))

The conclusion the wheelwright draws:

> "When the men of old died, they took with them the things that couldn't be handed down. So what you are reading there must be nothing but the chaff and dregs of the men of old." (Zhuangzi 13, tr. Burton Watson, [Wikiquote](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Zhuangzi))

The argument is about transmission, not about reading as a waste of time. A craft knowledge lives in timing and touch, dies with its holder, and has to be rebuilt by each person in their own practice.

**Plain words** A book can hand you the moves that survive words, but the knack that lives in hands and rhythm has to be rebuilt by each person in their own practice. Reading about a craft is a map of the terrain; walking the terrain is what makes feet know the ground. The two are complementary, and confusing the map for the ground is the error the story mocks.

*For example:* You can read every swimming manual ever written — the first time the water takes you, you'll find out which knowledge you actually have.
