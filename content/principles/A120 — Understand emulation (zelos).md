> **Principle 120** — Understand emulation (zelos)

The Rhetoric closes its catalogue of the emotions with emulation, defined against envy:

> "Emulation is pain caused by seeing the presence, in persons whose nature is like our own, of good things that are highly valued and are possible for ourselves to acquire; but it is felt not because others have these goods, but because we have not got them ourselves. It is therefore a good feeling felt by good persons, whereas envy is a bad feeling felt by bad persons. Emulation makes us take steps to secure the good things in question, envy makes us take steps to stop our neighbour having them." ([Rhetoric II.11, Rhys Roberts translation, MIT Classics](http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/rhetoric.2.ii.html))

Two features do the work: the goods must be "possible for ourselves to acquire" ("no one aspires to things which appear impossible"), and the comparison is with "persons whose nature is like our own", which is why it is "felt by the young and by persons of lofty disposition", and why its objects are those who have the honoured goods, courage, wisdom, office, "those whom many people wish to be like".

The compendium's exercise: when a comparison bites, write down the good thing wanted, whether it can be acquired, and the next step toward it.

**Plain words** Aristotle calls emulation the good twin of envy. Both hurt at not having what another has. Emulation takes a step to get the thing; envy takes a step to stop the other having it. And it only works for things you could actually get.

*For example (imagined):* A junior designer sees a colleague's polished portfolio and writes down one technique from it to study that week.
