> **Principle 6** — Understand that wanting and liking are different systems

Berridge and Robinson write that rewards are "liked" and "wanted", and that the circuitry of wanting "is dissociable from circuitry that mediates the degree to which it is 'liked'" ([Berridge & Robinson, 2016, American Psychologist 71, 670-679](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27977239/)). Wanting, or incentive salience, is "generated by large and robust neural systems that include mesolimbic dopamine". Liking is "mediated by smaller and fragile neural systems, and is not dependent on dopamine". Their incentive-sensitization theory proposes that addiction is "excessive amplification specifically of psychological 'wanting'" without a matching rise in liking. The abstract does not name opioids as the liking system, so that detail, and the claim that the dissociation explains habits like scrolling, go beyond what was opened.

Separately, [Schultz, Dayan and Montague (1997)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9054347/) report dopamine neurons in the primate whose output "apparently signals changes or errors in the predictions of future salient and rewarding events". That concerns prediction error, which is a related but different dopamine finding.

(Berridge & Robinson, 2016.)

**Plain words** Wanting something and enjoying it can come apart, and the research on addiction suggests a strong pull toward something does not guarantee pleasure from it. The next time an urge is strong, treat it as information about the wanting system, not as proof of what you will enjoy.

For example (imagined): you pick up your phone with no particular anticipation, and a minute later notice you are not enjoying it.

## Practice

- When you notice wanting something you will not actually enjoy (scrolling, sugar, compulsive behaviour), recognise this is the wanting system, not the liking system. Do not treat the urge as evidence of genuine value.
