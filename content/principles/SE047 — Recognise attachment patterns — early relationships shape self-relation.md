> **Principle 47** — Recognise attachment patterns: early relationships shape self-relation

Attachment research measures adult patterns along two dimensions of insecurity, attachment anxiety and avoidance; people low on both are considered secure. What security looks like in practice, in Shaver and Mikulincer's review:

> "Security-based strategies also include acknowledging and expressing distress without becoming emotionally disorganized, relying comfortably and gratefully on others' support, and being able to solve most problems calmly and effectively." (Shaver & Mikulincer, "Adult Attachment and Cognitive and Affective Reactions to Positive and Negative Events", Social and Personality Psychology Compass 2, 2008, full text)

A later review states the broader claim as theory: "According to attachment theory, a sense of attachment security provides a foundation for mental health, social adjustment, and psychological thriving" (Mikulincer & Shaver, 2015, abstract). On revision: Roisman, Padrón, Sroufe and Egeland's 23-year longitudinal study found that prospectively defined earned-secures, those classified insecure in infancy and secure later, "did indeed go on to have success in their close relationships". The same study adds a caution: retrospectively defined earned-secures were not more likely than continuous-secures to have been anxiously attached in infancy, so the popular reading of "earned security" is more complicated than it looks.

**Plain words**
The research frames the patterns as two dials, anxiety and avoidance, with security as both dials low. The secure strategy the reviewers describe is not the absence of distress; it is distress acknowledged without falling apart, support used without shame, problems solved without panic. The longitudinal finding on change is real but specific: infants classified insecure who later became secure did well in their close relationships.

*For example:* Imagine the person who reads a four-hour gap between texts as abandonment. She is not responding to the text; she is responding to a childhood where warmth could vanish without warning. Naming that, "that's my pattern, not the situation", is what keeps her from sending the third follow-up.

## Practice

- Notice your attachment pattern: do you tend toward anxiety (cling, worry) or avoidance (distance, withdraw)? Recognise this as a learned pattern, not a fixed trait. Earned security is possible through relationships and work.
