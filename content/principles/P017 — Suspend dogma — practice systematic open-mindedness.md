> **Principle 17** — Suspend dogma: practice systematic open-mindedness

Sextus defines the sceptic's ability and its results:

> "Scepticism is an ability, or mental attitude, which opposes appearances to judgments in any way whatsoever, with the result that, owing to the equipollence of the objects and reasons thus opposed, we are brought firstly to a state of mental suspense and next to a state of 'unperturbedness' or quietude." (Sextus Empiricus, Outlines of Pyrrhonism I.8, Bury trans., [selection](https://users.manchester.edu/Facstaff/SSNaragon/Online/texts/316/Sextus%20Empiricus,%20Pyrrhonism.pdf))

> "'Suspense' is a state of mental rest owing to which we neither deny nor affirm anything. 'Quietude' is an untroubled and tranquil condition of soul." (Outlines of Pyrrhonism I.9-10)

And he names the motive honestly:

> "The originating cause of Scepticism is, we say, the hope of attaining quietude." (I.12)

> "So, too, the Sceptics were in hopes of gaining quietude by means of a decision regarding the disparity of the objects of sense and of thought, and being unable to effect this they suspended judgment; and they found that quietude, as if by chance, followed upon their suspense, even as a shadow follows its substance." (I.12)

Peirce gives the other side of the same practice, the struggle that ends dogma:

> "The irritation of doubt causes a struggle to attain a state of belief. I shall term this struggle inquiry, though it must be admitted that this is sometimes not a very apt designation." (Peirce, "The Fixation of Belief," 1877, in The Essential Peirce vol. 1, [owner's library PDF](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1L3jvBMstbqfeNxOVCmm-z1ZRB56qw7ko/view))

> "The irritation of doubt is the only immediate motive for the struggle to attain belief." ("The Fixation of Belief")

And his description of what the a priori method does to inquiry:

> "It makes of inquiry something similar to the development of taste; but taste, unfortunately, is always more or less a matter of fashion, and accordingly metaphysicians have never come to any fixed agreement." ("The Fixation of Belief")

(Sextus Empiricus, Outlines of Pyrrhonism I; Peirce, "The Fixation of Belief," 1877.)

**Plain words** Sextus' terms are precise: equipollence means no one of the conflicting judgments takes precedence; suspense is the state of mental rest in which we neither deny nor affirm. Peirce supplies the engine: doubt is the only immediate motive for the struggle to attain belief, and he calls that struggle inquiry. Read together, the two say that the open question is not a failure to have decided; it is the working state of inquiry, and the quietude Sextus describes is what follows the suspense, not what precedes it.

*For example:* Imagine facing a decision where the considerations on each side still strike you as equal. You could list what evidence would tip them, and go look for that rather than tipping them by preference.
