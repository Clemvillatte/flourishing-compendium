> **Principle 79** — Recognise trauma's specific effects on affect: it is not the same as ordinary dysregulation

The American Psychiatric Association describes PTSD as a condition that "may occur in people who have experienced or witnessed a traumatic event or series of traumatic events", while noting that "most individuals who experience traumas do not go on to develop PTSD". Its symptoms "fall into four categories": intrusion (unwanted memories, nightmares, flashbacks), avoidance of reminders, changes in cognition and mood, and changes in arousal and reactivity such as irritability, being "easily startled" and trouble sleeping ([APA, What is PTSD?](https://www.psychiatry.org/patients-families/ptsd/what-is-ptsd)).

Diagnosis needs symptoms lasting more than a month that cause "significant distress or problems in the individual's daily functioning". Treatment is also specific: the APA names cognitive processing therapy, prolonged exposure and EMDR, with medication as an aid.

> One category of psychotherapy, cognitive behavior therapies (CBT), is very effective.
>
> American Psychiatric Association, What is PTSD? (March 2025)

(APA, 2025.)

**Plain words** Some strong reactions after a frightening event follow a recognisable pattern: unwanted memories, avoiding reminders, a changed mood and sense of the world, and being constantly on edge. Taking this for plain bad temper or poor coping misses what is going on. That is the reason to ask a clinician, since PTSD has treatments built for it.

For example (imagined): a person who was in a car crash snaps at family and grips the wheel at junctions. The pattern of intrusion, avoidance and being on edge points to asking a trauma-informed doctor or psychologist, and anger management alone would address only one visible part of it.

## Practice

- If your affective pattern involves intrusion, avoidance, hyperarousal, and numbing after a traumatic event, seek trauma-specific treatment. Ordinary regulation skills are not sufficient.
