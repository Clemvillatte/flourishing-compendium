> **Principle 20** — Distinguish pleasure from satisfaction; they are different systems

[[T022 — Kahneman, Daniel|Kahneman]] and Deaton distinguish two aspects of subjective well-being: the emotional quality of a person's everyday experience, and the appraisal that person makes when thinking about their life as a whole. ([Kahneman & Deaton, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1011492107)) My application is to record the two separately — the remembered emotional experience of a period and the overall appraisal of the life containing it — before letting either stand in for the other.

> "Emotional well-being refers to the emotional quality of an individual's everyday experience—the frequency and intensity of experiences of joy, stress, sadness, anger, and affection that make one's life pleasant or unpleasant. Life evaluation refers to the thoughts that people have about their life when they think about it." (Kahneman, D., & Deaton, A. (2010). High income improves evaluation of life but not emotional well-being. PNAS, 107(38), 16489-16493, [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2944762/))

**Plain words** You can enjoy a week — good food, good company, the sun on the porch — and still, when someone asks how your life is going, give a shrug. You can also be tired and mildly miserable all week and still say the life is fine. Both answers are honest, because they answer different questions. The trap is treating one as the other: either using how today felt to rule on the life, or using the verdict on the life to overrule how today actually felt. Kahneman and Deaton report that income tracked the two measures differently — life evaluation kept rising with income throughout their range, while positive affect showed no further improvement beyond roughly $75,000 a year — which is a reason to keep them as two entries, not one.

**For example:** A person enjoys a good dinner while judging their life overall unfavourably. The exercise is to record both reports separately, without assuming one contradicts the other.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[The wanting/liking dissociation|Wanting vs liking dissociation]]**, dopamine mediates wanting (pursuit) and opioids mediate liking (consumption); the two can come apart (Berridge & Robinson, 2016)
- **Satisfaction is more stable than pleasure**, evaluative well-being (life satisfaction) has different correlates than experienced well-being (momentary affect) (Kahneman & Deaton, 2010)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Eudaimonia|Eudaimonia vs hedonia]]** (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics)
- **[[The insatiability of desire|The insatiability of desire]]** (Buddhism, Dhammapada)
- **The distinction between lower and higher pleasures** (Mill, Utilitarianism)

## Practice

- When you notice chasing a pleasure that has stopped being enjoyable, this is wanting without liking. Shift to something that will be satisfying even if not immediately pleasurable.
