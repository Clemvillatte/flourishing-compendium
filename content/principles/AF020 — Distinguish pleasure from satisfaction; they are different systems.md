> **Principle 20** — Distinguish pleasure from satisfaction; they are different systems

In research on reward, the brain circuitry that mediates wanting a reward is dissociable from the circuitry that mediates how much it is liked. ([Berridge & Robinson, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1037/amp0000059)) In well-being research, Kahneman and Deaton observed a qualitative difference between their measures of emotional well-being and of life evaluation: the former satiates with high income, whereas the latter does not. ([Kahneman & Deaton, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1011492107)) The compendium's application is to notice, when a pursuit has stopped being enjoyable, that the pull and the pleasure may be running on different systems, and to consider funding something that will be satisfying even if not immediately pleasurable.

> "Incentive salience or 'wanting,' a form of motivation, is generated by large and robust neural systems that include mesolimbic dopamine. By comparison, 'liking,' or the actual pleasurable impact of reward consumption, is mediated by smaller and fragile neural systems, and is not dependent on dopamine." (Berridge, K. C., & Robinson, T. E. (2016). Liking, Wanting, and the Incentive-Sensitization Theory of Addiction. American Psychologist, 71(8), 670-679)

**Plain words** Two findings sit behind this page: the part of a person that pursues a reward can run on different machinery from the part that enjoys it, and the moods of a day are measured differently from the judgment of a life. The exercise here: when a chase stops being fun, ask whether the wanting is doing the pulling.

**For example (imagined):** A weekend of good food and screens ends flat. Instead of concluding the weekend failed, the person asks whether the pull had outlived the pleasure — whether wanting, not liking, was doing the work.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[The wanting/liking dissociation|Wanting vs liking dissociation]]**, dopamine mediates wanting (pursuit) and opioids mediate liking (consumption); the two can come apart (Berridge & Robinson, 2016)
- **Satisfaction is more stable than pleasure**, evaluative well-being (life satisfaction) has different correlates than experienced well-being (momentary affect) (Kahneman & Deaton, 2010)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Eudaimonia|Eudaimonia vs hedonia]]** (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics)
- **[[The insatiability of desire|The insatiability of desire]]** (Buddhism, Dhammapada)
- **The distinction between lower and higher pleasures** (Mill, Utilitarianism)

## Practice

- When you notice chasing a pleasure that has stopped being enjoyable, this is wanting without liking. Shift to something that will be satisfying even if not immediately pleasurable.
