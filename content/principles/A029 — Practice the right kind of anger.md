> **Principle 29** — Practice the right kind of anger

Aristotle treats anger as a passion that admits of a mean. Ross's translation calls it good temper, while noting that the middle state is unnamed and the label inclines toward deficiency:

> "The man who is angry at the right things and with the right people, and, further, as he ought, when he ought, and as long as he ought, is praised." (NE IV.5)

The mean is not calmness about everything. Aristotle blames the deficiency as well as the excess: "those who are not angry at the things they should be angry at are thought to be fools... and, since he does not get angry, he is thought unlikely to defend himself; and to endure being insulted and put up with insult to one's friends is slavish" (NE IV.5). Failing to defend oneself or one's friends against insult is one example of the deficiency he condemns.

Excess takes different forms. The irascible are "quick-tempered and ready to be angry with everything and on every occasion"; the sulky "retain their anger long" and "are most troublesome to themselves and to their dearest friends" (NE IV.5). Aristotle says it is difficult to specify exactly when anger becomes blameworthy: "the decision depends on the particular facts and on perception" (NE IV.5). The Eudemian Ethics restates the target: such a person "gets angry neither too soon nor too late, and he does not get angry with the wrong people or fail to get angry with those people who deserve it" (EE III.7, 1231b). (NE IV.5; EE III.7.)

**Plain words**
Suppose a colleague is blamed for someone else's mistake. Staying silent to avoid an awkward exchange can leave the accusation unchallenged. Getting angry may prompt someone to defend the colleague, but the response still needs judgment. Who is responsible? What actually happened? Whether a quiet correction or a stronger protest fits depends on the circumstances.

*For example:* Being furious that a landlord ignored mould in a child's room, and writing a firm letter, is anger well used. Shouting at the child about it isn't.
