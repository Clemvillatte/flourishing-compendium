> **Principle 35** — Practise social perspective-taking

The research behind this principle is Eyal, Steffel and Epley (2018). They report 25 experiments on whether being told to adopt another person's perspective makes you more accurate about their mind. Their finding: "we failed to find any consistent evidence that it actually did so. If anything, perspective taking decreased accuracy overall while occasionally increasing confidence in judgment." The tasks included reading emotions from faces and postures, spotting lies, and predicting a spouse's activity preferences. A final experiment "confirmed that getting another person's perspective directly, through conversation, increased accuracy but that perspective taking did not." The authors' reading is that accuracy "seems to require gaining new information rather than utilizing existing knowledge about another person" ([Eyal, Steffel and Epley 2018, J Pers Soc Psychol 114(4):547-571](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620401/)).

Why guessing fails: Epley and colleagues propose that people "adopt others' perspectives by serially adjusting from their own", and that "adjustments tend to be insufficient" because people stop once a plausible estimate is reached ([Epley et al. 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15382983/)). Kruger and colleagues found that e-mail senders overestimate how well their tone is understood, and attribute this to egocentrism ([Kruger et al. 2005](https://web-docs.stern.nyu.edu/pa/kruger_email_ego.pdf)).

The studies cited here do not test effects on conflict or relationship quality. Epley 2004 is about guessing, not about asking, so it supports the problem and not the remedy.

**Plain words** Imagining what someone thinks is a weaker route to understanding them than asking them. In 25 experiments, imagining did not make people more accurate, and asking did.

*For example:* This is an imagined case. Instead of guessing why your teenager is quiet, you say "I noticed you're quiet. What's going on?" and then listen to the answer.
