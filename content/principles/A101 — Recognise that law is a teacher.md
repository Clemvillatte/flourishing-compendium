> **Principle 101** — Recognise that law is a teacher

Aristotle's claim rests on how virtue is acquired: "the things we have to learn before we can do them, we learn by doing them", so "we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts", and "legislators make the citizens good by forming habits in them" (NE II.1). Law is the device that gets the doing started: it "bids us do both the acts of a brave man (e.g. not to desert our post nor take to flight nor throw away our arms)" and the corresponding acts of the temperate and good-tempered man, "commanding some acts and forbidding others", and "the rightly-framed law does this rightly, and the hastily conceived one less well" (V.1).

The need for law is a fact about most people: "most people obey necessity rather than argument, and punishments rather than the sense of what is noble", so habituation needs laws to cover the whole of life (X.9). Aristotle adds the limit: "It is hard, if not impossible, to remove by argument the traits that have long since been incorporated in the character" (X.9).

(NE II.1, V.1, X.9, W. D. Ross translation.)

**Plain words** You get good at fairness the way you get good at anything: by doing it, a lot, before you feel like it. Law is what does the making-people-do-it part at scale. Aristotle is blunt about why it is needed: most people respond to necessity and punishment more readily than to argument, so the habit-forming has to come with teeth. That is also why the quality of laws matters; a badly written one teaches badly.

*For example:* Following a seat-belt law until it becomes a habit turns a rule into your own care.
