> **Principle 87** — Seek the pleasure of contemplation

Book X's case for the contemplative life argues that it is the best activity (reason the best thing in us, its objects the best knowable), the most continuous, since we can contemplate truth more continuously than we can do anything, and the pleasantest of virtuous activities, its pleasures marvellous for their purity and enduringness. Self-sufficiency belongs to it most: the just man needs people towards whom to act justly, but the philosopher, even when by himself, can contemplate truth. The chapter's closing instruction is the quotation below. The compendium's exercise: give the pleasure of thinking its own room, and notice what it asks compared to what it gives.

(NE X.7, Ross; EE VIII.3 not opened.)

> We must not follow those who advise us, being men, to think of human things, and, being mortal, of mortal things, but must, so far as we can, make ourselves immortal, and strain every nerve to live in accordance with the best thing in us.
>
> Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.7, translated by W. D. Ross, [full text](https://people.bu.edu/wwildman/courses/wphil/readings/wphil_rdg09_nichomacheanethics_entire.htm).

**Plain words**
Bodily pleasures run on external supply: you need the thing, and the thing runs out. The pleasure of thinking runs on what you carry with you: once a subject has you, an afternoon alone is enough. Aristotle's argument is that this pleasure lasts longer and asks less. Building the capacity is ordinary work: read, ask, discuss, and let the difficulty be the interesting part.

**For example**
Spending a Sunday on a hard book and a long talk about it can leave someone more content than a day out spent buying things.
