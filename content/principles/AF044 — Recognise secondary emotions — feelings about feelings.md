> **Principle 44** — Recognise secondary emotions: feelings about feelings

In emotion-focused therapy, Leslie Greenberg separates the two layers this way: "Primary emotions are the person's most fundamental direct initial reactions to a situation like being sad at a loss." Secondary emotions are "responses to prior thoughts or feelings", for example "feeling angry in response to feeling hurt or feeling afraid or guilty about feeling angry" (Emotion-focused therapy, Clinical Psychology and Psychotherapy 11, 2004, pp. 8-9, [Greenberg PDF](http://www.emotionfocusedtherapy.org/Greenberg,%20L.S.%20(2004).%20Emotion-focused%20therapy.%20Clin%20Psychol%20Psychotherapy.%2011,%203-16.%5BEFT%20overview%5D.pdf)).

### Why name the layer

The layer matters because the treatment differs. Greenberg's rule is that "Secondary emotions need to be explored in order to get at their more primary generators" (same article). Elliott and Greenberg give a case: a client in danger "may become angry about the fear, even when angry behaviour increases the danger" ([Elliott and Greenberg, EFT chapter](https://pureportal.strath.ac.uk/files-asset/67046858/Elliott_Greenberg_McGraw_Hill_OUP_2017_Emotion_focused_therapy.pdf)).

The Buddha's two arrows (Sallatha Sutta, SN 36.6) are related but not identical. The uninstructed person "sorrows, grieves, & laments, beats his breast, becomes distraught. So he feels two pains, physical & mental" ([Thanissaro](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn36/sn36.006.than.html)). There the first arrow is bodily pain, not an emotion, so the sutta supports the shape of the idea and not Greenberg's definition.

**Plain words**
Some distress is the feeling itself, and some is how you feel about having it. Shame at being afraid, or fear of your own anger, adds a second layer on top of the first. Asking "what is this a reaction to?" tells you which layer you are dealing with. The same shape can follow pleasant feelings, such as guilt about [[Joy|feeling joy]], though the sources here give painful examples.

**For example**
Write one sentence for each layer: "I feel anxious, and I feel ashamed that I feel anxious." Work out which of the two you are really trying to get rid of, and start with what the second one is reacting to.

## Psychology parallels

- **Meta-emotion amplifies distress**, judging a feeling as unacceptable adds a layer of suffering on top of the feeling (Levitt et al., 2004)
- **[[The second arrow|The second arrow]]** (Buddhist concept, empirically supported), the reaction to the pain is often worse than the pain (Hofmann et al., 2010)

## Practice

- When you notice distress about a feeling, separate the layers: "I feel anxious, and I feel ashamed about feeling anxious." Address the shame about the feeling first; it is often the larger burden.
