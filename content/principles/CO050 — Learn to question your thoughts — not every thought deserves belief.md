> **Principle 50** — Learn to question your thoughts: not every thought deserves belief

The Beck Institute's clinical worksheet is the direct source for the procedure this principle describes. Its instruction is explicit about the act of checking evidence before accepting a thought.

> What makes me think the thought is true? What makes me think the thought is not true or not completely true? What's another way to look at this? (Beck Institute, "Testing Your Thoughts" worksheet, [beckinstitute.org](https://beckinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Testing-Your-Thoughts-Worksheet.pdf))

The Buddhist passage quoted below is a related practice from a different tradition, presented as a philosophical parallel; no claim of historical influence is being made here.

> "There is the case where, there being sensual desire present within, a monk discerns that 'There is sensual desire present within me.'" (Thanissaro Bhikkhu trans., [Satipatthana Sutta, MN 10](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.010.than.html), five hindrances)

What the Buddhist instruction describes is a specific act of recognition — naming a mental state as a state, rather than acting on it. The Beck worksheet is a different operation: checking a proposition against evidence. Both share the underlying move of inserting a gap between the thought and the response, but they are not the same technique.

**Plain words** A negative thought arrives in the mind and the reflex is to treat it as a report on reality. The skill being trained is to ask, before believing: what makes me think this is true, what makes me think it is not, and is there another way to read the same facts. The thought may still arrive; what changes is whether it is accepted on arrival.

*For example:* Imagine someone who notices the thought "I always get this wrong" arriving after a mistake. Instead of accepting it, they ask what the evidence is: one error this week against several tasks that went fine. Reading the same facts differently, the thought changes from "I always get this wrong" to "I got this one wrong"; the first is an unsupported generalisation, and the second is the one the evidence actually supports.

## Practice

- When you notice a strong negative thought, ask: "is this thought accurate? What is the evidence for and against? Is there another way to see this?" Do not believe every thought you think.
