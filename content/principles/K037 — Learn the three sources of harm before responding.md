> **Principle 37** — Learn the three sources of harm before responding

Shantideva's chapter on patience works by tracing harm to its sources, and the argument runs step by step. Harm arrives from things without minds and from beings with them: "Since injury is inflicted on embodied beings by both those with a mind and things having no mind, why single out and begrudge (only) those with a mind? Therefore, be patient with harm!" (Bodhicharyavatara 6.66). From beings, the analysis runs further: the person who wields the staff "was incited by anger, so he's secondary (too). It would be more fitting to get enraged with his anger" (6.41). And some of the harm is older than this moment: "Previously, I must have inflicted such pain on limited beings, therefore, it's fitting that harm comes to me, who've been a cause of violence toward limited ones" (6.42). The whole variety of harmful acts "arise from the force of conditions: there aren't any under their own power" (6.25) (translation carried on the opened [redzambala page](https://buddhism.redzambala.com/shantideva/bodhisattva-behaviour-by-shantideva-part-6.html)).

This entry's application maps each source to a first response: patience for people, problem-solving for events, ownership for consequences. Practically, in the next injury you receive, classify the source before speaking.

**Plain words** The chapter's move is to ask where harm comes from before reacting: from things without minds, from people driven by their own afflictions, from one's own past actions. This entry's exercise is to sort a given harm by source and let the source pick the first response: a repair, a patience, an ownership.

For example: A leaking roof calls for a repair first and maybe an investigation of responsibility after; a colleague's rudeness calls for something else first. Classify before responding.
