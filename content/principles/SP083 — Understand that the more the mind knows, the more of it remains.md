> **Principle 83** — Understand that the more the mind knows, the more of it remains

The proof gives the doctrine's core: "the more things a mind knows by the second and third kinds of knowledge, the greater the part of it that remains" when the body is destroyed ([Ethics V P38 proof, Bennett](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/spinoza1665.pdf)). The corollary adds that this remaining part, "however large or small it is, is more perfect than the rest" ([Ethics V P38 corollary, Bennett](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/spinoza1665.pdf)), and the note states the identity plainly: "our mind, insofar as it understands, is an eternal mode of thinking" ([Ethics V, note on 20-40, Bennett](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/spinoza1665.pdf)). The next proposition ties the doctrine to life in time: "Someone whose body is capable of a great many things has a mind whose greatest part is eternal" ([Ethics V P39, Bennett](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/spinoza1665.pdf)).

> "The more things a mind understands by the second and third kinds of knowledge, the less it is acted on by bad affects and the less it fears death." (Spinoza, Ethics V P38, [Bennett clear-language translation](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/spinoza1665.pdf))

**Plain words**
This is a claim about eternity as Spinoza understands it, not a promise that a person's memories continue indefinitely. His note on V P34 warns that people "confuse eternity with duration," and "credit their imagination = memory with being eternal, believing that it lasts after death" ([Ethics V, note on 34, Bennett](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/spinoza1665.pdf)).

**For example**
Reading example: compare V P38 with the note on V P34. Identify which kind of understanding the proposition names, and which interpretation of eternity the note rejects.

(Ethics V, P38-39, with the corollary, the note on 20-40, and the note on 34.)
