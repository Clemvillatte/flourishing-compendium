> **Principle 92** — Read the great statements as prompts, not doctrines

The four great statements (mahavakyas) are short lines lifted from longer teaching scenes. Read in place, the best known one does not arrive as a slogan. In the Chandogya, Uddalaka finishes an example and tells his son:

> Now that which is that subtile essence (the root of all), in it all that exists has its self. It is the True. It is the Self, and thou, O Svetaketu, art it.

(Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7, trans. F. Max Muller, *The Upanishads*, Sacred Books of the East 1, 1879, p. 101, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/upanishads01mluoft).)

Svetaketu answers, "Please, Sir, inform me still more." His father gives a new example, and the same sentence comes back. In Muller's text it returns nine times (6.8 to 6.16), each time after a fresh illustration. The sentence is the refrain of a lesson, not a line to repeat alone.

The other three read the same way in their places. Brihadaranyaka 1.4.10 has Brahman knowing "itself only, saying, 'I am Brahman'" (Muller, via [brhat.in](https://www.brhat.in/openlibrary/special/brihadaranyaka-upanishad/1-4-10)). Aitareya says "Knowledge is Brahman" (Muller, p. 246). Mandukya 2 says "This Self is Brahman" ([Swami Krishnananda's edition](https://www.swami-krishnananda.org/mand/mand_invoc.html)).

Using each for a week as a prompt is this page's own adaptation. The text shows a teacher pairing one sentence with examples and questions. It does not set a weekly schedule.

**Plain words**

These lines work best as the end of a conversation. Ask what the sentence is pointing at, and test it against a morning that goes badly.

**For example**

A man writes "thou art that" on a card and feels pleased for a day. In the second week he tries the Chandogya's way instead: he asks "that, in this irritation at the queue?" and stays with the question until it gets less sure of itself.
