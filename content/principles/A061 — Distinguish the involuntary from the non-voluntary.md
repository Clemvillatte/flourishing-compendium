> **Principle 61** — Distinguish the involuntary from the non-voluntary

Nicomachean Ethics III.1 opens with the voluntary and the involuntary, because praise and blame attach to the one, pardon and sometimes pity to the other. Within acts done through ignorance, the line is drawn by what follows the act: pain and repentance make it involuntary, and the man who feels no vexation is given a separate name, the not voluntary agent, because he differs from the one who repents. The quotation below carries Aristotle's own wording.

(NE III.1.)

> Everything that is done by reason of ignorance is not voluntary; it is only what produces pain and repentance that is involuntary. For the man who has done something owing to ignorance, and feels not the least vexation at his action, has not acted voluntarily, since he did not know what he was doing, nor yet involuntarily, since he is not pained.
>
> Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics III.1, translated by W. D. Ross, [full text](https://people.bu.edu/wwildman/courses/wphil/readings/wphil_rdg09_nichomacheanethics_entire.htm).

**Plain words**
Both cases start the same way: you acted without knowing a circumstance that mattered. What splits them is what happens after you learn it: pain and repentance make the act involuntary; no vexation at all leaves it not voluntary, with a name of its own because the person differs from the one who repents.

**For example**
Someone who forwards a message not knowing it was private, then feels pain and tries to recall it, fits the involuntary case. Someone who learns the same fact and feels no vexation does not, and the difference between them, on Aristotle's line, is that pain.
