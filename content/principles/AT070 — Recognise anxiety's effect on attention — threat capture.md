> **Principle 70** — Recognise anxiety's effect on attention: threat capture

Anxiety is associated with attention being drawn towards threat; Bar-Haim and colleagues found this pattern across 172 studies. That is a finding about groups, not a way to decide whether a particular person's concern is justified. ([Bar-Haim et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17201568/?dopt=Abstract))

The difficulty can involve noticing threat quickly, struggling to disengage from it, or both; the tasks used to measure these processes have important limitations. ([Cisler, Bacon and Williams, 2009](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2901130/)) Attentional control theory also distinguishes the effort a task costs from the quality of the result: anxious people may compensate by working harder. ([Eysenck et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17516812/?dopt=Abstract))

The practical invitation is to widen the evidence you consider. Check for real danger first, then ask what else deserves attention. A genuine safety problem calls for action; noticing an attentional pattern does not make danger disappear.

**Plain words** Anxiety may keep pulling your attention back to what could go wrong. Take the concern seriously enough to check it, without letting it supply the whole picture.

*For example:* After an ambiguous email, you could read the actual request before interpreting its tone. Try drafting a reply to that request, then check whether returning to the task helped or whether clarification from the sender is still needed.

## Related concepts

- [[Attentional control theory (Eysenck)]]: goal-directed attention and the cost of distraction.
- [[Cognitive bias modification theory]]: attempts to change information-processing biases.
- [[Metacognition]]: examining how one's own attention is working.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Attentional control theory (Eysenck)|Attentional control theory (Eysenck)]]**, anxiety impairs goal-directed attentional control and increases stimulus-driven attention (Eysenck et al., 2007; Eysenck & Derakshan, 2011)
- **Threat attention bias in anxiety**, anxious individuals attend preferentially to threat (Bar-Haim et al., 2007; Van Bockstaele et al., 2014)
- **Anxiety impairs processing efficiency but not always performance effectiveness**, anxious people can compensate with effort but at higher cost (Eysenck et al., 2007)
- **Treatment evidence is separate**: a meta-analysis of social-anxiety training found a small immediate symptom benefit but no significant benefit at four months, with study-quality concerns. ([Heeren et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26080314/)) A later registered replication with high worriers found evidence of no effect on bias, worry or trait anxiety; this is a preprint and concerns one probe-based training procedure. ([Pond et al., 2025, version 4](https://sciety.org/articles/activity/10.31234/osf.io/bt93k_v4))

## Practice

- Check what needs a safety response, then deliberately return to one relevant task. Review whether this helps; persistent anxiety may need professional assessment rather than repeated reassurance or an unproven training exercise.
