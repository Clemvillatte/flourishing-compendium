> **Principle 20** — Learn to manage naps: short or none

Napping has a dose-response curve, and it is steep. In a controlled study, 24 healthy young adults whose night's sleep was restricted to about five hours were given naps of precisely 5, 10, 20 and 30 minutes, or no nap, followed by three hours of testing. The 10-minute nap came out best, with immediate gains on every measure, some still visible 155 minutes later. The 30-minute nap began with a stretch of impaired alertness, the groggy state researchers call sleep inertia, before its benefits appeared.

> "The 10-minute nap produced immediate improvements in all outcome measures (including sleep latency, subjective sleepiness, fatigue, vigor, and cognitive performance), with some of these benefits maintained for as long as 155 minutes. The 20-minute nap was associated with improvements emerging 35 minutes after napping and lasting up to 125 minutes after napping. The 30-minute nap produced a period of impaired alertness and performance immediately after napping, indicative of sleep inertia, followed by improvements lasting up to 155 minutes after the nap." (Brooks & Lack, 2006, *Sleep* 29(6): 831-40, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16796222/))

Timing works on the same curve. The NIH sleep guide tells readers plainly: "Don't take naps after 3 p.m." and explains that "late afternoon naps can make falling asleep at night more difficult" ([NHLBI, Your Guide to Healthy Sleep](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/files/docs/public/sleep/healthy_sleep)). The same guide warns people with insomnia that daytime napping "can make it harder to fall asleep at night, which may prolong insomnia".

**Plain words**
The length of a nap decides what you get from it. Near the ten-minute mark you wake up refreshed; past thirty minutes you have gone down into deeper sleep, and you surface in the middle of it, groggier than when you lay down. The clock you choose also matters: an afternoon nap spends alertness you would otherwise bank for bedtime. If getting to sleep at night is already hard, the cheapest fix is to skip the nap altogether.

**For example**
*(imagined)* Two office workers hit the same 2 p.m. wall. One sets a 15-minute timer, wakes clear and works through the afternoon. The other dozes an hour on the couch after work, surfaces foggy, and is still awake at midnight, paying the nap back.

## Psychology parallels

- **Napping and sleep**, short naps (10-20 min) improve alertness; long or late naps disrupt night sleep (Brooks & Lack, 2006; Milner & Cote, 2009)

## Practice

- Nap wisely: if you nap, keep it short (10-20 minutes) and early (before 3pm). Do not nap long or late, as it disrupts night sleep. If you have insomnia, avoid napping.
