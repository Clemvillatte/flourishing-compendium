> **Principle 85** — Prefer humble freedom to gilded captivity

The tortoise drags its tail in the mud rather than being venerated in a temple; the marsh pheasant takes ten steps for one peck rather than live in a cage. Choose the muddy, free option.

(Zhuangzi 3, 17.)

### Two animals, one choice

The tortoise story comes from chapter 17, where Zhuangzi answers the king of Chu's offer of office:

> "Now would this tortoise rather be dead and have its remains venerated, or be alive and wagging its tail in the mud?" ... "It would rather be alive," replied the two officials, "and wagging its tail in the mud." "Begone!" cried Chuang Tzŭ. "I too will wag my tail in the mud." (Zhuangzi 17, tr. Herbert A. Giles, via [Wikiquote](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Zhuangzi))

The pheasant is chapter 3's shorter version of the same argument:

> "A pheasant of the marshes has to take ten steps to pick up a mouthful of food, and thirty steps to get a drink, but it does not seek to be nourished in a coop. Though its spirit would (there) enjoy a royal abundance, it does not think (such confinement) good." (Zhuangzi 3, tr. James Legge, [tianmu.org](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/daoist/zhuangzi/inner-chapters/zhuangzi-nourishing-the-lord-of-life))

**Plain words** The two stories price the same trade differently. The temple tortoise gets veneration, a chest, three thousand years of formal respect, and it is dead. The pheasant walks a hundred paces for a drink and keeps the walking. What the cage offers is comfort plus status; what it costs is the capacity to leave, choose, and forage, which is most of what a life is. The stories do not say comfort is worthless. They say the price is easy to miss until you notice you cannot pay it back.

*For example:* The corporate job with the title and the golden handcuffs versus the smaller role where you can look at yourself in the mirror — the pheasant knows.
