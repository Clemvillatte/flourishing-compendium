> **Principle 132** — If you must fight, grieve: never gloat

Daodejing 30 sets the limit on victory:

> "A skilful (commander) strikes a decisive blow, and stops. He does not dare (by continuing his operations) to assert and complete his mastery. He will strike the blow, but will be on his guard against being vain or boastful or arrogant in consequence of it. He strikes it as a matter of necessity; he strikes it, but not from a wish for mastery." (DDJ 30, Legge trans., [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

Chapter 31 then names what a victory celebrated as a victory becomes, and gives the mourning instruction:

> "To consider this desirable would be to delight in the slaughter of men; and he who delights in the slaughter of men cannot get his will in the kingdom." (DDJ 31)

> "He who has killed multitudes of men should weep for them with the bitterest grief; and the victor in battle has his place (rightly) according to those rites." (DDJ 31)

The chapter assigns the victorious general the position of mourning, not of festivity. The compendium reads these chapters as placing grief, not triumph, at the end of any fight.

(DDJ 30, 31.)

**Plain words** Both chapters treat fighting as something done under constraint, and they specify the spirit it is to end in: the commander stops at the decisive blow, guards against being vain or boastful, and the victor's place is in the rites of mourning. A practical reading: when a conflict ends, notice what your celebration is made of. The chapter's warning is that delight in the win itself is the mark of a course that will meet its own return.

*For example:* Imagine prevailing in a hard dispute at work. Instead of retelling how badly the other side lost, you note what the conflict cost both of you and turn to what is now possible.
