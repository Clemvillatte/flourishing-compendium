> **Principle 199** — Practise hospitality and kindness to strangers

The Stoic is a citizen of the world. Marcus says it plainly: "My city and country as I am Antoninus, is Rome; as a man, the whole world." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, tr. Long — printed as Bk VI §39 in the Gutenberg 2680 text.) If the whole world is the city, every human is a fellow citizen. Epictetus draws the duty out of the word: "you are a citizen of the world, and a part of it, not one of the subservient (serving), but one of the principal (ruling) parts... What then does the character of a citizen promise (profess)? To hold nothing as profitable to himself; to deliberate about nothing as if he were detached from the community." (Epictetus, Discourses, "How we may discover the duties of life from names.")

So treat strangers as you would treat neighbours — fellow citizens by definition. And do the kindness without keeping score. The doer of a good turn, Marcus writes, should be "like unto the vine, which beareth her grapes, and when once she hath borne her own proper fruit, is contented and seeks for no further recompense." (Printed as Bk V §6.) "Unto him that is a man, thou hast done a good turn; doth not that suffice thee?" (Printed as Bk IX §43.)

(Med. IV.29, VI.14, VIII.5; Disc. III.24; Seneca, Letters 3, 9, 95; Musonius Rufus, Lectures.)

**Plain words** Your passport is accidental; the city it belongs to is not — the human one. A stranger is a fellow citizen you haven't met yet, and the duties run the same. When you do the kindness, be like the vine: fruit borne, nothing owed either way.

*For example:* Giving directions to a lost tourist, walking her to the corner.
