> **Principle 486** — Practise the encompassing (das Umgreifende)

Jaspers starts from a plain observation about thinking. Whatever we think of is "always something other than ourselves". If we make ourselves the object, "we remain a thinking I, which thinks about itself but cannot aptly be thought as an object because it determines the objectness of all objects" (Way to Wisdom, p. 28, trans. Manheim). This ever-present split is the subject-object dichotomy.

### What the Comprehensive is

Being as a whole, he says, "is neither subject nor object but must be the Comprehensive, which is manifested in this dichotomy" (p. 29). This translation calls the Umgreifende "the Comprehensive"; the Stanford Encyclopedia gives "the encompassing". It "does not itself become an object", but "remains itself a background" which "boundlessly illumines the phenomenon" (p. 29). So "in our thinking we gain only an intimation of the Comprehensive. It is not manifested to us, but everything else is manifested in it" (p. 30).

### Where you stand in it

The modes include "that which we ourselves are", which "is called being-there, consciousness, mind, and existence" (p. 32). In existence we are "authentically ourselves in our historicity" (p. 32). Jaspers adds that the Comprehensive can only be approached indirectly, "through object thinking", and that the operation "does not bring knowledge of a new object" but "aspires with the help of the idea to transform our consciousness of being" (pp. 30-31).

**Plain words** Everything you know about yourself is something you have turned into an object: your habits, your history, your test scores. The one doing the knowing is not on that list. Jaspers says that does not make you mysterious in a spooky way. It means every description of you leaves something out, because the describer is not in it. The practice is to treat self-knowledge as useful and incomplete.

*For example:* Imagined example: after a personality assessment, someone reads their profile and recognises most of it. They also notice that the one reading it is not in the report, and they hold the results as a map, not as a verdict.

(Jaspers, Way to Wisdom, trans. Ralph Manheim (Yale, 1951), pp. 28-32, Internet Archive; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Karl Jaspers"; both opened 2026-10-21. Philosophy vol. 1 and Von der Wahrheit not opened.)
