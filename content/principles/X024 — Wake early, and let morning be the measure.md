> **Principle 24** — Wake early, and let morning be the measure

In "Where I Lived, and What I Lived For", Thoreau writes: "The morning, which is the most memorable season of the day, is the awakening hour. Then there is least somnolence in us; and for an hour, at least, some part of us awakes, which slumbers all the rest of the day and night" ([Walden, Kouroo edition](http://www.kouroo.info/kouroo/WALDEN/02_WhereILivedAnd.pdf)). He then separates the morning from the clock: "It matters not what the clocks say or the attitudes and labors of men. Morning is when I am awake and there is a dawn in me. Moral reform is the effort to throw off sleep... To be awake is to be alive" ([Walden, digital Thoreau](https://commons.digitalthoreau.org/walden/where-i-lived-and-what-i-lived-for/where-i-lived-and-what-i-lived-for-13-23/)). So his "morning" is a state of attention, and early rising is only one way into it.

The chronobiology claim is mixed. A systematic review found a synchrony effect, better performance at a person's optimal time of day, in 29 of 64 adult studies, while more than 80% showed no main effect of chronotype alone ([Chauhan et al. 2025, Chronobiology International](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/07420528.2025.2490495)). 

(Thoreau, Walden, the morning passages; "Morning.")

### Scope

I did not open a source on cortisol rhythms, so the seed's cortisol claim is omitted. Thoreau's "hour of the gods" is also not quoted here. The sixty-minute rule is the compendium's practice, not his.

**Plain words**
Thoreau ties being awake to being alive, and he finds that attention is freshest early. Research does not show that mornings suit everyone, so protect the hour when you think best.

**For example**
For two weeks, spend the first hour of your day on something deliberate, such as reading or a walk, before opening any messages. Note when your attention feels sharpest.
