> **Principle 269** — Honour the ancestors

Maintain continuity with those who came before you. In many African traditions, the ancestors are not dead and gone but are active participants in the life of the community. Honour them by remembering their names, telling their stories, and living in a way that would make them proud.

([[Ubuntu|Ubuntu philosophy]]; Mbiti, African Religions and Philosophy.)

### The living-dead

Mbiti's term for the remembered dead:

> "But while the departed person is remembered by name, he is not really dead: he is alive, and such a person I would call the living-dead. The living dead is a person who is physically dead but alive in the memory of those who knew him in his life as well as being alive in the world of the spirits." (Mbiti, African Religions and Philosophy, quoted in [Death and meaning(lessness): re-examining the African view, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/178F093294B00AE92F9276DF5B95750C/S0034412522000415a.pdf/death_and_meaninglessness_reexamining_the_african_view.pdf))

The duration of that state: "The appearance of the departed, and his being recognized by name, may continue for up to four or five generations, so long as someone is alive who once knew the departed personally and by name. When, however, the last person who knew the departed also dies, then the former passes out of the horizon of the Sasa period; and in effect he now becomes completely dead as far as family ties are concerned" (Mbiti, quoted in the same article).

From the book itself: "Belief in the continuation of life after death is found in all African societies, as far as I have been able to discover. But this belief does not constitute a hope for a future and better life" (Mbiti, African Religions and Philosophy, [africasocialwork.net PDF](https://africasocialwork.net/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/philosophy-of-mbiti.pdf)).

**Plain words** Mbiti's frame makes remembrance the thing that keeps a person socially alive. The remembered dead stay part of the family for four or five generations, which means every retelling of a name is doing structural work in the community, beyond sentiment. The practical version for someone outside the tradition: tell the names and the stories, especially the unflattering ones that carry the standards, and let the question of what they would recognise in your life do real steering.

*For example:* The grandmother's sayings that get quoted at the dinner table a generation after she's gone — and the teenager who rolls their eyes, then catches themselves repeating one verbatim at twenty-five.
