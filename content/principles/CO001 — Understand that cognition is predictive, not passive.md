> **Principle 1** — Understand that cognition is predictive, not passive

Your brain does not passively receive information; it actively generates predictions about what it expects and then corrects those predictions based on sensory input. Perception is inference; thinking is prediction. This is not a metaphor — it is the dominant framework in contemporary neuroscience. The practical implication is that your experience is shaped by your expectations, and changing expectations changes experience.

**Plain words** Your brain doesn't passively receive the world — it predicts it, then corrects against the sensory evidence. Perception is inference; thinking is prediction. The practical implication: your experience is shaped by your expectations, and changing expectations changes experience.

*For example:* The "rude" email that turns out to be rushed — the brain predicted hostility, filled in the blanks, and the filled-in version felt like perception. The re-read, with a different expectation, delivers a genuinely different experience. Same pixels.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Predictive processing|Predictive processing / active inference]]**, the brain minimises prediction error by updating internal models; perception is controlled hallucination constrained by sensory input (Clark, 2013, 2016; Friston, 2010)
- **[[Bayesian brain|The Bayesian brain hypothesis]]**, cognition is probabilistic inference; beliefs are priors that shape perception (Knill & Richards, 1996; Hohwy, 2013)
- **This is a dominant but contested framework**, critics argue it overextends and lacks falsifiability; the debate is ongoing (Clark, 2016; Hohwy, 2013)

## Practice

- Recognise that what you perceive is partly what you expect. When your perception of a person, situation, or yourself feels "obviously true," ask what expectations are shaping it.
