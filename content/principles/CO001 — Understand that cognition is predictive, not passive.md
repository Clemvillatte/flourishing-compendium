> **Principle 1** — Understand that cognition is predictive, not passive

Your brain does not passively receive information. It continually generates predictions about incoming sensory input and updates them against what arrives. Perception, on this account, is inference. The practical implication is that experience is shaped by expectation.

### The claim

Clark's summary of what the framework says brains are:

> "These are prediction machines equipped with multiple ecologically sound routes to effective adaptive response, forever in the business of predicting their own unfolding sensory arrays." — Andy Clark, "The many faces of precision" ([Frontiers in Psychology, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3659294/))

The machinery works by comparison: "prediction signals, as representations constructed from past experiences, are compared with incoming sensory information to form prediction errors; prediction errors can be encoded and learned to update stored experience, which is then available for use in future predictions" ([Hutchinson & Barrett 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6867616/)).

### How expectation reaches experience

The same review states the perceptual consequence: "perceptual reports will resemble a combination between a perceiver's internal model (i.e., the prediction) and a stimulus, rather than just the stimulus per se" (Hutchinson & Barrett 2019). One demonstration they cite: "people are experientially blind when presented with an apparently random selection of black and white blobs," and "these images are then dramatically perceived as coherent images after exposure to the natural source images that was used to make them" (Hutchinson & Barrett 2019).

### Status

On the framework's standing: "The scientific story of predictive processing is still evolving, but many believe this approach has ignited a paradigm shift in neuroscience" (Hutchinson & Barrett 2019). It remains contested; the parallels below note the objections.

**Plain words** Your mind arrives at the page already expecting something, and the expectation does part of the work of seeing. When a reading of a person or a situation feels obvious, the useful question is what you expected to find. Naming the expectation puts it where you can check it.

*For example:* An email arrives, short and unsigned. Read with the expectation of hostility, it comes across as hostile. Later, read again with the picture of a rushed sender, the same words look different, and the pixels have not changed.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Predictive processing|Predictive processing / active inference]]**, the brain minimises prediction error by updating internal models; perception is controlled hallucination constrained by sensory input (Clark, 2013, 2016; Friston, 2010)
- **[[Bayesian brain|The Bayesian brain hypothesis]]**, cognition is probabilistic inference; beliefs are priors that shape perception (Knill & Richards, 1996; Hohwy, 2013)
- **This is a dominant but contested framework**, critics argue it overextends and lacks falsifiability; the debate is ongoing (Clark, 2016; Hohwy, 2013)

## Practice

- Recognise that what you perceive is partly what you expect. When your perception of a person, situation, or yourself feels "obviously true," ask what expectations are shaping it.
