> **Principle 104** — Give up the doer's claim

> Those steadfast in karm yog, always think, 'I am not the doer,' even while engaged in seeing, hearing, touching, smelling, moving, sleeping, breathing, speaking, excreting, grasping, and opening or closing the eyes. With the light of divine knowledge, they see that it is only the material senses that are moving amongst their objects.

(Bhagavad Gita 5.8-9, trans. Swami Mukundananda, [holy-bhagavad-gita.org](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/5/verse/8).)

The verse the entry cites for the guṇas reads: "When wise persons see that in all work there is no agent of action other than the three guṇas, and they know Me to be transcendental to these guṇas, they attain My divine nature" (BG 14.19, same site; [verse page](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/14/verse/19), translator not stated on the page).

In both verses, the claim is that the actions of the body and senses belong to nature's processes. The wise person recognises this while acting. Neither verse tells the reader to leave "I" out of accomplishment stories or to credit collaborators and conditions. That bounded practice, one story per week, is the entry's own adaptation. The entry's phrase "the inner announcement of 'I do this'" is also its wording, not the translation's.

The psychology parallel names the fundamental attribution error (Ross, 1977) as a bias toward over-crediting the self. That description was not checked against Ross, so it is worth verifying before it is relied on. The Buddhist parallel was not opened.

**Plain words** The Gita says a wise person sees that the body, senses and natural forces do the acting, and that "I" is not the one doing it. As a modest exercise, tell one story of something you achieved without the word "I" and see how much of the credit belonged to other people and conditions.

*For example:* A musician in the middle of a solo is not announcing to themselves that they are playing it. The training does the work and the phrase comes.
