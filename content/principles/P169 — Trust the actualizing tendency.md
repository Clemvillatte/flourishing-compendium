> **Principle 169** — Trust the actualizing tendency

The actualizing tendency is Rogers's proposal that an organism's activity tends toward maintaining and developing itself. Barbara Temaner Brodley explains it as a starting assumption of client-centered theory, while emphasizing that capacities, learning and circumstances also shape the results ([“The Actualizing Tendency Concept in Client-Centered Theory,” pp. 109-113](https://adpca.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/6_2_4.pdf)).

> "The AT is a tendency; it is not a guarantee of full health or full realization or good behavior." (Brodley, 1999, p. 113.)

### Conditions matter

In Brodley's account, trusting this tendency directs a therapist toward respecting the client's capacities and freedom of choice. It does not make hardship beneficial or excuse harmful behavior. The compendium extends the question cautiously to ordinary relationships: are you helping someone express and consider their own aims, or deciding the answer for them?

(Rogers, On Becoming a Person, 1961.)

### Plain words

You can offer someone time to speak without making yourself responsible for their transformation. Ask what kind of help they want and what limits you need to keep. Trust here is a way of approaching the conversation, not certainty about where it will lead.

### For example

Imagine a colleague describing a difficult task. As a proposed exercise, ask whether they want listening, help considering options, or practical assistance. The example makes no prediction that a particular question will produce disclosure or growth.
