> **Principle 359** — Practise the ethical demand

Løgstrup's The Ethical Demand (1956; English edition 1997) describes a demand that is "radical" and "absolute" but also "unfulfillable", to respond to all persons with benevolent concern for their sake. The same review quotes him that it "provides no explicit directive" and "forces us to start afresh in each new situation" ([Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/beyond-the-ethical-demand-book-1-and-concern-for-the-other-perspectives-on-the-ethics-of-k-e-l-248-gstrup-book-2/)). In his words there is "an unspoken, and one might say anonymous, demand on us that we take care of the life that trust puts in our hands" ([Løgstrup centre paper](https://findresearcher.sdu.dk/ws/files/179601376/FINAL_Logstrup_Christensen_Eriksen_Basic_Trust.pdf)).

Three parts of the seed need adjusting. It is one-sided: "we will never be in a position to demand something in return" ([JSTOR summary](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvpj7dd8)), which fits "not dependent on reciprocity". It is not exactly "immediate and non-negotiable" either, since it is silent and the person must decide what serves the other. Commentators say you cannot "just do whatever it is that they ask you to do", nor rely on social norms ([White Rose paper](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/122851/3/Logstrup%20Manchester%20paper%20v2%20.pdf)). And the demand is not indifferent to consequences, because it aims to serve the other's life.

For psychology, Batson (2011) lists four forms of prosocial motivation: self-benefit (egoism), benefit another individual (altruism), benefit a group (collectivism), and "uphold a moral principle" (principlism) ([quoted in a PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9426642/)). That is a motive to uphold a principle. Løgstrup's demand is a claim arising from the situation, so the match is loose. I did not open Levinas (1961).

**Plain words** Løgstrup says that, simply by dealing with another person, you hold part of their life in your hands. The demand to care for that part comes without conditions, and it does not hand you a rule. You must judge what really serves the other. He called it unfulfillable.

*For example:* This is an imagined case. A driver stops for a stranger in a ditch. She cannot assume what help is wanted, so she asks and works out what would serve them.
