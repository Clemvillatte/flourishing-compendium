> **Principle 120** — Make activities autotelic: worth doing for their own sake

To describe the experience he is after, Csikszentmihalyi turns to the Chuang Tzu's ideal of Yu, which he treats as an early account of it:

> "Chuang Tzu believed that to Yu was the proper way to live—without concern for external rewards, spontaneously, with total commitment—in short, as a total autotelic experience." (Csikszentmihalyi, Flow, p. 150, [archive.org text](https://ia801906.us.archive.org/4/items/Csikszenthmihalyi1990/Csikszenthmihalyi-1990_text.pdf))

In the "Work as Flow" chapter he discusses Serafina and Joe alongside Ting, the cook from the Chuang Tzu parable. In his account they show what an autotelic personality looks like: despite the severe limitations of their environments, they changed constraints into opportunities for expressing freedom and creativity (Csikszentmihalyi, Flow, "Work as Flow," p. 152, [archive.org text](https://ia801906.us.archive.org/4/items/Csikszenthmihalyi1990/Csikszenthmihalyi-1990_text.pdf)). The chapter records what such transformation feels like from inside: work becomes enjoyable and, through a personal investment of psychic energy, feels freely chosen as well (p. 152).

The compendium's exercise: take one activity you currently do only for what it produces, and look for what Joe found in welding: a constraint that could be treated as an opportunity for expression. Then protect the activities that already carry their own reason.

**Plain words**
An autotelic activity provides its own reason to continue: set the external benefits aside and the doing still recommends itself. The work has two halves: try to make one forced activity more like a chosen one, and defend the time you already spend on things worth doing for their own sake.

*For example:* Imagine someone who spends a Saturday fixing a friend's bike, unpaid and unasked, and who enjoys diagnosing the fault and getting the mechanism to work. Helping the friend may be the occasion; the repair itself is what holds them.
