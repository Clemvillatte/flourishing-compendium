> **Principle 86** — Trust the love that cannot become hate

The proposition is V, P20:

> "This love towards God cannot be stained by the emotion of envy or jealousy: contrariwise, it is the more fostered, in proportion as we conceive a greater number of men to be joined to God by the same bond of love." (Ethics V, P20, [Elwes](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800))

The reason is in the proof: this love "is common to all men, and we desire that all should rejoice therein". Envy and jealousy require an exclusive good; a good that is completed by other people's sharing of it cannot be threatened by them. The note widens the claim: "there is no emotion directly contrary to this love, whereby this love can be destroyed; therefore we may conclude, that this love towards God is the most constant of all the emotions, and that, in so far as it is referred to the body, it cannot be destroyed, unless the body be destroyed also." The page's diagnostic drawn from the account: examine your deepest attachments by what happens to them when the loved one changes, leaves, or succeeds beyond you. A love that turns to hatred under that test was attached to an exclusive good, and another person's joy in the same good was its threat.

**Plain words**
Jealousy needs an exclusive good: a rival who takes what you love. Understanding is not used up by being shared, and other people rejoicing in the same good adds to it. So the test of a love is its behavior under sharing: if another's joy in the same thing shrinks yours, your good was exclusive, not common.

*For example:* Imagined: two scholars love the same field. One is poisoned by a rival's success; the other's understanding is fed by it. The second case is what sharing a common good looks like from inside.
