> **Principle 424** — Practise the distinction between facticity and transcendence

Recognise the two poles of your existence: facticity (the given facts of your situation — your body, your history, your context) and transcendence (your capacity to go beyond the given, to choose, to project). You are neither your facticity alone (determinism) nor your transcendence alone (abstract freedom). You are the tension between them.

(Sartre, 1943/2003, Being and Nothingness, Part IV, ch. 1; de Beauvoir, 1947/2018, The Ethics of Ambiguity, Part I.)

Sartre's insight is that a person is never simply what their situation says they are. He glosses the for-itself as "a being which is what it is not and is not what it is" (Sartre 1943 [1956: 79]). The two poles are real at once, and the trouble starts when either one is affirmed to the exclusion of the other:

> Any conduct can be seen from two perspectives – transcendence and facticity, being-for-itself and being-for-others. But it is the exclusive affirmation of one or the others of these (or a motivated and selective oscillation between them) which constitutes bad faith. — SEP, Sartre §4.2 ([opened](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sartre/))

His own illustration is the café waiter, who is a waiter and more than a waiter in the same breath:

> There is no doubt I am in a sense a café waiter – otherwise could I not just as well call myself a diplomat or reporter? But if I am one, this cannot be in the mode of being-in-itself. I am a waiter in the mode of *being what I am not*. — Sartre, Being and Nothingness [1956: 60], quoted in SEP §4.2

The practical core is that the given does not dictate its meaning:

> A factual state, even poverty, does not determine consciousness to apprehend it as a lack. No factual state, whatever it may be, can cause consciousness to respond to it in any one way. — SEP, Sartre §4.1 ([opened](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sartre/))

**Plain words** You did not choose your height, your family, or the year you were born. Those are fixed. What you make of them is not: the same shy childhood becomes one person's excuse and another person's material. The mistake runs in both directions. "I'm just an anxious person, it's in the family" treats the fixed part as the whole story. "I can be anyone, watch me" pretends the fixed part isn't there. Sartre's waiter knows he is a waiter and knows he is more than one, at the same time, and that double knowledge is the whole skill.

*For example:* Someone collapses into facticity ("I'm just an anxious person, it's in the family") and another into transcendence ("I can be anyone, watch me") — the workable life is the one that holds both: given the anxiety, the project.
