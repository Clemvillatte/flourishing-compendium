> **Principle 44** — Learn to track habits: monitoring sustains

What tracking does, on the evidence, is raise awareness. A review of habit breaking notes that people "may therefore lack awareness of cues to their unwanted actions, their responses to such cues, or the relationship between cues and their responses", and that "substitution attempts can be facilitated by an initial period of monitoring focal unwanted actions (self-monitoring) and the personally relevant contexts in which these actions are elicited (cue-monitoring), to raise awareness of likely cues and responses to such cues" ([Gardner et al., 2021, p. 14](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10123713/1/Lally_Breaking%20habits%20or%20breaking%20habitual%20behaviours%20ACCEPTED.pdf)). For unwanted habits, students judged vigilant monitoring, "monitoring contexts and actions for signs of an imminent habitual response", the most effective inhibition strategy (p. 7).

> "One study showed that participants who kept a seven-day snacking diary recording the specific snack consumed, and the time, location, prior activity, and presence of others at the point of consumption, subsequently reported less unhealthy snacking than did those who did not keep such a diary (Verhoeven et al, 2014)." (Gardner et al., 2021, Appetite 162, 105183, accepted manuscript, p. 14)

**Plain words** A habit runs on cues you mostly never notice. A one-week diary of the snack, the time, the place, what came just before and who was there turns the invisible machinery into a list you can read. Once you can see the cue, you can consider giving it a different job.

*For example:* The Verhoeven snacking diary above: seven days of recording what, when, where, after what, and with whom, and the diary group subsequently reported less unhealthy snacking than the no-diary group.

## Practice

- For an unwanted habit, keep a seven-day diary of each instance with its time, place, prior activity and company.
