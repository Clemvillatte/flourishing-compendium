> **Principle 11** — Prevent suffering before it arrives

Patanjali's line is "That which is to be escaped is pain yet to come" (Yoga Sutra 2.16, trans. Woods). In the principle's wording, "the suffering that has not yet come is to be avoided". The commentary explains why with a simple argument about time.

### Why only future pain

"Pain past, that is, transferred beyond experience, cannot properly be called ... a thing to be escaped." Present pain "in its own moment [of existence] has attained experience; so it cannot at the next moment be so changed that it can be escaped." Consequently "only that pain which is yet to come is that which hinders the yogin" (Vyasa, in Woods, p. 139). The pain that can still be changed is the pain that has not happened.

### Where it points

The next sutras give the means: the cause of future pain is the link between the seer and what is seen (2.17), and the root of that link is ignorance (2.24). Practice therefore works upstream, on how one sees. Woods' commentary does not give a method for tracing a particular storm back to its first link, so the principle's after-the-storm exercise is a modern practical addition.

**Plain words** You cannot fix pain that has already happened, and pain you are in now is already being felt. Patanjali's practical aim is to prevent what is still ahead. The tradition's way is to work on the cause, not to brace for each blow. Looking back to find the first warning sign is a sensible practice for this, but it comes from the principle, not the text.

*For example:* Imagined example: after a bad week, someone sees that the trouble started with two skipped nights of sleep. Next time they treat sleep as an early warning, not a symptom.

(Yoga Sutra 2.16 with Vyasa's Bhasya and Vacaspati Misra's gloss, trans. James Haughton Woods, The Yoga-System of Patanjali (1914), p. 139, Internet Archive, opened 2026-10-21.)
