> **Principle 388** — Practise ecological citizenship

> Ecological citizenship cannot be fully articulated in either liberal or civic republican terms. It is, rather, an example and an inflection of 'post-cosmopolitan' citizenship. Ecological citizenship focuses on duties as well as rights, and its conception of political space is not the state or the municipality, or the ideal speech community of cosmopolitanism, but the 'ecological footprint'.

(Abstract of Andrew Dobson, Citizenship and the Environment, Oxford University Press, 2003; [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/36215).)

Dobson's main obligation is spelled out in chapter 3: "the ecological citizen will want to ensure that her or his ecological footprint does not compromise or foreclose the ability of others in present and future generations to pursue options important to them" (chapter 3, [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/36215/chapter/315523039)). He calls these obligations "non-reciprocal" and "asymmetrical," owed by those whose footprints exceed their fair share, and "not unlimited": "They are owed because of an unjust distribution of ecological space, and they end when that imbalance has been addressed." Its political space, he says, "should include the private as well as the public realm" (chapter 2, [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/36215/chapter/315522874?searchresult=1)).

This differs from the entry in two ways. First, the entry speaks of being "a citizen of the Earth" with "rights" toward "the more-than-human world." In the passages above, Dobson's duties are owed to other people, strangers distant in space and time, and run through the effects of one's footprint. A summary of his view says ecological citizenship "is more about obligations than about rights" and that the obligations "are owed primarily to strangers" ([Planetary citizenship article, Dobson 2000 pp. 59-60 as quoted](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21622671.2026.2686254)). Second, voting and advocacy, which the entry stresses, are not the centre of his account, which stresses the conduct of daily life.

Eckersley's The Green State, where the more-than-human dimension is more likely to appear, and Clayton and Opotow (2003) were not opened.

**Plain words** On Dobson's account, ecological citizenship is a duty to keep your own footprint from narrowing other people's options, now and in the future. It mostly concerns the effect of how you live on people you will never meet. Voting and advocacy can support that, and the core is the impact of ordinary choices.

*For example (imagined):* A voter adds candidates' environmental records to their checklist, and also reviews their own energy use, travel and shopping, since Dobson locates the obligation in the footprint.
