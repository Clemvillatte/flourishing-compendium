> **Principle 55** — Prefer the affects of reason; they are stronger

Ethics V.7 states the claim with a time condition: emotions which spring from reason, if we take account of time, are stronger than those attributable to particular objects we regard as absent. The proof turns on what each kind of emotion is attached to. Reason-born emotions are referred to the common properties of things, which we always regard as present, so the emotion remains the same; an emotion tied to an absent object is of a nature to be controlled by the emotions that exclude that object's existence. V.8 adds that an emotion is stronger in proportion to the number of simultaneous concurrent causes whereby it is aroused.

As a compendium exercise, when you want a change to survive, state the reason it is worth making in a sentence that does not name today's mood.

> "Emotions which are aroused or spring from reason, if we take account of time, are stronger than those, which are attributable to particular objects that we regard as absent." (Ethics V.7, Elwes)

**Plain words**
Notice what a feeling is attached to. If it hangs on one absent thing, other feelings about present things can crowd it out; if it comes from what holds generally, it keeps the same shape across situations and time. The question "what is this attached to?" is more useful than "how strong does it feel right now?"

**For example**
In an imagined application, someone notes that their gym motivation is attached to a single image of a future summer, and that it weakens whenever that image is displaced. They restate the reason for exercising in terms they take to hold year-round, and watch whether the motive steadies.
