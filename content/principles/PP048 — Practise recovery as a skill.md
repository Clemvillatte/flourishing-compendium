> **Principle 48** — Practise recovery as a skill

Sonnentag, Venz and Casper's review defines recovery as a process that can be told apart from recovery as an outcome. They report that "recovery activities (e.g., physical exercise) and recovery experiences (e.g., psychological detachment from work) are negatively associated with strain symptoms (e.g., exhaustion) and positively associated with positive well-being indicators (e.g., vigor)". They add that these "suffer when employees face a high level of job stressors", and that the mechanisms "seem to be enhanced in natural environments" (Sonnentag, Venz & Casper, 2017, Journal of Occupational Health Psychology, [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28358572/)).

The earlier diary study of 100 Dutch teachers found that time on work-related and household activities hurt well-being, while "low-effort, social, and physical activities" helped, and "supported 4 of the 5 hypotheses" (Sonnentag, 2001, Journal of Occupational Health Psychology, [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11482632/)). That list includes low-effort activities, so these abstracts do not support the principle's claim that passive recovery is worse than active recovery. I make no claim about that, or about sleep, which I did not research.

**Plain words**
Recovery is something you do on purpose: switch off from work, move, see people, spend time outdoors. The better your recovery habits, the more energy you tend to have. When work is heaviest, recovery is hardest, so plan it ahead.

**For example**
After a heavy week, a nurse blocks Sunday morning for a long walk with a friend. She does not leave the time to chance.
