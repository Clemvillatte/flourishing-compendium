> **Principle 54** — Recognise guilt's adaptive function: it motivates repair

Tangney, Stuewig and Mashek's review of the moral emotions draws the line between the two self-conscious affects: "Guilt, on the other hand, corresponds with reparative actions including confessions, apologies, and undoing the consequences of the behavior," whereas shame "corresponds with attempts to deny, hide, or escape the shame-inducing situation" ([Tangney et al., 2007, Annual Review of Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3083636/)). The same review records the other side: "Clinical theory and case studies make frequent reference to a maladaptive guilt characterized by chronic self-blame and obsessive rumination over one's transgressions" ([Tangney et al., 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3083636/)). Shafran, Watkins and Charman measured the obsessional form: "Obsessional subjects reported significantly more trait guilt, state guilt, and higher moral standards than normal controls," and in both populations "trait guilt significantly predicted obsessional compulsive complaints, independent of anxiety and depression" ([Shafran et al., 1996, Journal of Anxiety Disorders](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/guilt-in-obsessive-compulsive-disorder/)).

> "Guilt, on the other hand, corresponds with reparative actions including confessions, apologies, and undoing the consequences of the behavior." (Tangney, Stuewig and Mashek, 2007)

**Plain words**
The studies separate two guilts by their objects and their outcomes. Guilt aimed at a specific harm moves toward confession, apology and undoing; the review's summary is that "guilt but not shame is most effective in motivating people to choose the moral paths in life" ([Tangney et al., 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3083636/)). Guilt that persists as chronic self-blame and rumination is the maladaptive kind the clinical literature describes ([Shafran et al., 1996](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/guilt-in-obsessive-compulsive-disorder/)). Butler's sermon on self-deceit gives the inverse case: David showed for near a year no sign of "the least remorse or contrition" ([Butler, Fifteen Sermons, Sermon X](https://anglicanhistory.org/butler/rolls/10.html)). The signal is the point: remorse present means the check is working.

**For example**
Hypothetical illustration: the parent who apologises for the unfair snap discharges the guilt in an evening; the parent who feels guilty for the child's every struggle, regardless of agency, keeps a system running that nothing repairs.

(Tangney et al., 2007; Shafran et al., 1996; Butler, Fifteen Sermons, Sermon X.)

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Guilt motivates reparative behaviour|Guilt motivates reparative behaviour]]**, guilt is associated with apology, confession, and reparation (Tangney et al., 2007)
- **Chronic or disproportionate guilt is maladaptive**, guilt that persists despite repair, or that attaches to uncontrollable events, is associated with depression and OCD (Tangney et al., 2007; Shafran et al., 1996)

## Philosophy parallels

- **Reparation and atonement** (Judaism, Teshuva)
- **Remorse as moral signal** (Butler, Fifteen Sermons)

## Practice

- When you feel guilty, ask: "did I actually harm someone, and can I repair it?" If yes, repair. If no (disproportionate or uncontrollable), this is not adaptive guilt and warrants [[Self-compassion|self-compassion]] rather than more guilt.
