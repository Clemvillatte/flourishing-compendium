> **Principle 34** — Distinguish shame from guilt's signal

Spinoza defines shame as pain with an audience:

> "Shame is pain accompanied by the idea of some action of our own, which we believe to be blamed by others." (Ethics III, Definitions of the Affects, XXXI, [Elwes](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800))

His definition links shame to an action we believe others blame. Spinoza's own assessment of the emotion is double-sided:

> "shame, like compassion, though not a virtue, is yet good, in so far as it shows, that the feeler of shame is really imbued with the desire to live honourably" (Ethics IV, LVIII note, [Elwes](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800))

The guilt/shame split named in the title comes from modern emotion research. Tangney and colleagues state it directly:

> "Feelings of shame involve a negative evaluation of the global self; feelings of guilt involve a negative evaluation of a specific behavior." (Tangney, Mashek & Stuewig 2007, [Journal of Social and Clinical Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3092296/))

The same paper describes the behavioral tendencies: "shame motivates a desire to 'duck the heat.' Guilt motivates a desire to 'face the music.'" The page's practical instruction is an application of Tangney's distinction: when shame arrives, translate it once into the specific act and the specific repair, and leave the self out of the sentence.

**Plain words**
If a mistake feels like a verdict on your whole character, name the particular behaviour you regret. Consider what could be corrected or repaired. Spinoza treats shame as evidence of a remaining desire to live honourably.

*For example:* A parent addresses a child's lie by identifying what was false and asking how to correct it, without labelling the child a dishonest person.
