> **Principle 34** — Learn approach behaviours for shame: show up, do not hide

Rizvi and Linehan tested the DBT skill of "opposite action" on shame in a small pilot: five women with borderline personality disorder, treated with a single-subject, multiple-baseline design. They report that "although state ratings of shame are highly variable, it is possible to reduce shame about a specific event over a short period of time" (Rizvi & Linehan 2005, [Cognitive and Behavioral Practice](https://www.sciencedirect.com/author/7201786926/shireen-l-rizvi), abstract listed on the author page).

The premise that shame always pushes people to hide needs a qualification. De Hooge and colleagues note that the literature "typically portrays shame as being bad for social relations, motivating social avoidance and withdrawal", but in two studies (N = 148 and N = 133) people experiencing shame "prefer[red] to be together with others (social approach) over being alone (social withdrawal)", and this was not found for guilt (de Hooge et al. 2018, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29303420/)).

**Plain words**
When shame arrives, the usual pull is to hide, though one pair of studies found that some people lean toward company instead. Opposite action means doing the reverse of whatever shame is urging, such as telling one safe person about the event. A pilot of five people found this could ease shame about one specific event over a short period. That is early evidence, so use it as an experiment and not a cure.

*For example:* You are ashamed of a mistake at work and want to say nothing. You choose one trusted colleague and describe what happened in two sentences. This is an imagined case of opposite action, and the pilot does not show how any individual will feel afterwards.

## Practice

- When ashamed, do not obey the urge to hide. Show up: be seen, disclose appropriately to safe others, do not conceal. Shame thrives in secrecy; it diminishes in the light.
