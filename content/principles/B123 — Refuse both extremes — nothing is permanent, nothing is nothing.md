> **Principle 123** — Refuse both extremes: nothing is permanent, nothing is nothing

The Kaccayanagotta Sutta states the two extremes directly. Asked what right view is, the Buddha names one extreme, "everything exists", a second, "everything doesn't exist", and says the Tathagata avoids both and teaches by the middle (SN 12.15, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, [text](https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn12/sn12.015.than.html)). The middle he then teaches is dependent origination, the chain from ignorance through feeling, craving and clinging to aging and death.

> "'Everything exists': That is one extreme. 'Everything doesn't exist': That is a second extreme. Avoiding these two extremes, the Tathagata teaches the Dhamma via the middle: From ignorance as a requisite condition come fabrications." (SN 12.15, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, [text](https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn12/sn12.015.than.html))

The sutta names the extremes and gives a causal account in place of either. It does not say that things change and therefore things matter; that step is the compendium's reading, an inference from the middle the sutta teaches. As an exercise, take the two total claims to a loss and ask what each one does with it: the permanence claim calls the loss an outrage against what was real; the nothing claim calls it proof that nothing was. The dependent account does neither: whatever arose, arose depending on conditions.

### Plain words

When something ends, two readings offer themselves. One says it should have lasted; the other says nothing lasts so nothing counts. Both are total claims about everything, and the sutta refuses them as extremes rather than as moods. Its middle is more specific: things arise depending on conditions, so they are neither fixed nor blank. A relationship that depended on daily attention did not last forever, and it was not nothing while it lasted. Ask of what ended: what did it depend on?

### For example

A diagnosis is devastating because nothing is permanent, and treatable for the same reason. One fact, read twice.
