> **Principle 13** — Clarify your values: motivation follows value

Self-determination theory describes a range of ways of taking on a goal. At one end is introjection, "taking in a regulation but not fully accepting it as one's own", in which "behaviors are performed to avoid guilt or anxiety or to attain ego enhancements such as pride". Further along is identification, which "reflects a conscious valuing of a behavioral goal or regulation, such that the action is accepted or owned as personally important". The most autonomous form, integration, occurs when identified regulations "have been evaluated and brought into congruence with one's other values and needs" (Ryan and Deci, 2000, section on extrinsic motivation, [American Psychologist 55(1)](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf)).

The two ends have different costs. In schoolchildren (Ryan and Connell, 1989, as reported there), "Introjected regulation was positively related to expending more effort, but it was also related to feeling more anxiety and coping more poorly with failures". In contrast, "identified regulation was associated with more interest and enjoyment of school and with more positive coping styles, as well as with expending more effort" (Ryan and Deci, 2000). Effort can come from borrowed values, but the cost differs.

This covers motivation for particular activities. It does not show that listing your values produces motivation, and I found no opened source on the ACT claim attached to this principle.

**Plain words**
You can push yourself with guilt, pride or other people's expectations, and it works for a while. Effort from something you have thought over and accept costs less. For each big commitment, ask whether you chose it or absorbed it.

**For example**
You keep telling yourself to "get promoted". Ask what for. If the answer is "so my father stops worrying", that is introjection. If it is "I want to lead the work on this project", you have a value you can act on.

## Practice

- Clarify your values: what do you care about most? What would you want your life to stand for? Use values clarification exercises. Distinguish your values from inherited or absorbed ones.
