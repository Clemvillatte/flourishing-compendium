> **Principle 131** — Make philosophy a way of life, not a set of doctrines

Stoicism is not a body of knowledge to master but a set of practices to embody. The point is not to be able to recite Stoic doctrine but to live differently. Philosophy is training (askesis) for the soul. Treat it as you would physical training: daily, progressive, applied.

> "And what was the harm for me to be known to be a philosopher by my acts and not by outward marks? See how I eat, how I drink, how I sleep, how I bear and forbear, how I co-operate, how I employ desire, how I employ aversion (turning from things), how I maintain the relations (to things) those which are natural or those which are acquired, how free from confusion, how free from hindrance." (Epictetus reporting Euphrates, Discourses III.21, trans. George Long, [Delphi Complete Works of Epictetus](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1EDkL63FuSpabjbB7amxNodYocj_q_vaH/view))

In the same chapter Epictetus mocks the idea that a philosopher is made by a cloak and long hair: the material of the philosopher is reason, and his end is to possess reason in a right state. Euphrates, he says, strove for a long time to be a philosopher without people knowing it, so that he would do well for his own sake and never endanger philosophy by behaving badly in public view. The compendium's reading is the practical one: the philosophy shows up in the eating and drinking, or it is dress-up.

(Disc. III.21; Seneca, Letters 16, 71; Med. I.1, II.1; Musonius Rufus, Lectures.)

**Plain words**
Knowing the arguments and living by them come apart easily: a person can pass a quiz on Epictetus and still snap at the waiter. The training is what closes that gap. On this principle the quiz does not count and the moment of provocation does. The page is working when a first reaction in a hard moment starts to change, and not before.

*For example:* A person who can quote Marcus Aurelius but still snaps at the waiter hasn't done the work. One who pauses before snapping has.
