> **Principle 76** — Combine the three into one skill

Book III opens by naming the tool it will use for everything that follows: "These supernormal powers are to be accomplished by constraints (samyama). And constraint is the combination of fixed-attention and of contemplation and of concentration" (Vyasa, p. 204). The three stages of the attention curriculum, taken separately in Book III's opening sutras, are then given a single name:

> The three in one are constraint.

(Yoga Sutra 3.4, trans. Woods, [p. 205](https://archive.org/details/yogasystemofpata00wooduoft).)

Vyasa's gloss: "When having a single object the three means are called constraint" (p. 205). The Tika explains why a technical term was laid down at all: "These three, fixed-attention and contemplation and concentration, are used in many places [as one]. It would be laboured to enunciate [each time] their respective technical terms. So for brevity's sake he introduces a sutra which [lays down] a technical term" (p. 206). The fruit follows immediately: "As a result of mastering this constraint, there follows the shining forth of insight" (sutra 3.5, p. 206), with Vyasa adding that "just in proportion as constraint enters the stable state, in that proportion the concentrated insight becomes clear" (p. 206).

### What the principle adds

The sutra is a naming act, not a new practice: three already-defined stages, applied to one object, are treated as a single skill. The practical reading, that attention trained as a unit transfers to any chosen object, belongs to the principle's modern application; the text supplies the single term, the single-object condition, and the insight that follows mastery.

**Plain words** The three stages, binding the attention, holding it, and absorption in the object, are practiced as one thing once they run together on a single object. Woods calls the one thing constraint, from samyama. The claim worth keeping is the combination: not three achievements to collect, but one skill that gets pointed at whatever you need to see into.

*For example:* Imagined: the same trained unit of attention, pointed at the breath for a season, then at a business problem, then at an old grievance, does the same work on each material. The tool does not care what it is pointed at.

(Yoga Sutra 3.4-3.5 with Vyasa's Bhasya and Vacaspati Misra's Tattva-vaicaradi, trans. James Haughton Woods, The Yoga-System of Patanjali (1914), pp. 204-206. Woods renders dharana as "fixed-attention," dhyana as "contemplation," samadhi as "concentration," and samyama as "constraint.")
