> **Principle 69** — Believe at the living edge: the will to believe

James's thesis in "The Will to Believe" is often read as permission to believe whatever you like. It is narrower: when a choice is genuine, live, forced, and momentous, and intellectual evidence cannot settle it, then your passional nature must decide, and declining to decide is itself a passional choice with the same risk of getting it wrong.

> Our passional nature not only lawfully may, but must, decide an option between propositions, whenever it is a genuine option that cannot by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds; for to say, under such circumstances, "Do not decide, but leave the question open," is itself a passional decision,—just like deciding yes or no,—and is attended with the same risk of losing the truth. (James, "The Will to Believe," § IV, p. 11, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/26659/26659-h/26659-h.htm))

The rule applies only where evidence has genuinely run out. Where evidence can decide, James says it should. His own example is a climber who has worked himself into a position where "the only escape is by a terrible leap":

> "Have faith that you can successfully make it, and your feet are nerved to its accomplishment. But mistrust yourself, and think of all the sweet things you have heard the scientists say of maybes, and you will hesitate so long that, at last, all unstrung and trembling, and launching yourself in a moment of despair, you roll in the abyss." (James, "The Will to Believe," [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/26659/pg26659.txt))

He introduces the example by saying that "often enough our faith beforehand in an uncertified result is the only thing that makes the result come true." The cases that matter here are ones where waiting is a choice too, because hesitating is part of what decides the outcome.

**Plain words** Sometimes a decision will not wait and no amount of evidence can settle it. In that situation, James says, refusing to choose is another way of choosing, with the same chance of being wrong. And when the outcome depends partly on your own action, believing that you can do it may be what makes it possible. That is the narrow case he is defending.

*For example:* James's climber is the clearest version. Another is a speaker who has prepared well, cannot know how the talk will go, and walks on stage expecting to be heard rather than waiting for proof. This second case is my illustration.
