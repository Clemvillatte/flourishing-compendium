> **Principle 140** — Accept loss without devastation

Epictetus asks readers to regard what they have as entrusted rather than permanently theirs. His instruction is a change of verb: "Never say about anything, I have lost it, but say I have restored it. Is your child dead? It has been restored. Is your wife dead? She has been restored. Has your estate been taken from you? Has not then this also been restored?" (Epictetus, Enchiridion 11, tr. Long, https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/10661/pg10661-images.html.) Epictetus treats the bad man's hands as beside the point: "what is it to you, by whose hands the giver demanded it back?" (Ibid.) What you hold, you hold like a traveller holding an inn room: "So long as he may allow you, take care of it as a thing which belongs to another, as travellers do with their inn."

The same teaching as a banquet: "Suppose that it passes by you. Do not detain it... Do so with respect to children, so with respect to a wife, so with respect to magisterial offices, so with respect to wealth, and you will be some time a worthy partner of the banquets of the gods." (Ench. 15.) What is truly yours is not among the things that pass: only your character, whose keeper you are.

(Ench. 11, 15.)

**Plain words** His exercise asks you to treat everything you have as on loan — the people, the work, the place. When a loan is called in, rehearse a different sentence: not "stolen from me" but "given back". It is a demanding exercise, not a mood — the grief stays real — and its point is to stop you acting as if the world broke a contract it never signed. What is actually yours is how you carry the loss.

*For example:* When a friend moves abroad, the practice is not to choose between missing her and being grateful she was in your life, but to hold both.
