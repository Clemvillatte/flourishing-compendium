> **Principle 51** — Treat creativity as a process, not a trait

Creativity research defines its object as the generation of products or ideas that are both novel and appropriate (Hennessey and Amabile, 2010, p. 569). Is that capacity a fixed endowment? The review's summary answer:

> "Although creativity in persons has some trait-like (stable) aspects, it is also a state subject to influence by the social environment." (Hennessey and Amabile, 2010, p. 590)

Hennessey and Amabile report Scott and colleagues' meta-analysis of seventy studies, which found that carefully constructed creativity training programs typically result in gains in performance, with benefits generalizing across criteria, setting, and target population; the more successful programs used realistic exercises that build cognitive skills and heuristics for applying them (Hennessey and Amabile, 2010, p. 577).

The process has named parts you can practise. Divergent thinking is spontaneous, free-flowing thinking with the goal of generating many different ideas in a short period; convergent thinking is more disciplined, narrowing possibilities to a workable solution (Hennessey and Amabile, 2010, pp. 574, 579). Kaufman and Beghetto's Four-C model describes levels of creative expression, from mini-c through little-c and Pro-c, and allows for different paths of development without requiring everyone to pass through the same sequence (Kaufman and Beghetto, 2009, pp. 1-6).

(Kaufman and Beghetto, 2009; Hennessey and Amabile, 2010.)

**Plain words**
Most people treat creativity as a label you either deserve or do not. The research splits it into parts you can work on. Some of it is stable, and the rest moves with your motivation, your environment and the skills you have practised. When an idea stalls, ask what the next task is: producing more possibilities, or assessing the ones already on the page. You can work on either without first deciding whether you are a creative person. Training helps; it does not promise everyone the same results.

**For example**
Suppose you need a title for an essay. Draft several possibilities without editing each as it appears, then compare them against what the essay actually argues: generating options, then evaluating them.
