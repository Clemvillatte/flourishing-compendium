> **Principle 389** — Practise ecological humility

Carolyn Merchant's 1980 book The Death of Nature argued, in her 2006 retrospective, that "seventeenth-century science could be implicated in the ecological crisis, the domination of nature, and the devaluation of women in the production of scientific knowledge". She summarises her reading of Francis Bacon as a project "to create a new method that would allow humanity to control and dominate the natural world". The same essay records that critics disputed her reading of Bacon, and she concedes that "Bacon did not use terms such as 'the torture of nature'", though "his followers, with some justification, interpreted his rhetoric in that light". Her own alternative is "an ethic of partnership with nature in which nature was no longer symbolized as mother, virgin, or witch but instead as an active partner with humanity" (Merchant, Isis 97, 2006, pp. 513-533, [PDF](https://r830.cnr.berkeley.edu/cmerchant/articles/84.pdf)).

Some experimental evidence points the same way. In a preregistered study of 998 people, "individuals walking in nature experienced higher levels of self-transcendent emotions, sanctification appraisals, and GH [general humility]" than those walking elsewhere. However, "being in nature was not associated with significantly different levels of humility than the urban outdoors" (Krumrei Mancuso, Trammell and Harriger, [Journal of Positive Psychology, 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17439760.2023.2257680)). I could not find the "Hooker et al., 2020" paper named in the principle, so I do not cite it.

**Plain words**
Treat nature as something you take part in, not something you run. Admit how little you know and how little control you have. Noticing this is easier outdoors, though the evidence on its effect on humility is thin.

**For example**
Before pulling out "weeds" in your garden, you spend a week noticing which insects and birds use them, and you leave a patch alone.
