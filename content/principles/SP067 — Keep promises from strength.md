> **Principle 67** — Keep promises from strength

Ethics IV.72 states the free man's rule: he never acts fraudulently, but always in good faith. The proof tests the hardest case, breaking faith to escape the danger of present death, and argues by generalisation: if reason persuaded a man to deceive in that case, it would persuade all men to act likewise, and then reason would persuade men not to agree in good faith to unite their forces, or to have laws in common — which is absurd. Spinoza links good faith to people's ability to unite their forces and share laws.

As a compendium exercise, audit your commitments and note the why: kept because watched, or kept because being believed is part of how you live with others.

> "The free man never acts fraudulently, but always in good faith." (Ethics IV.72, Elwes)

**Plain words**
Imagine everyone treating danger as a reason to break an agreement. Why would anyone rely on agreements to work together? Spinoza uses that difficulty to argue for good faith. As a practical exercise, consider which of your commitments others need to rely on.

**For example**
In an imagined application, an employee notes that they are honest only while supervised, and asks what their word is worth to the people who will still be around after the next restructuring.
