> **Principle 58** — Distinguish the "I" who acts from the "Me" who is known

Mead's distinction is compressed into one sentence: "The 'I' is the response of the organism to the attitudes of the others; the 'me' is the organized set of attitudes of others which one himself assumes" (Mind, Self and Society, p. 175, [text](https://archive.org/download/mead-1934/mead%201934.pdf)). Of the acting self he says: "The 'I' appears in our experience in memory. It is only after we have acted that we know what we have done; it is only after we have spoken that we know what we have said" (p. 196). The known self is the settled part: "The 'me' is a conventional, habitual individual. It is always there" (p. 197).

> "The 'I' both calls out the 'me' and responds to it. Taken together they constitute a personality as it appears in social experience. The self is essentially a social process going on with these two distinguishable phases." (Mead, Mind, Self and Society, p. 178, [text](https://archive.org/download/mead-1934/mead%201934.pdf))

Mead is describing the social genesis of the self, and his timing claim is retrospective: the response becomes available to experience only after it has taken place. The compendium's exercise is a proposed application he does not make: before an act, notice what the "me" expects of you; after it, compare what was actually said and done.

### Plain words

The self has two phases. The "me" is the organized set of attitudes taken over from others, the expectations and roles you answer to, and it can be inspected and narrated. The "I" is your response to all that, and it enters experience only afterward, in memory. The résumé is a photo of the river, not the river.

### For example

Imagine a meeting where you expect to agree, having learned to see yourself as the agreeable colleague. The exercise is to note that expectation before speaking, and afterward compare it with what was actually said. The comparison, between the internalized expectation and the response examined afterward, is the compendium's proposed application. The exercise does not promise the response will differ from the expectation; it makes the expectation visible and the response reviewable.
