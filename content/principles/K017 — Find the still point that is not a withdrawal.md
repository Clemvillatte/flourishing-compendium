> **Principle 17** — Find the still point that is not a withdrawal

The Kyoto School's nothingness is not an exit. The Stanford Encyclopedia entry gives the ground precisely: "the 'field of emptiness' is not a vacuum of relative nothingness that assaults beings from without; it is an open clearing wherein beings are neither nullified nor reified but rather let be in the mutual freedom of their coming to be and passing away in unobstructed interrelation with one another. It is also the place in which a genuine interpersonal encounter can take place" ([SEP, Kyoto School](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kyoto-school/)). The Zen formulation the entry cites: "a practice of living both completely unattached and completely engaged in the world of 'true emptiness, marvelous being.'" And Nishida's late epistemology makes the same point about knowing: "action-intuition" means "seeing a thing by becoming it," where "intuition happens only in the midst of the dialectical process of acting upon and in turn being acted upon by things."

My reading: the still point is not a withdrawal; the person on this ground works, loves, and grieves without the added weight of self-reference, and the sign of the ground is ordinary functioning rather than altered states. Practically, check any practice of letting go against whether your engagements stay available afterward; if they shrink, treat that as a warning and check for avoidance before calling the practice peaceful.

**Plain words** The wish behind much calm-seeking is distance: fewer demands, fewer complications, fewer people. This principle asks for the opposite move: stay in the conversation and drop the layer of self-management. One practical reading of the Kyoto School's account of genuine encounter: in a difficult conversation, try to hear what the other person is saying before preparing your defence; you can take their words seriously and still decide to disagree. That is the unattached half and the engaged half at once — the words are not a verdict on you, and the person still has your attention. Taking a break or setting a boundary can be part of the same response.

For example: during a hard week you still show up at dinner and can listen to your family — the ground is holding under load. If a practice that feels peaceful quietly takes those dinners away, that is the warning to check. (Attention dimension: where your attention goes when someone is talking to you.)
