> **Principle 10** — Understand that behaviour is social: others shape what you do

Three sources show both the pull and the limits of social influence. In a weight-loss trial, 166 participants were recruited either alone or with three friends or family members. Among those recruited alone with standard treatment, "76% completed treatment and 24% maintained their weight loss in full from Months 4 to 10"; among those recruited with friends and given extra social support strategies, "95% completed treatment and 66% maintained their weight loss in full" (Wing & Jeffery 1999, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10028217/)).

Norms need care. In a field experiment on household energy use, a message about the neighbourhood average "produced either desirable energy savings or the undesirable boomerang effect, depending on whether households were already consuming at a low or high rate", and adding a message of social approval "eliminated the boomerang effect" (Schultz et al. 2007, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576283/)). A 2025 meta-analysis of 89 studies (n = 85,759) found a small effect of norms messaging on health behaviours (d = 0.1), and "this effect disappeared after controlling for publication bias" (Papakonstantinou et al. 2025, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40983670/)).

**Plain words**
The people around you shape what you do, and the clearest evidence is for doing something with others. In the weight-loss trial, joining with friends and building in support went with much better follow-through. Telling people what others do is a weaker tool than it sounds, and it can backfire on people who were already doing well. Pick your company and your partners on purpose, and be wary of "everyone is doing it" as a lever.

*For example:* You plan to run three mornings a week, so you ask two friends to meet you at the same corner at the same time. This is an imagined case. It borrows the idea of doing the behaviour with others from Wing and Jeffery's trial, which tested a full programme and not this exact step.

## Psychology parallels

- **Social norms and behaviour (Cialdini)**, descriptive and injunctive norms shape behaviour (Cialdini et al., 1990; Schultz et al., 2007)
- **Social support**, participants recruited with friends and given support strategies completed treatment more often and kept weight off longer (Wing & Jeffery, 1999)

## Practice

- Use social influence: surround yourself with people who do the behaviour you want, use accountability partners, and make commitments public. Do not rely solely on individual willpower.
