> **Principle 391** — Practise ecological relationality as spiritual practice

Thomas Berry's The Great Work gives the principle's claim. His diagnosis is that in the industrial world the trees and meadows "are no longer intimate modes of spirit presence" and the world has become "an 'it' rather than a 'thou'" ([p. 17](https://thomasberry.org/quote/the-great-work-our-way-into-the-future/)). The positive view is in his chapter "The Wild and the Sacred":

> "Indeed, the universe is the primary sacred reality. We become sacred by our participation in this more sublime dimension of the world about us." (Thomas Berry, "The Wild and the Sacred," in The Great Work, [p. 49](https://thomasberry.org/quote/the-great-work-our-way-into-the-future/))

Berry also ties the human venture to "this quality of awe and reverence and joy in the Earth and all that lives and grows upon the Earth" ([p. 166](https://thomasberry.org/quote/the-great-work-our-way-into-the-future/)). The application, this page's suggestion rather than Berry's, is time in nature as contemplation: going out to listen, with the more-than-human world as the material of attention.

**Plain words**
As a compendium exercise, pick a familiar outdoor place and go there to notice plants, birds, water and weather. Treat the visit as a reflective exercise, then note what you noticed. The exercise applies Berry's view that the sacred dimension of the world is found in participating in it ([The Great Work, p. 49](https://thomasberry.org/quote/the-great-work-our-way-into-the-future/)).

*For example:* Imagine pausing beside the same tree on a daily walk, noticing its leaves, the birds it holds and the ground under it, and spending a few minutes there with no other aim.
