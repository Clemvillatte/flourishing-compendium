> **Principle 33** — Learn to hold your imperfections with humour: lightness is a virtue

Research separates two kinds of humour about the self. Martin and colleagues' Humor Styles Questionnaire has a self-enhancing scale, described as "a generally humorous outlook on life" and a tendency "to maintain a humorous perspective even in the face of stress or adversity", which they expected to relate to lower depression and anxiety and to higher self-esteem and well-being ([Martin et al. 2003, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0092656602005342)). It also has a self-defeating scale: humour used for "attempts to amuse others by doing or saying funny things at one's own expense as a means of ingratiating oneself or gaining approval" ([Conversational Indirectness and Humor Style, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11939602/)). Martin and colleagues found that self-enhancing humour was linked with higher self-esteem and lower depression and anxiety, and self-defeating humour with the opposite ([European Journal of Positive Psychology](https://ejop.psychopen.eu/index.php/ejop/article/download/753/753.html?inline=1)).

So laughing at your flaws is not one thing. Joking at your own expense to win approval is the style associated with worse outcomes, and keeping a humorous perspective under stress is the style associated with better ones. The evidence is correlational. A Japanese study found self-defeating humour correlated negatively with stress responses and positively with well-being indicators ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/ajsp.12369)), and an experimental review argued that the questionnaire has limited construct and criterion validity ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00616/full)).

### Scope

The seed's "lightness is a virtue" is the compendium's judgement; the sources measure correlations. Kuiper 1992, Dozois 2009 and Martin 2007 are listed as further reading, not summarized here.

**Plain words**
Seeing your own flaws as funny can help when it keeps perspective. Joking at your own expense to win approval is a different habit with worse associations.

**For example**
After a mistake, ask whether the joke you are about to make cheers you up or just buys approval.

## Psychology parallels

- **Self-deprecating humour and well-being**, the ability to laugh at oneself is associated with better mental health (Martin, 2007; Kuiper et al., 1992)
- **Humour as coping**, adaptive humour styles are associated with better outcomes (Martin, 2007; Dozois et al., 2009)

## Practice

- Practise lightness: notice your own absurdities, contradictions, and flaws with humour rather than contempt. Self-deprecating (but not self-denigrating) humour is a healthy self-relational skill.
