> **Principle 278** — Practise connection to family and kinship

In the Working Together framework, family and kinship are one domain of social and emotional wellbeing. Gee and colleagues write that "Family and kinship systems have always been central to the functioning of traditional and contemporary Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander societies", and that these systems "locate individuals in the community and neighbouring clans within relationships of caring, sharing, obligation and reciprocity" (Gee et al., 2014, p. 59, Working Together, ch. 4, [open PDF](https://www.thekids.org.au/globalassets/media/documents/aboriginal-health/working-together-second-edition/wt-part-1-chapt-4-final.pdf)).

(Aboriginal SEWB framework; Dudgeon et al., 2014, Working Together.)

### What this asks of you

The chapter says the "systems are complex and diverse", and that practitioners need to understand "the different language and family groups of the communities they work in" (p. 60). Kinship is therefore local. In traditional regions, it "usually includes moiety or skin group systems" with complex avoidance relationships (p. 60). The chapter does not mention totems, so this page does not describe them.

The chapter also says that "kinship and cultural obligations can place significant burdens on members of the family" and that grandmothers may lack the support and resources to care for large numbers of family (p. 60). Honouring obligations is real work, and the people who carry it need support.

**Plain words**
Your family relationships come with duties and with care, and they reach further than blood relatives. The details belong to specific peoples and places. Learn them from your own family and Elders, not from a general description.

**For example**
Ask an older relative or a community Elder to tell you how you are related to the people at the next gathering, and what each relationship asks of you. Write down what they say, with their permission.
