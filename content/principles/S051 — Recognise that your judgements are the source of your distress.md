> **Principle 173** — Recognise that your judgements are the source of your distress

In Enchiridion 5, Epictetus locates disturbance in opinions about events rather than in the events themselves. ([Enchiridion 5, Delphi Complete Works of Epictetus](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1EDkL63FuSpabjbB7amxNodYocj_q_vaH/view)) The compendium uses this Stoic claim as a prompt to examine the judgement involved in a distressing situation.

(Disc. I.1, I.11, I.18; Ench. 5; Med. IV.7, VIII.47, XII.8.)

> "Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things: for example, death is nothing terrible, for if it were, it would have seemed so to Socrates; for the opinion about death, that it is terrible, is the terrible thing. When then we are impeded or disturbed or grieved, let us never blame others, but ourselves, that is, our opinions." (Epictetus, Enchiridion 5, trans. George Long, [Delphi Complete Works of Epictetus](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1EDkL63FuSpabjbB7amxNodYocj_q_vaH/view))

The manual's example is death: nothing terrible in itself, since if it were, it would have seemed so to Socrates. ([Enchiridion 5](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1EDkL63FuSpabjbB7amxNodYocj_q_vaH/view)) What is terrible is the opinion that death is terrible. ([Enchiridion 5](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1EDkL63FuSpabjbB7amxNodYocj_q_vaH/view)) The same holds for being impeded, disturbed or grieved: the passage directs the practitioner to blame no one else, but to look to their own opinions. ([Enchiridion 5](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1EDkL63FuSpabjbB7amxNodYocj_q_vaH/view))

The passage then grades the learner. ([Enchiridion 5](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1EDkL63FuSpabjbB7amxNodYocj_q_vaH/view)) The ill-instructed man blames others for his own bad condition; the man who has begun to be instructed blames himself; the man whose instruction is completed blames neither another nor himself. ([Enchiridion 5](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1EDkL63FuSpabjbB7amxNodYocj_q_vaH/view)) For its exercise, the compendium recommends examining the judgement without turning that inquiry into self-reproach.

**Plain words**
Epictetus asks you to attend to your opinion of an event. ([Enchiridion 5](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1EDkL63FuSpabjbB7amxNodYocj_q_vaH/view)) Try stating the judgement involved. His passage ends with the trained person blaming neither themselves nor others. ([Enchiridion 5](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1EDkL63FuSpabjbB7amxNodYocj_q_vaH/view))

*For example:* Feeling worthless after a critical review, you find the judgement "one criticism means I'm incompetent," and see it's not true.
