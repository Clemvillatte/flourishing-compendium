> **Principle 110** — Use music and the arts for moral education

Aristotle's case begins from what virtue is: "virtue itself consists in rightly enjoying, loving, and hating", so "we ought not to learn or accustom ourselves to anything so much as to judge right and rejoice in honourable manners and noble actions" (Politics VIII.5). Music reaches that faculty directly: "anger and mildness, courage and modesty, and their contraries, as well as all other dispositions of the mind, are most naturally imitated by music and poetry", and "when we hear these our very soul is altered" (VIII.5). The conclusion follows: "if it can do this, most certainly it is what youth ought to be instructed in" (VIII.5).

His account of why art works sits in the Poetics: "the instinct of imitation is implanted in man from childhood", and man "through imitation learns his earliest lessons; and no less universal is the pleasure felt in things imitated" (Poetics 4). It connects to the Ethics: education should make us "delight in and to be pained by the things that we ought" (NE II.3).

(Politics VIII.5-7, trans. William Ellis; NE II.3; Poetics 4, trans. S. H. Butcher.)

**Plain words** Aristotle treats music and poetry as gym equipment for the feelings. Because virtue is largely about enjoying and hating the right things, and because imitations of anger, courage and gentleness actually stir those states in the listener, what you listen to is practice. His rule for the young is blunt: they will not attend to anything unpleasant, and music is naturally agreeable, so it is the obvious carrier.

*For example:* A play that makes you feel real sympathy for someone you'd normally dismiss trains something in you.
