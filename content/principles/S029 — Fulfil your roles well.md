> **Principle 151** — Fulfil your roles well

Epictetus devotes Discourses II.10 to exactly this question, "How we may discover the duties of life from names": a person is first a human being, with nothing superior to the faculty of the will, then a citizen of the world, then a son, a brother, a senator, a youth or an old man.

> "…if you are a senator of any state, remember that you are a senator: if a youth, that you are a youth: if an old man, that you are an old man; for each of such names, if it comes to be examined, marks out the proper duties." (Epictetus, Discourses II.10, Long)

Marcus uses the same image from the body: "As long as the foot doth that which belongeth unto it to do, and the hand that which belongs to it, their labour, whatsoever it be, is not unnatural" (Med. VI.31, Long). The failure mode has a name for Epictetus: "But if you go and blame your brother, I say to you, You have forgotten who you are and what is your name" (Disc. II.10). The instruction follows: ask what this role requires of me, right now, then do it.

(Disc. II.10, III.7, III.23; Med. VI.30; Seneca, Letters 44, 71, 95.)

**Plain words**
The method is naming before acting. Say what you are in this room: a father, a manager, a neighbour, a friend. The name brings its duties with it, and they are usually specific and visible: be at the recital, sign the form, make the call, listen without fixing. When you act badly inside a role, Epictetus' diagnosis is that you have forgotten your own name; the repair is to say it again and act from it.

*For example:* Dragging yourself to your daughter's recital after a long day, because being her father right now means being there.
