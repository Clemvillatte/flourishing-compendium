> **Principle 19** — Recognise the spotlight effect: you overestimate how much others notice you

Gilovich, Medvec and Savitsky named the bias after its structure:

> "People tend to believe that more people take note of their actions and appearance than is actually the case. We dub this putative phenomenon the spotlight effect: People tend to believe that the social spotlight shines more brightly on them than it really does." (Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky, 2000, [JPSP 78(2), full text](https://www.psychologyib.com/uploads/1/1/7/5/11758934/the_spotlight_effect_-_ib_psychology.pdf))

The demonstration used a T-shirt:

> "participants who were asked to don a T-shirt depicting either a flattering or potentially embarrassing image overestimated the number of observers who would be able to recall what was pictured on the shirt." (Gilovich et al., 2000, Studies 1 and 2)

The authors' interpretation is anchoring and adjustment: you estimate others' attention by starting from your own experience, where you are the main thing on stage, and you correct too little.

The compendium's instruction: when you feel scrutinised, treat the feeling as data about your own attention rather than about the room's.

**Plain words** The thing you cannot stop noticing about yourself — the stain, the flub, the pause — is one item among many in everyone else's field. You estimate the room's attention from your own attention, and the study says the correction runs too small. Before assuming everyone saw it, ask what you remember of other people's equivalent moments. Usually nothing.

*For example:* (imagined) The coffee spill that feels like a public spectacle. Ask anyone an hour later what they saw that morning and they cannot name it — they were replaying their own awkward email.

## Practice

- When you feel scrutinised, remember the spotlight effect: others are mostly attending to themselves, not to you. This reduces social anxiety and self-consciousness.
