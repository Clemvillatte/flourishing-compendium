> **Principle 30** — Practice humility as accuracy, not self-abasement

The humility speeches in Book VI of the Florentine Codex are formulas for the moments of rising status, and their self-abasement is aimed at the speaker's own office. When the new ruler replies to his nobles, the Codex chapter heading says he does so "in order to humble himself and in order to thank them", and he says of himself: "the truth is now that I have spent my life in excrement, in refuse" (Sahagún, Florentine Codex Book VI, chapter 12, trans. Anderson & Dibble, 1969; [chapter list](https://www.bibliovault.org/BV.book.epl?ISBN=9780874800104) and [scanned text](https://archive.org/stream/volume-11-book-11-earthly-things-florentine-codex-general-history-of-the-things-of-new-spain/Volume%206,%20Book%206%20-%20Rhetoric%20and%20Moral%20Philosophy%20-%20%5BFlorentine%20Codex%20General%20History%20of%20the%20Things%20of%20New%20Spain%5D_djvu.txt)).

Addressed to a young nobleman, the same book does not tell the hearer he is nothing. It says: "Thou art of noble lineage… thou art a ruler's son". It then says that "the humbling, the bowing, the inclining, the weeping, the tears, the sighing, the meekness — these same are nobility, the estimable, the valued; these are honor" (Book VI, same translation; I could not confirm the chapter number from the scanned text).

Psychology treats humility similarly. Tangney writes that "although humility frequently is equated with a sense of unworthiness and low self-regard, theoreticians view true humility as a rich, multifaceted construct that entails an accurate assessment of one's characteristics, an ability to acknowledge limitations, and a 'forgetting of the self'" (Tangney 2000, J Soc Clin Psychol 19(1):70-82, [abstract](https://guilfordjournals.com/doi/10.1521/jscp.2000.19.1.70)).

**Plain words**
The Codex speeches sound harsh to modern ears, but the harshness is ritual and tied to the moment of taking office. A ruler calls himself unworthy while accepting a duty. Nobles hear that bowing and meekness count as honor, not as shame. Psychologists describe real humility in a similar way: seeing yourself accurately, admitting limits, and not being fixated on yourself. A practical form: when you are praised, say who else and what else made it possible.

*For example:* Someone promoted young thanks the team members who did the work and says plainly what they are still learning. This is an imagined case, and nothing in it implies putting yourself down.
