> **Principle 46** — Learn to start: the hardest part is beginning

Two claims meet in this principle. The first is Laozi's arithmetic: whatever is large was assembled from something small. The compendium takes from it that the whole task never has to be faced at once, only its first piece. The second is the research finding that an unfinished goal keeps intruding on thought and attention until it is finished or planned.

> The tree which fills the arms grew from the tiniest sprout; the tower of nine storeys rose from a (small) heap of earth; the journey of a thousand li commenced with a single step.
> (Daodejing 64, trans. Legge)

Masicampo and Baumeister's abstract records that "Unfulfilled goals persist in the mind", and that "Allowing participants to formulate specific plans for their unfulfilled goals eliminated the various activation and interference effects". In their experiments, participants who formulated specific plans for unfulfilled goals showed elimination of the measured activation and interference effects. Beginnings can be bad ones. The compendium's five-minute rule, commit to five minutes and see, is an exercise in that spirit, and is the compendium's own.

**Plain words** Pick the smallest real piece of the thing you are avoiding, and name when and where you will do it. The research finding is narrow: in the experiments, formulating specific plans for unfulfilled goals eliminated the measured intrusions. Beginning badly is still beginning, and a first bad attempt gives the plan something to work on.

*For example:* Imagine an essay that has been avoided for a week. The first move is not "write the essay" but "open the document after dinner and write one bad sentence"; the plan then has a time and a place.
