> **Principle 30** — Respond to good news actively and constructively

Gable's group calls telling someone your good news capitalization: informing another person about the occurrence of a personal positive event and thereby deriving additional benefit from it. Their couple studies sorted what the listener does next into four types.

> "Thus, in parallel fashion we differentiated active–constructive responses (e.g., enthusiastic support), passive–constructive responses (e.g., quiet, understated support), active–destructive responses (e.g., quashing the event), and passive–destructive responses (e.g., ignoring the event)." (Gable, Reis, Impett and Asher, 2004, pp. 232-233)

In their data the active-constructive response is the one that paid: it correlated positively with commitment, satisfaction, intimacy and trust, while the other three types correlated negatively with the same measures (p. 233). The principle's claim follows that shape: [[Active constructive responding|Active-constructive responding, which involves]] enthusiastic engagement, questions and celebration, is the response that predicts relationship quality and the sharer's well-being. The measure asks how the partner usually responds, so it captures the response as the sharer perceives it, and the evidence is correlational.

(Gable et al., 2004; Gable and Reis, 2010.)

**Plain words** The test is what happens in the ten seconds after someone tells you their good news. Asking for the story lets them have the excitement twice, once in the event and once in the telling. Going quiet, changing the subject or finding the catch does the opposite, and the studies tie it to lower intimacy and trust.

*For example:* A colleague announces a promotion and you ask how it happened and what she's most excited about.
