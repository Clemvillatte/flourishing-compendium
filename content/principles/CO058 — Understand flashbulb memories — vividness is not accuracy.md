> **Principle 58** — Understand flashbulb memories: vividness is not accuracy

Talarico and Rubin tested this on the day after 11 September 2001. Fifty-four Duke students recorded their memory of first hearing about the attacks and of a recent everyday event, then were re-tested 1, 6 or 32 weeks later. "Consistency for the flashbulb and everyday memories did not differ, in both cases declining over time", yet "ratings of vividness, recollection, and belief in the accuracy of memory declined only for everyday memories". Their conclusion: "Flashbulb memories are not special in their accuracy, as previously claimed, but only in their perceived accuracy" (Talarico & Rubin 2003, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12930476/)).

A ten-year follow-up of a national sample reached a similar result: "there was rapid forgetting of both flashbulb and event memories within the first year", and "despite the initial rapid forgetting, confidence remained high throughout the 10-year period" (Hirst et al. 2015, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25751741/)).

**Plain words**
The vivid memory of where you were when big news broke feels like a photograph. The studies that wrote it down that week and asked again later found it had changed as much as an ordinary memory had, and people stayed just as sure. The feeling of certainty tells you how the memory feels now. It does not tell you what happened.

*For example:* Write down today where you are and what you are doing, then read it back in a year. If you are tempted to trust your memory over the note, you are seeing the effect the studies describe. This is an imagined exercise modelled on the studies' method of recording first and re-testing later.

## Psychology parallels

- **Flashbulb memories are vivid but not more accurate**, consistency declined at the same rate as for everyday memories while confidence stayed high (Talarico & Rubin, 2003; Hirst et al., 2015); Neisser & Harsch, 1992

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[The illusion of certainty|The illusion of certainty]]** (Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow)
- **Appearance vs reality** (Plato, Republic)

## Practice

- When a memory feels especially vivid and significant, do not assume it is more accurate. Vividness is a property of the emotional experience, not of the memory's accuracy.
