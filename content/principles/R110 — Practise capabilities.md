> **Principle 372** — Practise capabilities

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy states the approach plainly: "The capability approach purports that freedom to achieve well-being is a matter of what people are able to do and to be, and thus the kind of life they are effectively able to lead". Sen's complaint was that traditional models lacked "a notion of what activities we are able to undertake ('doings') and the kinds of persons we are able to be ('beings')". Capabilities are "defined as the real, or substantive, freedoms that people have to achieve certain doings and beings". They "denote the freedoms that have been cleared of potential obstacles, in contrast to mere formal rights and primary social goods" ([SEP, "The Capability Approach"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/)).

Whether a resource becomes a capability depends on "conversion factors". A bicycle, for example, becomes mobility only for someone who can ride it and has the means to do so. The same entry notes that "Many capability theorists have long believed that the capability approach has a normative commitment to expand certain doings and beings that people have reason to value", although empirical applications "do not necessarily have to be committed to ... the expansion of valuable capabilities". That qualification applies to the phrase "life they have reason to value" in the principle. I did not open Sen's or Nussbaum's books.

**Plain words**
A right on paper is different from being able to use it. Ask what people can actually do and be, and what stands in the way: money, health, skills, safety, or time. Work to remove those obstacles, starting where you have influence.

**For example**
A town builds a library, but the evening buses stop at six, so shift workers cannot reach it. Extending the bus route would give them the freedom the library only promised.
