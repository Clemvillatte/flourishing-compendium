> **Principle 41** — Learn to work with identity transitions: grieving and rebuilding

Research on how people narrate difficult life events finds a two-step pattern in the accounts of adults who emerge strengthened. ([McAdams & McLean 2013, open course mirror](https://www.studeersnel.nl/nl/document/hogeschool-van-amsterdam/psychologie-de-basis/mc-adams-dan-p-and-kate-c-mc-lean-2013-narrative-identity/44583338))

> "In the first step, the person explores the negative experience in depth, thinking long and hard about what the experience felt like, how it came to be, what it may lead to, and what role the negative event may play in the person's overall life story. In the second step, the person articulates and commits the self to a positive resolution of the event. Research suggests that the first step is associated with personal growth—the second, with happiness."

(McAdams & McLean, 2013, Current Directions in Psychological Science, [open course mirror](https://www.studeersnel.nl/nl/document/hogeschool-van-amsterdam/psychologie-de-basis/mc-adams-dan-p-and-kate-c-mc-lean-2013-narrative-identity/44583338).)

The compendium applies the two steps to identity transitions: grieving is the first step, exploring what the old self was and what losing it means; rebuilding is the second, committing to the new chapter. The passage links the first step to growth and the second to happiness, so both halves of the transition carry weight of their own.

**Plain words** When your life changes shape — the kids leave, the job ends, the marriage dissolves — part of you dies with the old routine, and that part needs a funeral, not a pep talk. Let the grief for what's gone happen alongside the building of the new version — for many people the two run together rather than in sequence. Grief that's pressed down can leak out sideways for a long time.

*For example:* The new retiree who feels oddly weepy in week three may be mourning a self that showed up at an office every morning for thirty years — grief can look exactly like this, and if the distress persists, it deserves care. Let the mourning happen; the fishing-and-grandkids self gets built on top of it, not instead of it.

## Practice

- When in an identity transition, allow both: (1) grieving what is lost (the old self, role, identity), and (2) authoring what is emerging (the new self). Many people find the rebuilding goes better when the grief has been allowed; if the distress persists, it deserves care.
