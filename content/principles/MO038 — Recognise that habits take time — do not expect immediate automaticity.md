> **Principle 38** — Recognise that habits take time: do not expect immediate automaticity

The famous number comes from Lally's study, and it needs its qualifiers stated. The study's abstract gives the range:

> "The time it took participants to reach 95% of their asymptote of automaticity ranged from 18 to 254 days; indicating considerable variation in how long it takes people to reach their limit of automaticity and highlighting that it can take a very long time." (Lally et al., 2010)

A close reading of the study (Hreha, 2026) pins the famous figure down: "66 days was the median estimated time to reach 95% of a modeled automaticity plateau for 39 participants whose data met the researchers' criteria". That is a modeled median for a selected group, not a deadline, and the median for exercise behaviours was 91 days. Two of the study's other findings shape the expectation: automaticity follows an asymptotic curve, so early repetitions feel hardest while buying the most, and "missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit formation process". The compendium's suggested takeaway: judge a young habit against the curve, not against a day count.

**Plain words** The slogan says 66 days. The study says a median of 66 for fewer than half the participants, a range of 18 to 254 days, and a curve that is steep first and flat later. A new practice feeling hard in week one is not a verdict; it is the schedule of the curve. One missed day did not set the participants back.

*For example (imagined):* A person three weeks into a morning stretching routine finds it still effortful and checks the numbers: for exercise behaviours the modeled median was 91 days, so effortful at day 21 is on schedule.

## Practice

- Be patient with habit formation: expect weeks to months, not days, and expect the early repetitions to be the hardest. One missed day did not materially set the study's participants back; the curve, not the streak, is what to track.
