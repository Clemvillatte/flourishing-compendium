> **Principle 38** — Recognise that habits take time: do not expect immediate automaticity

The famous number comes from Lally's study, and it needs its qualifiers stated. The study's abstract gives the range:

> "The time it took participants to reach 95% of their asymptote of automaticity ranged from 18 to 254 days; indicating considerable variation in how long it takes people to reach their limit of automaticity and highlighting that it can take a very long time." (Lally et al., 2010, abstract)

Gardner, Lally and Wardle's review, describing the same study's result, puts the average with its caveat: "More relevant research found that automaticity plateaued on average around 66 days after the first daily performance, although there was considerable variation across participants and behaviours" (2012). The same review offers a tentative account of the 21-day slogan's origin: "This myth appears to have originated from anecdotal evidence of patients who had received plastic surgery treatment and typically adjusted psychologically to their new appearance within 21 days". These are study-level estimates with substantial variation, not a deadline. The compendium's suggested takeaway: judge a young habit by whether the repetitions are happening, not by a day count.

**Plain words** These estimates describe substantial variation, not a deadline. Continuing to need deliberate attention after several weeks does not, by itself, establish whether an attempt is succeeding or failing. One missed day did not materially set the study's participants back.

*For example (imagined):* Someone three weeks into a stretching routine records that it still requires deliberate attention, without treating day 21 as either a deadline or proof of failure.

## Practice

- Observe repetition and perceived automaticity over weeks to months, not days. One missed day did not materially set the study's participants back. Do not treat any day count as a deadline.
