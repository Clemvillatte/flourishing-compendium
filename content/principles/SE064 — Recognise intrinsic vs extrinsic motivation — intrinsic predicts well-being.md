> **Principle 64** — Recognise intrinsic vs extrinsic motivation: intrinsic predicts well-being

Ryan and Deci define intrinsic motivation as "doing an activity for the inherent satisfaction of the activity itself", and extrinsic motivation as doing it "in order to attain some separable outcome". Comparing people whose motivation is "authentic (literally, self-authored or endorsed)" with those "merely externally controlled", they report that the former "have more interest, excitement, and confidence, which in turn is manifest both as enhanced performance, persistence, and creativity" as well as "heightened vitality" and self-esteem (Ryan & Deci, 2000, American Psychologist 55(1), 68-78). A meta-analysis they cite "confirmed [...] that all expected tangible rewards made contingent on task performance do reliably undermine intrinsic motivation".

Their evidence on well-being concerns life goals more than single activities. Kasser and Ryan found that "placing strong relative importance on intrinsic aspirations was positively associated with well-being indicators such as self-esteem, self-actualization, and the inverse of depression and anxiety, whereas placing strong relative importance on extrinsic aspirations was negatively related to these well-being indicators." Ryan and Deci add that extrinsic motivation "can vary greatly in its relative autonomy", so a reward-linked activity is not automatically harmful.

**Plain words**
Doing something for its own sake tends to bring more energy, persistence and creativity, and organising your life around money, image or fame tends to go with lower well-being. Reward-driven work is not forbidden, and it can be endorsed as your own. Ask of each commitment whether you would still do it if no one saw.

**For example**
Two lawyers work the same hours. One enjoys the puzzles, and the other wants the partner track. Studies like those above would lead you to expect the first to report more energy, though not to predict any single person.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Self-determination|Self-determination theory (Deci & Ryan)]]**, intrinsic motivation (autonomous) outperforms extrinsic (controlled) for quality and well-being (Deci & Ryan, 2000; Ryan & Deci, 2017)
- **Extrinsic goals do not predict well-being**, pursuing money, fame, and image is associated with lower well-being (Kasser & Ryan, 1993; Kasser, 2002)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Eudaimonia|Eudaimonia vs external rewards]]** (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics)
- **[[Right intention|Right intention]]** (Buddhism, Eightfold Path)
- **[[Autonomy|Autonomy as need]]** (Rogers)

## Practice

- Notice whether your goals are intrinsic (aligned with values, interesting) or extrinsic (for reward, approval). When possible, pursue intrinsic goals. When extrinsic goals are necessary, connect them to intrinsic values.
