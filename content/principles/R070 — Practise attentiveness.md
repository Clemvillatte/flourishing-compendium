> **Principle 332** — Practise attentiveness

Tronto makes noticing the first phase of care, and gives it moral weight. A recent analysis of her position summarises it this way: care is composed of four moral categories, attentiveness, responsibility, competence and responsiveness, each setting in motion a phase of the caring process, with attentiveness the defining moral aspect of the first stage ([Acta Philosophica supplement article on Weil and Tronto](https://forum-phil.pusc.it/article/download/4948/2736/4519)). She draws the moral line hard: attention is "a moral achievement", and "ignoring others, ignorance, is a form of moral evil" (Tronto, Moral Boundaries, p. 127, as carried in [the same article](https://forum-phil.pusc.it/article/download/4948/2736/4519)).

Her model for the noticing itself is Simone Weil, whom she cites directly. Weil's definition of attention, from her essay on school studies:

> "Attention consists of suspending our thought, leaving it detached, empty, and ready to be penetrated by the object; it means holding in our minds, within reach of this thought, but on a lower level and not in contact with it, the diverse knowledge we have acquired which we are forced to make use of. Our thought should be in relation to all particular and already formulated thoughts, as a man on a mountain who, as he looks forward, sees also below him, without actually looking at them, a great many forests and plains. Above all our thought should be empty, waiting, not seeking anything, but ready to receive in its naked truth the object that is to penetrate it." (Weil, [Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies](https://www.themathesontrust.org/papers/christianity/Weil-Reflections.pdf))

Tronto's use of Weil makes the link explicit: "It is necessary, in a sense, to suspend one's goals, ambitions, life plans and concerns in order to recognize and be attentive to others" (Tronto, Moral Boundaries, p. 128, as carried in [the same article](https://forum-phil.pusc.it/article/download/4948/2736/4519)).

(Tronto, 1993; Weil, 1942.)

**Plain words** Before deciding what help to give, make room to notice what is needed. Try setting aside your own plan for the moment and listening to the person you mean to help.

*For example:* Imagine a teacher noticing that a pupil's uniform no longer fits and privately asking whether the family would welcome help.
