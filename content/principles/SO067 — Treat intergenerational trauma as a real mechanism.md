> **Principle 67** — Treat intergenerational trauma as a real mechanism

Whitbeck and colleagues built two scales to measure historical trauma among American Indian adults. In a sample of 143 parents, "the current generation of American Indian adults have frequent thoughts pertaining to historical losses and that they associate these losses with negative feelings", and the symptoms loaded on "one anxiety/depression component and one anger/avoidance component" (Whitbeck et al., 2004, American Journal of Community Psychology, [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15212173/)). This supports the claim that history is carried in families and communities, though a scale of thoughts and feelings does not itself show a biological pathway.

The biological claim is more tentative. Yehuda and colleagues measured FKBP5 methylation in 32 Holocaust survivors and 22 adult offspring and found an effect "observed in exposed parents as well in their offspring", calling it "the first demonstration of an association of preconception parental trauma with epigenetic alterations" in both generations ([Biological Psychiatry, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26410355/)). A later review by Yehuda and Lehrner concludes that "given the paucity of human studies and the methodological challenges", it is "not possible to attribute intergenerational effects in humans to a single set of biological or other determinants at this time" ([World Psychiatry](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30192087/)). Heard and Martienssen similarly write that how much such inheritance occurs "in humans remain[s] unclear" ([Cell, 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24679529/)).

**Plain words**
Painful history can echo through families and communities, so some of your struggles may begin before you. The best-supported routes are the social ones: silence, fear and grief. The biological route is still being tested. Either way, naming the pattern gives you more room to change it.

**For example**
A grandmother never talked about the war, and her grandchildren grow up wary without knowing why. When the family finally tells the story, the wariness has an explanation.
