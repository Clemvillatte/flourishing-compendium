> **Principle 41** — Respect fame as the most dangerous of the three

In the Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect, Spinoza compares the three ordinary goods — riches, fame, and the pleasures of sense — and writes that in the case of fame the mind is still more absorbed. ([Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect [4]-[5], Elwes tr.](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1016)) Fame, he says, is conceived as always good for its own sake, and as the ultimate end to which all actions are directed; and the more of riches and fame we acquire, the greater our delight and the more we are incited to increase. The compendium's application is to choose a task with no audience and ask which decisions you would make differently.

> "Fame has the further drawback that it compels its votaries to order their lives according to the opinions of their fellow-men, shunning what they usually shun, and seeking what they usually seek." (Treatise on the Emendation of the Intellect [5], Elwes tr., [Gutenberg #1016](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1016))

**Plain words** Fame, in Spinoza's comparison, absorbs the mind more than riches or pleasure do, and the drawback he singles out is living by other people's preferences ([TIE [4]-[5], Elwes tr.](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1016)). Ask yourself which of your usual decisions would change if nobody was watching; that question is this page's exercise.

**For example (imagined):** A creator who finally has an audience notices the pattern Spinoza describes: the more approval arrives, the more they are incited to seek, and their choices quietly bend toward what the audience usually rewards. They set aside work with no audience and ask which decisions they would make differently.
