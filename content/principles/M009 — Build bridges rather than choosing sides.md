> **Principle 9** — Build bridges rather than choosing sides

The nepantla position pulls toward two exits: assimilating into one side, or fortifying against it. Anzaldúa, who brought the Nahuatl word nepantla into her theory, names a third figure, the person who bridges:

> "I use the word nepantla to theorize liminality and to talk about those who facilitate passages between worlds, whom I've named nepantleras." (Anzaldúa, "(Un)natural bridges, (Un)safe spaces", in This Bridge We Call Home, 2002, p. 1)

She rejects the fortified exit directly. "Staying “home” and not venturing out from our own group comes from woundedness, and stagnates our growth", she writes, and "to bridge means loosening our borders, not closing off to others" (Preface). She also names the cost: "To bridge is to attempt community, and for that we must risk being open to personal, political, and spiritual intimacy, to risk being wounded." Her account of the role is demanding:

> "For nepantleras, to bridge is an act of will, an act of love, an attempt toward compassion and reconciliation, and a promise to be present with the pain of others without losing themselves to it." (Preface)

(The nepantla bridge analysis; the mestiza consciousness, labeled modern.)

**Plain words**
When you stand between two groups, it is tempting to join one and drop the other, or to dig in against the one you left. Anzaldúa describes a third path: stay connected to both and help people cross, carrying understanding in both directions. It will not feel like home, and she says plainly that there are no safe spaces. You choose it deliberately, because drifting into it leaves you resentful.

*For example:* An engineer moves into management. One way out is to stop talking to the engineers; another is to resent the executives. The bridging path is to explain each side's constraints to the other in plain terms, and to accept that neither side will fully see you as one of them. This is an imagined case of the role Anzaldúa describes.
