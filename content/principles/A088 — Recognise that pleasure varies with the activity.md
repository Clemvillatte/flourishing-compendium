> **Principle 88** — Recognise that pleasure varies with the activity

Book X argues the point from the nature of activities: things different in kind are completed by different things, and "activities differing in kind are completed by things differing in kind". The activities of thought differ from those of the senses, "so, therefore, do the pleasures that complete them". The fit matters: activities are made more precise and better by their proper pleasure, and injured by alien ones, which do pretty much what proper pains do. The goodness follows the activity, in the quotation below. The practical instruction: do not judge pleasures on one scale; ask what activity this pleasure completes.

(NE X.5, Ross.)

> For to each activity there is a proper pleasure. The pleasure proper to a worthy activity is good and that proper to an unworthy activity bad; just as the appetites for noble objects are laudable, those for base objects culpable.
>
> Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.5, translated by W. D. Ross, [full text](https://people.bu.edu/wwildman/courses/wphil/readings/wphil_rdg09_nichomacheanethics_entire.htm).

**Plain words**
The pleasure is glued to the activity it finishes. Thinking, eating, and time with a friend each have their own feel, and that feel is information about the activity, not a score on a single scale. Aristotle's sharpest point is the negative one: a pleasure from somewhere else, like sweets at a bad play, injures the activity the way its own pain would.

**For example**
The satisfaction of solving a puzzle is not comparable to the warmth of a long dinner, and neither needs to be ranked against the other.
