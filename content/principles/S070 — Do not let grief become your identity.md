> **Principle 192** — Do not let grief become your identity

Seneca does not tell Lucilius to feel nothing. In Letter 63 he writes, in Gummere's translation: "Let not the eyes be dry when we have lost a friend, nor let them overflow. We may weep, but we must not wail" (¶1). He wants the memory of the dead to become "a pleasant memory", and he adds that "there is a pleasure even in this sting" (¶4). Of his own dead friends he says: "I have had them as if I should one day lose them; I have lost them as if I have them still" (¶7) ([Letter 63, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_63)).

Letter 99 warns against grief kept up for show: "The display of grief makes more demands than grief itself: how few men are sad in their own company!" (¶16). It also says "nothing is more foolish than to court a reputation for sadness", and that tears, "whether restrained or outpoured, ... are never so disgraceful as when feigned. Let them flow naturally" (¶18, ¶20) ([Letter 99, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_99)).

(Seneca, Letters 63, 99, 107; Med. IV.1, IV.41; Disc. I.11, III.24.)

### What Seneca does not say

I found no sentence in Letters 63 or 99 about grief becoming an identity. That framing, and the label "passion", come from the compendium. What Seneca does describe is grief performed for an audience or held onto after it has done its work. This page does not draw on Letter 107, Marcus Aurelius or Epictetus.

**Plain words**
Cry, remember, say the name. What Seneca objects to is turning mourning into a performance or a role. Let the memory become something you can hold without it hurting every time.

**For example**
Each year on the anniversary, spend ten minutes telling someone a story about the person that you enjoy remembering, then carry on with the day.
