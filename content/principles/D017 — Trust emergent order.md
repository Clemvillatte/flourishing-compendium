> **Principle 17** — Trust emergent order

An instruction can become part of the trouble it was meant to fix. Chapter 57 of the Daodejing makes that accusation against rulers: multiplying prohibitions impoverishes people, while displaying more legislation brings more thieves. It answers with a ruler's restraint:

> "I will do nothing (of purpose), and the people will be transformed of themselves; I will be fond of keeping still, and the people will of themselves become correct." ([Daodejing 57, James Legge translation](https://www.sacred-texts.com/tao/taote.htm))

That is a political claim in the text, not a finding about every family, market or ecosystem. I take a smaller lesson from it. Before adding a rule, I can ask what people already know how to arrange and whether my intervention is getting in their way. I still have to notice who is being harmed. The title's "trust" cannot excuse leaving them there.

Chapter 64 supplies a check on the temptation to do nothing: "Action should be taken before a thing has made its appearance; order should be secured before disorder has begun." ([Legge translation](https://www.sacred-texts.com/tao/taote.htm)) Read together, these passages ask me to judge when help is needed and when my wish to direct has become the problem.

**Plain words**
Give people room to work things out before telling them how. Keep watching what happens, including what happens to the person with least say.

*For example:* A writing-group organiser stops assigning the order of every discussion. Members choose the next piece themselves. When one writer keeps being passed over, the organiser raises it. This is my illustration, not a reported result.

## Psychology parallels

- Deci, Koestner and Ryan's 1999 meta-analysis found that some contingent rewards reduced free-choice intrinsic motivation, while positive feedback enhanced it. This is a narrower finding about rewards, not evidence that all direction obstructs coordination ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/)).
