> **Principle 96** — Keep urgency without hurry

The Katha Upanishad puts the summons in the mouth of Yama, the god of death, speaking to Naciketas:

> Arise, awake; having reached the great, learn; the edge of a razor is sharp and impassable; that path, the intelligent say, is hard to go by.

(Katha Upanishad 1.3.14, S. Sitarama Sastri's translation with Shankara's commentary, [Wisdom Library](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/katha-upanishad-shankara-bhashya/d/doc145217.html).)

Shankara reads "arise" as turning toward knowledge of the self and "awake" as ending "the sleep of ignorance". The "great" are "excellent preceptors", teachers who know it, and "the sruti, like a mother, says from compassion" that this is not to be neglected. The urgency is a warning from someone who cares.

The razor is the other half. Shankara says only that the road of knowledge is hard to reach because what it leads to is so subtle. Reading it as a reason to walk carefully and not fast is this principle's own gloss: nobody sprints along an edge.

Vivekananda turned the verse into a slogan, "Arise, awake, and stop not till the goal is reached" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arise,_awake,_and_stop_not_till_the_goal_is_reached)). Everything after "awake" in the slogan is his, not the verse's.

**Plain words**

People put off what matters by waiting for a calmer month. The verse says the calm month is not coming, and the clock keeps running while you wait. But it also says the path is narrow, so being in a panic about it will not help. Treat the date as real and the pace as steady: start soon, do a little on a schedule, and keep turning up.

**For example**

Someone has said for ten years that they will learn to meditate. They book a teacher for next Tuesday, put twenty minutes a day in the diary, and skip the weekend retreat meant to make up the lost decade in one go. Scheduling the practice is this page's suggestion, not something the Katha says.
