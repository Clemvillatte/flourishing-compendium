> **Principle 100** — Redefine skill as evenness

Two verses later Krishna ends his case for working without clinging to results by naming yoga a second time:

> One who prudently practices the science of work without attachment can get rid of both good and bad reactions in this life itself. Therefore, strive for Yog, which is the art of working skillfully (in proper consciousness).

(BG 2.50, trans. Swami Mukundananda, [holy-bhagavad-gita.org](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/2/verse/50/). The Sanskrit is yogaḥ karmasu kauśalam.)

Read next to 2.48, where yoga is "equanimity", the skill is a mental one. Mukundananda's commentary says that working without personal motivation "does not reduce the quality of our work" and that without attachment "we can do so at our maximum skill level, without feeling nervous, jittery, scared, tense, or excited". His example is a surgeon who operates calmly on strangers but cannot bring himself to operate on his own child, because attachment to the outcome makes him fear he will not do it skillfully.

Treating skill as output minus turbulence is this principle's own measure. The commentary supports the claim that turbulence costs performance, and gives no way to count it. Research on choking under pressure, linked below, is a comparison of process, not a proof of the Gita.

**Plain words**

We usually call someone skilled when they are fast or brilliant. The verse adds something we can all see in people who are good at what they do: they are not rattled. A craftsperson who needs the result badly tends to grip too hard and make mistakes. So part of getting better at a craft is learning to work without that grip, and a session should be judged by how steady it was as well as by what came out of it.

**For example**

Two surgeons face the same emergency with the same hands. One is white-knuckled and tight, the other level and fully engaged. On this reading the second is the more skilled, whoever wins the round.
