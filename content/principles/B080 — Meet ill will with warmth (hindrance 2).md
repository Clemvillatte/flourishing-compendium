> **Principle 80** — Meet ill will with warmth (hindrance 2)

[MN 10](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.010.than.html) distinguishes recognising aversion from understanding how ill will arises, is abandoned and does not arise again. The response proposed here draws on the goodwill cultivated in Sn 1.8:

> "As a mother would risk her life to protect her child, her only child, even so should one cultivate a limitless heart with regard to all beings. With good will for the entire cosmos, cultivate a limitless heart: Above, below, & all around, unobstructed, without enmity or hate." (Sn 1.8, [Thanissaro](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/snp/snp.1.08.than.html))

The instruction to wish no one suffering also appears in that [sutta](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/snp/snp.1.08.than.html), while [Dhammapada 5](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/dhp/dhp.01.budd.html) describes non-hatred as what appeases hatred. Thanissaro's [Metta Means Goodwill](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/thanissaro/metta_means_goodwill) explains why the wish can include people from whom we need to remain apart; goodwill does not require intimacy.

**Plain words**
You can want someone's harmful behaviour to stop without wanting them to suffer. There may still be a complaint to make, a boundary to keep or a person to avoid. In this application, warmth begins with refusing to wish them misery. It need not feel affectionate, and it need not make the disagreement disappear.

For example: Before a difficult meeting, someone tries two silent wishes: that her colleague recognise a mistake, and that they find a less harmful way to act. She chooses the wish she can mean without rehearsing revenge, then returns to it before their next conversation. She still prepares the concern she needs to raise.
