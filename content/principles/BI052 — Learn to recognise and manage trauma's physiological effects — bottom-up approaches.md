> **Principle 52** — Learn to recognise and manage trauma's physiological effects: bottom-up approaches

Trauma can leave lasting bodily effects, and some clinicians therefore work from the body upward instead of starting with talk. The evidence for that route is real but modest. In the first randomised trial of Somatic Experiencing, 63 people with PTSD were assigned to 15 weekly sessions or a waitlist, and the treated group showed "significant intervention effects for posttraumatic symptoms severity (Cohen's d = 0.94 to 1.26)", though the authors add that "further research is needed to understand who shall benefit most" (Brom et al. 2017, J Trauma Stress 30(3):304-312, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28585761/)). A meta-analysis of 29 studies of body- and movement-oriented interventions found a mean Hedges' g of 0.50 for PTSD symptoms with very high heterogeneity, and noted that "only one study had a low risk of bias" (van de Kamp et al. 2023, J Trauma Stress 36(5):835-848, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37702005/)).

The stronger claims are contested. A BJPsych Bulletin review of The Body Keeps the Score concludes that its assertions on "the unique efficacy of body-based treatments" are "not supported by the current weight of research", and that "the available evidence does not demonstrate superiority over established, evidence-based interventions such as cognitive-behavioural therapy" (Scheeringa, [BJPsych Bulletin](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-bulletin/article/evaluating-evidence-behind-popular-trauma-narratives-neurobiological-and-treatment-claims-in-the-body-keeps-the-score/5DE000F0254747495B1CAFF4051C3B75)). The same author adds: "This does not mean such treatments lack value".

**Plain words**
Trauma can show up as a racing heart, a jumpy body or numbness, and some people find that working with sensation and movement helps where talking alone has not. Small trials and early reviews are encouraging, but the studies are weak, and nothing shows that body-based methods beat trauma-focused talking therapies. Treat them as one option to discuss with a qualified clinician, alongside the approaches with the strongest evidence.

*For example:* Someone who understands their history well still jumps at a slammed door. They ask a trauma clinician about adding body-based work to talk therapy, and ask what evidence supports each option. This is an imagined case, not a prediction about any real person.

## Psychology parallels

- **Trauma as physiological (van der Kolk)**, the view that trauma is stored in the body; contested (Scheeringa, BJPsych Bulletin; van der Kolk, 2014; Levine, 1997)
- **Body-based therapies for trauma**, Somatic Experiencing showed large effects in one waitlist RCT (Brom et al., 2017); a 29-study meta-analysis found g = 0.50 with high bias risk (van de Kamp et al., 2023); Ogden et al., 2006

## Philosophy parallels

- **The body keeps the score** (van der Kolk)

## Practice

- If trauma has left physiological patterns (hypervigilance, freeze, dissociation), consider body-based therapies (somatic experiencing, sensorimotor therapy, EMDR), alongside — not instead of — other approaches.
