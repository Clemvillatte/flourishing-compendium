> **Principle 28** — Use the outside view: base rates beat inside views

Kahneman and Lovallo drew the distinction from an incident in 1976. Each member of a curriculum team in Israel wrote down how many months the project would need, and the guesses ran "from 18 to 30 months". Kahneman then asked the team's curriculum expert how long similar teams had taken. His answer: about 40% of such teams eventually gave up, and of the rest he could not think of any finished in under seven years, "nor of any that took more than ten" (Kahneman and Lovallo, 1993, [Management Science 39(1), p. 25](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/kahneman-lovallo-mansci1993.pdf)).

They name the two ways to forecast:

> "An inside view forecast is generated by focusing on the case at hand, by considering the plan and the obstacles to its completion, by constructing scenarios of future progress, and by extrapolating current trends." The outside view, by contrast, "focuses on the statistics of a class of cases chosen to be similar in relevant respects to the present one." (Kahneman and Lovallo, 1993, p. 25)

They add that "the inside view is overwhelmingly preferred in intuitive forecasting" (p. 26). Flyvbjerg reports that cost forecasts for transport projects are off by 44.7% on average for rail, 33.8% for bridges and tunnels, and 20.4% for roads, with no improvement over the 70 years of data (Flyvbjerg, 2006, [arXiv:1302.3642](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1302.3642)).

The method has a limit that Kahneman and Lovallo admit. Picking the comparison class is a judgment call. Their advice when two classes are plausible is to "try both" (p. 25).

**Plain words**
Before you predict how your own project will go, find out how projects like it have gone, and start from that. Your plan feels special because you know all its details, which is why it misleads you. Adjust for real differences, but begin there.

**For example**
You are planning a book, and you expect it to take a year. Ask five writers who have finished a comparable book how long theirs took, and how many they know who never finished. If the answers are three years and one abandoned draft, a one-year plan needs a specific reason to be believed.

## Practice

- When predicting how long something will take or how likely success is, first ask: "what has happened in similar cases?" Use the base rate as your starting estimate, then adjust for specifics.
