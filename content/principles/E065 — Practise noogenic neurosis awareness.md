> **Principle 457** — Practise noogenic neurosis awareness

Frankl coined the term to mark where a suffering comes from. Ordinary, psychogenic neuroses arise from the psychological dimension; noogenic ones arise from what he calls the noological, the specifically human dimension, where a life's meaning is at stake. His definition, verbatim:

> "Noogenic neuroses do not emerge from conflicts between drives and instincts but rather from existential problems. Among such problems, the frustration of the will to meaning plays a large role." (Frankl, 1959/1984, Man's Search for Meaning, Part Two: "Noogenic Neuroses".)

The clinical edge is precise: "in noogenic cases the appropriate and adequate therapy is not psychotherapy in general but rather logotherapy". Not every conflict is neurotic, and "a man's concern, even his despair, over the worthwhileness of life is an existential distress but by no means a mental disease"; the failure mode is misdiagnosis, burying existential despair "under a heap of tranquilizing drugs". The correction runs both ways, since he also holds logotherapy indicated in ordinary psychogenic cases.

(Frankl, 1969, The Will to Meaning; Frankl, 1978, The Unheard Cry for Meaning.)

**Plain words**
Some misery comes from the machine: fear, trauma, drives, chemistry. Some comes from the fact that a life has nothing it is for. Frankl's term names the second kind. The practice is to ask, before treating the feeling, what the feeling is made of. If the missing thing is a task the person would call their own, quieting the feeling will not supply it. The question is a real question, not a verdict; cases mix both kinds.

**For example**
Frankl's own instance: a high-ranking American diplomat arrived at his Vienna office after five years of psychoanalysis in which his career dissatisfaction had been read as hatred of his father. A few interviews showed his will to meaning was frustrated by his vocation; he changed professions, and more than five years later reported himself contented in the new work. Frankl concluded he had never been dealing with a neurotic condition; the man "was not actually a patient".
