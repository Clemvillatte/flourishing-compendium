> **Principle 156** — Engage the world's suffering (engaged Buddhism)

Thich Nhat Hanh coined the term "engaged Buddhism" in the 1950s, in the middle of the Vietnam War, to name a refusal that was also a commitment: the refusal to let meditation become an exit from history. The Order of Interbeing's fourteen mindfulness trainings make the point structural — training one is openness, training two is non-attachment to views, and the later trainings address directly the suffering caused by war, economic systems and exploitation ([Thich Nhat Hanh, Interbeing, and the Fourteen Mindfulness Trainings](https://plumvillage.org/about/thich-nhat-hanh/thich-nhat-hanhs-five-mindfulness-trainings)). The mind that sits is not a mind that has opted out.

The argument is not that meditation is insufficient; it is that suffering has causes, and some of the causes are in structures rather than in minds. A practice that only works on the mind leaves those causes in place, and the lotus position becomes a comfortable distance from the very thing the practice was meant to address.

> "Buddhism means to be awake, mindful of what is happening in your body, in your feelings, in your mind, and in the world. And if you are awake, you cannot do otherwise than act." (Thich Nhat Hanh, quoted in discussions of engaged Buddhism, [Awakin.org](https://www.awakin.org/v2/read/view.php?tid=222))

**Plain words** Sitting is not a substitute for acting, and acting is not a substitute for sitting. The tradition's own term — engaged Buddhism — was coined by a monk in a war zone, and it does not mean being calm instead of being useful. It means being calm enough to be useful, in the room, without the reactivity that usually replaces effectiveness. The lotus position is a position from which to enter history, steadier.

*For example:* Two people are both disturbed by the same injustice. One meditates on equanimity and treats the disturbance as the problem; the injustice continues. The other sits in the morning, shows up to the meeting in the evening, and is the person in the room who can disagree without escalation. Both have a practice; only one of them has a practice that touches the world.
