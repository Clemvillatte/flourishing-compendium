> **Principle 58** — Recognise self and solitude: the self needs alone time

Winnicott treated the capacity to be alone as "one of the most important signs of maturity in emotional development", and gave its basis a paradox: "it is the experience of being alone while someone else is present" (1958). In his account the ego-supportive environment is "introjected and built into the individual's personality, so that there comes about a capacity actually to be alone"; even in the adult, "theoretically, there is always someone present". Solitude in this sense is not the absence of company but company internalised. The Daodejing's stillness chapter offers a comparison from another register: all things "go through their processes of activity" and then return to their root, and "that stillness may be called a reporting that they have fulfilled their appointed end" (DDJ 16, Legge).

> "Thus the basis of the capacity to be alone is a paradox; it is the experience of being alone while someone else is present." (Winnicott, 1958)

**Plain words** You need time alone with yourself — not as punishment, not as failure to have plans, but as the place where you hear your own thoughts instead of everyone else's. The capacity to sit alone without a screen is a skill, and it's the soil everything else grows in.

*For example:* The woman who drives twenty minutes to a coffee shop alone every Saturday morning isn't antisocial. She's collecting herself before the week takes her apart.
## Psychology parallels

- **Solitude and self-renewal**, constructive solitude is associated with self-reflection, creativity, and well-being (Larson, 1990; Long & Averill, 2003)
- **[[Capacity to be alone (Winnicott)|Capacity to be alone (Winnicott)]]**, the capacity to be alone is a developmental achievement associated with security (Winnicott, 1958)

## Practice

- Schedule solitude: time alone without distraction (not screens, not productivity). Use it for reflection, renewal, and simply being with yourself. The capacity to be alone is a strength.
