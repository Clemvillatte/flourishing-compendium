> **Principle 13** — Train emotional recovery: the ability to return to baseline

The compendium's claim is narrow: a hard event knocks functioning off its line, and the return to baseline is a capacity that varies between people and can be practiced. Bonanno's review of loss and trauma separates the two patterns. The recovery trajectory is a dip followed by return: normal functioning "temporarily gives way to threshold or subthreshold psychopathology (e.g., symptoms of depression or posttraumatic stress disorder [PTSD]), usually for a period of at least several months, and then gradually returns to pre-event levels" ([Bonanno, 2004, American Psychologist 59, author-hosted full text](https://www.tc.columbia.edu/faculty/gab38/faculty-profile/files/americanPsychologist.pdf)). The resilient pattern still wobbles: resilient individuals "may experience transient perturbations in normal functioning (e.g., several weeks of sporadic preoccupation or restless sleep) but generally exhibit a stable trajectory of healthy functioning across time, as well as the capacity for generative experiences and positive emotions" ([Bonanno, 2004, same text](https://www.tc.columbia.edu/faculty/gab38/faculty-profile/files/americanPsychologist.pdf)). In the same passage Bonanno adds that "resilience is more than the simple absence of psychopathology": it is a pattern of functioning, not a bare lack of symptoms.

**Plain words** A hard event knocks you off your line. The question the principle asks is how long you stay off it. Some people are back at ordinary functioning in days; others carry the same event for months. The return, not the hit, is the part that responds to practice.

*For example:* (Imagined case.) After a layoff, one person still avoids the rejection-email folder three months later, while a colleague is interviewing again within two weeks. The slower pattern is a documented, ordinary trajectory in Bonanno's data, not evidence of weakness. The practice is to notice which pattern is running and what the return looks like this week.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Resilience|Resilience as trajectory, not trait]]**, most people exposed to potentially traumatic events show a resilient trajectory, not a pathological one (Bonanno, 2004)
- **Emotional inertia as a depression risk factor**, slow recovery from negative affect predicts depressive symptoms prospectively (Kuppens et al., 2012)

## Practice

- After a negative event, deliberately do something that has historically helped you recover (walk, connect with a friend, sleep, exercise) rather than waiting for the feeling to pass on its own.
