> **Principle 13** — Train emotional recovery: the ability to return to baseline

Bonanno distinguishes two trajectories after loss or trauma. Recovery involves disrupted functioning followed by a gradual return to pre-event levels. Resilience involves maintaining relatively stable functioning, although temporary disruptions can occur. Here, the compendium proposes observing changes in ordinary activities after a setback, without treating a particular recovery speed as proof of personal strength.

> "By contrast, resilience reflects the ability to maintain a stable equilibrium." ([Bonanno, 2004, American Psychologist 59(1), 20-28, author-hosted full text](https://www.tc.columbia.edu/faculty/gab38/faculty-profile/files/americanPsychologist.pdf))

**Plain words** Notice what has become harder and what you can do again. Keep a record of changes in your ordinary activities instead of judging yourself against another person's timetable.

*For example:* In an imagined layoff scenario, you update your CV but still avoid your inbox. Record both observations, then revisit them next week.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Resilience|Resilience as trajectory, not trait]]**, most people exposed to potentially traumatic events show a resilient trajectory, not a pathological one (Bonanno, 2004)
- **Emotional inertia as a depression risk factor**, slow recovery from negative affect predicts depressive symptoms prospectively (Kuppens et al., 2012)

## Practice

- After a negative event, deliberately do something that has historically helped you recover (walk, connect with a friend, sleep, exercise) rather than waiting for the feeling to pass on its own.
