> **Principle 68** — Recognise chronic illness and mood: illness affects mental health

The diabetes evidence is the best-studied version of the principle. A meta-analysis of prospective studies found the relationship runs both directions, with unequal strength: baseline depression was followed by a 60% increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes, while baseline diabetes carried only a modest 15% increased risk of developing depression ([Mezuk et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19033418/)). A later meta-analysis of longitudinal HbA1c studies found the same two-way pattern in smaller print: higher baseline depressive symptoms predicted subsequent higher HbA1c (partial r = 0.07), and higher baseline HbA1c predicted an 18% increased risk of probable depression ([2021 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34407250/)).

The practical reading the authors offer is concurrent targeting rather than a promise that treating one cures the other: depressive symptoms and HbA1c levels could be addressed together in prevention and treatment, while the observed effect sizes were small and the cross-benefit question remains open ([2021 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34407250/)). The wider pattern the principle names, heart disease, autoimmune conditions and cancer carrying higher depression and anxiety rates, is a clinical commonplace, but the sources opened for this page concern diabetes; the broader associations stay in the parallels below.

> "Depression is associated with a 60% increased risk of type 2 diabetes. Type 2 diabetes is associated with only modest increased risk of depression." (Mezuk et al. 2008, [Diabetes Care](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19033418/))

**Plain words**
Illness and mood bill each other, and the diabetes numbers show the exchange rate: depression carried a 60% higher risk of later diabetes, and diabetes a modest 15% higher risk of later depression. Not weakness in either direction. The honest version of treating both accounts is concurrent care — watch the mood while you treat the illness — because the opened studies establish association, not that fixing one fixes the other.

*For example:* An imagined case: someone newly diagnosed with diabetes whose mood sinks over the following months is not being weak. The association runs that way, and the mood questions a diabetes nurse asks are the concurrent targeting the meta-analysis suggests ([2021 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34407250/)).

## Practice

- If you have a chronic illness, recognise its association with mood and raise it with your clinicians; address the illness (medical care) alongside the mood (psychological support) ([Mezuk et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19033418/); [2021 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34407250/)). Do not treat illness-related mood as purely psychological.
