> **Principle 68** — Recognise chronic illness and mood: illness affects mental health

For diabetes, a meta-analysis of prospective studies found the relationship runs both directions, with unequal strength: baseline depression was followed by a 60% increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes, while baseline diabetes carried only a modest 15% increased risk of developing depression ([Mezuk et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19033418/)). A later meta-analysis of eleven longitudinal studies covering 48,793 adults, most of them conducted among participants with diabetes, found the same two-way pattern in smaller print: higher baseline depressive symptoms were associated with subsequent higher HbA1c (partial r = 0.07), and higher baseline HbA1c was associated with higher odds of probable depression (OR 1.18) ([2021 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34407250/)).

The practical reading the authors offer is concurrent targeting rather than a promise that treating one cures the other: depressive symptoms and HbA1c levels could be addressed together in prevention and treatment, while the observed effect sizes were small and the cross-benefit question remains open ([2021 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34407250/)). The sources opened for this page concern diabetes.

> "Depression is associated with a 60% increased risk of type 2 diabetes. Type 2 diabetes is associated with only modest increased risk of depression." (Mezuk et al. 2008, [Diabetes Care](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19033418/))

**Plain words**
Diabetes and depression travel together more often than chance would suggest, in both directions, so mood belongs in the conversation when diabetes is being treated. The studies behind this are associations: they do not show that treating one fixes the other, only that attending to both at once is the reading the authors offer.

*For example:* An imagined case: a person with diabetes notices persistent low mood and mentions it at their next appointment. The studies do not establish what caused that person's mood change; they support raising the question.

## Psychology parallels

- **Chronic illness and depression**, chronic physical illness is associated with depression (Barnett et al., 2012; Naylor et al., 2012)
- **Bidirectional relationship**, illness worsens mood; mood affects illness outcomes (Naylor et al., 2012; Cohen et al., 2019)

## Practice

- If you have a chronic illness, recognise its association with mood and raise it with your clinicians; address the illness (medical care) alongside the mood (psychological support) ([Mezuk et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19033418/); [2021 meta-analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34407250/)). Do not treat illness-related mood as purely psychological.
