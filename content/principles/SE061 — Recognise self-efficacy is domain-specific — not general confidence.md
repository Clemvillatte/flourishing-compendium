> **Principle 61** — Recognise self-efficacy is domain-specific: not general confidence

Self-efficacy is not general; it is domain-specific. You can have high self-efficacy in your profession and low in relationships or health. Treating self-efficacy as general (or expecting transfer) is a mistake.

**Plain words** "Confident" is not a personality you have; it's a score you hold domain by domain. Brilliant at work, helpless in the kitchen, hopeless at dating — that's normal. The mistake is exporting confidence from one domain to "I'm a confident person" or one failure to "I'm incapable." Watch the domain boundaries.

*For example:* The surgeon who runs a theatre calmly and can't assemble a bookshelf doesn't say "I'm incompetent with my hands." He says "I haven't done this before." Same person, different domain. The bookshelf is not a verdict on the surgeon.
## Psychology parallels

- **Domain-specificity of self-efficacy**, self-efficacy is specific to domains and tasks (Bandura, 1997; Pajares, 1997)
- **Limited transfer**, efficacy in one domain does not automatically transfer to another (Bandura, 1997; Pajares, 1997)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Phronesis|Phronesis as domain-specific judgment]]** (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI)
- **Know thyself in each domain** (Delphic maxim)
- **[[The craft-specificity of skill|The craft-specificity of skill]]** (Dreyfus & Dreyfus, Mind Over Machine)

## Practice

- Identify your self-efficacy domain by domain: where do you feel capable, where not? Build efficacy in the specific domains where you need it. Do not expect confidence to transfer.
