> **Principle 175** — Distinguish between what you control and what you influence

The Stoic dichotomy comes first. Epictetus opens the Enchiridion: "Of things some are in our power, and others are not. In our power are opinion, movement towards a thing, desire, aversion, turning from a thing; and in a word, whatever are our acts. Not in our power are the body, property, reputation, offices (magisterial power), and in a word, whatever are not our own acts" (Enchiridion 1, tr. George Long, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/10661/pg10661.txt)). He adds that if you take what is not your own to be your own, "you will be hindered, you will lament, you will be disturbed, you will blame both gods and men" (same passage).

Epictetus draws a two-way line. This principle, which is a modern gloss and not Epictetus's wording, splits the far side of it by proximity: some things you can influence but not control (other people's behaviour and opinions, the outcomes of projects), and some you cannot touch at all (the weather, the past). The instruction follows: focus your energy on what you control, invest in what you can influence, and accept what you cannot control.

**Plain words** Before spending energy on anything, ask which of three bins it belongs in: yours to decide, yours to nudge, or not yours. Effort belongs in the first bin. The second takes effort too — you work on the nudge without counting on it landing. The third bin gets acceptance, not energy.

*For example (my illustration):* You can't make an editor accept your piece. You can control the draft, the pitch and the follow-up.
