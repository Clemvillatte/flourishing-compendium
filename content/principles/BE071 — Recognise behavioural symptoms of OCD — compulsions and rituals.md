> **Principle 71** — Recognise behavioural symptoms of OCD: compulsions and rituals

Compulsions (often called rituals) are excessive, repetitive, purposeful behaviours that people feel they must do to prevent or reduce the anxiety caused by their obsessive thoughts or to neutralise their obsessions: hand washing, ordering, checking, or mental acts like counting and silent repetition. The compulsion is the answer the disorder demands, not a preference. The behaviours or mental acts are either not realistically connected to what they are designed to prevent, or are clearly excessive.

The treatment with the strongest evidence is behavioural therapy, whose essential element in OCD is exposure and ritual prevention: gradually exposing patients to situations that trigger the anxiety-provoking obsessions and rituals while asking them not to perform their rituals. In a network meta-analysis of 54 trials (6,652 participants), behavioural therapy reduced symptom severity more than any other single option (mean difference -14.48 Y-BOCS points vs drug placebo), ahead of cognitive therapy (-13.36), CBT (-5.37), clomipramine (-4.72) and SSRIs (-3.49). The review's interpretation is measured: a range of interventions is effective, and in severe cases combination with medication is likely better than psychotherapy alone.

> "Compulsions (often called rituals) are excessive, repetitive, purposeful behaviors that people feel they must do to prevent or reduce the anxiety caused by their obsessive thoughts or to neutralize their obsessions." (MSD Manual Professional Version, [Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder](https://www.msdmanuals.com/professional/psychiatric-disorders/obsessive-compulsive-and-related-disorders/obsessive-compulsive-disorder-ocd))

**Plain words**
A compulsion is not a quirk and not a choice; it is a behaviour the disorder makes the person feel driven to perform. The tell is the fit between act and purpose: washing for thirty minutes is either not connected in a realistic way to germs, or clearly excessive. That gap, the ritual doing far more than the fear could justify, is the shape of the illness. The first-line treatment works on exactly that behaviour: face the trigger, don't perform the ritual.

*For example:* An imagined case: a person checks the stove nine times before leaving, not out of carelessness but because an intrusive thought of the house burning makes the tenth check feel required. The nine checks, not the worry alone, are what a clinician would recognise as the symptom.

## Practice

- If you have compulsive behaviours, recognise them as symptoms, not choices. Treatment is ERP: approach the obsession without doing the compulsion. Seek professional help; OCD is treatable.
