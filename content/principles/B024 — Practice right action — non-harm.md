> **Principle 24** — Practice right action: non-harm

AN 10.176 distinguishes bodily, verbal and mental conduct. Its three forms of bodily restraint concern killing, taking what is not given, and sexual misconduct; avoiding killing is paired with active concern for living beings (trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, "Skillful Bodily Action," [text](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an10/an10.176.than.html)).

> "He dwells with his rod laid down, his knife laid down, scrupulous, merciful, compassionate for the welfare of all living beings." (AN 10.176, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, [text](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an10/an10.176.than.html))

The teaching makes ethical conduct central to purification, regardless of whether someone performs the rituals discussed at the opening. Its account of consequences includes rebirth; it is not an empirical finding that harm reaches its author before anyone else (AN 10.176, [text](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an10/an10.176.than.html)).

### Plain words

Before acting, ask what you might damage, what you have permission to take, and whose boundaries are involved. The compendium applies the principle by making those questions specific to the situation. Concern for consequences does not depend on expecting an immediate reward for behaving well.

### For example

Imagine borrowing a neighbour's equipment for a job. Ask permission, agree how it may be used, and return it in the agreed condition. If something is damaged, acknowledge it and discuss repair; a good intention does not settle what you owe the other person.
