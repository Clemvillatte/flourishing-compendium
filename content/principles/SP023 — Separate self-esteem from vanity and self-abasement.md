> **Principle 23** — Separate self-esteem from vanity and self-abasement

In the Definitions of the Emotions, Spinoza defines self-approval as pleasure arising from a man's contemplation of himself and his own power of action, and its two neighbours: pride, thinking too highly of oneself from self-love, and humility, pain arising from contemplation of one's own weakness of body or mind. ([Ethics, Definitions of the Emotions XXV–XXVIII, Elwes tr.](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800)) In the explanation following the definition of pride, he notes that a man may think too meanly of himself, in the passage quoted below. The compendium's application is to separate what you know about your abilities from what you imagine other people think of them.

> "it may happen, that a man, sorrowfully regarding his own weakness, should imagine that he is despised by all men, while the rest of the world are thinking of nothing less than of despising him." (Ethics, Definitions of the Emotions XXVIII, Explanation, Elwes tr., [Gutenberg #3800](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800))

**Plain words** Pride is thinking too highly of yourself; humility is pain when you look at your own weakness ([Ethics, Definitions of the Emotions XXVIII and XXVI](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800)). Neither feeling tells you what you can actually do. This page proposes a two-column exercise: one column for what you know about your abilities, one for what you only assume other people think, and then a look at which column carries more weight in your day.

**For example (imagined):** A colleague makes a real mistake in a report and reads the team's silence afterward as contempt. The exercise is to distinguish the mistake, which is on the page, from the assumed audience, which is not.
