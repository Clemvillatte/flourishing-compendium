> **Principle 23** — Separate self-esteem from vanity and self-abasement

In the Definitions of the Emotions, Spinoza defines self-approval as pleasure arising from a man's contemplation of himself and his own power of action, and its two neighbours: pride, thinking too highly of oneself from self-love, and humility, pain arising from contemplation of one's own weakness of body or mind. ([Ethics, Definitions of the Emotions XXV–XXVIII, Elwes tr.](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800)) In the explanation following the definition of pride, he notes that a man may think too meanly of himself: sorrowfully regarding his own weakness, he may imagine that he is despised by all men, while the rest of the world is thinking of nothing less than of despising him. The compendium's application is to separate what you know about your abilities from what you imagine other people think of them.

> "it may happen, that a man, sorrowfully regarding his own weakness, should imagine that he is despised by all men, while the rest of the world are thinking of nothing less than of despising him." (Ethics, Definitions of the Emotions XXVIII, Explanation, Elwes tr., [Gutenberg #3800](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800))

**Plain words** Being upset about a weakness does not by itself mean you have underestimated yourself. For the exercise here, separate what you know about your abilities from what you imagine other people think.

**For example (imagined):** A colleague makes a real mistake in a report and reads the team's silence afterward as contempt. The exercise is to distinguish the mistake, which is on the page, from the assumed audience, which is not.
