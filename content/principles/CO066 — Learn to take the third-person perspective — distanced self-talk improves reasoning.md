> **Principle 66** — Learn to take the third-person perspective: distanced self-talk improves reasoning

The Kross et al. (2014) paper opens with the question the research was built to answer, and the answer it found was that a small change in grammar changes performance under stress.

> Does the language people use to refer to the self during introspection influence how they think, feel, and behave under social stress? (Kross et al., "Self-Talk as a Regulatory Mechanism: How You Do It Matters," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 106(2), 2014, [author-site PDF](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/emotion-selfcontrol-psych/wp-content/uploads/sites/1322/2024/07/KrossJ_Pers_Soc_Psychol2014Self-talk_as_a_regulatory_mechanism_How_you_do_it_matters.pdf))

Seven studies addressed that question (total N = 585). The two proof-of-principle studies showed that using one's own name or non-first-person pronouns during introspection increased self-distancing; the two performance studies showed the non-first-person group did better on rated speech and first-impression tasks and reported less distress; the appraisal studies showed they read upcoming stressors as more challenge and less threat; the meta-analysis found the effects held regardless of trait social anxiety.

> Together, these findings demonstrate that small shifts in the language people use to refer to the self during introspection consequentially influence their ability to regulate their thoughts, feelings, and behavior under social stress, even for vulnerable individuals. (Kross et al., 2014, same PDF)

The mechanism the paper proposes is that the third-person form works like the language you already use for other people, and that borrowing it for yourself buys a step of distance from the emotion without requiring effortful suppression.

**Plain words** The technique is one word of grammar. Instead of asking "why am I anxious about this," ask "why is [your name] anxious about this." The research found this shift is enough to change how people perform and how much distress they report under stress, without their having to argue themselves out of the feeling.

*For example:* Imagine someone preparing for an interview the night before, using their own name internally: "Maria has an interview tomorrow. Maria has prepared for this. Maria has done this before." The studies found that people instructed to reflect this way, rather than with "I," performed better on rated tasks and reported less distress.

## Practice

- When emotionally activated, describe the situation in the third person ("[Name] is feeling anxious about this meeting") or from a temporal distance ("how will this look in a year?"). This creates psychological distance and improves reasoning.
