> **Principle 62** — Learn to manage social media attention: infinite scroll and variable rewards

Tristan Harris, a former design ethicist at Google, set out the design pattern in a 2016 essay. Its slot-machine section names the mechanism:

> "One major reason why is the #1 psychological ingredient in slot machines: intermittent variable rewards."
>
> (Harris, 2016, "How Technology is Hijacking Your Mind", [Hijack #2](https://social.cs.washington.edu/cse481social/readings/W3S1/how-technology-hijacks-peoples-minds-TristanHarris.pdf))

His examples are the everyday gestures: pulling the phone out to see what notifications arrived, refreshing email, scrolling a feed to see what comes next. He adds that "Apps and websites sprinkle intermittent variable rewards all over their products because it's good for business." The same essay describes the removal of endings:

> "Take an experience that was bounded and finite, and turn it into a bottomless flow that keeps going."
>
> (Harris, 2016, Hijack #6)

For a cognitive correlate, a Stanford study compared chronic heavy and light media multitaskers:

> "Results showed that heavy media multitaskers are more susceptible to interference from irrelevant environmental stimuli and from irrelevant representations in memory."
>
> (Ophir, Nass and Wagner, 2009, [PNAS](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19706386/))

The abstract adds the task-switching result: heavy multitaskers "performed worse on a test of task-switching ability, likely due to reduced ability to filter out interference from the irrelevant task set". The study compares people by a media-multitasking index and reports associations; it does not test specific apps.

The compendium's exercise from these two sources: when using an app, notice which parts carry the two patterns (a reward arriving at unpredictable times; a stream with no natural end), and decide before opening the app what you came to do.

**Plain words**
Two things keep you in an app longer than the reason you opened it. The first is the chance of a small surprise: a like, a message, something new, arriving at a time you cannot predict. The second is the absence of an end: the feed does not finish, so nothing tells you it is time to stop. Knowing both, you can supply what the design leaves out: pick the thing you came to do, and set an end for the session.

*For example:* Imagine opening an app to answer one reply. The exercise: answer the reply, and set the end (close the app) before you begin scrolling the feed.

## Psychology parallels

- **Social media and attention**, heavy social media use is associated with poorer attentional control and higher distractibility (Ophir et al., 2009; Uncapher et al., 2016)
- **Infinite scroll removes natural stopping cues**, the absence of stopping points increases usage time (Harris, 2016; Eyal, 2014)
- **Variable rewards create compulsive checking**, unpredictable social feedback creates compulsive engagement (Rosen et al., 2013; Andreassen et al., 2016)

## Practice

As a compendium exercise, before opening an app, name what you came to do and what will count as done. When you notice you are scrolling past the reason you came, stop and check whether you want to continue.
