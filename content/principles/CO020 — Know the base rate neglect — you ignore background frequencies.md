> **Principle 20** — Know the base rate neglect: you ignore background frequencies

The 1974 Science paper illustrates with Steve, described by a former neighbor as very shy and withdrawn, invariably helpful, with little interest in people or the world of reality, a meek and tidy soul with a need for order and structure and a passion for detail ([Tversky & Kahneman 1974](https://stanford.edu/~jlmcc/Presentations/tversky_kahneman_1974.pdf)). Asked how likely Steve is a librarian rather than a farmer, people assess the degree to which he matches the librarian stereotype, and the paper's point is what that match leaves out: the fact that there are many more farmers than librarians in the population should enter into any reasonable estimate, but considerations of base-rate frequency do not affect the similarity of Steve to the stereotypes, so if people evaluate probability by representativeness, prior probabilities will be neglected.

The experiment behind the section made the neglect measurable. Subjects assessed brief personality descriptions of individuals allegedly sampled at random from a group of 100 professionals, engineers and lawyers, in one condition told the group was 70 engineers and 30 lawyers, in the other 30 and 70. The subjects used prior probabilities correctly when they had no other information, judging the probability that an unknown individual is an engineer at .7 and .3 in the two conditions; but prior probabilities were effectively ignored when a description was introduced, even when this description was totally uninformative.

> "In the case of Steve, for example, the fact that there are many more farmers than librarians in the population should enter into any reasonable estimate of the probability that Steve is a librarian rather than a farmer. Considerations of base-rate frequency, however, do not affect the similarity of Steve to the stereotypes of librarians and farmers." (Tversky & Kahneman 1974, [Science 185: 1124-1131, p. 1124](https://stanford.edu/~jlmcc/Presentations/tversky_kahneman_1974.pdf))

**Plain words**
The story about the person is loud and the population is quiet. A good match to a stereotype feels like information, and the count of how many of each kind there are to begin with gets dropped. The discipline the principle names: before asking how well the case fits, ask how common each candidate is.

*For example:* An imagined case: an investor reads a founder profile that matches the visionary stereotype trait for trait, and checks one more thing before committing money: what fraction of startups with this profile fail. The profile is unchanged; the check adds the count.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Base rate neglect / representativeness heuristic|Base rate neglect / representativeness heuristic]]**, people judge by similarity to a prototype rather than by prior probability (Kahneman & Tversky, 1973; Tversky & Kahneman, 1974)
- **Bayesian reasoning is unnatural but trainable**, explicit training in base rates improves judgment (Gigerenzer & Hoffrage, 1995; Sedlmeier & Gigerenzer, 2001)

## Practice

- When judging how likely something is, first ask: "how common is this category in the relevant population?" Then adjust for the specific case. Do not skip the base rate step.
