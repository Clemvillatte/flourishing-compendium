> **Principle 382** — Practise ecological justice

Schlosberg's central argument is that "a theory and practice of environmental justice necessarily includes distributive conceptions of justice, but must also embrace notions of justice based in recognition, capabilities, and participation" ([Schlosberg, Defining Environmental Justice, OUP 2007](https://academic.oup.com/book/4798)). Fair sharing of burdens is the starting point. Being recognised, having real capabilities and having a say are also part of the justice at issue.

(Schlosberg, 2007, Defining Environmental Justice; Bullard, 1990, Dumping in Dixie.)

### The evidence base

The study that launched the movement's data work was the 1987 United Church of Christ report, which "found race to be the most potent variable in predicting where these facilities were located—more powerful than household income, the value of homes and the estimated amount of hazardous waste generated by industry" ([UCC, Toxic Wastes and Race at Twenty, 1987-2007](https://www.ucc.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/toxic-wastes-and-race-at-twenty-1987-2007_Part1.pdf)). The 2007 update, co-authored by Bullard, reports that host neighbourhoods of commercial hazardous waste facilities are "56% people of color whereas non-host areas are 30% people of color". It also notes that other reviews found disparities "often ... modest" ([UCC, chapter 3 summary](https://www.ucc.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/toxic-wastes-and-race-at-twenty-1987-2007_Part5.pdf)). Both findings concern commercial hazardous waste sites in the United States.

I did not open Dumping in Dixie, and the claims about climate change in the principle are not tested by these sources.

**Plain words**
Ask who lives next to the harm and who gets the benefit. Then ask whether those people were consulted and counted.

**For example**
Look up your local council's most recent planning decision for a waste site, road or industrial facility. Note which neighbourhood it affects, whether residents were consulted, and what they said.
