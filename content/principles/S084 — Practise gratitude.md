> **Principle 206** — Practise gratitude

Marcus's first book is mostly a list of what he learned from particular people. Most entries start "From ...": "From my grandfather Verus I learned good morals and the government of my temper", and "From my mother, piety and beneficence" (Long's translation, Meditations I.1 and I.3). Only the closing section turns to explicit thanks: "To the gods I am indebted for having good grandfathers, good parents, a good sister, good teachers, good associates, good kinsmen and friends, nearly everything good" (I.17) ([Meditations Book 1, MIT Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.1.one.html)). The list names specific people and says what each gave him, which is what makes it more than a mood.

(Med. I.1, I.3, I.5, I.11, I.15, I.16, I.17; Seneca, Letters 9, 31; Disc. I.6, I.9, I.14, III.24.)

### What research adds

A preregistered meta-analysis of 145 papers (24,804 participants, 28 countries) found that gratitude interventions "led to small overall increases in well-being, Hedges' g = 0.19, 95% CI [0.15, 0.22]" ([PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2425193122)). An earlier meta-analysis of 38 studies concluded that the unique benefits of gratitude "may be overemphasized in the literature" ([Dickens 2017](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/01973533.2017.1323638)). So the effect is real and small.

I did not open Seneca's Letters 9 and 31 or Epictetus for this page.

**Plain words**
Gratitude works best when it is specific: a person, and what they did. Expect a modest lift, not a transformation.

**For example**
Once a week, write down one person and one concrete thing they did for you, as Marcus does in Book 1. If it fits, tell them.
