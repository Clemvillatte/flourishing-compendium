> **Principle 75** — Recognise that friendships change over time

Aristotle sorts friendships by what grounds them: usefulness, pleasure, or character. The first two are what he calls accidental, since the friend is loved for the benefit or pleasure supplied, and both dissolve when the ground moves. The durable kind is grounded in character, and even it can end when one party changes enough that the two no longer resemble each other. (NE VIII.3, VIII.13.)

> "Consequently friendships of this kind are easily broken off, in the event of the parties themselves changing, for if no longer pleasant or useful to each other, they cease to love each other. And utility is not a permanent quality; it differs at different times. Hence when the motive of the friendship has passed away, the friendship itself is dissolved, having existed merely as a means to that end." (NE VIII.3, Rackham)

> "This is clearly seen when a wide disparity arises between two friends in point of virtue or vice, or of wealth, or anything else; they no longer remain nor indeed expect to remain friends." (NE VIII, Rackham)

His formula for the break-up is mechanical rather than moral: the friendship existed for a reason, the reason went, and the friendship went with it.

**Plain words**
Friendships usually have a floor: the project, the neighborhood, the life stage, the shared appetite. When the floor goes, most of the friendship goes with it, and that is a description of the mechanism rather than an accusation. The durable ones are built on who the two of you are, not on what you exchange. Check which you are holding before you grieve a drift or blame yourself.

*For example:* Two parents who became close while their kids were in the same class often drift apart after the school years. That's a season ending, not a failure.
