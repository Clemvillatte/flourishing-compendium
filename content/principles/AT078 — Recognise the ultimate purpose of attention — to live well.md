> **Principle 78** — Recognise the ultimate purpose of attention: to live well

The case for attention as an instrument of living well has a clean empirical anchor. Killingsworth and Gilbert sampled real-time experience from 2,250 people via iPhone, gathering roughly a quarter of a million reports of what people were doing, thinking and feeling in the moment. Minds wandered in 46.9% of samples, people were less happy when wandering, and the study's third finding carried the principle: what people were thinking predicted their happiness better than what they were doing.

> "Third, what people were thinking was a better predictor of their happiness than was what they were doing. The nature of people's activities explained 4.6% of the within-person variance in happiness and 3.2% of the between-person variance in happiness, but mind wandering explained 10.8% of within-person variance in happiness and 17.7% of between-person variance in happiness." (Killingsworth & Gilbert, "A Wandering Mind Is an Unhappy Mind", Science 330, 2010, p. 932, [PDF](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/A_Wandering_Mind_Is_an_Unhappy_Mind.pdf))

The authors' conclusion names the trade-off: a human mind is a wandering mind and a wandering mind is an unhappy mind, and the ability to think about what is not happening is a cognitive achievement that comes at an emotional cost (p. 932).

So the felt quality of a life tracks where attention sits more closely than it tracks the activity on the calendar. That is the whole argument for treating attention as an instrument: it is trained, protected and spent for the sake of what it delivers — presence, engagement, a life actually experienced.

**Plain words**
Where your mind sits matters more than what your hands are doing. People checked at random moments were less happy when their mind had left the scene, whatever the scene was. So the point of attention training is not a polished lens. It is more of your life actually attended.

**For example**
The person who optimises morning routines and focus blocks, and has nothing scheduled in the freed-up hours but more optimisation. The lens is polished. A lens earns its keep by what you look at through it.

## Practice

- Ask: "what is my attention serving?" If it is serving distraction, rumination, or things that do not matter, redirect. Let the criterion be: does my attentional life support the life I want to live?
