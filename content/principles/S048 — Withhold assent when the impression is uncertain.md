> **Principle 170** — Withhold assent when the impression is uncertain

A conviction can feel settled before I have checked its grounds. Epictetus asks why we assent to something and answers that it appears true to us ([Discourses I.28, Long translation](https://www.stoicsource.com/epictetus/discourses/1.28/)). His account of understanding gives uncertainty a place:

> "Because this is the nature of the understanding: to incline to the true, to be dissatisfied with the false, and in matters uncertain to withhold assent." ([Discourses I.28](https://www.stoicsource.com/epictetus/discourses/1.28/))

The Enchiridion makes a narrower distinction. Someone bathes quickly or drinks a great quantity; chapter 45 asks us to describe that behaviour before calling it bad, since the person's reason for acting remains to be understood ([Elizabeth Carter translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html)).

That gives me a practical question: what do I actually know, and what judgement have I attached to it? "They have not answered" can be accurate while "they have no respect for me" remains uncertain. I can look for evidence that would distinguish my first explanation from another plausible one.

I also need a way to end the inquiry. For an ordinary decision, I can choose a proportionate check and a time to decide. When something needs attention now, I can respond to that need while leaving a person's motives unsettled. These are my applications of the discipline, including the responsibility to act under incomplete information.

**Plain words**
Keep an uncertain explanation open while checking it. Say what you know, identify what is missing and decide what evidence is enough for the decision in front of you.

**For example**

A colleague misses a deadline. In this imagined situation, you arrange cover for the urgent work and ask what happened before deciding that they were careless.
