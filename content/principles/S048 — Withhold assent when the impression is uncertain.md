> **Principle 170** — Withhold assent when the impression is uncertain

Epictetus grounds the discipline in the mind's own constitution:

> "What is the cause of assenting to anything? The fact that it appears to be true. It is not possible then to assent to that which appears not to be true. Why? Because this is the nature of the understanding: to incline to the true, to be dissatisfied with the false, and in matters uncertain to withhold assent." (Epictetus, [Discourses I.28, Long translation](https://www.stoicsource.com/epictetus/discourses/1.28/))

The same chapter turns the discipline into a diagnostic: those who follow every appearance are called madmen; and in the matters that decide happiness and unhappiness, where a scales-and-rule judgment is most needed, is exactly where people act rashly, straightway according to some appearance ([Disc. I.28](https://www.stoicsource.com/epictetus/discourses/1.28/)). The Enchiridion puts the same point as a hazard: assenting to an appearance you do not fully comprehend ([Ench. 45](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html)).

(Disc. I.28, II.18, III.2-3; Ench. 45.)

**Plain words**
The understanding already has a default policy: incline to the true, be dissatisfied with the false, and on the uncertain, wait. Withholding assent is not adding a rule; it is suspending an override. The rush comes from wanting the discomfort of not-knowing to end. The discipline treats that discomfort as the price of not believing the false thing.

*For example:*
You don't reply to a worrying message until you've heard the full story.
