> **Principle 31** — Learn self-forgiveness: release what cannot be changed

Marcus grounds the release in the structure of time:

> All those things at which thou wishest to arrive by a circuitous road thou canst have now, if thou dost not refuse them to thyself. And this means, if thou wilt take no notice of all the past, and trust the future to providence, and direct the present only conformably to piety and justice.

(Marcus Aurelius, Meditations XII.1, Long translation; [Gutenberg text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt))

Marcus directs attention toward present conduct. In VIII.36, he asks the reader to focus on the difficulty currently at hand. One suggested application is to distinguish actions still available, such as an apology or repair, from repeatedly reproaching yourself about the past.

**Plain words**
Name the action you regret. Identify any repair still possible and one step you can take now. Consider what further reflection would help you understand, decide or act.

*For example:* Imagine someone who hurt a friend years ago, apologised, and changed; the question the application raises is which repair remains available now.

## Psychology parallels

- **Self-forgiveness and well-being**, self-forgiveness is associated with better mental health and reduced rumination (Wohl et al., 2008; Davis et al., 2015)
- **Self-forgiveness involves acknowledging, making amends, and releasing**, a process, not a single act (Enright, 1996; Worthington et al., 2007)

- **[[Teshuva (return/repentance)|Teshuva (return/repentance)]]** (Jewish tradition, Maimonides)
- **[[Forgiveness|Forgiveness as release]]** (Christian tradition, Lord's Prayer)
- **The past is gone** (Stoics, Marcus Aurelius, Meditations)

## Practice

If you are carrying guilt or shame about a past action: acknowledge what you did, make amends if possible, commit to doing better, and deliberately release the self-punishment.
