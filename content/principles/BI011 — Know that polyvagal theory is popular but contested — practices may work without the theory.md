> **Principle 11** — Know that polyvagal theory is popular but contested: practices may work without the theory

Polyvagal theory, developed by Stephen Porges, proposes that the mammalian vagus has functionally distinct dorsal and ventral circuits that map onto behavioural states: a dorsal branch associated with defensive immobilisation, and a ventral branch associated with heart-rate control and social engagement. Therapists adopted the framework widely, and its vocabulary now runs through popular psychology.

Among psychophysiologists the theory is contested, and at the moment, openly so. A 2026 evaluation co-signed by 38 researchers put the case against: "All co-authors agree that major tenets of the PVT are not supported by past or current knowledge and, in several instances, are inconsistent with the broader evidence base", concluding that "the PVT is untenable, because it is not defensible based on existing neurophysiological and evolutionary evidence" (Grossman et al., 2026, Clinical Neuropsychiatry 23(1):100-112, [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12937499/)). Porges's response in the same issue disputes the framing: "It is concluded that the charge of scientific untenability does not apply to Polyvagal Theory as formulated, but instead reflects a critique that fails to engage the theory on its own terms" (Porges, 2026, Clinical Neuropsychiatry 23(1):113-128, [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12937496/)). The debate is live; neither side treats it as closed.

One practice that travels under the theory's flag has evidence of its own, and it is narrower than the theory's vocabulary suggests:

> "In recent years there has been substantial support for heart rate variability biofeedback (HRVB) as a treatment for a variety of disorders and for performance enhancement (Gevirtz, 2013)." (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014, Frontiers in Psychology 5:756, Abstract, [full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00756/full))

That evidence concerns structured heart-rate-variability biofeedback as an intervention; it is not automatically evidence for standalone breathing exercises, safety cues, social connection, humming or cold water. The mechanisms the paper discusses are candidate ones, in its own words: baroreceptor homeostasis is the "most supported possible mechanism", and a vagal-afferent pathway effect "has been proposed". Whether or not the polyvagal map survives, this intervention's evidence does not depend on it.

**Plain words** The theory gives people a ladder of nervous-system states. The critics say the anatomy behind the ladder does not hold up; the theory's author says the critique misses the theory's actual claims. Separately, one practice that often travels with the theory, structured heart-rate-variability biofeedback, has evidence from trials of that intervention. So when a course teaches "vagal toning" with humming or cold water, note that the opened evidence concerns biofeedback, not those exercises; whether they help is not something this page's sources establish. If the theory is ever settled, the exhale will still be there.

**For example:** Imagined: a course teaches "vagal toning" with humming and cold water. The mechanism on the label is contested science, and the opened trial evidence concerns a different, structured intervention. You do not need the theory to be true for a practice to be worth discussing with a professional.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Polyvagal theory (Porges)|Polyvagal theory (Porges)]]**, proposes distinct dorsal/ventral vagal branches mapping to emotional states (Porges, 1995, 2011)
- **The theory is contested (Grossman et al., 2026)**, 38 co-authors conclude major tenets are not supported by current knowledge; Porges's response in the same issue disputes the critique
- **One practice has its own evidence (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014)**, substantial support reported for structured heart-rate-variability biofeedback; not for the full bundle of practices that travel under polyvagal banners

## Practice

- If a therapist or course uses polyvagal language, ask which specific intervention is being proposed and what evidence supports that intervention. Do not treat the theory as settled science, and do not take the vocabulary as evidence for the exercises.
