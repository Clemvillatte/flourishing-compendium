> **Principle 11** — Know that polyvagal theory is popular but contested: practices may work without the theory

Polyvagal theory (Porges) proposes that the vagus nerve has distinct "dorsal" (immobilisation) and "ventral" (social engagement) branches that map to emotional states. The theory is widely used in therapy but is not supported by current neuroscience. The practices (breathing, safety cues, social connection) have evidence; the theory does not.

**Plain words** Polyvagal theory is popular and contested — the science is shaky, but many of the practices attached to it (breath, social engagement, safety cues) work anyway. Hold the theory loosely; use the tools freely. The map may be wrong while the road is real.

*For example:* The course that taught "vagal toning" with humming and cold water — the mechanism may be bunk, the calming often isn't. You don't need the theory to be true for the exhale to work.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Polyvagal theory (Porges)|Polyvagal theory (Porges)]]**, proposes distinct dorsal/ventral vagal branches mapping to emotional states (Porges, 1995, 2011)
- **The theory is scientifically contested**, critiques argue it is unfalsifiable and not supported by neuroanatomy (Grossman & Taylor, 2007; Grossman, 2023)
- **The practices (breathing, safety, co-regulation) have evidence**, but the specific mechanism is not the theory (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014; Thayer et al., 2012)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Distinguishing practice from theory|Distinguishing practice from theory]]** (Buddhism: raft parable)
- **Pragmatism: what works over what's true in theory** (James, Pragmatism)
- **[[Fallibilism|Fallibilism about mechanisms]]** (Peirce)

## Practice

- Use the practices (slow breathing, safety cues, social connection) without needing to accept the theory. If a therapist uses polyvagal language, the techniques may still help; just do not treat the theory as settled science.
