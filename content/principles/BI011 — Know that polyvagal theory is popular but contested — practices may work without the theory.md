> **Principle 11** — Know that polyvagal theory is popular but contested: practices may work without the theory

Polyvagal theory, developed by Stephen Porges, proposes that the mammalian vagus has functionally distinct dorsal and ventral circuits that map onto behavioural states: a dorsal branch associated with defensive immobilisation, and a ventral branch associated with heart-rate control and social engagement. Therapists adopted the framework widely, and its vocabulary now runs through popular psychology.

Among psychophysiologists the theory is contested, and at the moment, openly so. A 2026 evaluation co-signed by 38 researchers put the case against: "All co-authors agree that major tenets of the PVT are not supported by past or current knowledge and, in several instances, are inconsistent with the broader evidence base", concluding that "the PVT is untenable, because it is not defensible based on existing neurophysiological and evolutionary evidence" (Grossman et al., 2026, Clinical Neuropsychiatry 23(1):100-112, [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12937499/)). Porges's response in the same issue disputes the framing: "It is concluded that the charge of scientific untenability does not apply to Polyvagal Theory as formulated, but instead reflects a critique that fails to engage the theory on its own terms" (Porges, 2026, Clinical Neuropsychiatry 23(1):113-128, [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12937496/)). The debate is live; neither side treats it as closed.

The practices that travel under the theory's flag have evidence of their own that runs on separate rails. On slow breathing at around six breaths per minute: "In recent years there has been substantial support for heart rate variability biofeedback (HRVB) as a treatment for a variety of disorders and for performance enhancement (Gevirtz, 2013)" (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014, Frontiers in Psychology 5:756, [full text](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00756/full)). That paper's mechanisms are baroreflex and vagal-afferent ones; whether or not the polyvagal map survives, the practice evidence does not depend on it.

**Plain words** The theory gives people a ladder of nervous-system states, and the ladder is genuinely useful vocabulary for many. The critics say the anatomy behind the ladder does not hold up. Both can be true in daily life: slow breathing, cues of safety and social connection calm many people, and the trials supporting them do not lean on the contested theory. So when a course teaches "vagal toning" with humming or cold water, you can do the exercises without buying the map. If the theory is ever settled, the exhale will still be there.

**For example:** Imagined: a course teaches "vagal toning" with humming and cold water. The calming, when it comes, is real enough to use; the mechanism on the label is contested science. You do not need the theory to be true for the practice to be worth trying.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Polyvagal theory (Porges)|Polyvagal theory (Porges)]]**, proposes distinct dorsal/ventral vagal branches mapping to emotional states (Porges, 1995, 2011)
- **The theory is contested (Grossman et al., 2026)**, 38 co-authors conclude major tenets are not supported by current knowledge; Porges's response in the same issue disputes the critique
- **The practices have their own evidence (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014)**, substantial support reported for heart-rate-variability biofeedback across disorders and performance enhancement

## Practice

- Use the practices (slow breathing, safety cues, social connection) without needing to accept the theory. If a therapist uses polyvagal language, the techniques may still help; just do not treat the theory as settled science.
