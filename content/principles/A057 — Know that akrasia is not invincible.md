> **Principle 57** — Know that akrasia is not invincible

The person who knows the good but fails to do it is in a state that can be changed. ([Nicomachean Ethics VII.8, trans. W. D. Ross](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0054:book=7)) Habituation, practice, and the cultivation of phronesis gradually close the gap between knowing and doing; the gap is a developmental stage, not a metaphysical fact.

(NE VII.3, VII.8-10.)

> "it is the profligate who cannot be cured, whereas the unrestrained man can; for Vice resembles diseases like dropsy and consumption, whereas Unrestraint is like epilepsy, Vice being a chronic, Unrestraint an intermittent evil." (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VII.8, trans. W. D. Ross, [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0054:book=7))

The chapter grounds the curability claim in the akratic's own awareness: "the unrestrained man is aware of his infirmity," and "the former can easily be persuaded to change, but the latter cannot." ([NE VII.8, Ross](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0054:book=7)) Aristotle also records the habituation route: "those who have become unrestrained through habit are more easily cured than those who are unrestrained by nature, since habit is easier to change than nature." ([NE VII.10, Ross](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0054:book=7))

**Plain words**
The gap between knowing and doing isn't permanent. Aristotle contrasts the akratic person, who knows better and repents, with the intemperate one, who is convinced; the first can be cured, the second not. ([NE VII.8, Ross](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0054:book=7)) Habit and growing wisdom narrow the gap. So don't read your slips as final.

*For example:* A new runner who skips training days gets more reliable after a season of showing up, even without any new motivation.
