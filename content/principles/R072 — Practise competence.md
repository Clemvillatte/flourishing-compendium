> **Principle 334** — Practise competence

Tronto draws four ethical elements of care from four phases of caring: attentiveness, responsibility, competence and responsiveness. Competence belongs to the third phase: "The third phase of caring gives rise to the importance of competence in care-giving as a moral notion" ([Tronto, Moral Boundaries, text copy](https://syllabus.pirate.care/library/Joan%20C.%20Tronto/Moral%20Boundaries_%20A%20Political%20Argument%20for%20an%20Ethic%20of%20Care%20(416)/Moral%20Boundaries_%20A%20Political%20Argument%20for%20-%20Joan%20C.%20Tronto.pdf)). Her reason is practical: "Intending to provide care, even accepting responsibility for it, but then failing to provide good care, means that in the end the need for care is not met." She adds that "sometimes care will be inadequate because the resources available to provide for care are inadequate", so competence is not only a personal virtue (same text). She also says counting competence as part of the moral quality of care aligns her approach with moral consequentialism.

Critics treat this less simply. One Feminist Philosophy Quarterly article argues that competence is better understood as instrumental to responsiveness, not as an independent moral consideration ([Feminist Philosophy Quarterly](https://ojs.lib.uwo.ca/index.php/fpq/article/download/5781/4860/10455)). A study of general practitioners and partner violence uses the competence element to ask whether a doctor who notices a need and takes responsibility has the training or tools to respond ([Bristol University Press article](https://bristoluniversitypressdigital.com/downloadpdf/view/journals/ijcc/7/1/article-p149.pdf)).

(Tronto, 1993, Moral Boundaries, pp. 127-130.)

### Scope

The seed's pages 127-130 may not match where Tronto discusses competence; the Feminist Philosophy Quarterly article cites p. 133 for the quoted reasoning, and I did not check the printed book. I did not open the psychology parallel, Bandura 1977.

**Plain words**
Good intentions do not meet a need. Tronto holds that care must actually work, and that this takes skill and enough resources.

**For example**
Before you help with something, such as a relative's medication, ask what you do not know and who could check it.
