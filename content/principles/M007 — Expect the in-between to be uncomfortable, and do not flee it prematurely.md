> **Principle 7** — Expect the in-between to be uncomfortable, and do not flee it prematurely

Nepantla's signature affect is vertigo: the old certainties are gone and the new ones have not formed. Anzaldua's account of the mestiza names the condition:

> "In a constant state of mental nepantilism, an Aztec word meaning torn between ways, la mestiza is a product of the transfer of the cultural and spiritual values of one group to another." (Anzaldua, "La conciencia de la mestiza / Towards a New Consciousness", p. 100 of the printed text)

She describes the clash of frames as producing "mental and emotional states of perplexity" and "internal strife" that show up as "insecurity and indecisiveness" (p. 100), and she points toward a synthesis, "a third element which is greater than the sum of its severed parts" (pp. 101-102). This page's application to transitions: when the expectations you carry conflict, the discomfort of holding both is part of the account, not a malfunction; the counsel is patience with the condition rather than the first offer of closure (the quick new commitment, the premature identity, the borrowed certainty).

**Plain words**
Being between cultures or roles feels like two sets of instructions talking at once. That discomfort is part of Anzaldua's account, not a sign you are doing it wrong. Before deciding, list what each setting asks of you, then consider what to keep, reject or combine.

*For example:* Suppose you have moved between two workplaces with different norms of directness. In the middle months you can list what each workplace assumes about feedback, and what you want to keep from each.

## Psychology parallels

- **Rebound relationships and premature closure**, where the empirical record cuts against the folk wisdom: Brumbaugh and Fraley (2015) found people who began new relationships quickly after a breakup were not worse off, in some measures better — the principle's warning about premature closure is a claim about identity work, not a settled clinical finding about rebounds
