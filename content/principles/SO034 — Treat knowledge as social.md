> **Principle 34** — Treat knowledge as social

Treat knowledge as social. Human knowledge is not the achievement of isolated minds; it is a collective achievement, distributed across communities of inquiry, institutions, and practices. This is the sociocultural version of the principle (treat cognition as embodied and embedded). The adaptive stance is to participate in knowledge communities deliberately.

**Plain words** Knowledge is social — not isolated minds achieving truths, but communities of inquiry, institutions, and practices. Even your own "private" thoughts are built from inherited language and concepts. Your mind has co-authors.

*For example:* The brilliant insight you had in the shower — assembled from a teacher's phrase, a book's argument, a friend's joke. Original, yes. Solo, never.

### The community of inquiry

The classic statement of the social view of knowledge is Peirce's 1877 essay on belief. His account of inquiry begins with doubt, and frames it as a struggle rather than a private act:

> "The irritation of doubt causes a struggle to attain a state of belief. I shall term this struggle inquiry, though it must be admitted that this is sometimes not a very apt designation." (Charles S. Peirce, [The Fixation of Belief, 1877](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Fixation_of_Belief))

> "Hence, the sole object of inquiry is the settlement of opinion. We may fancy that this is not enough for us, and that we seek, not merely an opinion, but a true opinion." (Charles S. Peirce, [The Fixation of Belief, 1877](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Fixation_of_Belief))

The decisive move for this principle is what Peirce requires of a method for settling belief: it cannot be private.

> "Our external permanency would not be external, in our sense, if it was restricted in its influence to one individual. It must be something which affects, or might affect, every man. And, though these affections are necessarily as various as are individual conditions, yet the method must be such that the ultimate conclusion of every man shall be the same. To bring about this effect is the prerogative of the method of science." (Charles S. Peirce, [The Fixation of Belief, 1877](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Fixation_of_Belief))

And the hypothesis that makes a shared conclusion possible:

> "There are Real things, whose characters are entirely independent of our opinions about them; those Reals affect our senses according to regular laws, and, though our sensations are as different as are our relations to the objects, yet, by taking advantage of the laws of perception, we can ascertain by reasoning how things really and truly are; and any man, if he have sufficient experience and he reason enough about it, will be led to the one True conclusion." (Charles S. Peirce, [The Fixation of Belief, 1877](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Fixation_of_Belief))

The shower insight in the example is Peirce's point in miniature: the reason one person's reasoning can be checked by another is that the material it works from, language, concepts, and the world it refers to, was never that person's alone to begin with. The four methods Peirce catalogues, tenacity, authority, a priori taste, and science, are all ways communities settle belief, and his argument for science is precisely that it is the only one that builds the community into the method rather than into the enforcement.
