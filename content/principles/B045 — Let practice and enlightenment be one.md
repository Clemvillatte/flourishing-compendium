> **Principle 45** — Let practice and enlightenment be one

Dogen's clearest statement of this principle is not in the Genjokoan but in the Bendowa, his early manifesto written around age thirty: "In buddha-dharma, practice and enlightenment are one and the same. Because it is the practice of enlightenment, a beginner's wholehearted practice of the Way is exactly the totality of original enlightenment" ([Dogen, Bendowa, translation quoted in Maitri Retreats](https://www.maitri-retreats.com/post/practice-and-realization-are-one)). The claim is structural, not motivational. Practice is not a means that produces enlightenment as a later output; the wholehearted act of practice is already the thing it was supposed to lead to.

This is the teaching Dogen is most famous for — shusho-itto, practice and realization are one — and it inverts the ordinary picture of a path with a finish line. The person who sits is not a candidate working toward a distant graduation; in the act of wholehearted practice, the realization is already fully here ([Epoché, Practice Is Realization](https://www.epoche.online/lesson/dogen/dogen-shusho)). The consequence is that the student anxiety of "not being there yet" is not a sign of insufficient progress; it is a misunderstanding of what kind of activity this is.

> "In buddha-dharma, practice and enlightenment are one and the same. Because it is the practice of enlightenment, a beginner's wholehearted practice of the Way is exactly the totality of original enlightenment. For this reason, in conveying the essential attitude for practice, it is said not to wait for enlightenment outside of practice. Since it is already the enlightenment of practice, enlightenment is endless, practice is beginningless." (Dogen, Bendowa, [translation quoted in Maitri Retreats](https://www.maitri-retreats.com/post/practice-and-realization-are-one))

**Plain words** Most people treat practice as a payment toward a future result, which makes every session a small disappointment for not being the result yet. Dogen's claim is that the session is the result. The dancer absorbed in a step is not dancing toward a certificate; the step is the whole thing. This is not motivational language about the journey being the destination; it is a claim about what practice actually is — the thing, enacted, rather than a rehearsal for it.

*For example:* Two people sit for the same ten minutes. One is waiting for the meditation to start working, and evaluates each session by whether anything happened. The other treats the sitting itself as the complete thing, and the only question is whether they were actually present. Same ten minutes; one of them has been doing the thing the whole time.
