> **Principle 43** — Recognise the limits of analysis: overthinking can be worse than deciding

Wilson and Schooler tested the idea on strawberry jam. Students who "analyzed why they felt the way they did agreed less with the experts than students who did not" ([Wilson & Schooler, 1991, J Pers Soc Psychol 60(2), abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2016668/)). A second study on choosing college courses found the same pattern, with both kinds of introspection producing choices that "corresponded less with expert opinion."

The authors give a mechanism: "Analyzing reasons can focus people's attention on nonoptimal criteria, causing them to base their subsequent choices on these criteria." The reasons that are easiest to put into words are not always the ones that matter most. Both studies concern preferences (jams, courses), so the finding does not say that all careful analysis backfires. It does show that adding analysis to a choice can lower its quality, which is the limit this principle asks you to watch for. The satisficing principle ([[CO044 — Learn to satisfice — good enough is often optimal]]) covers the cost side.

**Plain words** More thinking is not automatically better thinking. When you force yourself to list reasons for a gut preference, the reasons that come easily to mind take over, and they may not be the ones that actually made you like the thing. For a choice of taste, there is a point where another round of analysis starts to move you away from your best judgment.

*For example:* In the jam study, the students who analyzed why they liked the jams ended up agreeing less with the expert ratings than the students who just chose. Before a taste-based choice, such as which jam, course or paint colour, set a short time limit and notice whether your reasons are tracking what you liked.

## Psychology parallels

- **Analysis paralysis / maximising**, people who seek to maximise (find the absolute best option) are less satisfied than those who satisfice (find a good-enough option) (Schwartz et al., 2002; Schwartz, 2004)
- **[[Paradox of choice|Paradox of choice]]**, more options can lead to worse decisions and lower satisfaction, though the effect's magnitude is debated (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000; Scheibehenne et al., 2010)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[The sufficient good|The sufficient good]]** (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics)
- **[[Wu wei|Wu wei as non-overthinking]]** (Daoism, Daodejing 48)
- **Perfect is the enemy of good** (Voltaire, Dictionnaire Philosophique)

## Practice

- For most decisions, satisfice rather than maximise. Set a time limit for deliberation. Ask: "is more information going to change my decision?" If not, decide.
