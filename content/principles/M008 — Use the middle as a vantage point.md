> **Principle 8** — Use the middle as a vantage point

The person in nepantla sees two worlds and belongs fully to neither, which is a position with an epistemic yield, not only a wound. Anzaldua's image for the position is a deliberate double stance:

> "At some point, on our way to a new consciousness, we will have to leave the opposite bank, the split between the two mortal combatants somehow healed so that we are on both shores at once and, at once, see through serpent and eagle eyes." (Anzaldua, "La conciencia de la mestiza / Towards a New Consciousness", pp. 100-101 of the printed text)

From the middle one can notice what insiders of each side can miss: the juncture where the mestiza stands is "where phenomena tend to collide" and "where the possibility of uniting all that is separate occurs" (p. 101). This page's application: the immigrant child translating between parents and school, the mediator, the scholar between fields may each be able to notice assumptions and mismatches that monoculture lacks; whether they do is something to check, not assume. Practically, in your in-between places, write down what you can see from there that neither side seems to, then verify it with people on both sides.

**Plain words**
When two groups expect different things from you, compare their assumptions before choosing how to respond. The inventory: what each side takes for granted, and where those assumptions mismatch. The check is to ask people on both sides whether you have understood them; keep the list of what only your position shows.

*For example:* Imagine a child of immigrants translating between parents and school: each side misreads the other in ways the child can sometimes notice, being the only one regularly inside both.
