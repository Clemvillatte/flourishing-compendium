> **Principle 11** — Treat the sociocultural level as plural

Markus and Kitayama open their 1991 paper with this claim: "People in different cultures have strikingly different construals of the self, of others, and of the interdependence of the 2." In many Asian cultures the emphasis is on "attending to others, fitting in, and harmonious interdependence", while in American culture "individuals seek to maintain their independence from others by attending to the self and by discovering and expressing their unique inner attributes" (Markus & Kitayama, 1991, Psychological Review 98(2), 224-253, abstract). Henrich, Heine and Norenzayan add a warning about method: WEIRD samples are "frequent outliers", so "there are no obvious a priori grounds for claiming that a particular behavioral phenomenon is universal based on sampling from a single subpopulation" (Behavioral and Brain Sciences 33, 2010, 61-83, abstract).

Other traditions describe wellbeing in their own terms. Durie's Te Whare Tapa Whā pictures Māori health as a meeting house with four sides, spiritual, mental, physical and family, and says that "if one of the 4 dimensions is missing or damaged in some way, a person or collective may become 'unbalanced' and subsequently unwell" ([New Zealand Ministry of Health](https://www.health.govt.nz/maori-health/maori-health-models/te-whare-tapa-wha)). Southern African ubuntu is expressed in "a person is a person through other persons" ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/hunhu-ubuntu-southern-african-thought/)).

None of these sources addresses whether pluralism risks relativism, so that part of the principle rests on the wiki's own reasoning.

**Plain words**
No single culture holds the final version of a good life. Yours is one version, built from its own assumptions. Learn how others define flourishing before you rank them.

**For example**
An Australian and a Japanese colleague disagree about how much individuality a good life needs. Each tells you what they would lose without their own way.
