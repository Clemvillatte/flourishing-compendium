> **Principle 53** — Work with shame: it is about the self, not the act

Tangney and colleagues discuss Lewis's distinction between evaluating the whole self negatively and evaluating a particular behavior negatively. The two emotions then pull in different directions. Shame corresponds with attempts to deny, hide, or escape the shame-inducing situation; guilt corresponds with reparative actions including confessions, apologies, and undoing the consequences of the behavior. In their review of research on moral emotions, Tangney, Stuewig and Mashek also record that proneness to shame is related to a wide variety of psychological symptoms ([Tangney et al., 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3083636/)).

> "According to Lewis (1971), shame involves a negative evaluation of the global self; guilt involves a negative evaluation of a specific behavior." (Tangney, J. P., Stuewig, J., & Mashek, D. J. (2007). Moral Emotions and Moral Behavior. Annual Review of Psychology, 58, 345-372, section "What's the difference between shame and guilt?", [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3083636/), doi:10.1146/annurev.psych.56.091103.070145)

**Plain words** After a mistake, notice whether you are judging one action or condemning yourself as a person. In this distinction, guilt concerns the action; shame concerns the person. As a reflection exercise, describe what happened specifically and consider whether there is something you can put right.

*For example (imagined):* Two employees miss the same deadline. One spends the week dodging the manager's corridor (shame: I am a fraud); the other sends the revision plan by Friday (guilt: the miss was mine).

## Practice

- Describe the action you regret separately from a judgment about your whole self. Consider whether an apology or a practical repair is possible.
