> **Principle 62** — Moderate the pleasures of the table and of comfort

Spinoza's rule is exact, and it is a body-mechanics rule rather than a moral one: localized pleasure, stimulation (titillatio), can be excessive and bad because it fixes one part of the body and renders the rest numb, while mirth cannot be excessive because it affects all parts equally; and the pleasures of eating, drinking, and comfort, when they become the end, are the avarice and lust that the note to IV P44 names species of madness. The body's pleasures are instruments of restoration and capacity, and they become prisons exactly when they stop serving those functions. Practically, apply the power test to each comfort: does it restore, or does it anesthetize? Restoration earns its place; anesthesia needs a schedule.

(Ethics IV, P43 with its whole-body versus localized distinction; P44's note on avarice, ambition, and lust as species of madness.)

**Plain words** The body-mechanics rule: localized pleasure (stimulation) can be excessive because it fixes one part of the body and renders the rest numb, while mirth spreads. Moderate the pleasures of the table and of comfort — not a moral rule, a mechanics rule about attention and sensation.

*For example:* The fourth hour of the same game — the eyes still stimulated, the rest of the body quietly numb — versus the walk with friends where everything is engaged at a lower wattage. Same "fun" on the calendar; only one of them leaves the system stronger.
