> **Principle 73** — Recognise attentional changes with ageing: some decline, some stability

Two findings anchor this principle. Salthouse's summary of the cognitive-ageing evidence:

> age-related declines in measures of cognitive functioning (a) are relatively large, (b) begin in early adulthood, (c) are evident in several different types of cognitive abilities, and (d) are not always accompanied by increases in between-person variability.
>
> (Salthouse, "What and When of Cognitive Aging", Current Directions in Psychological Science 13(4), 2004)

The stability half comes from Hartshorne and Germine's lifespan data (48,537 web participants plus normative test data): "Our results reveal considerable heterogeneity in when cognitive abilities peak: some abilities peak and begin to decline around high school graduation; some abilities plateau in early adulthood, beginning to decline in the 30s; still others do not peak until the 40s or later" (2015). In their data, processing speed peaks in the late teens, the working memory tasks near 30, emotion recognition shows "a long period of relative stability in performance between the ages of 40 and 60", and vocabulary peaks later still, around 65 in the web sample. The compendium's reading: the attention portfolio shifts with age rather than shrinking uniformly.

**Plain words**
"Getting slower" is one line in a whole ledger. Speed peaks early; face-reading and vocabulary keep improving for decades after. The practical task is knowing which ability is in which phase and scheduling accordingly.

*For example:*
Imagined: a 58-year-old banker a step behind the graduate on a new spreadsheet shortcut, and the first to notice the number that does not reconcile. Different peaks, same meeting.

## Practice

- As you age, do not fight the decline in processing speed. Lean into the strengths: experience, wisdom, positivity bias. Use compensatory strategies (notes, reminders, routines) to support attention.
