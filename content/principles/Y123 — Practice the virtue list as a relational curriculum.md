> **Principle 123** — Practice the virtue list as a relational curriculum

The list sits in Chapter 13, verses 8 to 12, and Krishna does not call it a list of virtues. He calls it knowledge:

> Humbleness; freedom from hypocrisy; non-violence; forgiveness; simplicity; service of the Guru; cleanliness of body and mind; steadfastness; and self-control; dispassion toward the objects of the senses; absence of egotism; keeping in mind the evils of birth, disease, old age, and death; non-attachment; absence of clinging to spouse, children, home, and so on; even-mindedness amidst desired and undesired events in life; constant and exclusive devotion toward Me; an inclination for solitary places and an aversion for mundane society; constancy in spiritual knowledge; and philosophical pursuit of the Absolute Truth—all these I declare to be knowledge, and what is contrary to it, I call ignorance.

(Bhagavad Gita 13.8-12, trans. Swami Mukundananda, [holy-bhagavad-gita.org](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/13/verse/8/).)

Several items do name a way of treating others: non-violence, forgiveness, service of the Guru, and not clinging to spouse, children and home. Others are about the self, such as self-control or keeping in mind the evils of birth, disease, old age and death. And the same list includes "an inclination for solitary places and an aversion for mundane society". So the text does not support calling the whole list social, and the claim that most clauses are about relationships is not borne out by this translation.

The reading of the list as a relational curriculum, with one clause practised each week and a Sunday review, is this page's own proposal. It is a reasonable use for the clauses that do concern others. Verse 13.7 only introduces "the field", which comes just before the list.

**Plain words**

The Gita's list is long, and some of its items are about how you meet people while others are about being alone. If you want to practise it, pick a clause that involves someone else, like forgiveness or non-violence, and see how it holds up in a real conversation. Leave the solitude clause for solitude.

**For example**

A man chooses forgiveness for a week and keeps a note each evening of the moments it was tested. On Sunday he sees that the hardest test was a sibling, and the easiest was a stranger.
