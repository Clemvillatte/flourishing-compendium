> **Principle 10** — Recognise that affect has temporal structure: intensity, duration, variability, recovery

A feeling is not just what it is; it is how intense, how long, how variable, and how quickly it returns to baseline. These four properties are separately regulated and separately relevant to well-being. Chronic low-grade dysphoria is a different problem from acute panic; a quick return to baseline after a setback is a different capacity from never being triggered.

**Plain words** A feeling isn't just what it is — it's how intense, how long, how variable, and how fast it returns to baseline. Four properties, separately regulated and separately trainable. Track the structure, not just the label.

*For example:* Two people report "anxiety" — one has spikes that resolve in an hour, one has a flat hum that lasts weeks. Same word, different animals, different treatments. The label alone was hiding the diagnosis.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Affective dynamics framework|Affective dynamics framework]]**, intensity, duration, variability, and recovery are distinct properties with distinct correlates (Houben et al., 2015)
- **Emotional inertia (slow recovery) predicts depression**, difficulty recovering from negative affect is a risk factor for depressive episodes (Kuppens et al., 2010, 2012)
- **Emotional variability is not uniformly bad; the relationship depends on context and regulation capacity** (Hollenstein, 2015)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Panta rhei|Panta rhei (everything flows)]]** (Heraclitus, fragments)
- **Process over product** (Whitehead, Process and Reality)

## Practice

- After an emotional episode, note: how intense (0-10), how long it lasted, how quickly you returned to baseline, and whether it fluctuated or was flat.
