> **Principle 10** — Recognise that affect has temporal structure: intensity, duration, variability, recovery

Affect research has converged on a simple structural point: a feeling is not fully described by its name or its average level. A meta-analysis by Houben and colleagues distinguishes variability, instability, and inertia as separately measurable properties of emotional life, each with its own relationship to well-being. (Houben, Van Den Noortgate & Kuppens, 2015.)

> "Not only how good or bad people feel on average, but also how their feelings fluctuate across time is crucial for psychological health." (Houben et al., 2015, Psychological Bulletin)

> "The findings demonstrate that psychological flourishing is characterized by specific patterns of emotional fluctuations across time, and provide insight into what constitutes optimal and suboptimal emotional functioning." (Houben et al., 2015)

The abstract operationalizes the constructs it tests: variability as the within-person standard deviation of emotions across time, instability as the magnitude of consecutive emotional changes, and inertia as the autocorrelation of emotions over time. The four-property tracking named in this principle's title is this compendium's suggested exercise, framed with those dynamics; the meta-analysis itself does not measure intensity, duration, or recovery as such.

**Plain words**
A feeling comes with more than a label: how strong, how long, how jumpy, how fast it fades. Noting those properties before reaching for the word is the exercise; the word is a summary, not the phenomenon.

*For example:* Imagine two people both reporting "anxiety". One describes spikes that resolve within an hour; the other describes a flat hum lasting weeks. The word is the same; the reported patterns are not.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Affective dynamics framework|Affective dynamics framework]]**, intensity, duration, variability, and recovery are distinct properties with distinct correlates (Houben et al., 2015)
- **Emotional inertia (slow recovery) predicts depression**, difficulty recovering from negative affect is a risk factor for depressive episodes (Kuppens et al., 2010, 2012)
- **Emotional variability is not uniformly bad; the relationship depends on context and regulation capacity** (Hollenstein, 2015)

## Practice

- After an emotional episode, note: how intense (0-10), how long it lasted, how quickly you returned to baseline, and whether it fluctuated or was flat.
