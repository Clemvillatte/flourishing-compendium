> **Principle 24** — Expect latent material to surface when practice deepens

Sutra 2.4 makes avidya the field of the other four hindrances "whether they be dormant or attenuated or intercepted or sustained" (trans. Woods, p. 106). A dormant hindrance, Vyasa's commentary explains, is "the tendency [of the hindrances] which remain merely potential in the mind"; its awakening is "the coming face-to-face with the [particular] object" (p. 107). The gloss's image for this potentiality is curd: dormant hindrances "exist merely potentially, as curds exist in milk" (p. 108). A hindrance whose seed has been burned no longer awakens even face-to-face with its object, while an attenuated one is thinned by cultivating its opposite (p. 107).

### The gaps in practice

Sutra 4.27 adds that a mind turned toward discrimination still has interruptions:

> In the intervals of this [mind-stuff] there are other presented-ideas [coming] from subliminal-impressions.

(Yoga Sutra 4.27, trans. Woods, p. 339.)

Vyasa places these interruptions in a mind whose flow is toward nothing but discernment of the difference between the sattva and the Self; the intruding ideas come "From the dwindling seeds, from previous subliminal-impressions" (p. 339). Sutra 4.28 sets the escape from these impressions "like [the escape from] the hindrances" (p. 340).

### What the principle adds

The opened passages establish that a hindrance can be quiet without being gone, and that old ideas recur in the gaps of practice even alongside developed discrimination. They do not say that deepening practice itself causes old material to surface; that expectation is the principle's framing of the two passages, kept in the title.

**Plain words** The useful distinction is between a calm spell and a tested one. If nothing angered you this month, the month does not show whether the anger is gone; it may show only that its occasion never appeared. In the commentary's account, what wakes a dormant hindrance is the encounter with its object, and a quiet period is not that encounter.

*For example:* Imagined: you feel calm for a month after a falling-out and read the calm as forgiveness. At a party you meet the other person and the anger returns at once. The month was an interval without the object; the meeting supplied the test the month lacked.

(Yoga Sutra 2.4 and 4.27-4.28 with Vyasa's Bhasya and Vacaspati Misra's gloss, trans. James Haughton Woods, The Yoga-System of Patanjali (1914), pp. 106-108, 339-340, Internet Archive, opened 2026-10-21; edition identity confirmed against the archive.org catalogue page.)
