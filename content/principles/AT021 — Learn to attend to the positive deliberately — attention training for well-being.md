> **Principle 21** — Learn to attend to the positive deliberately: attention training for well-being

Two different things go by the name of attention training, and the evidence differs.

### Lab training of gaze

Wadlinger and Isaacowitz trained 55 people to look toward positive words instead of neutral ones. Afterward, the group trained toward positive information "looked significantly less at the negative images in the visual stress task", while those trained toward neutral information did not ([Wadlinger & Isaacowitz, 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18266522/)). A meta-analysis of 49 trials of this kind of bias modification for anxiety and depression was much less encouraging:

> CBM may have small effects on mental health problems, but it is also very well possible that there are no significant clinically relevant effects.
>
> Cristea, Kok & Cuijpers, British Journal of Psychiatry (2015)

### Writing down what went well

A different line of work tested a writing exercise. In Seligman and colleagues' randomized, placebo-controlled Internet study, participants wrote "three things that went well each day and their causes every night for one week". Of 577 participants at baseline, 411 completed every follow-up. This was one of two exercises that "made people happier (and less depressed) up to six months later" ([Seligman et al., 2005](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Seligman-PosPsychProgress.pdf)). Longer benefits were associated with continuing the exercises. The study did not establish an attention-network mechanism or compare the exercise directly with gaze training.

(Seligman et al., 2005; Wadlinger & Isaacowitz, 2008; Cristea et al., 2015.)

**Plain words** Writing about good events and practising where to look on a screen are different activities with different evidence. The writing study supports a possible benefit in its sample, not a promise to every reader. If you try the exercise, there is no need to deny what went badly that day.

For example: before bed you record a meal with a friend and a piece of work finished, then write what helped each happen. This does not require calling the whole day good.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Positive attention training / attentional deployment toward positive stimuli|Positive attention training / attentional deployment toward positive stimuli]]**, training attention toward positive information changed gaze under stress in one laboratory experiment (Wadlinger & Isaacowitz, 2008)
- **Gaze training toward positive information is not established as a depression treatment**, a meta-analysis of 49 trials found small, mostly non-significant effects in patient samples (Cristea et al., 2015)

## Practice

- Deliberately attend to positive aspects of experience. When you notice something good, linger on it. Practise gratitude as an attentional exercise: notice what is working, what is beautiful, what you appreciate.
