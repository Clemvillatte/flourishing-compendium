> **Principle 63** — Recognise attentional costs of media multitasking: heavy multitaskers have worse attention

The evidence behind this principle is an association, not a training experiment. Ophir, Nass and Wagner sorted people by how much they used several media at once, then compared the two groups on tests of cognitive control. The heavy multitaskers were more susceptible to interference and performed worse on a test of task-switching ability. (Ophir et al., 2009.)

> "Results showed that heavy media multitaskers are more susceptible to interference from irrelevant environmental stimuli and from irrelevant representations in memory." (Ophir et al., 2009, [PNAS 106(37):15583-15587](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2747164/))

Participants were grouped by reported media habits rather than assigned to acquire those habits. These comparisons do not determine whether media use caused the observed differences. (Ophir et al., 2009.)

The finding has since been tested again. Wiradhany and Nieuwenstein report two replication studies in which, of 14 tests, five yielded statistically significant effects in the predicted direction, and two of those effects held in a more conservative Bayesian analysis; their meta-analysis of 39 effect sizes yielded a weak association that turned nonsignificant after correction for small-study effects, and they conclude by questioning the existence of an association between media multitasking and distractibility in laboratory tasks of information processing. The compendium keeps this principle's title claim as the original study's association, read against that later record. (Wiradhany & Nieuwenstein, 2017.)

**Plain words**
In that study, the group reporting more simultaneous media use had more trouble ignoring information that was irrelevant to the tests. This group comparison cannot tell you that your own media habits caused an attention problem, and later replications questioned whether the association holds in laboratory tasks at all.

*For example:* As a compendium exercise, record which media you use while studying and which interruptions you choose to postpone.

- **Media multitasking and cognitive outcomes, contested**, later replications and a meta-analysis questioned the distractor-filtering association in laboratory tasks (Wiradhany & Nieuwenstein, 2017)

## Practice

- Reduce media multitasking: do not use phone while watching TV, do not have multiple screens open while working, do not text while doing other things. Single-tasking is both more effective and more satisfying.
