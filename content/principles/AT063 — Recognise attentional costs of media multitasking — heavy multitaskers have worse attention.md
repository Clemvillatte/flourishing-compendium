> **Principle 63** — Recognise attentional costs of media multitasking: heavy multitaskers have worse attention

Heavy media multitasking (using multiple media simultaneously or rapidly switching) is associated with poorer sustained attention, greater distractibility, and worse attentional control. This is a trained deficit.

**Plain words** Heavy media multitaskers have measurably worse attention — the switching practised all day becomes the brain's default setting. The feed trains a mind that can't sit still anywhere. You are what you switch.

*For example:* The student who "studies" with three screens has trained a mind that treats the exam as just another boring tab. The exam notices.
## Psychology parallels

- **Media multitasking and attention (Ophir et al., 2009)**, heavy media multitaskers show worse sustained attention and greater distractibility (Ophir et al., 2009; Uncapher et al., 2016)
- **Media multitasking is associated with poorer academic and cognitive outcomes**, heavy multitaskers show worse performance on attention and memory tasks (Ophir et al., 2009; Uncapher et al., 2016)

## Practice

- Reduce media multitasking: do not use phone while watching TV, do not have multiple screens open while working, do not text while doing other things. Single-tasking is both more effective and more satisfying.
