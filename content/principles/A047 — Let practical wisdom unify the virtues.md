> **Principle 47** — Let practical wisdom unify the virtues

[[T002 — Aristotle|Aristotle]] closes Book VI with a two-way lock: "it is not possible to be good in the true sense without Prudence, nor to be prudent without Moral Virtue" ([NE VI.13, Rackham](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0054:book=6)). Someone can have natural courage or natural honesty the way a child has them, as dispositions that work until the situation turns. Prudence is what settles them into virtues, and it comes as a set: "For if a man have the one virtue of Prudence he will also have all the Moral Virtues together with it" (VI.13).

**Plain words** Aristotle's claim, read plainly, is that virtues come as a bundle or not at all, and the reason is a piece of engineering rather than piety. Each virtue is a reading of a situation, seeing what fairness asks here, what courage asks here, and the eye doing that reading is one eye, not a drawer of separate glasses. So a person honest at home and crooked at work is not running two characters, he is running one with a blind spot, and training that drills virtues one by one keeps polishing lenses for an eye that never learns to read.

*For example:* pick two virtues you are working on, say honesty and patience. Take one situation from this week where they pulled against each other, and write down what the reading of the situation (not the rule, the reading) would have had each one do.
