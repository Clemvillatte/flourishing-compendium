> **Principle 213** — Practise negative visualisation

> Let us summon to our minds beforehand, not as great an evil as oftentimes happens, but the very greatest evil that possibly can happen. We must reflect upon fortune fully and completely.

(Seneca, Letter 91, section 8, trans. Richard Gummere, [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_91), page opened.)

The same letter gives the reason: "Nothing, whether public or private, is stable," and "evils burst forth from sources whence they were least expected." Epictetus applies the practice to what one loves: "If you kiss your child, or your wife, say that you only kiss things which are human, and thus you will not be disturbed if either of them dies" (Enchiridion 3, [Carter trans., Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html), opened). Both texts aim at steadiness when loss arrives. Neither mentions appreciation, so the entry's second purpose, noticing what is present, is the compendium's addition. The other Seneca letters and the Meditations passages cited were not opened.

For psychology, Koo, Algoe, Wilson and Gilbert asked people to imagine that a positive event "might never have happened." Those who did reported more positive affect, and forecasters did not predict this ([Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2008; PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18954203/)). That task subtracts a past event and does not rehearse a future loss, so it is a nearby finding and not a test of the Stoic practice. Oettingen, Meichenbaum and Taleb were not opened.

**Plain words** Seneca advises picturing, ahead of time, the worst that could happen, because fortune is unstable. Epictetus tells his reader to remember that a loved one is mortal. Both aim at being less shaken when loss comes.

*For example (imagined):* While a partner is in the next room, someone pictures life without them for a minute, then goes in and listens to them talk.
