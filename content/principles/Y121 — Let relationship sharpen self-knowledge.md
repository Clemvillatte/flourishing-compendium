> **Principle 121** — Let relationship sharpen self-knowledge

The Gita's picture of the settled yogi has a relational test in it:

> I regard them to be perfect yogis who see the true equality of all living beings and respond to the joys and sorrows of others as if they were their own.

(BG 6.32, trans. Swami Mukundananda, [holy-bhagavad-gita.org](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/6/verse/32/).)

Mukundananda's commentary explains the sign by the body: "We consider all the limbs of our body as ours, and are equally concerned if any of them is damaged." In the same way, he says, such yogis "consider the joys and sorrows of others as their own".

So the verse makes the other person's experience the measure of the yogi's vision. It does not say that a difficult relationship is a test of whether your calm is real. That is this page's reading, and it is a fair extension: if equanimity cannot survive a person you find hard, it was not yet the kind the verse describes. The verse's measure is feeling the other person's joys and sorrows, which goes further than staying composed in front of them.

The suggestion to treat each flare-up as an experiment, asking what it showed about your practice, is a modern exercise.

**Plain words**

It is easy to be calm on your own. The verse asks you to feel other people's good and bad as yours. If you can do that with someone who irritates you, the practice is working. If you can't, that tells you where to work. After a flare-up, ask what it showed.

**For example**

A meditator who is serene on retreat finds that fifteen minutes with a sibling brings back old irritation. They take it as information, write down what set it off, and bring it to their next sitting.
