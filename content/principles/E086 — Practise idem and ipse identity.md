> **Principle 478** — Practise idem and ipse identity

Ricoeur splits identity in two so that a single word stops covering both. In Oneself as Another (pp. 116ff.) he separates "identity as sameness (Latin idem, German Gleichheit, French mêmeté)" from "identity as selfhood (Latin ipse, German Selbstheit, French ipséité)" (quoted in Dierckxsens, 2017). The Stanford Encyclopedia sums up the difference: "Idem identity is the identity of something that is always the same which never changes, ipse identity is sameness across and through change." It adds: "Self-identity involves both dimensions: I am and am not the person I was ten years ago" (SEP, Paul Ricoeur).

### What counts as each

Idem covers what stays put. Dierckxsens gives the example of a person's genetic code (Ricoeur 1992, p. 117, as reported). The clearest case is character, which Rodrigues glosses as "the set of lasting dispositions by which a person is recognized" and Ricoeur calls the "what of the who" (Oneself as Another, pp. 121-122, as reported by Rodrigues, 2023). Ipse is "in the sense of the self-designation of a subject of discourse, action, narrative or ethical commitment" (Oneself as Another, p. 335, as quoted by Rodrigues): who speaks, acts, is accountable.

### Not authorship alone

The principle reads ipse as "the capacity to be author of your life". That is close to Dierckxsens's gloss that selfness is the ability to turn life into a "singular story", which is his wording, not Ricoeur's. For Ricoeur, ipse also includes the answerable "who" who keeps a promise. Truc adds that selfhood "allows for other modalities of non-identical identity".

**Plain words** Part of you stays the same: your body, many habits, your history. Part of you is the person who answers for what you do, across change. Ricoeur says you need both words, because you can be recognisably the same and still not be accountable, or be accountable through large change. Keep both in view rather than choosing one.

*For example:* Imagined example: someone is still stubborn at fifty, as at twenty (idem). They also own an apology they made at thirty and keep acting on it (ipse). Both are true of the same person.

(Ricoeur, Oneself as Another, trans. Blamey (1992), pp. 116-117; as quoted in Dierckxsens (Antwerp repository), Rodrigues (Études Ricœuriennes), and Truc; plus the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy; all opened 2026-10-21. The book itself is not openly available.)
