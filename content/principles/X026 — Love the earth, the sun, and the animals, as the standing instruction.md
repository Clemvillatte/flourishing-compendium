> **Principle 26** — Love the earth, the sun, and the animals, as the standing instruction

The sentence is longer than the principle's title suggests. In the 1855 Preface to Leaves of Grass, printed on pages v and vi of the first edition, Whitman writes a single run of instructions that opens with three loves, the earth, the sun and the animals, and runs to thirteen instructions in all:

> This is what you shall do: Love the earth and sun and the animals, despise riches, give alms to every one that asks, stand up for the stupid and crazy, devote your income and labor to others, hate tyrants, argue not concerning God, have patience and indulgence toward the people, take off your hat to nothing known or unknown or to any man or number of men, go freely with powerful uneducated persons and with the young and with the mothers of families, read these leaves in the open air every season of every year of your life, re examine all you have been told at school or church or in any book, dismiss whatever insults your own soul, and your very flesh shall be a great poem
>
> (Whitman, Preface, Leaves of Grass, 1855, [Whitman Archive variorum](https://whitmanarchive.org/published-writings/leaves-of-grass/1855-variorum/main.html); the 1855 printing has no hyphen in "re examine", and the line breaks are the archive's.)

### What the list contains

The first three loves are the standing instruction the principle names. The rest widens it: money, alms, the stupid and the crazy, tyrants, authority, schooling, and the reader's own soul. Only some of it is a list of loves. Several items are refusals ("despise riches", "hate tyrants", "take off your hat to nothing known or unknown"), and one is an instruction to check inherited belief against your own judgment: re-examine "all you have been told at school or church or in any book".

**Plain words**
Whitman is describing a way of living that starts outward: love what is plainly there, such as ground, light and animals, and then ask what your wealth, your deference and your inherited opinions are doing to that love. The line about the flesh being a great poem is the promise attached to the list, and he states it as a promise, not a measured result.

**For example**
Choose three clauses from the list and give each a week. Week one, the first line: spend thirty minutes outdoors with no purpose. Week two, "give alms to every one that asks": say what you will and will not do and keep to it. Week three, re-examine what you were told: pick one belief you took from school or church and look for its evidence. The choice and the order are yours; Whitman gives no schedule.
