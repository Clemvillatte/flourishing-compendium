> **Principle 454** — Practise meaning through creative work

Frankl lays out three routes to meaning in Man's Search for Meaning, as reproduced on [Ed Batista's page](https://edbatista.com/2010/04/life.html) (pp. 111-115, edition not stated there):

> We can discover this meaning of life in three different ways: (1) by creating a work or doing a deed; (2) by experiencing something or encountering someone; and (3) by the attitude we take toward unavoidable suffering. The first, by way of achievement or accomplishment, is quite obvious.

Dik and Duffy, in their review of calling at work, describe a calling as an orientation toward "demonstrating or deriving a sense of purpose or meaningfulness" with "other-oriented values and goals as primary sources of motivation", and say it is "an ongoing process" of evaluating whether activities in a job contribute to others ([Dik & Duffy, 2009, The Counseling Psychologist 37, 424-450](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/5f515c06be95ce214b7e209d/t/615fe7e3c9bccf523c874fbc/1633675256037/Dik,+B.+J.,+&+Duffy,+R.+D.+(2009).+Calling+and+Vocation+at+Work:+Definitions+and+prospects+for+research+and+practice.+The+Counseling+Psychologist,+37,+424%E2%80%93+450.)). That is a definition and research agenda, not evidence that creative work yields meaning.

(Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning.)

**Plain words** Frankl's first route is making something or doing something that matters. Dik and Duffy suggest meaning at work grows when a person links the task to a wider purpose. The compendium's phrase about creation over consumption is its own gloss.

For example (imagined): a teacher who rewrites a lesson so a struggling student finally follows it may feel more meaning than from a day of routine marking, though both are work.
