> **Principle 150** — Distinguish between needing and wanting

Epictetus separates what is up to us ("opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion") from what is not ("body, property, reputation, command"). He warns that aiming at power and riches as well as freedom means you "will absolutely fail of the former, by which alone happiness and freedom are achieved" (Enchiridion 1, Carter translation, [Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html)). In Enchiridion 15 he advises taking your share "with moderation" and not stretching "your desire towards" what has not arrived (same source). Seneca's Letter 2.6 says "It is not the man who has too little, but the man who craves more, that is poor", and he sets the limit of wealth as first "to have what is necessary" and second "to have what is enough" (Gummere translation, [Standard Ebooks](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/seneca/moral-letters-to-lucilius/richard-m-gummere/text/letter-2)).

The seed's "virtue, reason, the basic necessities" needs care. In the mainstream Stoic view only virtue is good. Wealth and health are "preferred indifferents": it is normally appropriate to select them, but they make no difference to happiness ([Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/)). So the basics are not "needs for flourishing" in the strict sense, and "treat the rest as optional" is a fair summary only with that qualifier. Marcus Aurelius and the rest of the cited passages were not opened.

Kasser and Ryan (1993) found that when financial success was relatively central to students, their well-being was lower, and in an adult sample, interview-rated adjustment was lower too ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8366427/)). The relationship is correlational. The page labels Brown and Kasser (2005) as "sufficiency research", but its abstract covers values, mindfulness, lifestyle and ecologically responsible behaviour, with voluntary simplicity linked to such behaviour ([ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ749101)). Treat the label as loose.

**Plain words** The Stoics say only virtue is good, and things like wealth and comfort are acceptable to choose but do not decide how your life goes. Seneca's test is to have what is necessary, then what is enough. The research on materialism is correlational but points in the same direction.

*For example:* This is an imagined case. You look at a full wardrobe and notice you wear a fifth of it.
