> **Principle 40** — Recognise identity and culture: your self is culturally shaped

Identity is not purely personal; it is shaped by culture. What counts as a "good" self, what roles are valued, what identities are available — these are culturally specific. Understanding this prevents both arrogance (assuming your culture's self is universal) and confusion (not knowing which self is yours).

Markus and Kitayama's formulation starts from a plain claim:

> "People in different cultures have strikingly different construals of the self, of others, and of the interdependence of the 2. These construals can influence, and in many cases determine, the very nature of individual experience, including cognition, emotion, and motivation." (Markus & Kitayama, 1991, p. 224)

Their example of one culture's construal is the Western person, in Geertz's phrase quoted by the authors, as "a bounded, unique, more or less integrated motivational and cognitive universe" (Markus & Kitayama, 1991, p. 226). Other cultures mandate relatedness instead. The practical consequence for identity work: the "good self" you measure yourself against is one culture's answer, not a universal.

**Plain words** The self you call yours was assembled from parts your culture supplied: what counts as success, which roles get respect, what a "good person" means. You did not choose the starting kit. Noticing which parts are cultural stops you mistaking local rules for human nature, and tells you which parts are negotiable.

*For example:* The person whose culture says ambition is vulgar and whose self-judgment has been quietly running that rule — and the discovery that the "limit" was a setting, not a ceiling. The self was culturally furnished; the furniture can be rearranged.

## Practice

- Recognise that your identity is partly cultural. If you are bicultural or multicultural, work on integration rather than choosing one. If monocultural, remember that your way of being a self is not the only way.
