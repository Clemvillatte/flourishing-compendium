> **Principle 152** — Act for the common good

> For we are made for co-operation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth. To act against one another then is contrary to nature.

(Marcus Aurelius, Meditations II.1, trans. George Long, [Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.2.two.html), page opened.)

Marcus ties the claim to a view of nature. In Book Nine he says "the universal nature has made rational animals for the sake of one another to help one another according to their deserts" ([Meditations IX](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.9.nine.html), opened). In Book Six comes the line "That which is not good for the swarm, neither is it good for the bee" ([Meditations VI](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.6.six.html), opened). The rule in the entry, to ask of each action whether it serves the common good, is the compendium's formulation of these passages. The Epictetus and Seneca references were not opened.

A modern parallel exists on the psychology side. Dunn, Aknin and Norton found that people randomly assigned to spend money on others reported greater happiness than those assigned to spend it on themselves ([Science, 2008; PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18356530/)). A later registered replication confirmed the effect with small differences, and one of its three experiments (N = 1,950) found no effect ([Aknin et al., 2020; PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32250135/)). That research studies a happiness benefit of giving. Marcus makes a claim about what a human being is for, which is a different question.

**Plain words** Marcus says people are built to work together, like the parts of a body, and that what harms the group harms each member. So before acting, it helps to ask who else the action touches.

*For example (imagined):* A team member could skip a testing step to finish sooner. They think about the colleague who will inherit the bugs and run the test.
