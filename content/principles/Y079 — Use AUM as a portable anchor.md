> **Principle 79** — Use AUM as a portable anchor

Three short sutras give the syllable its place. Sutra 1.27 names it and 1.28 says what to do with it:

> The word-expressing Him is the Mystic-syllable (pranava). Repetition of it and reflection upon its meaning [should be made].

(Yoga Sutras 1.27-1.28, trans. James Haughton Woods, *The Yoga-System of Patanjali*, Harvard Oriental Series 17, 1914, pp. 60-62, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/yogasystemofpata00wooduoft).)

"Him" is the Isvara, the special Self introduced just before. So the syllable is the name of a particular object of devotion, and the instruction has two parts: repetition and reflection on what it means. The commentary states the effect: "Then in the case of this yogin who thus repeats the Mystic Syllable and reflects upon its meaning, mind-stuff attains to singleness-of-intent." Sutra 1.29 adds the result: "Thereafter comes the right-knowledge of him who thinks in an inverse way, and the removal of obstacles" (the Tika words it as the yogin coming "to a sight of his own real self").

In the commentary the chain runs devotion, repetition with reflection, singleness of mind, and fewer obstacles. That differs from presenting AUM as a general-purpose calming sound. The Mandukya Upanishad analyses the syllable differently, which has not been checked for this page. The portable anchor idea, using it in waiting rooms or before hard conversations, relies on the sound alone and is this page's own adaptation. In the text the meaning is part of the practice.

**Plain words**

Patanjali links the syllable to a specific object of devotion and asks for both repetition and thought about its meaning. Using just the sound as a pocket steadier is a modern simplification. If you try it, decide whether you want the meaning too.

**For example**

A man waiting outside a difficult meeting repeats one chosen word silently and keeps asking what it stands for in his life. By the time the door opens, his breathing has slowed.
