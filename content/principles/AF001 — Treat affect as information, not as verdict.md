> **Principle 1** — Treat affect as information, not as verdict

Feelings carry useful information about your body, your goals, and your circumstances, but they are not a transparent readout of the world. A feeling of anxiety can be generated by threat, caffeine, sleep deprivation, low blood sugar, or misinterpretation. The first move in adaptive affect is to ask what the feeling is actually about before acting on it.

**Plain words** Feelings carry real information — about your body, your goals, your circumstances — but they're not a transparent readout of the world. Anxiety can come from threat, caffeine, sleep debt, or low blood sugar. Before acting on a feeling, ask what it's actually about.

*For example:* The person who "senses doom" all afternoon and treats it as intuition — then checks the inputs: four coffees, five hours of sleep. The signal was real; it was about the coffee. The afternoon's decisions get re-scheduled.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Affect as information|Affect-as-information]]**, people use their current mood as a source of information about the world, and this can be corrected when the mood's true source is disclosed (Schwarz & Clore, 1983; Schwarz, 2012)
- **Mood-congruent judgement is attenuated by attribution**, misattributing a mood to a target amplifies the effect; correcting the attribution reverses it (Schwarz & Clore, 1983)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Apatheia|Apatheia]]** (Stoics, Epictetus, Discourses)
- **Judgement as the source of passion** (Spinoza, Ethics III)
- **Scepticism about first impressions** (Buddha, Kalama Sutta)

## Practice

- When a strong feeling arrives, ask "what is this actually about?" — threat, body state, expectation, or memory — before deciding what to do.
