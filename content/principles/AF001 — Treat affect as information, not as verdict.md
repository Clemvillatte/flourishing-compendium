> **Principle 1** — Treat affect as information, not as verdict

Schwarz and Clore's account is that when people judge something, they implicitly ask how they feel about it, and the answer becomes evidence. The risk is that the feeling came from somewhere else.

> evaluative judgments involve people asking themselves (implicitly), 'How do I feel about this?'
>
> — Schwarz & Clore, "Mood as Information: 20 Years Later", Psychological Inquiry (2003), p. 299

They add that a feeling loses its pull on a judgment once its source is found to be irrelevant to the thing being judged ([Schwarz & Clore, 2003](https://dornsife.usc.edu/norbert-schwarz/wp-content/uploads/sites/231/2023/11/03_pi_schwarz___clore_mood.pdf)). The best-known demonstration was a 1983 phone survey, where the weather was the irrelevant source. The weather link itself is shaky. A later sample of over a million Americans found that [weather does not reliably affect judgments of life satisfaction](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3695376/) (Lucas & Lawless, 2013). The mood-induction experiments are a separate line of evidence.

(Schwarz & Clore, 1983, 2003.)

**Plain words** Before you trust a feeling about something, ask where the feeling came from. If it came from the thing itself, it is evidence. If it came from elsewhere, it says little about the thing, and noticing that is often enough to loosen its hold.

For example (imagined): you feel dread about a colleague's email, then remember you slept badly and skipped lunch. The dread is real. What it tells you about the email is much less than it seemed. Reply after you have eaten.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Affect as information|Affect-as-information]]**, people use their current mood as a source of information about the world, and this can be corrected when the mood's true source is disclosed (Schwarz & Clore, 1983; Schwarz, 2012)
- **Mood effects on judgement shrink when the mood is attributed to an irrelevant source**, shown for sad moods in the original studies; good moods were not affected by the manipulation (Schwarz & Clore, 1983)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Apatheia|Apatheia]]** (Stoics, Epictetus, Discourses)
- **Judgement as the source of passion** (Spinoza, Ethics III)
- **Scepticism about first impressions** (Buddha, Kalama Sutta)

## Practice

- When a strong feeling arrives, ask "what is this actually about?" — threat, body state, expectation, or memory — before deciding what to do.
