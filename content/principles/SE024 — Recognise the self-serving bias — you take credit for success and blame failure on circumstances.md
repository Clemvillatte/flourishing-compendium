> **Principle 24** — Recognise the self-serving bias: you take credit for success and blame failure on circumstances

The construct is the self-serving attributional bias: more internal, stable, and global attributions for positive events than for negative ones. The classic review that named it actually found the bias asymmetric, and a later meta-analysis confirmed its overall pervasiveness.

> "The literature provides some support for the contention that individuals engage in self-enhancing attributions under conditions of success, but only minimal evidence was found to suggest that individuals engage in self-protective attributions under conditions of failure." (Miller & Ross, "Self-serving biases in the attribution of causality: Fact or fiction?", Psychological Bulletin 82, 1975, abstract)

Miller and Ross also proposed the success-side effect may not be motivational distortion at all, but the tendency of people to expect their behaviour to produce success and to misconstrue contingency. Mezulis, Abramson, Hyde and Hankin's meta-analysis of 266 studies (average d = 0.96, a large effect) later confirmed the bias is pervasive in the general population, with variability across age, culture and psychopathology. The compendium's application: audit your wins for the luck and your losses for the responsibility, since the ledger your mind keeps is not neutral.

**Plain words**
The mind keeps the score with a thumb on the scale: my wins were skill, my losses were circumstances. The researchers who named the bias found the thumb mostly presses on the win side, and a later review of 266 studies found the tilt large and nearly universal. The corrective habit runs both directions anyway: on a win, ask what circumstances contributed; on a loss, ask what you contributed.

*For example:* Imagine the deal that closed was "my skill" and the deal that died was "the market", and the honest audit that finds skill and market in both.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Self-serving attribution bias|Self-serving attribution bias]]**, people attribute success to themselves and failure to external factors (Miller & Ross, 1975; Mezulis et al., 2004)
- **The bias impairs learning**, self-serving attributions prevent accurate learning from failures (Martinko et al., 2006)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[The examined life|The examined life]] requires examined attributions** (Socrates, Apology)
- **[[Humility|Humility about success]]** (Aquinas, Summa Theologica)
- **[[Right view|Right view about causation]]** (Buddhism, dependent origination)

## Practice

- When you succeed, ask: "what did circumstances contribute?" When you fail, ask: "what did I contribute?" Deliberately counter the bias in both directions.
