> **Principle 68** — Recognise addiction and motivation: wanting without liking

Berridge and Robinson separate two things that usually travel together. Rewards are "both 'liked' and 'wanted'", but "the brain circuitry that mediates the psychological process of 'wanting' a particular reward is dissociable from circuitry that mediates the degree to which it is 'liked'". Wanting, which they call incentive salience, "is generated by large and robust neural systems that include mesolimbic dopamine", whereas liking, "the actual pleasurable impact of reward consumption, is mediated by smaller and fragile neural systems, and is not dependent on dopamine". Their theory holds that "the essence of drug addiction" is "excessive amplification specifically of psychological 'wanting', especially triggered by cues, without necessarily an amplification of 'liking'" (Berridge & Robinson, 2016, American Psychologist 71(8), 670-679, [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5171207/)).

The wanting can also oppose what a person decides. It "occurs when a recovering addict has a genuine cognitive desire to abstain from taking drugs, but still 'wants' drugs, so relapses anyway when exposed to drug cues" (same paper). This is one theory among several. Koob and Le Moal describe addiction as "a cycle of decreased function of brain reward systems and recruitment of antireward systems" ([Annual Review of Psychology, 2008, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18154498/)). I did not open clinical sources on treatment, so I leave that part of the principle unsupported here.

**Plain words**
Craving and enjoyment are separate systems. You can want something badly and get little from it. When you notice a pull that outruns the pleasure, treat it as a cue-driven urge and not as proof that this is what you really want.

**For example**
You reach for your phone at 11pm, scroll for an hour and feel worse. The pull came from the phone's cues, not from the evening you wanted.

## Practice

- If you notice wanting something you do not enjoy (substances, behaviours, compulsive checking), recognise this as a dysregulated wanting system. Seek professional help. Willpower alone is not sufficient.
