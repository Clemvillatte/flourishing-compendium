> **Principle 164** — Practise non-retaliation

When wronged, the Stoic does not retaliate. Marcus gives the reason in a line: "The best kind of revenge is, not to become like unto them." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, Project Gutenberg ebook 2680, translator not stated in that edition; printed as Bk VI §5.) The refusal is of the imitation: answering injury with injury makes you what you punish. The Stoic may still seek justice — restitution, protection, correction — and the distinction this principle draws is in the motive: justice seeks restoration; revenge seeks suffering.

Epictetus asks where the anger comes from: "This man who has been mistaken and deceived about the most important things, and blinded... should we not destroy him? If you speak thus you will see how inhuman this is which you say, and that it is just as if you would say, Ought we not to destroy this blind and deaf man?... Man, you ought not to be affected contrary to nature by the bad things of another. Pity him rather; drop this readiness to be offended and to hate" (Epictetus, Discourses I.18, tr. Long.) — the passage's targets are anger and hatred, not the ordinary pursuit of remedy. The ground of it is what we are: "we are all born to be fellow-workers, as the feet, the hands, and the eyelids... for such therefore to be in opposition, is against nature; and what is it to chafe at, and to be averse from, but to be in opposition?" (Marcus, printed as Bk I §15 in the Gutenberg text.)

(Disc. I.18; Med., Gutenberg 2680.)

**Plain words** Someone wrongs you. Two questions are available: what repair does this situation need, and how do I make them feel it? The first can be acted on; the second only fed. Marcus's line keeps the arithmetic honest — if you become like the person who hurt you, you haven't won, you've doubled them.

*For example:* Reporting a fraud to the authorities is justice. Posting the fraudster's address online is something else.
