> **Principle 53** — Learn to give and receive support: social support behaviours

Support runs in two directions, and both are behaviour: giving it (helping, listening, being present) and receiving it (asking, accepting, allowing).

The mortality evidence comes from a meta-analysis: 148 studies covering 308,849 participants found a "50% increased likelihood of survival for participants with stronger social relationships" (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010, PLoS Medicine 7(7):e1000316, [full text](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316)).

Two classic models explain how support does its work, and the review that compared them found "evidence consistent with both models" (Cohen & Wills, 1985, Psychological Bulletin 98(2):310-357, [abstract record](https://oamonitor.ireland.openaire.eu/rfo/health-research-board3/search/publication?pid=10.1037/0033-2909.98.2.310)).

> "Evidence for the buffering model is found when the social support measure assesses the perceived availability of interpersonal resources that are responsive to the needs elicited by stressful events." (Cohen & Wills, 1985, Psychological Bulletin 98(2):310-357, [abstract record](https://oamonitor.ireland.openaire.eu/rfo/health-research-board3/search/publication?pid=10.1037/0033-2909.98.2.310))

A later review adds a caution about measurement: perceived and received support are separable, with different links to health outcomes (Uchino, 2009, Perspectives on Psychological Science 4(3):236-255, [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26158961/)).

**Plain words** Having someone you could call is different from receiving help today. Researchers distinguish the help people believe is available from the help they actually receive. As an everyday exercise, make a specific request or decide whether to accept an offer.

**For example:** Imagined: after a minor operation, a neighbour offers to take over the shopping for the week, and the person says yes instead of managing it alone. Accepting the offer is the behaviour under practice.

## Psychology parallels

- **Social support and health**, a meta-analysis of 148 studies (308,849 participants) found a 50% increased likelihood of survival for participants with stronger social relationships (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010)
- **Two models of support (Cohen & Wills, 1985)**, buffering evidence appears when support is measured as perceived availability of interpersonal resources; main effects when it is measured as integration in a social network
- **Perceived and received support are separable** (Uchino, 2009), the two measures track different links to health outcomes

## Practice

- Practise giving and receiving support: offer help, listen, be present. And ask for help, accept help, allow yourself to be supported. Both are connection behaviours.
