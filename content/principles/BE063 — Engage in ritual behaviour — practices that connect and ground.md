> **Principle 63** — Engage in ritual behaviour: practices that connect and ground

The evidence for personal rituals is real but narrower than the principle's three claims (meaning, less anxiety, connection to something larger). In three experiments on loss, "participants who were directed to reflect on past rituals or who were assigned to complete novel rituals after experiencing losses reported lower levels of grief", and the authors point to "regained feelings of control" as the mechanism; the benefits "accrued not only to individuals who professed a belief in rituals' effectiveness but also to those who did not" (Norton & Gino 2014, [HBS record](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=43988)). In four experiments, Vohs and colleagues found that people who performed a ritual before eating chocolate enjoyed it more, and conclude that "rituals enhance the enjoyment of consumption because of the greater involvement in the experience that they prompt" (Vohs et al. 2013, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23863754/)).

The anxiety claim is the weak one. A 2016 paper that said rituals improve performance by lowering anxiety (Brooks et al.) was retracted because its authors "no longer have confidence in the integrity of the data" ([retraction notice](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S074959781630437X)). In a small pre-registered study of one week of an arbitrary ritual, "there were no changes in self-reported affect to indicate that ritual made people consciously feel less anxious" (Hobson et al. 2017, [PeerJ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5452956/)).

**Plain words**
A ritual is a set sequence of actions done on purpose. The best-supported effects here are that after a loss it can help you feel more in control and less grief-stricken, and that a small ritual before a pleasure can make you enjoy it more. The case that rituals calm anxiety is weak right now, so treat that part as an open question.

*For example:* After a disappointment, you write what you feel on a page, tear it up and say one sentence aloud. This is an imagined ritual in the spirit of Norton and Gino's lottery experiment, which used a different set of actions, and the result for any one person is uncertain.

## Psychology parallels

- **Rituals and grief**, reflecting on or performing rituals after a loss lowered grief, through regained feelings of control (Norton & Gino, 2014)
- **Rituals and enjoyment**, performing a ritual before eating raised enjoyment through greater involvement (Vohs et al., 2013)
- **Ritual and anxiety**, one week of an arbitrary ritual changed a neural error marker but not self-reported affect (Hobson et al., 2017); the 2016 study linking ritual to lower anxiety and better performance was retracted

## Practice

- Engage in ritual behaviour: develop personal rituals (morning practice, evening reflection, seasonal celebrations). Rituals ground, connect, and provide meaning. Do not dismiss them as irrational.
