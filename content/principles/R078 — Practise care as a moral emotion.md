> **Principle 340** — Practise care as a moral emotion

The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy says that "care ethics affirms the importance of caring motivation, emotion and the body in moral deliberation, as well as reasoning from particulars". For Nel Noddings, ethical action starts from "two motives, the human affective response that is a natural caring sentiment, and the memory of being cared-for that gives rise to an ideal self". She described caring as "engrossment", in which the one-caring "receives the cared-for on their own terms, resisting projection of the self onto the cared-for, and displacing selfish motives in order to act on the behalf of the cared-for". Annette Baier stressed "sentimental character traits, including gentleness, agreeability, compassion, sympathy, and good temperedness", and "recommends carving out room for the development of moral emotions and harmonizing the ideals of care and justice" ([IEP, "Care Ethics"](https://iep.utm.edu/care-eth/)).

In this tradition, emotions are part of moral reasoning. The same article records objections: critics call care ethics "a kind of slave morality" and "empirically flawed". I did not open Held's or Noddings's books, and nothing I read says that suppressed emotions "leak", so I leave that claim out.

**Plain words**
Compassion and concern can guide moral action. Use them to see what a person needs, then check whether your response fits them and not just your own comfort. Caring feelings are information, not weakness.

**For example**
A friend's brother has died. You notice your pull to fix things with advice, put it aside, and ask what would help today.
