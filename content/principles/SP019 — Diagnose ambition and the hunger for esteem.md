> **Principle 19** — Diagnose ambition and the hunger for esteem

Spinoza gives ambition a cold entry in his catalogue of the emotions: "Ambition is the immoderate desire of power" (Ethics III, Definitions of the Emotions, XLIV). In the scholium to III.39 he shows what that looks like from inside: "an ambitious man desires nothing so much as glory, and fears nothing so much as shame." The glory is his "Honour" — "pleasure accompanied by the idea of some action of our own, which we believe to be praised by others" (III, Definitions of the Emotions, XXX). Notice the wiring: the pleasure runs on a belief about other people's opinions, and beliefs about opinions shift with every comment thread. Spinoza reserves a name for the unstable kind — "Empty honour... is self-approval, fostered only by the good opinion of the populace; when this good opinion ceases there ceases also the self-approval," so that "he whose honour is rooted in popular approval must, day by day, anxiously strive, act, and scheme in order to retain his reputation" (IV, note to LVIII).

The diagnosis is not that esteem is worthless. It is that esteem placed at the center of identity produces a life run by remote control. Practically, notice where your plans change shape when you imagine an audience, and run one important project entirely without one.

(Spinoza, Ethics, Elwes translation — III, P39 scholium; III, Definitions of the Emotions XXX and XLIV; IV, note to LVIII.)

**Plain words** Ambition is Spinoza's name for the drive to be seen as powerful or glorious. Its fuel is other people's approval — which is real, and which you do not control. If your week turns on one comment thread, that is the mechanism Spinoza described three centuries before there were likes. The diagnosis is the first line of the treatment: whose opinions are holding the keys?

*For example:* The executive who checks the likes at traffic lights, whose week is set by one comment thread — Spinoza's description, running in their pocket. The diagnosis isn't shame; it's information: now they can see the strings.
