> **Principle 54** — Learn to train attention through deliberate practice: not just meditation

The principle borrows the idea of deliberate practice: sustained effort at a task, with feedback. The research on what such training does to attention is narrower than the principle's wording. Simons and colleagues reviewed the brain-training literature and found "extensive evidence that brain-training interventions improve performance on the trained tasks, less evidence that such interventions improve performance on closely related tasks, and little evidence that training enhances performance on distantly related tasks or that training improves everyday cognitive performance" ([Simons et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27697851/)).

A meta-analysis of working-memory training reached a similar verdict: "no convincing evidence of the generalization of working memory training to other skills", including "inhibitory processes in attention" ([Melby-Lervåg & Hulme, 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22612437/)).

> The authors conclude that memory training programs appear to produce short-term, specific training effects that do not generalize.
>
> Melby-Lervåg & Hulme, Developmental Psychology (2013)

Those reviews cover computer-based programs. They do not test practising an instrument or reading hard texts, and I found no study among those I opened that does.

(Ericsson et al., 1993; Melby-Lervåg & Hulme, 2013; Simons et al., 2016.)

**Plain words** Practice makes you better at the thing you practise. Whether it also makes you generally better at paying attention is not proven. A good reason to take up a demanding hobby is the hobby itself. Think of any gain in focus as a possible extra, and do not count on it.

For example (imagined): an hour of piano practice with a teacher correcting your mistakes will improve your piano playing. Expecting it to improve how well you concentrate on a spreadsheet is a larger claim than the evidence supports.

## Psychology parallels

- **Deliberate practice is the model behind the principle**, with training gains clearest on the practised task (Ericsson et al., 1993; Simons et al., 2016)
- **Note: "brain training" games show limited far transfer**, working-memory training improves trained tasks but does not broadly improve attention (Melby-Lervåg & Hulme, 2013; Simons et al., 2016)

## Practice

- Train attention through meaningful activities: read challenging books, learn an instrument, practise a craft, solve problems. Choose activities that require sustained focus and provide feedback.
