> **Principle 59** — Know the obstacles by name

Sutra 1.30 names nine things that pull the mind off practice:

> Sickness and languor and doubt and heedlessness and listlessness and worldliness (avirati) and erroneous perception and failure to attain any stage [of concentration] and instability in the state [when attained]: these distractions of the mind-stuff are the obstacles.

(Yoga Sutras 1.30, trans. James Haughton Woods, *The Yoga-System of Patanjali*, Harvard Oriental Series 17, 1914, pp. 64-65, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/yogasystemofpata00wooduoft).)

The commentary defines each. Languor is "a lack of activity in the mind-stuff". Doubt is "a kind of thinking which touches both alternatives [of a dilemma], so that one thinks 'This might be so; might not be so.'" Heedlessness is "a lack of reflection upon the means of attaining concentration". Listlessness is "a lack of effort due to heaviness of body or of mind-stuff". Worldliness is "greed of the mind-stuff". Instability is "the failure of the mind-stuff to remain in the stage attained."

The commentary does not say that each obstacle has its own remedy. The next sutra gives a single one for the whole set: "To check them [let there be] practice upon a single entity" (1.32). So the claim that misdiagnosis wastes months, along with the rule to name the obstacle before adjusting anything, is this page's own. The wording used here also differs from Woods: "laziness" and "mental inertia" correspond to listlessness and languor, and "craving" to worldliness.

**Plain words**

The text lists nine named obstacles and defines each one carefully. Naming what is going wrong is a fair first step, even though the sutras themselves prescribe one practice for all of them.

**For example**

A woman's practice stalls. She reads the list and sees it was doubt: she keeps thinking "this might work; might not". She writes the doubt down in those words and asks one question of her teacher.
