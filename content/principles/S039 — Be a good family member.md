> **Principle 161** — Be a good family member

The Stoic does not treat family as a distraction from philosophy. Marcus opens the Meditations with a list of debts to the people who shaped him, and one of the debts is love of family itself:

> "From my brother Severus, to love my kin, and to love truth, and to love justice" (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, Book I, section 14, George Long translation)

Marcus also names what he learned from his mother:

> "From my mother, piety and beneficence, and abstinence, not only from evil deeds, but even from evil thoughts; and further, simplicity in my way of living, far removed from the habits of the rich." (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations, Book I, section 3, George Long translation)

Musonius discusses whether marriage interferes with philosophy:

> "Again when someone said that marriage and living with a wife seemed to him a handicap to the pursuit of philosophy, Musonius said that it was no handicap to Pythagoras, nor to Socrates, nor to Crates, each of whom lived with a wife, and one could not mention better philosophers than these." (Musonius Rufus, Lecture XIV, Cora Lutz translation)

Musonius's ground is that "marriage, if anything, is manifestly in accord with nature". For this principle, the practical question is how to bring what you study into family life.

(Med. I.3, I.14, George Long translation; Musonius Rufus, Lecture XIV, Cora Lutz translation.)

**Plain words** Reading about patience leaves a practical question: what will you do when a relative interrupts your plans? You might listen before answering, keep an agreed promise, or explain calmly why you cannot help today. These are ways to try the principle in daily life.

For example: Imagined: someone whose evening practice is Stoic reading, and whose mother calls every night to worry, takes the call itself as the practice: listening without fixing, keeping the tone even, ending warmly.
