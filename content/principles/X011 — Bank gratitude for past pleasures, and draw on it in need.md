> **Principle 11** — Bank gratitude for past pleasures, and draw on it in need

Epicurus treats memory as a source of present pleasure, and the clearest evidence is his own last letter. Diogenes Laertius preserves it:

> On this blissful day, which is also the last of my life, I write this to you. My continual sufferings from strangury and dysentery are so great that nothing could augment them; but over against them all I set gladness of mind at the remembrance of our past conversations.
>
> (Epicurus to Idomeneus, in Diogenes Laertius X.22, Hicks, [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0258:book=10:chapter=1).)

### The sayings on remembering

The Vatican Sayings make it advice. VS 55: "We must heal our misfortunes by the grateful recollection of what has been, and by the recognition that it is impossible to undo that which has been done." VS 19 gives the opposite case: "Forgetting the good that has been, he has become old this very day." And VS 17 describes the old man who has "come to anchor in old age, as though in port", and brought his earlier good things "into safe harbor in his grateful recollections" (all Bailey, [Epicurean Friends](https://www.epicureanfriends.com/wcf/vatican-list-of-sayings/)).

The savings-account picture is this compendium's own image for the practice, not an Epicurean phrase. What the texts support is narrower: grateful memory eases present trouble, and forgetting past good costs something now.

**Plain words**
A good evening does not vanish when it ends. You can still take something from it later by calling it back in detail. Epicurus did this at the end of his life, setting an old conversation against bodily pain. The practice is to keep specific past good things written down, so that on a bad week you are not trying to remember them from scratch.

**For example**
Once a week, write one line about a particular good moment, such as the meal, the person and the place. In a hard stretch, read the list and pick the entry that is easiest to see clearly, then spend a few minutes with it. Epicurus does not promise a particular result, so judge the effect by whether it eases the day for you.
