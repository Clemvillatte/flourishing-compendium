> **Principle 57** — Learn grounding techniques: present-moment body awareness

Grounding, in Lisa Najavits's formulation from Seeking Safety, is a set of simple strategies to detach from the effects of trauma, emotional pain, or stress by focusing outward on the external world rather than inward toward the self ([Najavits, 2002](https://politics.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2022/01/Grounding-Exercises.pdf)). Clinical guidance for dissociative disorders describes the same move in emergency terms: connecting the patient back to reality and the present moment, reducing the intensity of the episode, through simple instructions that improve sensory or cognitive awareness of the present ([Kedare et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10096208/)). The evidence base deserves honesty: a 2025 review found grounding techniques have not been subjected to formal empirical investigation, with no study establishing efficacy ([Hammond & Brown, 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/15248380251343189)).

> "Grounding is a set of simple strategies to detach from the effects of trauma, emotional pain, or stress. Grounding works by focusing outward on the external world, rather than inward toward the self." (Najavits, 2002, Seeking Safety, via the UBC SVPRO adaptation, [PDF](https://politics.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2022/01/Grounding-Exercises.pdf))

> "Grounding techniques are designed to connect the patient back to reality and the present moment, thereby reducing the intensity of the dissociative (trance, depersonalization, etc.) episode." (Kedare, Baliga & Kadiani, 2023, Indian J Psychiatry, [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10096208/))

> "Yet, grounding techniques have yet to be subjected to formal empirical investigation; to date, no study has established efficacy in support of this commonly used intervention." (Hammond & Brown, 2025, J Trauma Dissociation, [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/15248380251343189))

**Plain words**
Grounding is a deliberate turn outward: name what is in the room, feel the chair, count what you can hear ([Najavits, 2002](https://politics.ubc.ca/wp-content/uploads/sites/31/2022/01/Grounding-Exercises.pdf)). The clinical idea is that attention is the lever, and the honest status of the evidence is that no trial has established that it works ([Hammond & Brown, 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/15248380251343189)). Clinicians kept using it anyway, because someone in the middle of dissociating needs something to do now.

**For example**
(Imagined) During a meeting that begins to feel unreal, someone presses their heels into the floor and names five objects in the room, the way the guideline's sensory-awareness instructions describe ([Kedare et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10096208/)).

## Practice

- Use grounding: "5 things I see, 4 I hear, 3 I touch, 2 I smell, 1 I taste." Or feel your feet on the floor, the weight of your body. Use for anxiety, dissociation, overwhelm. Widely taught, easy to carry, and not yet trial-tested ([Hammond & Brown, 2025](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/15248380251343189)).
