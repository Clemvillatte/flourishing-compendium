> **Principle 101** — Work as offering

In the third chapter of the Gita, Krishna answers Arjuna's doubt about action versus meditation by putting work inside a ritual frame. In Arnold's translation, he quotes the creator Prajapati: "In the beginning, when all men were made, and, with mankind, the sacrifice, 'Do this! Work! sacrifice! Increase and multiply with sacrifice!'" He then says that "he that abstains to help the rolling wheels of this great world, glutting his idle sense, lives a lost life, shameful and vain" (Bhagavad-Gita ch. 3, trans. Arnold).

### The wheel and the unattached worker

The chain is explicit: "By food the living live; food comes of rain, and rain comes by the pious sacrifice, and sacrifice is paid with tithes of toil" (ch. 3, Arnold). The one who works only for himself is described as "existing for himself, self-concentrated, serving self alone", and the advice is to do the "task prescribed with spirit unattached" (ch. 3, Arnold). Earlier in the chapter, the worker who gives "his mortal powers to worthy work, not seeking gain" is called honourable (ch. 3, Arnold).

### What the principle changes

The principle strips out the ritual and the gods and keeps the structure of work as a gift. Arnold's text does not mention naming whom the work serves each morning, or a dedication of work to a craft or a generation. It does say to act "for the upholding of thy kind". The one-sentence morning practice is the principle's own, a modern adaptation, not a quotation.

**Plain words** The Gita says work done only for oneself is a lost life, and work done without grasping for results, as a contribution to the world's cycle, is honourable. The original frame is a sacrifice to the gods. Writing down whom your work serves before you begin is a modern way of keeping the spirit of that teaching.

*For example:* Imagined example: before starting, a teacher writes "for the students who will read this next term". The sentence does not change the task but it changes what the task is for.

(Bhagavad-Gita ch. 3, trans. Sir Edwin Arnold, The Song Celestial, Project Gutenberg #2388, opened 2026-10-21. Arnold's verses are unnumbered, so the old cite, 3.9-3.16, is not checked against a numbered edition.)
