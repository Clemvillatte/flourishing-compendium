> **Principle 111** — Rest in contentment: the greatest wealth

Dhammapada 204 states the ranking in one verse: health is the most precious gain, contentment the greatest wealth, a trustworthy person the best kinsman, Nibbana the highest bliss. Contentment is listed as the wealth itself, so the verse treats wanting as the expense and rest as the asset. (Dhammapada 204.)

> "Health is the most precious gain and contentment the greatest wealth. A trustworthy person is the best kinsman, Nibbana the highest bliss." (Dhammapada 204, [Buddharakkhita, Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/dhp/dhp.15.budd.html))

SN 1.10 gives the mechanism in one exchange. A devata asks why forest monks' faces are so bright and serene; the Buddha answers that they don't sorrow over the past or long for the future, and survive on the present. In the compendium's reading, contentment is not a verdict on what you have; it is where the attention is pointed. (SN 1.10.)

> "They don't sorrow over the past, don't long for the future. They survive on the present. That's why their faces are bright & serene." (SN 1.10, [Thanissaro, Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn01/sn01.010.than.html))

**Plain words**
Take an inventory of what is already running well: health enough to read this, someone who would answer if you called, a roof over the next week. State these out loud without comparison for one minute. The count is the exercise; the comparison is what usually spoils it.

*For example:* Imagine two neighbors with identical lawns: one is planning the upgrade, one is barefoot in it. The wealth difference for that hour is not in the lawns.
