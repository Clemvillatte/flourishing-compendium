> **Principle 423** — Practise radical freedom

Sartre's clearest public statement of the position is in the 1946 lecture published as Existentialism Is a Humanism. The word "condemned" is doing the work: the freedom is not something you can decline.

> That is what I mean when I say that man is condemned to be free. Condemned, because he did not create himself, yet is nevertheless at liberty, and from the moment that he is thrown into this world he is responsible for everything he does. (Sartre, Existentialism Is a Humanism, 1946, [Warwick PDF](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/cross_fac/complexity/people/students/dtc/students2011/maitland/philosophy/sartre-eih.pdf))

The sentence that follows rules out the two standard escapes. The existentialist, Sartre says, "will never regard a grand passion as a destructive torrent upon which a man is swept into certain actions as by fate, and which, therefore, is an excuse for them"; and the same lecture rejects the reading of a "sign" from outside as a guide, because the person interprets the sign as they choose. What is left, on this account, is responsibility for what you make of a situation you did not choose to be in.

**Plain words** You did not choose your birth, your body, your century, or most of what has happened to you. You do choose, in every case, what you do next inside those limits. The common escape is "I had no choice"; the position here is that the no-choice version is itself a choice, and it is the one that costs the most.

*For example:* Imagine two people in the same failing department over the same year. One spends it in "what could I have done"; the other spends it retraining, applying, and moving. The situation was the same; the difference was what each made of it.
