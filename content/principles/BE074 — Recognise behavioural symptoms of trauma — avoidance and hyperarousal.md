> **Principle 74** — Recognise behavioural symptoms of trauma: avoidance and hyperarousal

After a traumatic event, the behaviour can change in recognisable directions. The UK guideline describes the presentation:

> "Be aware that people with post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), including complex PTSD, may present with a range of symptoms associated with functional impairment, including:
> - re-experiencing
> - avoidance
> - hyperarousal (including hypervigilance, anger and irritability)
> - negative alterations in mood and thinking
> - emotional numbing
> - dissociation
> - emotional dysregulation
> - interpersonal difficulties or problems in relationships
> - negative self-perception (including feeling diminished, defeated or worthless)."
>
> (NICE guideline NG116, 2018, [recommendations](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng116/chapter/Recommendations); the guideline's original bulleted list is preserved)

The two behavioural clusters this principle names appear on that list. The US National Center for PTSD describes avoidance as: "You may try to avoid situations or people that remind you of the trauma. You may even avoid talking or thinking about the event." Hyperarousal reads as: "You may be jittery, or always alert and on the lookout for danger. You might suddenly become angry or irritable" (National Center for PTSD, [PTSD Basics](https://www.ptsd.va.gov/understand/what/ptsd_basics.asp)).

Treatment guidance is specific. NICE: "Offer an individual trauma-focused CBT intervention to adults with a diagnosis of PTSD or clinically important symptoms of PTSD who have presented more than 1 month after a traumatic event", listing cognitive processing therapy, cognitive therapy for PTSD, narrative exposure therapy and prolonged exposure therapy; and "Offer EMDR to adults with a diagnosis of PTSD or clinically important symptoms of PTSD who have presented more than 3 months after a non-combat-related trauma" (NICE NG116, 2018).

**Plain words** Two behaviour changes are on the guideline's list. First, avoidance: steering clear of situations or people that recall the event, not talking or thinking about it. Second, hyperarousal: feeling jittery, always alert and on the lookout for danger, sudden anger or irritability. The guideline treats these as symptoms with named treatments, not character flaws; if they are affecting daily life, the next step is an assessment, not a verdict.

**For example:** Imagined: after a car crash, someone stops driving on motorways, takes long detours, and startles when a car brakes hard nearby. Both behaviours are traceable to the event; both belong in the conversation with a clinician about what to do next.

## Psychology parallels

- **PTSD behavioural symptoms (NICE NG116, 2018)**, the guideline lists re-experiencing, avoidance, and hyperarousal (including hypervigilance, anger and irritability) among symptoms associated with functional impairment
- **Trauma-focused treatments (NICE NG116, 2018)**, individual trauma-focused CBT (including cognitive processing therapy and prolonged exposure) for adults presenting more than 1 month after an event; EMDR for adults presenting more than 3 months after a non-combat-related trauma

## Practice

- If trauma-related behavioural symptoms (avoidance, hypervigilance, intrusion) are affecting daily life, seek an assessment from a qualified professional and discuss trauma-focused treatment options.
