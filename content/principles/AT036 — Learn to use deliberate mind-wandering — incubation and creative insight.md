> **Principle 36** — Learn to use deliberate mind-wandering: incubation and creative insight

Sio and Ormerod's meta-analysis of 117 studies found a positive incubation effect: setting a problem aside before returning to it improved solution rates, with divergent-thinking tasks benefiting most. The effect was larger when the incubation period was filled with a low cognitive demand task than with a demanding one, and for linguistic insight problems low-demand fillers even beat pure rest.

The best-known experimental demonstration, Baird and colleagues' undemanding-task study, reported that only participants whose incubation interval was filled with an undemanding task improved on previously encountered creative problems. That specific finding has not replicated: a 2024 two-study attempt with 443 participants found no evidence that mind wandering during a creative-incubation interval facilitates divergent-thinking creativity. The meta-analytic case for breaks and low-demand filler is stronger than the case for the mind-wandering mechanism itself.

> "The authors identified a positive incubation effect, with divergent thinking tasks benefiting more than linguistic and visual insight tasks from incubation. Longer preparation periods gave a greater incubation effect, whereas filling an incubation period with high cognitive demand tasks gave a smaller incubation effect." (Sio & Ormerod, 2009 — [abstract](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ827110) — Psychol Bull 135(1), pp. 94-120)

The compendium's practice takes the robust part: when stuck, set the problem aside and fill the break with something undemanding rather than something demanding or nothing in particular. Whether the benefit comes specifically from mind-wandering is not settled.

**Plain words**
When a problem stops yielding, continuing to hammer it has a poor record. The meta-analytic result is plain: set it aside, keep busy with something easy rather than something hard, and a longer initial effort before the break helped too. What the break does inside the head is not settled; the eureka-in-the-shower story is anecdote, and the one laboratory demonstration of the mind-wandering mechanism did not replicate.

**For example**
As an imagined illustration: stuck on a paragraph, you weed the garden for twenty minutes and come back to the page.

## Practice

- When stuck on a problem, deliberately step away and do something undemanding (walk, shower, wash dishes). The meta-analytic evidence favours low-demand filler over demanding tasks and over rest for some problem types. Insight is not guaranteed; the mechanism is not settled.
