> **Principle 24** — Recognise affect's relationship to motivation; use it, do not obey it

Functional accounts of emotion, as Keltner and Gross summarize them, define emotions as solutions to problems and opportunities related to physical and social survival, and focus on their beneficial consequences. ([Keltner & Gross, 1999](https://doi.org/10.1080/026999399379140)) In the exposure-therapy literature, avoidance is treated as a problem partly because it interferes with new learning by preventing violation in expectancy. ([Craske et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2014.04.006)) The compendium's application is to read what a strong feeling seems to carry — this matters, something was lost, a line was crossed — and then separately decide whether the action it suggests is wise.

> "Summarising diverse theories and observations, we propose that functional accounts of emotions: (1) address why humans have emotions; (2) define emotions as solutions to problems and opportunities related to physical and social survival; (3) treat emotions as systems of interrelated components; and (4) focus on the beneficial consequences of emotions." (Keltner, D., & Gross, J. J. (1999). Functional Accounts of Emotions. Cognition and Emotion, 13(5), 467-480)

**Plain words** Emotions, in the functionalist reading, exist because they solved problems for our ancestors — they carry information, not orders. The exercise here: when a feeling gets loud, write down what it seems to be saying, then separately decide what to do about it.

**For example (imagined):** The anxiety before a launch reads as "abort" if obeyed, and as "this matters, prepare" if read. Same feeling, two relationships to it.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Functional accounts of emotion|Functional accounts of emotion]]**, each emotion carries adaptive information about the environment (Keltner & Gross, 1999)
- **Avoidance maintains anxiety; approach reduces fear over time**, avoidance is negatively reinforcing and maintains anxiety disorders (Craske et al., 2014)

## Philosophy parallels

- **Emotion as judgement (pathē)** (Stoics, Epictetus)
- **[[Right action|Right action over right feeling]]** (Buddhism, Eightfold Path)
- **The passions as signals, not masters** (Hume, Treatise)

## Practice

- When you feel a strong emotion, extract the message ("this matters to me," "a boundary was crossed") and then decide whether the action it suggests is wise.
