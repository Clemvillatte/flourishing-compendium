> **Principle 102** — Act for the world's welfare

Krishna's advice to Arjuna on work in the world turns on one phrase, loka-sangraha, which Swami Mukundananda glosses as "the welfare of the masses". In 3.25 he sets the ignorant and the wise side by side:

> As ignorant people perform their duties with attachment to the results, O scion of Bharat, so should the wise act without attachment, for the sake of leading people on the right path.

(Bhagavad Gita 3.25, trans. Swami Mukundananda, [holy-bhagavad-gita.org](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/3/verse/25/).)

The wise do not stop acting. They act the same way the unattached do, with a different motive. Verse 3.20 gives the example: "By performing their prescribed duties, King Janak and others attained perfection. You should also perform your duties to set an example for the good of the world" ([BG 3.20-21](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/3/verse/20/)). Verse 3.4 states the other side: "One cannot achieve freedom from karmic reactions by merely abstaining from work, nor can one attain perfection of knowledge by mere physical renunciation" ([BG 3.4](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/3/verse/4/)). And 3.8 says that "action is superior to inaction".

The text does not contain an outright ban on abandoning work, and this translation renders loka-sangraha as welfare, not as holding the world together. What 3.4 and 3.8 show is that giving up work does not get you what you hoped for. The practical step of holding one role as service, a committee or a mentoring hour, is this page's own suggestion.

**Plain words**

Krishna does not ask Arjuna to withdraw. He asks him to act with less grip on results and more care for the people watching. Quitting duties to be more spiritual is the move the Gita warns against.

**For example**

A woman who has lately found meditation thinks about dropping her place on the school's parents' committee, which she finds draining. She keeps it, with the aim of doing it more cleanly rather than doing it less.
