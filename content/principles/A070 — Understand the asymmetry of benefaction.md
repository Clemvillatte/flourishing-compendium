> **Principle 70** — Understand the asymmetry of benefaction

Aristotle opens the question with the observation itself, verbatim:

> "Benefactors are thought to love those they have benefited, more than those who have been well treated love those that have treated them well, and this is discussed as though it were paradoxical." (NE IX.7, [Ross, MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.9.ix.html))

The common explanation he reports is debtor and creditor: "Most people think it is because the latter are in the position of debtors and the former of creditors... benefactors wish the objects of their action to exist since they will then get their gratitude, while the beneficiaries take no interest in making this return" (NE IX.7), helped along by the fact that "most people are forgetful, and are more anxious to be well treated than to treat others well." His own account is different: the beneficiary is the benefactor's handiwork. "Every man loves his own handiwork better than he would be loved by it if it came alive... for that which they have treated well is their handiwork, and therefore they love this more than the handiwork does its maker" (NE IX.7). The work contains the maker's activity, and "he loves his handiwork, therefore, because he loves existence" (NE IX.7).

The asymmetry is a fact about where love comes from, not a defect to be corrected. The practical reading: do not keep a ledger of what you have given. The giver's attachment is already the natural reward, and listing the giving as a debt converts the handiwork into a loan.

(NE IX.7.)

**Plain words** The one who gives usually ends up more attached than the one who receives. Aristotle's explanation: what you help create carries your own activity in it, and everyone loves what their own activity made. So the imbalance is normal, not a betrayal. Give freely and expect the ledger to stay unbalanced. If you catch yourself listing what you did for someone, you have started lending instead of giving.

*For example:* A parent who drove their kid to training for years doesn't hold it as a debt. If they start listing it, the giving has gone sour.
