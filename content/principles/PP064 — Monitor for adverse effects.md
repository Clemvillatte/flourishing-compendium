> **Principle 64** — Monitor for adverse effects

The strongest evidence is for meditation. A systematic review of 83 studies found that 55 (65%) reported at least one adverse event, with a pooled prevalence of 8.3%, which was 3.7% in experimental studies and 33.2% in observational studies. Anxiety (33%), depression (27%) and cognitive anomalies (25%) were the most common, and the events could occur in people with no history of mental health problems ([Farias et al. 2020, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/acps.13225)). The review's authors note this is similar to rates reported for psychotherapy in general. NCCIH adds that in three studies of mindfulness-based stress reduction, the programme was not more harmful than no treatment ([NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/meditation-and-mindfulness-effectiveness-and-safety)).

Monitoring is hard in practice. In a review of 231 mindfulness trials, 84.4% contained no information about measuring or monitoring adverse effects, so current reporting cannot give an accurate rate ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6612475/)). An implementation guide for mindfulness-based cognitive therapy lists possible risks including flashbacks during meditation for people with trauma histories ([Van Dam et al. 2018, repository copy](https://researchrepository.wvu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1733&context=ctsi)).

(Van Gordon et al., 2017; Dobkin, 2021.)

### Scope

Sources for the seed's claims about gratitude producing guilt or positive reframing feeling invalidating were not found or opened, so they are not stated as findings. Van Gordon 2017 and Dobkin 2021 are listed as further reading, not summarized here.

**Plain words**
Wellbeing practices can sometimes make people feel worse. Check how a practice is affecting you, and change or stop it if it does harm.

**For example**
After two weeks of a new practice, note whether anxiety, low mood or intrusive memories have risen, and talk to a clinician if they have.
