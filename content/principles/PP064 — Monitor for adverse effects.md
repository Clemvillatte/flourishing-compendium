> **Principle 64** — Monitor for adverse effects

An unwanted experience deserves attention even when a practice has helped before. A 2020 review of 83 studies, involving 6,703 meditation participants, estimated adverse-event prevalence at 8.3%, with considerable variation between study designs. Anxiety and depression were frequently reported; the pooled percentage is not a prediction of any one person's risk ([Farias et al., abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32820538/)).

Britton's 2019 review examines whether mindfulness-related processes can become unhelpful under particular conditions. It proposes studying dose, starting characteristics and context rather than assuming that more practice always helps ([Britton, abstract and “Mindfulness-related processes, non-monotonicity, and influencing factors”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6612475/)).

### Limits of the evidence

NCCIH cautions:

> However, few studies have examined these practices for potentially harmful effects, so it isn’t possible to make definite statements about safety.

([“Are meditation and mindfulness practices safe?”](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/meditation-and-mindfulness-effectiveness-and-safety).)

### Plain words

Ask whether the practice is helping you live, sleep and function, rather than judging success by minutes completed. These suggested checks are ways to notice difficulty, not a diagnosis or a validated safety test.

### For example

Optional monitoring routine (Attention; Overt behaviour): note sleep, distress and daily functioning before changing a self-directed practice. Compare subsequent notes with that starting point; if things worsen, pause and seek qualified guidance before deciding what to resume or retain. Do not wait for a fixed tracking period or use meditation to postpone professional care ([NCCIH, “Tips To Consider”](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/meditation-and-mindfulness-effectiveness-and-safety)). The research discussed here concerns meditation, not every flourishing practice.

## Psychology parallels

- **Adverse-event reporting**: the review by Farias and colleagues combines varied study designs and unwanted experiences. It supports taking reports seriously without treating one pooled rate as a personal risk forecast ([2020 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32820538/)).

## Related concepts

- [[Mindfulness]]: the clinical and contemplative practice family addressed by this evidence.
- [[Outcome monitoring in psychological treatment]]: a companion question about recognising a poor response, not proof that a self-kept log prevents harm.
- [[Self-monitoring (Michie et al.)]]: compare observing behaviour with the separate task of judging whether a practice is safe.
