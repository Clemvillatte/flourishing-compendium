> **Principle 178** — Practice without gaining ideas

Suzuki's name for the contaminant is the "gaining idea": sitting in order to acquire calm, insight, or enlightenment, which quietly turns practice into a transaction. His description of right practice keeps the effort and removes the goal. The effort goes into noticing the extra idea whenever it appears and dropping it, which he calls getting rid of "something extra." (Suzuki, ZMBM.)

> "When there is no gaining idea in what you do, then you do something. In zazen what you are doing is not for the sake of anything. You may feel as if you are doing something special, but actually it is only the expression of your true nature; it is the activity which appeases your inmost desire. But as long as you think you are practicing zazen for the sake of something, that is not true practice." (Suzuki, ZMBM, Weatherhill 1970)

In the Los Altos talks that became the book, he answers the obvious objection directly: if there is nothing to gain, what does effort even consist of?

> "People may ask you if you practice -- what do you mean to practice zazen with gaining idea? What kind of effort is necessary in your practice -- if you have no gaining idea? The answer is to get rid of something extra from our practice. So if some extra idea comes over you, you should try to stop it -- you should remain in pure practice. That is the point our effort is directed to." (Suzuki, lecture "Mistakes in Practice," Los Altos, April 13, 1967; ZMBM p. 71)

**Plain words**
When you sit, cook, or write in order to get something out of it, the getting stands between you and the doing. Try one practice activity with the getting set aside: sit without waiting for calm, cook without aiming at a compliment. When you catch yourself checking whether it worked, that catching is the practice. Suzuki's instruction is to drop the extra idea each time it shows up.

*For example:* A man starts meditating to reduce stress and rates each session by how calm he feels afterwards. The rating becomes the practice, and the sitting never gets a clean try. Setting the score aside is itself the exercise Suzuki describes, dropping the extra idea.
