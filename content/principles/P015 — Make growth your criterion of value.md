> **Principle 15** — Make growth your criterion of value

Dewey's criterion comes from Part III of Human Nature and Conduct: morality is a continuing process rather than a fixed achievement, and the good of growth in present action is the only good within our control ([Dewey 1922, HNC III.I](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/john-dewey/human-nature-and-conduct/text/chapter-4-1)).

> The good, satisfaction, "end," of growth of present action in shades and scope of meaning is the only good within our control, and the only one, accordingly, for which responsibility exists. (Dewey, Human Nature and Conduct, III.I, [Standard Ebooks](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/john-dewey/human-nature-and-conduct/text/chapter-4-1))

The surrounding paragraph closes the loop: morals is education, learning the meaning of what we are about and employing that meaning in action; growing and growth are the same fact ([Dewey 1922, HNC III.I](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/john-dewey/human-nature-and-conduct/text/chapter-4-1)). Growth as criterion therefore asks a specific question of any course of action: does it expand the meaning and scope of what you are doing, or merely its comfort?

(Dewey, Human Nature and Conduct, 1922; Experience and Nature, 1925.)

**Plain words**
Money, status and comfort all have a ceiling: past some point, more of them changes nothing you do. Learning compounds: this year's skill is next year's lever for skills you cannot yet name. That is why the growth criterion is bookkeeping as much as idealism: it counts the only asset that never finishes.

**For example**
When a job offer lands, run the two-column test: what you would actually be doing in month six under each option, and which column teaches more. The pay gap is a fact; the learning gap is the criterion.
