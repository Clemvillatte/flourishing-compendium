> **Principle 43** — Understand attentional restoration through meditation: rest in awareness

Tang, Hölzel and Posner discuss how effort during meditation may vary with experience, including the possibility that experienced practitioners use less deliberate top-down control ([review PDF](https://nazarethcastellanos.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/1_Tang_Holzel_Posner_2015_Nat_Rev_Neurosci.pdf)):

> "Expert meditators might not use this prefrontal control. Rather, they might have automated an accepting stance towards their experience and thus no longer engage in top-down control efforts but instead show enhanced bottom-up processing." (Tang, Hölzel & Posner, "The neuroscience of mindfulness meditation", Nature Reviews Neuroscience 16, 2015, "Neural mechanisms of improved emotion regulation" section, [review PDF](https://nazarethcastellanos.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/1_Tang_Holzel_Posner_2015_Nat_Rev_Neurosci.pdf))

The review itself marks this as unresolved:

> "Whether effort has a key role in PFC and ACC activation during or following meditation needs further investigation." (Tang, Hölzel & Posner, 2015, "Neural mechanisms of improved emotion regulation" section, [review PDF](https://nazarethcastellanos.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/1_Tang_Holzel_Posner_2015_Nat_Rev_Neurosci.pdf))

A theoretical account of the rest side comes from Kaplan, who analysed meditation from the perspective of attention restoration theory ([Kaplan 2001](https://www.nrs.fs.usda.gov/pubs/jrnl/2001/nc_2001_kaplan_005.pdf)). Kaplan notes a contrast first: meditation is discipline and training, while the restorative experience appears to require no training, and the exercise of discipline seems antithetical to rest and recovery — the two seem to be contrasting routes to the same goal ([Kaplan 2001](https://www.nrs.fs.usda.gov/pubs/jrnl/2001/nc_2001_kaplan_005.pdf), p. 483). His analysis then proposes a common ground in how the techniques treat directed attention:

> "What is striking about these techniques is that each in a different way discourages active control of the thought process. In part, this is achieved indirectly by fascination, such as fascination with slow, patterned movements. In taking over the mental process, fascination achieves control without effort." (Kaplan, "Meditation, restoration, and the management of mental fatigue", Environment and Behavior 33, 2001, p. 484, [PDF](https://www.nrs.fs.usda.gov/pubs/jrnl/2001/nc_2001_kaplan_005.pdf))

Kaplan proposes avoiding unnecessary directed attention through fascination or reduced effortful participation in thought ([Kaplan 2001](https://www.nrs.fs.usda.gov/pubs/jrnl/2001/nc_2001_kaplan_005.pdf), p. 484). This is a proposed mechanism; the passages quoted here do not establish measured recovery from attentional fatigue ([Kaplan 2001](https://www.nrs.fs.usda.gov/pubs/jrnl/2001/nc_2001_kaplan_005.pdf), p. 484).

**Plain words**
Kaplan's idea is that letting thoughts pass could spare some of the effort used to manage them ([Kaplan 2001](https://www.nrs.fs.usda.gov/pubs/jrnl/2001/nc_2001_kaplan_005.pdf), p. 484). You might notice a thought without following it into planning or problem-solving ([Kaplan 2001](https://www.nrs.fs.usda.gov/pubs/jrnl/2001/nc_2001_kaplan_005.pdf), p. 484).

**For example**
Imagine sitting quietly and noticing a sound, a bodily sensation or a thought as it arises. Practise allowing each to pass without repeatedly trying to hold attention on one object.

## Psychology parallels

- **Meditation and conservation of directed attention**, Kaplan proposes avoiding unnecessary effortful control of thought ([Kaplan 2001](https://www.nrs.fs.usda.gov/pubs/jrnl/2001/nc_2001_kaplan_005.pdf), p. 484)
- **Meditation is associated with stress reduction and attentional benefits**, the review reports broad support for beneficial effects on mental health and cognitive performance, and calls for more rigorous research designs ([Tang et al. 2015 review](https://nazarethcastellanos.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/1_Tang_Holzel_Posner_2015_Nat_Rev_Neurosci.pdf))

## Practice

- Alternate concentration practice (focused attention) with restorative practice (open monitoring, [[Body scan in MBSR (Kabat-Zinn)|body scan]], non-directive rest in awareness). Do not make all meditation effortful; include rest.
