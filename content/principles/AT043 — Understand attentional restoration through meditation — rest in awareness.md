> **Principle 43** — Understand attentional restoration through meditation: rest in awareness

Meditation — particularly open monitoring and non-directive meditation — allows attention to rest in awareness without effortful focus. This is restorative in a way distinct from concentration practice. It reduces cognitive load and allows recovery.

**Plain words** Certain kinds of meditation are genuine rest for attention — not another task, but the release of gripping. Open, easy awareness lets the focusing machinery power down. It's the difference between idling the engine and turning it off.

*For example:* Twenty minutes of non-striving meditation and the afternoon's focus is quietly different — not because you tried harder, but because the grip finally let go.
## Psychology parallels

- **Open monitoring meditation reduces cognitive load**, non-directive and open monitoring practices allow rest in awareness without effortful control (Lutz et al., 2008; Tang et al., 2015)
- **Meditation reduces stress and improves attentional capacity**, both directly and through restoration (Goleman & Davidson, 2017; Tang et al., 2015)

## Philosophy parallels

- **Shamatha and vipassana complementarity** (Buddhist meditation)
- **[[Wu wei|Wu wei as receptive awareness]]** (Daoism, Daodejing 16)
- **[[Gelassenheit|Gelassenheit (releasement)]]** (Heidegger, Discourse on Thinking)

## Practice

- Alternate concentration practice (focused attention) with restorative practice (open monitoring, body scan, non-directive rest in awareness). Do not make all meditation effortful; include rest.
