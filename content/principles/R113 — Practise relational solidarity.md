> **Principle 375** — Practise relational solidarity

Scholz defines political solidarity as "a unity of individuals who have made a conscious commitment to challenge a situation of injustice, oppression, tyranny, or social vulnerability". It is "fundamentally oppositional", and the commitment, with its positive moral duties, comes before the social bond ([Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews](https://ndpr.nd.edu/reviews/political-solidarity/)). Participants act on "a recognized interdependence" with others in the movement ([Cambridge chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/question-of-solidarity-in-law-and-politics/risking-solidarity/1C181DB7DBD844F05BEED3F3BE95F2A2)). That supports the seed's "your fate is bound up with" the fate of others, but I found no passage in these sources that contrasts solidarity with charity, so that contrast is the page's gloss.

Rorty (1989) gives a different route. In his words, solidarity "is not discovered by reflection but created", through "increasing our sensitivity to the particular details of the pain and humiliation of other, unfamiliar sorts of people" ([Rorty, Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity (PDF excerpt)](https://sites.pitt.edu/~rbrandom/Courses/Antirepresentationalism%20(2020)/Texts/rorty-contingency-irony-and-solidarity-1989.pdf)). He assigns this to the novel, ethnography and reportage, not to theory ([quoted on Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/8625455-in-my-utopia-human-solidarity-would-be-seen-not-as)).

The psychology citation does not match its label. Foster-Bey (2008) is a CIRCLE working paper on whether race, ethnicity, citizenship and socio-economic status determine civic engagement. It reports that civic engagement differs by race and ethnicity, and that lower socio-economic status goes with less engagement ([ERIC full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED505266.pdf)). The excerpt I read does not discuss well-being, so I do not claim that collective action improves well-being. Arendt (1958) was not opened.

**Plain words** Solidarity, as Scholz describes it, is a commitment to oppose an injustice together with others. It does not require shared identity. Rorty adds that it grows when we attend closely to how others suffer. Whether it improves your own well-being is not shown by the study cited here.

*For example:* This is an imagined case. A well-off resident joins the tenants' campaign, attends the meetings, and accepts the same risks as the other tenants.
