> **Principle 392** — Practise the more-than-human relational self

> The simple premise of this book is that we are human only in contact, and conviviality, with what is not human.

(Abram, The Spell of the Sensuous: Perception and Language in a More-Than-Human World, Pantheon, 1996, Preface (p. ix in the first edition); the line is quoted with that page in a [Scandinavian University Press chapter](https://www.scup.com/doi/full/10.18261/9788215069197-23-01) and others. In the Vintage edition it appears on p. 22 according to a [reader's notes](https://readingandwalking.ca/2021/07/12/david-abram-the-spell-of-the-sensuous-perception-and-language-in-a-more-than-human-world/amp/). The book itself was not opened in full.)

Abram links this to perception: "Our bodies have formed themselves in delicate reciprocity with the manifold textures, sounds, and shapes of an animate earth," and "To define the other being as an inert or passive object is to deny its ability to actively engage us and to provoke our senses; we thus block our perceptual reciprocity with that being" (as quoted in the same reader's notes, citing pp. 22 and 56 of the Vintage edition). Abram draws on Merleau-Ponty's account of perception here, and what he calls the more-than-human world includes human beings.

Two limits apply to the entry. It says these relationships "are not metaphorical." Abram says so in a phenomenological and anthropological register, drawing on his time with traditional healers, and the passages above are not empirical evidence about psychology. Plumwood's Feminism and the Mastery of Nature and Naess were not opened.

For the psychological parallel, Mayer and Frantz's Connectedness to Nature Scale appeared in the Journal of Environmental Psychology 24, 503-515 (2004), as confirmed by [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1016/j.jenvp.2004.10.001), but its abstract was not opened. A meta-analysis found that "individuals more strongly connected to nature demonstrated a greater engagement in self-reported" pro-environmental behaviour ([PMC7027494](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7027494/), discussion excerpt). That finding is about self-report and association.

**Plain words** Abram's claim is that human senses and thinking grew up in exchange with animals, landscapes and weather, so being cut off from them diminishes us. The compendium turns this into an invitation to count those relations as real parts of who you are.

*For example (imagined):* Someone who has walked the same river for years notices its changes with the seasons and finds that the habit shapes how they notice everything else.
