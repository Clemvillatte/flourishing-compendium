> **Principle 83** — Treat well-being as a practice, not a destination

The sentence behind this principle is Rogers's: "The good life is a process, not a state of being. It is a direction not a destination." It appears in his 1953 essay "The Good Life and the Fully Functioning Person", reprinted in the 1961 collection On Becoming a Person ([Panarchy.org excerpt](https://www.panarchy.org/rogers/person.html)). Before it he writes that "the good life is not any fixed state. It is not, in my estimation, a state of virtue, or contentment, or nirvana, or happiness." After it he adds that the direction is "selected by the total organism, when there is psychological freedom to move in any direction" (same excerpt). A longitudinal study of good-life coherence cites the passage at pp. 186-187 ([PMC8608581](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8608581/)).

That is Rogers's claim about the good life, not a finding about daily practice. The compendium's step from process to maintained, varied practices is its own inference.

Ryff's 1989 paper supplies a six-part list of well-being aspects: self-acceptance, positive relations with others, autonomy, environmental mastery, purpose in life and personal growth. She measured them in 321 adults of different ages, using self-ratings ([Ryff 1989](https://thefpr.org/wp-content/uploads/Ryff1989_happiness.pdf)). The paper is a cross-sectional measurement study, so it does not show that well-being is ongoing.

(Rogers, 1961; Ryff, 1989.)

### Scope

I read the Rogers passage in an online excerpt, not the book. I did not open the full Ryff paper beyond its abstract and opening pages.

**Plain words**
Rogers said the good life is a direction you keep moving in, not a place you reach. Ryff gave a way to measure parts of well-being, but did not test whether it needs daily upkeep.

**For example**
Once a year, rate yourself on Ryff's six aspects, then pick the one you most want to move.
