> **Principle 21** — Understand affective presence: your feelings influence others' feelings

Eisenkraft and Elfenbein asked the question directly: "How much do individuals consistently influence the way other people feel?" Their data came from 48 work groups, and the analysis suggested "consistent individual differences both in the emotions that people tend to experience (*trait affect*) and in the emotions that people tend to elicit in others (*trait affective presence*)" ([Eisenkraft & Elfenbein, 2010, Psychological Science 21(4), abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797610364117)). The same abstract reports the variance split: trait affective presence accounted for 10% of the variance in positive affect and 23% in negative affect, after controlling for emotional contagion. The compendium's exercise follows from the finding's limits: it was measured in groups that worked together, and it establishes that the pattern exists, not that any given interaction transfers a mood.

**Plain words** The finding is about a pattern, not a moment. Some people leave others feeling a bit better across many interactions, some a bit worse, and the people around them can tell you which. The useful question to a trusted colleague is not how you felt today but how they tend to feel after time with you.

*For example:* (Imagined case.) A manager asks two team members, separately, how they usually feel after a one-on-one. Both hesitate, then one says drained, the other says clearer. Two private answers are not the study's measurement; they are where the compendium's practice starts.

- **Affective presence as a stable trait**, some people reliably make others feel better or worse across interactions (Anderson et al., 2003)
- **Emotional contagion is measurable and automatic**, facial mimicry and affective transfer occur without conscious awareness (Hatfield et al., 2014)
