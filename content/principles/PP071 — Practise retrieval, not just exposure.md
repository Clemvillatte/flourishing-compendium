> **Principle 71** — Practise retrieval, not just exposure

Reading something again feels like learning it. Roediger and Karpicke's experiments on the testing effect found the opposite at delays: students who took free-recall tests on prose passages, without feedback, retained more a week later than students who reread the same passages the same number of times. Their abstract names the effect:

> "Taking a memory test not only assesses what one knows, but also enhances later retention, a phenomenon known as the testing effect." ([Roediger and Karpicke, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/))

The time course is what makes it practical. Rereading won on the test given five minutes later; retrieval won at two days and a week:

> "When the final test was given after 5 min, repeated studying improved recall relative to repeated testing. However, on the delayed tests, prior testing produced substantially greater retention than studying, even though repeated studying increased students' confidence in their ability to remember the material." ([Roediger and Karpicke, 2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/))

A later experiment extended the result from recall to understanding. Students who practiced retrieval outperformed students who built concept maps, and the advantage held on questions that assessed comprehension:

> "The advantage of retrieval practice was observed with test questions that assessed comprehension and required students to make inferences." ([Karpicke and Blunt, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21252317/))

(Roediger and Karpicke, 2006; Karpicke and Blunt, 2011.)

**Plain words** The page supplies the words whenever you reread it. Close it and try to supply them yourself: what was the author's main point? That is the distinction to practise here—recalling the material instead of having it in front of you.

*For example:* After reading an essay, close it and jot down its argument from memory. Then compare your account with the text.
