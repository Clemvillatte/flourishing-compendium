> **Principle 23** — Set specific and difficult goals: vagueness kills motivation


Goal-setting theory comes from decades of experiments. Locke and Latham's review of 35 years of research covers the theory's "core findings", the mechanisms by which goals operate, the moderators of goal effects, and its limitations (American Psychologist 57(9):705-717, [abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12237980/)). The summary of Locke and Latham 2002 here is drawn from its abstract, and the details below come from other sources.

One of those sources is a meta-analysis of group goals (Kleingeld, van Mierlo and Arends 2011, J Applied Psychology 96(6):1289-1304). It found that "specific difficult goals yield considerably higher group performance compared with nonspecific goals" (d = 0.80, k = 23). Moderately difficult and easy goals also helped, "but these effects were smaller" ([abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21744940/)). The same abstract warns that individual goals aimed at maximising personal performance did badly inside interdependent groups (d = -1.75, k = 6).

The principle's title says vagueness kills motivation. The evidence supports a weaker claim: specific, hard goals usually beat "do your best". It does not show that vague goals destroy motivation.

Critics argue the benefits are overstated. Ordóñez and colleagues list side effects of goal setting: "a narrow focus that neglects nongoal areas, distorted risk preferences, a rise in unethical behavior, inhibited learning, corrosion of organizational culture, and reduced intrinsic motivation" (Academy of Management Perspectives 23(1), 2009, [abstract](https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/amp.2009.37007999)). They call goals "prescription-strength medication" that needs careful dosing.

**Plain words** A clear target with some stretch usually gets more effort than "do your best". Use it with care, since the target can narrow your attention and crowd out other things you value.

*For example:* This is an imagined case. A writer replaces "write more" with "300 words before breakfast, five days a week". After a month she checks whether the rule is still serving the essay, or only the count.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Goal setting theory (Locke & Latham)|Goal setting theory (Locke & Latham)]]**, specific, difficult goals outperformed nonspecific goals in a group-goal meta-analysis (Kleingeld et al., 2011); the 35-year review is Locke & Latham (2002); Latham & Locke (2007) and Locke & Latham (2006) were not opened
- **Side effects and limits**, goal setting can narrow focus, distort risk preferences, raise unethical behaviour and reduce intrinsic motivation (Ordóñez et al., 2009); the older note citing Miner (2005) is unopened

## Practice

- Set specific, difficult goals: not "exercise more" but "walk 30 minutes, 4 times per week." Not "write more" but "write 500 words daily." Specific and challenging beats vague and easy.
