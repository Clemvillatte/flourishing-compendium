> **Principle 66** — Watch states arise and pass

The Satipatthana Sutta makes the mind something you can watch. In its contemplation of mind, the practitioner notes states as present or absent, aversion among them:

> "When the mind has aversion, he discerns that the mind has aversion. When the mind is without aversion, he discerns that the mind is without aversion." (MN 10, contemplation of mind, trans. Thanissaro, [Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.010.than.html))

The same contemplation then trains attention on the change itself:

> "Or he remains focused on the phenomenon of origination with regard to the mind, on the phenomenon of passing away with regard to the mind, or on the phenomenon of origination & passing away with regard to the mind." (MN 10, contemplation of mind, trans. Thanissaro, [Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.010.than.html))

The verse the principle pairs with it:

> "'All conditioned things are impermanent' — when one sees this with wisdom, one turns away from suffering." (Dhammapada 277, trans. Buddharakkhita, [Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/dhp/dhp.20.budd.html))

Dhammapada 277 connects seeing impermanence with wisdom to turning away from suffering.

(MN 10; Dhammapada 277.)

### Plain words

As a practical exercise, describe the feeling before choosing a response. Ask what you can notice about its arrival and any changes since. If you are still angry, include that in the description; you do not have to report a change you have not observed.

### For example

Imagine receiving a message that angers you. Leave the reply unsent while you note what is happening, then decide whether and how to respond.
