> **Principle 300** — Practise whakawhanaungatanga

Whakawhanaungatanga is the process of establishing relationships. Bishop, Berryman, Cavanagh and Teddy (2007) list it with related terms: "whānaunga (relatives), whānaungatanga (relationships), whakawhānaungatanga (the process of establishing relationships) and whakapapa (literally, the means of establishing relationships)". In their classroom study they write that "trust, connectedness and commitment, as evidenced in for example caring and the setting of high expectations" develop through the involvement of teachers with students, and that this is "fundamental to the process of establishing relationships, that is whakawhānaungatanga". They also report that "when Māori students have good relationships with their teachers, they are able to thrive at school" ([Bishop et al. 2007, Te Kōtahitanga Phase 3](https://thehub.sia.govt.nz/assets/documents/40792_Te_Kotahitanga_Phase3_0.pdf)). The report's strongest numbers (for example numeracy effect size d = .76 against .52 for a comparison group) come from a whole programme of teacher change, so they cannot be credited to relationship-building alone.

The seed frames the practice as building relationships "across cultural, social or personal difference". In this source, the setting is a school, and the relationship is between teachers and Māori students. The wider reading is an extension, not a quotation.

For the psychology parallel, Pettigrew and Tropp (2006) analysed 713 samples from 515 studies and found that "intergroup contact typically reduces intergroup prejudice" ([abstract](https://www.ovid.com/journals/jpspy/abstract/00005205-200605000-00004~a-meta-analytic-test-of-intergroup-contact-theory)). A later re-evaluation using only randomised studies agreed with that headline but found that "interventions directed at ethnic or racial prejudice" generated "substantially weaker effects" and noted gaps in studies of adults ([Paluck, Green and Green 2019](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/bpubpo/v3y2019i02p129-158_00.html)). Contact research measures prejudice between groups, which is related to whakawhanaungatanga but not the same thing.

**Plain words** Whakawhanaungatanga means the process of establishing relationships. In the main source, it is shown through caring, trust and high expectations between teachers and students. Contact between groups also tends to reduce prejudice, with weaker effects for racial and ethnic prejudice in the randomised studies.

*For example:* This is an imagined case. A nurse learns a greeting in each language spoken on her ward, and uses it each morning.
