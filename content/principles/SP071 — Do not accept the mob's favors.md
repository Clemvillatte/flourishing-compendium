> **Principle 71** — Do not accept the mob's favors

The anchor is IV, P70:

> "The free man, who lives among the ignorant, strives, as far as he can, to avoid receiving favours from them." (Ethics IV, P70, [Elwes](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800))

The source says "the ignorant", not crowds as such, and the problem it describes is mismatched valuation, not a scheming giver. The proof: the ignorant benefactor "puts his own estimate upon" the benefit, and "if it appears to be estimated less highly by the receiver, will feel pain", while the free man desires to join others in friendship, "not repaying their benefits with others reckoned as of like value", but guiding himself and others "by the free decision of reason". The difficulty arises when the giver expects the receiver to share the valuation: matching it becomes the pressure, and the free person seeks to act by reason rather than make matching the rule.

The note supplies the qualification the title omits: "it is often necessary to accept favours from them... we must, therefore, exercise caution in declining favours, lest we should have the appearance of despising those who bestow them... and so give ground for offence by the very fact of striving to avoid it." Necessity can require accepting, and then repaying in kind; courtesy governs the occasions of declining. The page's exercise drawn from the account: distinguish gifts from investments in your future compliance, and decline the second kind early, when the price is low.

**Plain words**
A favour carries the giver's estimate of its worth. If they expect you to share it and your gratitude falls short, they take offence, and the pressure is to follow their desires rather than reason. The free person avoids that difficulty where he can, accepts and repays in kind where he must, and declines with courtesy, because a rude refusal creates the very offence the rule was meant to avoid.

*For example:* Imagined: a neighbour insists on paying for a repair you could have managed, then expects hours of help with his renovation in return. The free response thanks him, names the imbalance kindly, and either settles it in kind or politely declines the next favour.
