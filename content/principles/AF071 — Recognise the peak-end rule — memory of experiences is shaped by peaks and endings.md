> **Principle 71** — Recognise the peak-end rule: memory of experiences is shaped by peaks and endings

In the original cold-water experiment, participants held a hand in 14 degree water for 60 seconds, and in a second trial for 60 seconds plus 30 more as the water warmed slightly. A significant majority chose to repeat the longer trial, "apparently preferring more pain over less". The authors concluded that evaluations of aversive experiences "are often dominated by the discomfort at the worst and at the final moments of episodes" ([Kahneman et al. 1993](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x)). In a colonoscopy trial of 682 outpatients, a milder ending made the final moments rate as less painful (1.7 vs 2.5) and the whole experience as less unpleasant (4.4 vs 4.9) ([Redelmeier, Katz and Kahneman 2003](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/redelmeier_et_al._2003_-_memories_of_colonoscopy.pdf)).

The claim that you do not remember the average needs one correction. A meta-analysis of 174 effect sizes found a large peak-end effect (r = 0.581), "comparable to the effect of the overall average (mean) score", with duration "essentially nil" ([Alaybek et al. 2022](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jobhdp/v170y2022ics0749597822000334.html)). So peaks and ends matter a lot, but so does the average.

### Scope

[[T022 — Kahneman, Daniel|Kahneman]]'s 1993 full text and the Redelmeier and Kahneman 1996 paper are cited at second hand, and the evidence concerns ratings of specific episodes, such as pain, films and workdays; no study of family holidays was found, so the holiday example is dropped.

**Plain words**
Memory keeps two souvenirs from each experience: the moment that hurt or thrilled most, and the moment it ended. Everything else, including how long it went on, is thrown away. This is why a longer ordeal can be remembered as the better one if it faded out gently, and why the last minute matters more than fairness says it should.

**For example**
When you plan a long meeting or a hard task, decide in advance how it will end, and let the last few minutes be the gentlest.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Peak-end rule|Peak-end rule]]**, retrospective evaluation of experiences is disproportionately shaped by the peak and the final moments (Kahneman et al., 1993; Redelmeier & Kahneman, 1996)

## Philosophy parallels

- **The whole is in the part** (Whitehead, Process and Reality)
- **[[Ending as completion|Ending as completion]]** ([[T002 — Aristotle|Aristotle]], Nicomachean Ethics I)

## Practice

- When designing an experience (a trip, a project, a gathering), invest in the peak and the ending. A strong ending can redeem a mediocre middle.
