> **Principle 61** — Learn to use breath as the master regulator: many techniques

A systematic review of slow-breathing studies finds the techniques promote autonomic changes, increasing heart rate variability and respiratory sinus arrhythmia, with psychological outputs of increased comfort, relaxation, pleasantness, vigor and alertness, and reduced symptoms of arousal, anxiety, depression, anger and confusion ([Zaccaro et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353/full)). A meta-analysis of randomised breathwork trials puts the stress effect at a small-to-medium size: across twelve trials with 785 adults, breathwork was associated with lower self-reported stress than control conditions, g = -0.35, though the authors note most studies carried moderate risk of bias and urge caution about hype ([Fincham et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36624160/)).

One head-to-head trial compared techniques. In the Stanford remote randomized study, three daily five-minute breathwork exercises were compared with an equivalent period of mindfulness meditation over one month: cyclic sighing, which emphasizes prolonged exhalations; box breathing, with equal duration of inhalations, breath retentions and exhalations; and cyclic hyperventilation with retention, with longer inhalations and shorter exhalations ([Balban et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/)). Breathwork, especially the exhale-focused cyclic sighing, produced greater improvement in mood and reduction in respiratory rate than mindfulness meditation ([Balban et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/)).

> "Daily 5-min cyclic sighing has promise as an effective stress management exercise." (Balban et al. 2023, [Cell Reports Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/))

**Plain words**
Slow breathing reliably raises heart rate variability and respiratory sinus arrhythmia, and one month-long trial found the exhale-lengthened sigh a better mood and respiratory-rate tool than mindfulness at five minutes a day. This is not a licence for any breath protocol: the review is explicit that each technique has specific effects rather than one shared recipe, and the hyperventilation-style exercises made some trial participants uncomfortable. Learn one slow technique, prefer the exhale-lengthened one, and stop anything that feels bad.

*For example:* An imagined case: someone learns one technique, the exhale-lengthened sigh, and does it for five minutes a day, the dose the opened trial tested; other techniques wait until that one is habitual.

## Psychology parallels

- **Breath as autonomic regulator (Lehrer, Zaccaro)**, breathing directly regulates the autonomic nervous system (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014; Zaccaro et al., 2018)
- **Specific techniques**, slow breathing (relaxation), physiological sigh (exhale-focused practice), coherent breathing (~6 breaths/min for HRV) (Balban et al., 2023; Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014)

## Practice

- Learn breath techniques: slow breathing for relaxation ([Zaccaro et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353/full)); cyclic sighing, emphasizing prolonged exhalations, for acute stress and daily mood ([Balban et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/)). Five minutes daily is the dose the opened trial tested.
