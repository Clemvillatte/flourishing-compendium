> **Principle 61** — Learn to use breath as the master regulator: many techniques

The physiological case for slow breathing is the best documented. A systematic review of slow-breathing studies finds the techniques promote autonomic changes, increasing heart rate variability and respiratory sinus arrhythmia, with psychological outputs of increased comfort, relaxation, pleasantness, vigor and alertness, and reduced symptoms of arousal, anxiety, depression, anger and confusion ([Zaccaro et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353/full)). A meta-analysis of randomised breathwork trials puts the stress effect at a small-to-medium size: across twelve trials with 785 adults, breathwork was associated with lower self-reported stress than control conditions, g = -0.35, though the authors note most studies carried moderate risk of bias and urge caution about hype ([Fincham et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36624160/)).

The head-to-head trial evidence concerns which technique. In the Stanford remote randomized study, three daily five-minute breathwork exercises were compared with an equivalent period of mindfulness meditation over one month: cyclic sighing, which emphasizes prolonged exhalations; box breathing, with equal duration of inhalations, breath retentions and exhalations; and cyclic hyperventilation with retention, with longer inhalations and shorter exhalations ([Balban et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/)). Breathwork, especially the exhale-focused cyclic sighing, produced greater improvement in mood and reduction in respiratory rate than mindfulness meditation ([Balban et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/)).

> "Daily 5-min cyclic sighing has promise as an effective stress management exercise." (Balban et al. 2023, [Cell Reports Medicine](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/))

**Plain words**
Breathing is the one autonomic function you can steer by hand, and the slow exhale is the lever the trial liked best. The toolkit claim survives with an honest note: the opened studies compared techniques against each other and found differences, with sighing ahead of mindfulness on mood and respiratory rate, so "each has specific effects" is roughly right; but the trials have not mapped every technique to every situation. Learn one exhale-lengthening technique well; that is where the evidence is ([Balban et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/)).

*For example:* An imagined case: someone who learns one technique, the exhale-lengthened sigh, and uses it before difficult conversations and at bedtime. Five minutes a day is the dose the opened trial tested; the rest of the toolkit can wait until one technique is habitual ([Balban et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/)).

## Psychology parallels

- **Breath as autonomic regulator (Lehrer, Zaccaro)**, breathing directly regulates the autonomic nervous system (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014; Zaccaro et al., 2018)
- **Specific techniques**, slow breathing (relaxation), physiological sigh (exhale-focused practice), coherent breathing (~6 breaths/min for HRV) (Balban et al., 2023; Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014)

## Practice

- Learn breath techniques: slow breathing for relaxation ([Zaccaro et al. 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353/full)); cyclic sighing, emphasizing prolonged exhalations, for acute stress and daily mood ([Balban et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/)). Five minutes daily is the dose the opened trial tested.
