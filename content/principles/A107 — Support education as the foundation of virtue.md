> **Principle 107** — Support education as the foundation of virtue

Aristotle does not hand out a ready-made curriculum. His case is that character depends on training, and that law and the city have to supply it. In [NE X.9 (Ross, MIT Classics)](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.10.x.html) arguments can encourage "the generous-minded among our youth" but "are not able to encourage the many to nobility and goodness", and "it is difficult to get from youth up a right training for virtue if one has not been brought up under right laws."

The city, he says in [Politics I.2 (Jowett, MIT Classics)](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.1.one.html), continues in existence "for the sake of a good life". So the [Politics VIII.1](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.8.eight.html) opening follows:

> No one will doubt that the legislator should direct his attention above all to the education of youth; for the neglect of education does harm to the constitution.

He also admits (VIII.2) that nobody agrees on the aim: "should the useful in life, or should virtue, or should the higher knowledge, be the aim of our training; all three opinions have been entertained." His own plan is far more state-directed than most readers will want. He says education "should be public, and not private" and that no citizen "belongs to himself". The compendium keeps his question, what schooling is for, and not his answer.

(NE X.9; Politics I.2, VIII.1-3.)

**Plain words** Aristotle thinks people become good through habits formed early, and that most people will not get there through argument alone. That makes the shape of schooling a moral matter. When he asks whether teaching should aim at usefulness, virtue or higher knowledge, he leaves it open, and so does this principle.

For example (imagined): a parent-teacher group that argues for regular class time on cooperation and honest work, alongside exam results, is treating education as character formation.
