> **Principle 107** — Support education as the foundation of virtue

[[T002 — Aristotle|Aristotle]] treats education as a public concern because character depends on training. In [NE X.9 (Ross, MIT Classics)](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.10.x.html) arguments can encourage "the generous-minded among our youth" but "are not able to encourage the many to nobility and goodness", and "it is difficult to get from youth up a right training for virtue if one has not been brought up under right laws."

The city, he says in [Politics I.2 (Jowett, MIT Classics)](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.1.one.html), continues in existence "for the sake of a good life". In [Politics VIII.1](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.8.eight.html), he connects virtue with preparation:

> Again, for the exercise of any faculty or art a previous training and habituation are required; clearly therefore for the practice of virtue.

He reports disagreement about the aim (VIII.2): "should the useful in life, or should virtue, or should the higher knowledge, be the aim of our training; all three opinions have been entertained." He nevertheless argues for education that is "public, and not private" (VIII.1), limits narrowly useful training, and considers learning for the good use of leisure (VIII.2–3). The precise liberal curriculum remains for further discussion.

(NE X.9; Politics I.2, VIII.1-3.)

**Plain words** What schools teach matters to the kind of people children become. Aristotle thinks arguments alone cannot replace years of practice, and he gives the state responsibility for that practice. He reports disagreement about education's purpose without remaining neutral himself. A contemporary reader can ask what schooling should cultivate without accepting his political programme.

For example: a parent-teacher group that argues for regular class time on cooperation and honest work, alongside exam results, is treating education as character formation.
