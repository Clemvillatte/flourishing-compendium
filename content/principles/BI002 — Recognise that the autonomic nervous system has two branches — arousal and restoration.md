> **Principle 2** — Recognise that the autonomic nervous system has two branches: arousal and restoration

The two branches are the textbook fact: sympathetic arousal and parasympathetic restoration. The research the page leans on is about their balance. In a meta-analysis of HRV and neuroimaging studies, Thayer and colleagues report that HRV is associated with the amygdala and ventromedial prefrontal cortex, and propose HRV as a proxy for the flexible control their model calls "vertical integration."

> "Thus, HRV may serve as a proxy for 'vertical integration' of the brain mechanisms that guide flexible control over behavior with peripheral physiology, and as such provides an important window into understanding stress and health." (Thayer et al. 2012, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22178086/))

Lehrer and Gevirtz's review of HRV biofeedback finds the most supported mechanism "the strengthening of homeostasis in the baroreceptor", with effects on the vagal afferent pathway to frontal cortical areas as a recent proposal. The compendium applies the two papers as a question: which branch is holding the day, and can the balance be trained?

**Plain words**
The gas-and-brake picture is a picture: two settings, both needed, trouble when stuck in one. The research adds that the balance is measurable (HRV) and possibly trainable (biofeedback). The question for a stressed week is not whether you feel activated but whether you can still shift.

*For example:* Suppose a jaw clenched since the morning email. As a modern application, the principle's question is which pedal the week is holding, and the reviewed training (HRV biofeedback) is one candidate answer, not a single-exhale fix.
