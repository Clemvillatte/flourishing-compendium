> **Principle 32** — Learn to build exercise habits: consistency over intensity

Lally and colleagues tracked habit formation directly: 96 volunteers chose an everyday eating, drinking or activity behaviour to carry out daily in the same context for 12 weeks, self-reporting each day. Automaticity rose with repetition in a consistent context along an asymptotic curve, and the study's model fit best for those who performed the behaviour more consistently. (Lally et al., 2010.)

> "With repetition of a behaviour in a consistent context, automaticity increases following an asymptotic curve which can be modelled at the individual level." (Lally et al., 2010, [abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674))

The study's numbers put the two practical rules in scale. Reaching near-plateau took anywhere from 18 to 254 days depending on the person and behaviour, so one habit should not be judged by another's calendar. And a missed day was not a verdict. (Lally et al., 2010.)

> "Missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit formation process." (Lally et al., 2010, [abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674))

**Plain words**
Pick the smallest version of the exercise you will actually do on a bad day, and anchor it to one fixed context: after breakfast, at the desk, before the shower. The plan's job is to keep the repetitions coming in that same context; the intensity can stay small. A missed day is a data point about the day, not about the habit.

*For example:* January's gym membership versus the person who does ten minutes after work every day. The compounding follows the repetitions, not the resolution.

## Practice

- Build exercise habits: schedule it, make it specific (time, place, type), and start small. Consistency beats intensity. Do not wait for motivation; build the habit.
