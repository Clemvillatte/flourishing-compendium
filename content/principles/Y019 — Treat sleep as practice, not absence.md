> **Principle 19** — Treat sleep as practice, not absence

Sutra 1.6 names five fluctuations of the mind, and sleep is one of them. Sutra 1.10 defines it:

> Sleep is a fluctuation [of mind-stuff] supported by the cause of the [transient] negation [of the waking and the dreaming fluctuations].

(Yoga Sutras 1.10, trans. James Haughton Woods, *The Yoga-System of Patanjali*, Harvard Oriental Series 17, 1914, p. 29, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/yogasystemofpata00wooduoft).)

The commentary argues that sleep has content, because a person wakes with a memory of it: "I have slept well, my mind is calm, it makes my understanding clear; I have slept poorly, my mind is dull, it wanders unsteadfast." Without some experience during sleep, it says, the sleeper would have no such memory on waking. It adds that "in concentration it also, like any other presented-idea, must be restricted".

So the text treats sleep as a state of mind that shows in its effects, and in concentration it is something to be stilled like any other movement. That second point cuts against the idea that sleep is a pillar of practice. The reading that a rested mind is the practicable mind is this page's own, and it leans on the Gita: those who "sleep too much or too little" cannot succeed in yoga, while those "regulated in sleep" can ease their sorrows (BG 6.16-17, trans. Swami Mukundananda, [holy-bhagavad-gita.org](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/6/verse/16/)).

**Plain words**

It is easy to count sleep as time off from your inner life. The old commentary says otherwise: how your mind feels on waking tells you how you slept, so sleep is doing something to it. Treating the first minute of waking as a deliberate pause is a modern suggestion for noticing that.

**For example**

A person who sees sleep as lost time and meditation as the fix tries a week of protected bedtimes before adding more sitting. The sitting goes better, which fits the commentary's remark about sleeping well or poorly.
