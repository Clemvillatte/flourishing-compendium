> **Principle 22** — Recognise affective contagion in groups; do not mistake it for your own feeling

Groups amplify and synchronise affect. A meeting that feels "tense," a crowd that feels "angry," a family that feels "anxious" — these are partly group-level phenomena that you are picking up, not purely your own individual response.

**Plain words** Groups amplify and synchronise affect: a meeting that feels "tense," a crowd that feels "angry," a family that feels "anxious" — partly group-level phenomena you're picking up, not generating. Don't mistake the group's weather for your own feeling.

*For example:* The person who leaves every family gathering convinced they have an anxiety problem — and the experiment of a weekend alone: no anxiety. The signal was real; the source was the room. Some feelings need to be attributed to the ventilation, not the lungs.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Group affective synchronisation|Group affective synchronisation]]**, emotional states converge within groups over time (Barsade, 2002)
- **[[Emotional contagion|Emotional contagion via mimicry and feedback]]**, unconsciously imitating expressions and postures feeds back into felt experience (Hatfield et al., 2014)

## Philosophy parallels

- **The crowd as a source of untruth** (Kierkegaard, The Crowd is Untruth)
- **Nietzsche's critique of herd morality** (Nietzsche, Beyond Good and Evil)

## Practice

- After leaving a group that felt emotionally charged, ask: "was this my feeling, or did I pick it up?" This is not a denial of the feeling; it is a question about its source.
