> **Principle 31** — Recognise loss aversion: losses hurt more than gains feel good

The 1979 prospect theory paper names the asymmetry in the attitudes data: a salient characteristic of attitudes to changes in welfare is that losses loom larger than gains, and the aggravation of losing a sum of money appears greater than the pleasure of gaining the same amount, so most people find symmetric bets distinctly unattractive ([Kahneman & Tversky 1979](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Kahneman_Tversky_1979_Prospect_theory.pdf)). The paper's model encodes it: the value function is generally concave for gains and commonly convex for losses, and is steeper for losses than for gains ([Kahneman & Tversky 1979](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Kahneman_Tversky_1979_Prospect_theory.pdf)).

> "A salient characteristic of attitudes to changes in welfare is that losses loom larger than gains. The aggravation that one experiences in losing a sum of money appears to be greater than the pleasure associated with gaining the same amount." (Kahneman & Tversky 1979, [Econometrica 47(2): 263-291, p. 279](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Kahneman_Tversky_1979_Prospect_theory.pdf))

**Plain words**
In this account, equal cash gains and losses need not carry equal psychological weight ([Kahneman & Tversky 1979](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Kahneman_Tversky_1979_Prospect_theory.pdf)). Someone can dislike an even bet because the possible loss counts more heavily for them ([Kahneman & Tversky 1979](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Kahneman_Tversky_1979_Prospect_theory.pdf)). The paper describes a tendency, so check how you respond to a particular gamble.

*For example:* An imagined case: someone is offered an even bet, equal gain, equal loss, and watches which side their mind dwells on.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Prospect theory|Prospect theory and loss aversion]]**, losses loom larger than gains by a factor of ~2 (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979; Tversky & Kahneman, 1992)
- **Loss aversion is robust across domains**, including money, status, and relationships (Kahneman et al., 1990)

## Practice

- When you notice reluctance to give something up, ask: "is this proportional to the actual loss, or is loss aversion amplifying it?" Deliberately reframe in terms of both gains and losses.
