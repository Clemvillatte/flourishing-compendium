> **Principle 331** — Practise care as a practice, not a feeling

Tronto's account has four phases, each tied to a moral element: caring about (attentiveness), taking care of (responsibility), care giving (competence) and care receiving (responsiveness) ([ANA guide](https://www.nursingworld.org/globalassets/catalog/book-toc/guide-to-the-code-of-ethics-sample-chapter.pdf); [Oxford University Research Archive](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:3bcc060c-20d4-4af3-bd15-3f3c914f996d/files/r5712m751d), citing Tronto 1993, pp. 105-108). The seed's four elements match, but they are tied to phases of a process and are not four parallel tasks. Competence, for instance, means care "effectively meet[s] needs" and not only that it is well meant ([BMC Medical Ethics, 2026](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12910-026-01474-8)). Responsiveness means the recipient's reaction counts: care is only clear to have happened when the receiver affirms it, and recipients "can refuse or amend the way their needs are being met".

The claim "care isn't a feeling" needs care. Nurse researchers summarise the first phase, caring about, as one that "includes concern, worry about someone or something" ([PMC study of nurses](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10976790/)). That is their reading of Tronto, but the seed's "the feeling is optional" is not in the sources I read. Later work also adds a fifth phase, caring with, linked to solidarity and justice ([PMC, care ethics and eating](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11308360/)). I did not read Moral Boundaries itself.

The psychology bullet cites Goetz et al. (2010) as "compassion training", but that is a review, not a training study. Its abstract argues compassion evolved as "a distinct affective experience whose primary function is to facilitate cooperation and protection of the weak and those who suffer" ([Psychological Bulletin, ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ884804)). Psychology here treats compassion as the feeling that motivates caregiving, which complements Tronto's focus on the work.

**Plain words** Tronto treats care as a process: notice a need, take responsibility, do the work competently, and check whether the person received what they needed. Feeling concern starts it, and the practice finishes it.

*For example:* This is an imagined case. A sibling worries about a parent's missed medication, takes responsibility, sets up a pill organiser, and later asks whether it works for them.
