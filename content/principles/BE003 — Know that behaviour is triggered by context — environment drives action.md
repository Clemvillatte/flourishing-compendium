> **Principle 3** — Know that behaviour is triggered by context: environment drives action

Wood and Neal describe how habits form: they "emerge from the gradual learning of associations between responses and the features of performance contexts that have historically covaried with them (e.g., physical settings, preceding actions)." ([Wood & Neal, 2007, Psychological Review 114, 843-863](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17907866/)). Once a habit exists, they write:

> perception of contexts triggers the associated response without a mediating goal.

Goals still matter, since they motivate the repetition that builds a habit. Marteau and colleagues add a health angle. Interventions that ask people to reflect "are often ineffectual, which is in keeping with the observation that much human behavior is automatic, cued by environmental stimuli" ([Marteau, Hollands & Fletcher, 2012, Science 337, 1492-1495](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22997327/)). They propose that targeting these automatic processes "may be more effective", which is a hypothesis, not a result. Gardner's review adds a caution: most habit research uses correlational designs and self-report ([Gardner, 2015, Health Psychology Review 9, 277-295](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25207647/)).

**Plain words** Where a behaviour has often happened, the setting can set it off without a decision. So changing the setting is one lever for change. The sources do not show that willpower or motivation play a minor role overall.

For example (imagined): someone who always scrolls their phone on the sofa after dinner may find the urge weaker at the kitchen table, because the sofa is the cue.

## Practice

- Design your environment: make desired behaviours easy (reduce friction) and undesired behaviours hard (increase friction). Do not rely on willpower; rely on context.
