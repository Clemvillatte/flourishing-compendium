> **Principle 156** — Let your actions be consistent with your words

Integrity is the alignment of speech and action. The Stoic does not say one thing and do another. This is not moralism but practical wisdom: inconsistency breeds inner conflict, and inner conflict breeds suffering. Practise saying less and doing more.

Seneca gives Lucilius a short instruction: "Prove your words by your deeds." ([Letter 20.1, Richard Mott Gummere translation](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_20)) He then brings the test home: do you spend lavishly on yourself while treating your family meanly, or display a public way of life at odds with your private one ([Letter 20.3](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_20))?

The standard is demanding without requiring an unbroken performance of perfection: Seneca allows that the philosopher cannot always keep the same pace, though he can keep travelling the same path ([Letter 20.2](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_20)). Marcus makes room for a similar return after failure, telling himself not to become discouraged when he falls short of his principles ([Meditations V.9](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.5.five.html)).

**Plain words** Try checking a professed value against something another person could observe this week. If you find a gap, choose a repair you can make rather than a better explanation of your intentions.

*For example:* Suppose you tell your child that honesty matters, then notice a shop has undercharged you. Going back to correct the bill gives your child something more concrete than another talk about honesty.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Authenticity|Authenticity research]]**: Kernis and Goldman's chapter examines associations between authentic functioning and psychological and interpersonal functioning, including well-being; its abstract also acknowledges possible costs of authenticity ([2006 abstract](https://oamonitor.ireland.openaire.eu/national/search/publication?pid=10.1016/s0065-2601(06)38006-9)).
- **[[Congruence|Congruence in therapy and life]]**: Rogers proposes that a therapist be congruent or integrated within the relationship; that therapeutic condition is a comparison with, rather than a restatement of, Seneca's ethical demand ([1957 article, abstract of the 2007 reprint](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22122245/)).

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Congruence|Congruence]]**: Rogers places integration within a particular therapeutic relationship, while Seneca asks for consistency across the conduct of a life ([Rogers, condition 3](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22122245/); [Seneca, Letter 20.2–3](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_20)).
- **[[Parrhesia (frank speech)|Parrhesia (frank speech)]]**: Foucault's account adds a different demand, freely speaking a truth that exposes the speaker to risk; matching words and deeds alone does not exhaust it ([Discourse and Truth, first lecture](https://foucault.info/parrhesia/foucault.DT1.wordParrhesia.en/)).
