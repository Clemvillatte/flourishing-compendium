> **Principle 36** — Practice veneration; refuse contempt

Spinoza files both affects under wonder and comparison. On veneration, from the note to III, P52:

> "if, however, the object of wonder be a man's prudence, industry, or anything of that sort, inasmuch as the said man, is thereby regarded as far surpassing ourselves, wonder is called Veneration" (Ethics III, P52 note, [Elwes](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800))

The same note says of wonder generally that the mind "is determined to the contemplation of that object only": attention locks on the thing wondered at, and when the thing is a person's excellence, the attention is a learning channel. The downward twin comes from the note to III, P26:

> "the pleasure which arises from thinking too little of a man is called disdain." (Ethics III, P26 note, [Elwes](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/3800))

Elwes' names are veneration and disdain; contempt appears in the same region, with derision springing "from contempt of a thing which we hate or fear, and scorn from contempt of folly, as veneration from wonder at prudence" (III, P52 note). Disdain is a pleasure, which is what makes it sticky: the cheap joy of the discount. The compendium's use of the pair: keep the attention channel of veneration open toward people who genuinely excel, and treat the pleasure of disdain as a signal to check the discount. Both affects are to be governed by reason rather than obeyed.

**Plain words**
Veneration happens when someone's competence stops you: you stare, you ask how. That stare is attention pointed at a source of skill, and it can be put to use. Disdain runs the other way: it feels good to write someone off, and the pleasure is exactly why it is unreliable. When you feel either, check its object. Is the person really that good, or really that bad? The feeling gives you a pointer; the checking is the work.

*For example:* A team has two experts, one the members venerate, one they quietly despise. The veneration points at a real skill worth copying. The despising may be pointing at nothing but the pleasure of the discount: ask what each of them specifically does well or badly.
