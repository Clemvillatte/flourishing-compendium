> **Principle 61** — Treat economic inequality as a health determinant

Income inequality asks how resources are distributed, not only how much a person earns. Whether that distribution affects health beyond individual circumstances remains contested: Pickett and Wilkinson's 2015 review argued for a causal relationship across a broad set of health and social outcomes ([abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25577953/)).

A 2024 review examined the narrower outcomes of self-rated health and all-cause mortality, adjusting for individual socioeconomic position. It found small associations, but concluded that:

> the current evidence base is too methodologically limited to support a causal relationship.

([Shimonovich et al., abstract, “Conclusions”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10938942/).)

The review rated certainty very low for self-rated health and low for mortality, and identified few natural experiments or national-level multilevel studies. That leaves an unresolved causal question, not proof that inequality is harmless or that all health outcomes respond alike ([Shimonovich et al., abstract and conclusion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10938942/)).

### Plain words

A person's own income and the gap between incomes are different measures. Ask whether a study concerns individual resources or an additional effect of their distribution; the 2024 review cannot settle the causal question about that second effect ([Shimonovich et al., “Policy Points”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10938942/)).

### For example

Optional policy-reading exercise (Cognition; Overt behaviour; sociocultural level): compare two explanations of a reported health gap. Record whether each measures poverty, income distribution or both, and whether it reports an association or tests a policy change. Keep that distinction in your next comparison; revise your judgment when stronger evidence arrives. Advocacy needs an ethical case as well as an accurately stated empirical one.

## Related concepts

- [[Social determinants of health]]: the broader question of how living conditions shape health, distinct from the specific contextual-inequality hypothesis.
- [[Social determinants of well-being]]: a comparison for subjective life evaluations, not an interchangeable health outcome.
- [[Relative deprivation]]: a proposed comparison-based pathway to examine, not proof that income gaps cause mortality.

## Psychology parallels

- **Contextual inequality**: compare the 2015 review's broad causal argument with the 2024 assessment's uncertainty for two health endpoints. They differ in scope as well as conclusion ([Pickett and Wilkinson](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25577953/); [Shimonovich et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10938942/)).

## Philosophy parallels

- **Rawls**: justice as fairness asks how institutions distribute benefits and burdens, with inequalities answerable to the least advantaged. That normative standard is not an empirical claim about a health effect ([Wenar, sections 4.1-4.3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rawls/)).
- **Sen's capability approach**: income is a means, not a complete measure of what someone has a real opportunity to do or be. This helps distinguish distributional measures from the lives they are meant to support ([Robeyns and Byskov, sections 2.2-2.4](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/)).
- **Compendium application**: an EEMM-guided sociocultural formulation can ask about resource distribution without assuming that an area-level association predicts an individual's health.

## Practice

- When discussing policy, state the justice-based reasons for a proposal separately from its expected health effects. Specify what outcome would count as evidence for or against those expectations; do not turn the uncertain contextual-health association into a promise ([Shimonovich et al., “Policy Points”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10938942/)).
