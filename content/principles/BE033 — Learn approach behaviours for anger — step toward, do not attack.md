> **Principle 33** — Learn approach behaviours for anger: step toward, do not attack

Anger's action urge is attack. The DBT opposite-action skill names the alternative in its summary table: for anger, the opposite action is "Gently avoid/be a little nice" ([Linehan, DBT Skills Training Manual, 2nd ed., Emotion Regulation Handout 10](https://www.guilford.com/add/linehan7_old/lin-p-handouts.pdf?t=1)). The companion handout spells the suggestions out:

> "Do the opposite of your angry action urges. For example: 1. Gently avoid the person you are angry with (rather than attacking). 2. Take a time out, and breathe in and out deeply and slowly. 3. Be kind (rather than mean or insulting)." ([Linehan, Emotion Regulation Handout 11, "Figuring Out Opposite Actions"](https://www.guilford.com/add/linehan7_old/lin-p-handouts.pdf?t=1))

The same handout lists all-the-way additions: "Imagine understanding and empathy for the other person", stepping "into the other person's shoes", and posture and breathing changes such as unclenched hands, a half-smile and paced breathing. The procedure checks the facts first: opposite action is for anger that does not fit the facts, or when acting on the emotion would not be effective, and the instruction is to "Act opposite all the way to your action urges" and to "Repeat acting opposite to your action urges until your emotion changes". The title's "step toward" should not be read as an instruction to move physically closer. Linehan's anger instructions include gentle avoidance and taking time out, alongside kindness and considering the other person's perspective.

**Plain words** First consider whether acting on anger would help in this situation. If it would be ineffective, choose an alternative to attacking. The handout offers gentle avoidance, a time-out, kindness and considering the other person's perspective. Choose an appropriate response without assuming that you must return immediately to the conversation.

*For example* Imagined: after a partner forgets a commitment, someone takes a slow-breathing time-out instead of opening with the list of past failures, then returns, asks what happened, and tries out a genuinely good reason for it before responding.
