> **Principle 15** — Learn to filter: not all information deserves attention

Lavie's review of the lab evidence starts from a plain finding: "simply instructing people to ignore goal-irrelevant stimuli is not sufficient for preventing their processing" ([Lavie, 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15668100/)). Whether a distractor gets processed depends on the load of the task at hand. High perceptual load can eliminate distractor processing, while high load on "frontal" cognitive control processes increases it.

Forster and Lavie found that distractors entirely unrelated to the task can interfere as much as ones that compete for the response, and that high perceptual load in the main task removes the interference.

> an entirely irrelevant distractor can interfere with task performance to the same extent as a response-competing distractor
>
> Forster & Lavie, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Applied (2008)

These are laboratory tasks with brief displays. Carrying them over to newsletters and notifications is the compendium's extrapolation, though the direction is consistent: filtering that depends on willpower in the moment is unreliable, so it helps to remove sources before they arrive.

(Lavie, 1995, 2005; Forster & Lavie, 2008.)

**Plain words** You do not control what your mind takes in as much as you would like. Telling yourself to ignore something often fails, even when it is plainly irrelevant. What works better is changing the situation: take away the thing, or make the main task absorbing enough that the extras barely register.

For example (imagined): you spend an afternoon unsubscribing from newsletters that you never act on. Nothing in your inbox now competes for a decision about whether to open it.

## Practice

- Before engaging with information (news, social media, conversation), ask: "does this deserve my attention?" Filter aggressively. Attention is a scarce resource.
