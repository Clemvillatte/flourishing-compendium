> **Principle 31** — Cultivate attentional endurance: build capacity gradually

The endurance problem and its training route, in the deliberate-practice review's own words:

> [E]vidence [shows] that the duration of effective daily practice that can be sustained for long periods is limited... [we] finally consider evidence that through training the daily duration of deliberate practice can be slowly increased over extended periods of time.
>
> (Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer, "The Role of Deliberate Practice in the Acquisition of Expert Performance", Psychological Review, 1993)

The paper's account of why duration is limited is attentional: "according to teachers and training instructions, it is necessary to maintain full attention during the entire period of deliberate practice" ([Ericsson et al. 1993](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/writing/1993-ericsson.pdf)). The starting point is small: "When individuals, especially children, start practicing in a given domain, the amount of practice is an hour or less per day" ([Ericsson et al. 1993](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/writing/1993-ericsson.pdf)). And there is a ceiling: "These studies show essentially no benefit from durations exceeding 4 hr per day and reduced benefits from practice exceeding 2 hr" ([Ericsson et al. 1993](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/writing/1993-ericsson.pdf)).

The accumulation evidence comes from the Berlin music academy: "for all four groups the reported amount of practice increases monotonically from the start of practice until the age of 20", and by eighteen "the best young violinists had accumulated an average of 7,410 hr of practice... different from 5,301 hr, the average number of hours accumulated by the good violinists" ([Ericsson et al. 1993](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/writing/1993-ericsson.pdf)). The paper adds a recovery detail: "the two best groups, who practice more, also sleep reliably longer" ([Ericsson et al. 1993](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/writing/1993-ericsson.pdf)).

(Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer, Psychological Review 100(3), 1993.)

**Plain words**
Even elite performers sustain full-attention practice for a limited number of hours a day; the daily amount starts at an hour or less and grows slowly. What separated skill levels in the study's data was not heroic sessions but years of accumulated practice, plus more sleep for those who practiced more.

*For example:*
Imagined: A writer starts with 45 focused minutes each morning and keeps a note of the session's interruptions. After a month, the writer reviews the notes before deciding whether to lengthen the session or add a second one.

## Practice

- Start with a focused session you can complete without straining. Keep notes on interruptions and session length, and review them before lengthening the session or adding a second one.
