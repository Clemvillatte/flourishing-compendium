> **Principle 175** — To receive, first give

Chapter 36 of the Daodejing lists reversals: when one is about to take an inspiration, he is sure to make a previous expiration; when he is going to weaken another, he will first strengthen him; when he is going to overthrow another, he will first have raised him up; when he is going to despoil another, he will first have made gifts to him. This, the chapter says, is called hiding the light of his procedure. Two maxims follow: the soft overcomes the hard, and the weak the strong; and fishes should not be taken from the deep, nor instruments for the profit of a state shown to the people.

(DDJ 36.)

> "When one is about to take an inspiration, he is sure to make a (previous) expiration; when he is going to weaken another, he will first strengthen him; when he is going to overthrow another, he will first have raised him up; when he is going to despoil another, he will first have made gifts to him:--this is called 'Hiding the light (of his procedure).'" (DDJ 36, [Legge](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/216/216-h/216-h.htm))

The chapter's gifts precede takings, in the service of strategy. The compendium's reading takes the sequence without the strategy: in exchange, the giving comes first, and the principle's reversal is that what is offered is what returns.

**Plain words**
The chapter's pattern is reversal: the giving comes before the taking, the strengthening before the weakening. The compendium's exercise: pick something you want to receive — help, goodwill, attention — and offer it first somewhere, then watch what follows rather than assuming the return.

*For example:* The compendium's hypothetical: a mentor gives away their best advice freely — the exercise is to notice what, if anything, comes back.
