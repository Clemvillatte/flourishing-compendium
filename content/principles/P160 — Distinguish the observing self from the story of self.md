> **Principle 160** — Distinguish the observing self from the story of self

In Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, the aim is stated plainly in an [ACT progress review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3635495/): to "undermine an attachment to a conceptualized self (our fused, evaluative stories about who we are) and to promote contact with a sense of self based on the 'I/here/nowness' of conscious experience."

### Two senses of "I"

The same review explains: "'I' does not refer to the content of the answers but to the perspective from which observations are made". It is usually called self-as-context; other terms are "noticing self," "observer perspective" and "transcendent sense of self". Self-as-content is the opposite. [One ACT training article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8320414/) gives the example "I'm a mother; that's all I know how to be," a person defining themselves by one piece of content.

### The sky and the weather

A metaphor attributed to Harris (2009, p. 175) in a [scale-validation paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12292914/): "Your observing self is like the sky. Thoughts and feelings are like the weather. The weather changes continually, but no matter how bad it gets, it can't harm the sky in any way."

### What it does and does not claim

Hayes and colleagues' progress review of ACT (Hayes, Levin, Plumb-Vilardaga, Villatte and Pistorello) says self-as-context interventions "help clients see inner experiences as distinct from consciousness as such, and thus not necessarily a threat", which "undermines excessive rule control and increases psychological flexibility." That is a clinical claim about perspective. It is not a proof that there is a separate observer inside you, and the principle's "not a metaphysical claim" fits that. The compendium's "rest identity in the observing" goes further than these sources.

(Hayes, Strosahl and Wilson, Acceptance and Commitment Therapy; Mead's "I" and "Me.")

**Plain words** You can describe yourself in sentences: "I'm the one who failed that", "I'm the anxious one". ACT calls that self-as-content. You can also notice that you are the one doing the describing. [[Self-as-context|That second position is self-as-context]], and the point is to be able to step into it when a story about yourself is gripping too hard.

*For example:* Instead of "I'm a no good drunk", a health-behaviour paper suggests the wording "I am having the thought that I am a no good drunk" ([ACT for health behaviour change](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5769281/)). The thought stays; the person's relation to it changes.
