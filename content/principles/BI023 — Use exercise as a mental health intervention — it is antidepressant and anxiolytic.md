> **Principle 23** — Use exercise as a mental health intervention: it is antidepressant and anxiolytic

The sources address different questions: whether physical activity predicts later depression, and whether activity interventions change symptoms in non-clinical adults ([Schuch et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29690792/); [Rebar et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25739893/)). For the first, a pooled analysis of 49 prospective studies following 266,939 people for 1,837,794 person-years found that people with high physical activity had lower odds of developing depression than people with low activity, a gap observed across age groups and regions ([Schuch et al., 2018, Am J Psychiatry](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29690792/)). For the second, a meta-meta-analysis of randomised trials measured the change in non-clinical adults ([Rebar et al., 2015, Health Psychol Rev](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25739893/)):

> "Physical activity reduced depression by a medium effect [standardised mean difference (SMD) = -0.50; 95% CI: -0.93 to -0.06] and anxiety by a small effect (SMD = -0.38; 95% CI: -0.66 to -0.11)." (Abstract, Results; Rebar et al., 2015, Health Psychol Rev 9(3): 366-78, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25739893/))

> "Available evidence supports the notion that physical activity can confer protection against the emergence of depression regardless of age and geographical region." (Abstract, Conclusions; Schuch et al., 2018, Am J Psychiatry 175(7): 631-48, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29690792/))

**Plain words**
Two findings sit behind this principle, and both are averages. More active people were less likely to develop depression later; that is what following 266,939 people for years found, and an association is all it establishes ([Schuch et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29690792/)). Among non-clinical adults, activity made depression and anxiety symptoms smaller, by a small-to-medium margin ([Rebar et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25739893/)). Neither finding promises a particular person a particular result; together they support regular movement as one input among others.

**For example**
(Imagined) Someone with a flat stretch of weeks puts three brisk walks in the calendar, shoes by the door. Scheduling the walks is the part they control; the studies behind this page describe groups and averages, not what the walks will do for them.

## Sources

- [Schuch et al., 2018, Am J Psychiatry 175(7): 631-48, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29690792/) — abstract: 49 prospective studies, adjusted odds ratio 0.83 for incident depression
- [Rebar et al., 2015, Health Psychol Rev 9(3): 366-78, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25739893/) — abstract: meta-meta-analysis, medium effect on depression, small effect on anxiety

## Psychology parallels

- **Exercise and depression**, higher physical activity is associated with lower odds of developing depression (Schuch et al., 2018)
- **Exercise and anxiety**, physical activity reduced anxiety symptoms by a small effect in non-clinical adults (Rebar et al., 2015)
- **Note: trial effects in non-clinical adults were small-to-medium; these sources establish no dose** (Rebar et al., 2015)

## Practice

- Use exercise as a mental health input: schedule regular activity (the compendium's suggestion is moderate movement most days; the sources here establish an association and small-to-medium trial effects, not a dose). Do not wait for motivation; schedule it.
