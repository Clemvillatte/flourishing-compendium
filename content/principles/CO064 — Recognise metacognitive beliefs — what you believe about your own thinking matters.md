> **Principle 64** — Recognise metacognitive beliefs: what you believe about your own thinking matters

People hold beliefs about their own cognition ("worrying helps me prepare," "I cannot control my thoughts," "I must think about this until it is resolved"). These metacognitive beliefs drive cognitive processes like worry and rumination. Challenging them is a core therapeutic mechanism.

**Plain words** You hold beliefs about your own thinking — "worrying helps me prepare," "I can't control my thoughts," "I must resolve this before I sleep." These metacognitive beliefs drive the patterns themselves. Audit them: is worry actually helping? Test it like a hypothesis.

*For example:* The person who believes their worry is preparation — and the week they log it: 14 hours of worry, zero plans produced. The belief said "preparing"; the data said "rehearsing." Metacognitive therapy starts exactly there — the belief about the habit is the habit's engine.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Metacognitive model of anxiety and depression (Wells)|Metacognitive model of anxiety and depression (Wells)]]**, positive and negative metacognitive beliefs maintain worry and rumination (Wells, 2009)
- **Metacognitive therapy is effective for GAD and depression**, challenging metacognitive beliefs reduces symptoms (Wells et al., 2010; Normann et al., 2014)

## Practice

- Notice your beliefs about your own thinking. "I must worry to be prepared" — is this true? "I cannot stop thinking about this" — is this true? Challenge the metacognitive belief, not just the content of the thought.
