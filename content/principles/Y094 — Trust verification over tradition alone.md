> **Principle 94** — Trust verification over tradition alone

The Kena Upanishad (also called the Talavakara) opens with a pupil's questions, and the teacher's answers do not hand over a doctrine to accept:

> The Pupil asks: 'At whose wish does the mind sent forth proceed on its errand? At whose command does the first breath go forth? At whose wish do we utter this speech? What god directs the eye, or the ear?' The Teacher replies: 'It is the ear of the ear, the mind of the mind, the speech of speech, the breath of breath, and the eye of the eye.'

(Kena (Talavakara) Upanishad 1.1-1.2, trans. F. Max Muller, *The Upanishads*, Sacred Books of the East 1, 1879, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/upanishads01mluoft).)

The questions point to something behind the senses. The teacher then turns down a first answer: "that alone know as Brahman, not that which people here adore." In the second section he unsettles the pupil's confidence: "If thou thinkest I know it well, then thou knowest surely but little." The pupil replies, "I do not think I know it well, nor do I know that I do not know it." So the text marks the limit of ordinary knowing as much as it invites inquiry.

The Gita asks the student to approach a teacher: "Learn the Truth by approaching a spiritual master. Inquire from him with reverence and render service unto him" (BG 4.34, trans. Swami Mukundananda, [holy-bhagavad-gita.org](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/4/verse/34/)). That is inquiry within a relationship of reverence and service.

Neither text sets up a test of experience against authority. Reading the Kena as a demand for verification, and keeping a notebook with a column for received truth and one for personal experience, are this page's own modern adaptation.

**Plain words**

The Kena begins with questions, and its teacher puts its pupil's certainty under strain. The Gita asks you to inquire, with respect for the teacher. Checking what you have been told against your own experience is a good habit, and it is yours, not the texts'.

**For example**

A student writes one line in a notebook from a teaching he admires. Beside it he writes what the week showed him. Some weeks the two lines agree, and some weeks he leaves the second column blank.
