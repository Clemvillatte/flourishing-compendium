> **Principle 227** — Handle insults with equanimity

Epictetus treats the judgment that you have been insulted as the thing to examine, not the words that arrived:

> "Remember, that not he who gives ill language or a blow insults, but the principle which represents these things as insulting. When, therefore, anyone provokes you, be assured that it is your own opinion which provokes you. Try, therefore, in the first place, not to be hurried away with the appearance. For if you once gain time and respite, you will more easily command yourself." (Enchiridion 20, [Carter translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html))

On his account the provocation is an appearance, and the wound is supplied by the opinion you form about it. The remedy he offers is unheroic: gain time before answering, because time and respite make self-command easier. A pause before replying, or silence, is what that bought time looks like in practice.

(Disc. I.18, I.28, III.7, III.22; Ench. 1, 42; Med. II.1, II.13, IV.3, VI.6, VI.16, VII.22, IX.4, IX.11, XI.18; Seneca, De Ira III.36, III.38, III.40.)

**Plain words** Give yourself time before answering a rude remark. Ask what you have concluded about it, and what you want your reply to do.

*For example:* A stranger yells at you in traffic. You let it go by and notice it only as weather.
