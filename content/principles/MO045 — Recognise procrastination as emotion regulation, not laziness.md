> **Principle 45** — Recognise procrastination as emotion regulation, not laziness

Procrastination is often understood as laziness or a time-management failure. Sirois and Pychyl's review argues for a different account: that procrastination is, in significant part, a way of regulating emotion.

> In this paper, we argue that as a form of self-regulation failure, procrastination has a great deal to do with short-term mood repair and emotion regulation. (Sirois & Pychyl, "Procrastination and the Priority of Short-Term Mood Regulation: Consequences for Future Self," Social and Personality Psychology Compass 7(2), 2013, Abstract; author manuscript p. 3, [White Rose eprints PDF](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/91793/1/Compass%20Paper%20revision%20FINAL.pdf); published article pp. 115-127)

The review's conclusion states the claim more fully:

> In this paper, we have argued that procrastination may be best understood as a form of self-regulation failure that involves the primacy of short-term mood repair and emotion regulation over the longer-term pursuit of intended actions. (Sirois & Pychyl, 2013, Conclusions and Future Directions, author manuscript p. 19, same PDF)

The account is not offered as the sole explanation. The same review notes that susceptibility to goal-derailing temptations "may involve more than just trying to escape the unpleasant feelings associated with a task" (author manuscript p. 14). What the mood-repair framing changes is the intervention: if the avoidance is managing a feeling, then only addressing the schedule leaves the feeling unaddressed.

**Plain words** One account of procrastination is that it is mood management. The task produces a feeling you would rather not have, so you avoid the task, and the avoidance relieves the feeling for a while. This is one account among others, and the review that advances it does not claim it is the whole story. What it does suggest is that a schedule alone will not address the part that is a feeling.

*For example:* Imagine someone who says "I'll do the tax return tomorrow." One reading is that this is a scheduling decision. The mood-repair account suggests a different reading: that the dread the return produces is what is being avoided, and the deferral is how.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Temporal motivation theory|Temporal motivation theory (Steel)]]**, procrastination is driven by preference for immediate comfort over delayed benefit (Steel, 2007; Steel & König, 2006)

## Practice

- When procrastinating, one question worth asking is: "what feeling am I avoiding?" The mood-repair account suggests the feeling is part of what is being managed. The review associates procrastination with low self-compassion; approaches that address the feeling ([[Self-compassion|self-compassion]], breaking the task down, starting tiny) are one avenue among others.
