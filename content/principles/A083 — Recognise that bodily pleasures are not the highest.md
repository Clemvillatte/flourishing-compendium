> **Principle 83** — Recognise that bodily pleasures are not the highest

The view that pleasure is replenishment "seems to be based on the pains and pleasures connected with nutrition": people are pleased by food because they were first in pain from hunger (NE X.3). Aristotle answers that this cannot cover the field: "the pleasures of learning and, among the sensuous pleasures, those of smell, and also many sounds and sights, and memories and hopes, do not presuppose pain" (X.3). And he offers a trade nobody would take: "no one would choose to live with the intellect of a child throughout his life, however much he were to be pleased at the things that children are pleased at" (X.3).

The ranking follows from what pleasures complete. Activities of thought differ in kind from those of the senses, "so, therefore, do the pleasures that complete them" (X.5). His friend differs from the flatterer by consorting with us "with a view to the good" rather than "with a view to our pleasure" (X.3). And "there are many things we should be keen about even if they brought no pleasure, e.g. seeing, remembering, knowing, possessing the virtues" (X.3).

(NE X.3, X.5, W. D. Ross translation.)

**Plain words** Bodily pleasures are the easiest to notice, so they get mistaken for the whole category. Some are just the relief of a need, and only as good as the need was urgent. Others belong to activities you would want anyway, like learning something or seeing a friend do well. Aristotle's test is to subtract the pleasure and see what is left standing.

*For example:* A holiday planned entirely around meals can be lovely. A life planned that way gets thin.
