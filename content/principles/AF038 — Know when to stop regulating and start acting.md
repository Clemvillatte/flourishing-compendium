> **Principle 38** — Know when to stop regulating and start acting

Troy, Shallcross and Mauss tested the fit between strategy and situation in 170 participants measured for cognitive-reappraisal ability, the severity of recent life stressors, stressor controllability and depression: "For participants with uncontrollable stress, higher cognitive-reappraisal ability was associated with lower levels of depression. In contrast, for participants with controllable stress, higher cognitive-reappraisal ability was associated with greater levels of depression" ([Troy et al., 2013, Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797613496434)). Their model sentence: "reappraisal may be adaptive when stressors are uncontrollable (when the person can regulate only the self) but maladaptive when stressors can be controlled (when the person can change the situation)" ([Troy et al., 2013](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0956797613496434)). Ford and Troy's review gathers the cost side: "In controllable situations, using reappraisal to reduce negative emotion can work against effectively managing those situations," and "when reappraisal is used to reduce emotions that would otherwise be useful for solving a problem, successful reappraisal may reduce individuals' immediate motivation to engage in effective problem solving and decision making" ([Ford and Troy, 2019, Current Directions in Psychological Science](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/56f5c928356fb063f3e181fb/t/5ce2f0e5dc6ab10001c157c1/1558376677807/Ford&Troy_2019_CDPS_corrected.pdf)).

> "reappraisal may be adaptive when stressors are uncontrollable (when the person can regulate only the self) but maladaptive when stressors can be controlled (when the person can change the situation)." (Troy, Shallcross and Mauss, 2013, Psychological Science)

**Plain words**
The studies draw one line: when a stressor cannot be changed, working on your response to it is the available move; when it can be changed, damping the feeling can also damp the motive to act. The Enchiridion draws the line from the other side: "There are things which are within our power, and there are things which are beyond our power" ([Epictetus, Enchiridion ch. 1, Higginson translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45109/45109-h/45109-h.htm)); its counsel is to quit or postpone the one and act on the other.

**For example**
Hypothetical illustration: the person who spends two years perfecting coping strategies for an unbearable job, an ever-better swimmer in a draining pool, and the day they redirect the skill to the exit.

(Troy et al., 2013; Ford and Troy, 2019; Epictetus, Enchiridion chs 1-2.)

## Psychology parallels

- **Problem-solving outperforms reappraisal for controllable stressors**, for situations that can be changed, addressing the situation is more effective than changing the interpretation (Troy et al., 2013)
- **Misapplied regulation is associated with avoidance and worse outcomes**, using regulation to tolerate the intolerable is maladaptive (Ford & Troy, 2019)

## Philosophy parallels

- **Wisdom as knowing what is up to you** (Stoics, Epictetus, Enchiridion)
- **[[Phronesis|Phronesis as practical judgement]]** (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI)
- **[[Right action|Right action over acceptance of harm]]** (Buddhism, Right Action)

## Practice

- Ask: "is this a feeling problem or a situation problem?" If the situation is harmful, change the situation. Regulating your feelings about it without changing it is avoidance dressed as stoicism.
