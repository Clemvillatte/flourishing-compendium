> **Principle 32** — Recognise the endowment effect: you value what you own more

> Contrary to theoretical expectations, measures of willingness to accept greatly exceed measures of willingness to pay. This paper reports several experiments that demonstrate that this "endowment effect" persists even in market settings with opportunities to learn.

(Kahneman, Knetsch and Thaler, "Experimental Tests of the Endowment Effect and the Coase Theorem," Journal of Political Economy 98(6), 1990, abstract; [PDF copy hosted by MIT](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Behavioral_Decision_Theory/Kahneman_et_al_1990_Experimental_tests.pdf).)

In one of the mug experiments, "the median selling price of $5.75 was over twice the median buying price of $2.25." Coffee mugs were given at random to half the participants. When the researchers ran the same market with tokens that had an assigned cash value, the expected volume of trade appeared, which the authors take to suggest that transaction costs cannot explain the undertrading of mugs.

Culture seems to matter. Maddux and colleagues tested buyers and sellers of a coffee mug and of a box of chocolates and found a smaller endowment effect for East Asians than for Westerners. They suggest the effect "may be influenced by the degree to which independence and self-enhancement (vs. interdependence and self-criticism) are culturally valued or normative" (Maddux et al., Psychological Science 21(12), 2010, abstract; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21097722/)).

The entry's description of the effect as "weaker in cultures with less individual ownership norms" is a loose paraphrase of that finding. Thaler (1980), which the entry also cites, was not opened. The "would I buy this today?" test is a heuristic from the compendium and has not been tested in the studies above.

**Plain words** People asked to sell something they hold tend to ask for much more than people asked to buy the same thing are willing to pay. In the mug experiments the gap was about double. Owning something seems to add value in the owner's mind, and the size of that effect varies by culture. Asking "would I choose this today if I did not own it?" is a way to check how much of the price is ownership.

*For example (imagined):* A seller lists a house well above comparable sales because it is theirs, and the house sits unsold for months.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Endowment effect|Endowment effect]]**, ownership increases valuation (Kahneman et al., 1990; Thaler, 1980)
- **The effect is robust but moderated by culture and context**, it is weaker in cultures with less individual ownership norms (Maddux et al., 2010)

## Practice

- When deciding whether to keep or release something, ask: "if I did not already own this, would I choose to acquire it today?" If no, ownership is driving the valuation.
