> **Principle 126** — Sequence by need, not prestige

Patanjali lists the practice in a fixed order, and the most advanced items come last:

> Abstentions and observances and postures and regulations-of-the-breath and withdrawal-of-the-senses and fixed attention and contemplation and concentration are the eight aids.

(Yoga Sutras 2.29, trans. James Haughton Woods, *The Yoga-System of Patanjali*, Harvard Oriental Series 17, 1914, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/yogasystemofpata00wooduoft).)

The commentary adds: "The following up of these must be performed in succession." Sutra 2.28 explains what the climb is for: "After the aids to yoga have been followed up, when the impurity has dwindled, there is an enlightenment of perception reaching up to the discriminative discernment." So concentration comes at the top of a stair whose first steps are the abstentions and observances. The stair does not start at the top.

The Gita applies the same idea to the body: those who "eat too much or too little, sleep too much or too little, cannot attain success in Yog" (BG 6.16, trans. Swami Mukundananda, [holy-bhagavad-gita.org](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/6/verse/16/)).

Neither text talks about ranking your own needs each season. That exercise is this page's own, built on the texts' ordering.

**Plain words**

It is tempting to practise the part that feels impressive and skip the part that is dull but necessary. Both texts treat the dull parts as the base: conduct comes before advanced concentration, and the Gita says too little or too much sleep and food rules out success. Once a season, write down what you actually need and what you actually practise. If the two lists do not match, give the needed thing your time.

**For example**

A meditator with an elaborate technique and a chronic sleep debt makes the honest list and finds sleep at the top. The technique waits until the nights are fixed, since it was being built over a gap.
