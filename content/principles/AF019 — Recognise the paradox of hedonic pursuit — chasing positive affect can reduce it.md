> **Principle 19** — Recognise the paradox of hedonic pursuit: chasing positive affect can reduce it

Deliberately trying to feel happy is associated with lower happiness, particularly in cultures that value happiness highly. The pursuit of positive affect as a goal in itself backfires.

**Plain words** Deliberately trying to feel happy is associated with lower happiness — especially in cultures that value happiness highly. The pursuit of positive affect as a goal in itself backfires. Aim at the activities, not the feeling; the feeling arrives as a by-product.

*For example:* The person who checks hourly whether they're happy yet — the monitoring IS the interference — versus the one absorbed in the gardening, the friend, the work. The second one is having the good afternoon; the first one is auditing it.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Valuing happiness paradox|Valuing happiness paradox]]**, people who place a high value on happiness report lower happiness and greater depressive symptoms, particularly in Western cultural contexts (Mauss et al., 2011)
- **Indirect strategies outperform direct strategies**, pursuing meaning and connection increases positive affect more than pursuing positive affect directly (Ford et al., 2015)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Wu wei|Wu wei: non-forcing]]** (Daoism, Daodejing 48)
- **[[Eudaimonia|Eudaimonia as activity, not feeling]]** (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics I)
- **[[Hedonic adaptation|The hedonic treadmill]]** (Mill, Utilitarianism)

## Practice

- Replace "I want to feel happy today" with "I want to do things that matter today." Let the feeling follow from the activity, not the other way around.
