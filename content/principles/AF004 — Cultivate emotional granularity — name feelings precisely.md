> **Principle 4** — Cultivate emotional granularity: name feelings precisely

The research behind this principle has two legs, and they are different studies. The definition: negative emotion differentiation is "adeptness at using discrete negative emotion categories to capture one's felt experience" (Kashdan et al., 2014). That study examined how differentiation and self-esteem relate to neural responses to rejection: low self-esteem with low differentiation was associated with stronger distress-related activation, while individuals with greater differentiation did not show stronger activation in these regions, regardless of their level of self-esteem. The other leg is labeling, not differentiation: in Lieberman and colleagues' imaging study, naming an emotional image, compared with other ways of encoding it, diminished the amygdala and limbic response; the authors read this as naming diminishing reactivity along a prefrontal pathway (Lieberman et al., 2007). My exercise: when a feeling arrives, name it with a specific word before acting on it.

(Kashdan et al., 2014, PLoS One; Lieberman et al., 2007, Psychological Science.)

> affect labeling, relative to other forms of encoding, diminished the response of the amygdala and other limbic regions to negative emotional images
>
> Lieberman et al., 2007, Psychological Science, [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576282/).

**Plain words**
A feeling arrives as weather, a grey mass with no edges. Putting a precise word on it, resentful rather than bad, does something the vague word cannot: the brain's alarm centre quietens as the name lands. The studies behind this use small samples and pictures on a screen, so treat the size of the effect with care, but the direction is the same in both.

**For example**
When a vague bad feeling arrives, try a more specific word for it, resentful rather than bad, before acting.

## Practice

- Build a fine-grained emotion vocabulary. When you notice "I feel bad," try to specify: irritated, disappointed, lonely, ashamed, tired, anxious, envious, bored.
