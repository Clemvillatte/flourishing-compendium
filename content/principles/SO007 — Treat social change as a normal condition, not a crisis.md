> **Principle 7** — Treat social change as a normal condition, not a crisis

The research I could open supports one slice of this principle: how people adapt when they move between cultures. Sam and Berry report that "there are large variations in how people acculturate and in how well they adapt", and that "often those who integrate (defined as being engaged in both their heritage culture and in the larger society) are better adapted" than those who orient to one culture or to neither ([Sam and Berry 2010, Perspectives on Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/1745691610373075)). Berry's 1997 paper adds that the long-term outcomes "are highly variable, depending on social and personal variables that reside in the society of origin, the society of settlement" and on what happens during the process ([Berry 1997, Applied Psychology abstract](https://scispace.com/papers/immigration-acculturation-and-adaptation-2fex8p9g5r)).

Adaptation also depends on the society, not only the person. Integration "can only be chosen and successfully pursued by immigrants when the receiving society is open and inclusive in its orientation toward cultural diversity" ([A Psychology of Immigration (online copy)](https://utopia.duth.gr/xsakonid/index_htm_files/1_5_2015_Sapountzis_Berry_paper.pdf)).

### Limits

These studies are about acculturation. They do not show that all rapid change, including technological or economic change, is best met by the same stance. I did not open Kirmayer and Minas, Inglehart and Welzel, or Henrich et al.

**Plain words**
Change is going to keep arriving, so the useful question is how to build the skills and the institutions that help people adapt. Adapting well is easier in places that make room for it.

**For example**
List one change you have been waiting out, such as a new tool at work or a new neighbour group. Write down one skill or contact that would help you work with it, and take one step this week.
