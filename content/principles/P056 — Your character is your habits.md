> **Principle 56** — Your character is your habits

James gives both halves of the principle. The self, "in its widest possible sense," is "the sum total of all that he CAN call his," extending to clothes, house, family, ancestors and friends, reputation and works, lands and horses, and yacht and bank-account (The Principles of Psychology, ch. 10, [text](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin10.htm)). Of habit he writes:

> "Habit is thus the enormous fly-wheel of society, its most precious conservative agent" (ch. 4, [text](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin4.htm))

His practical instruction follows:

> "For this we must make automatic and habitual, as early as possible, as many useful actions as we can, and guard against the growing into ways that are likely to be disadvantageous to us, as we should guard against the plague." (James, The Principles of Psychology, ch. 4, [text](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin4.htm))

The fly-wheel sentence describes society, and the automatic-and-habitual sentence is advice about useful actions; neither is a proof that character equals habit. The modern record narrows the mechanism: in Wood and Neal's review, habits are learned context-response associations in which "perception of contexts triggers the associated response without a mediating goal" (Wood and Neal, 2007, [abstract](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=EXT_ID:17907866&resultType=core&format=json)). The compendium's application: compare stated values with the week's repeated behavior.

### Plain words

The statement is a claim about intent; the schedule is a record of behavior. "I value fitness" is a statement, and Tuesday 7 a.m. on the bike is an event that either happened this week or did not. The comparison is between words and recent behavior, and it can be run again next week.

### For example

Take the person who says they value fitness and owns a dusty bike, next to the person who rides it Tuesdays. The exercise is to write down what you claim to value, then find it in the week's calendar or not. The exercise does not say the calendar cannot change; it says next week's calendar is the place to look for the change.
