> **Principle 33** — Learn self-distancing: the ability to step back from your own experience

The studies asked whether the language people use to refer to the self during introspection changes how they think, feel, and behave under social stress ([Kross et al., 2014](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/emotion-selfcontrol-psych/wp-content/uploads/sites/1322/2024/07/KrossJ_Pers_Soc_Psychol2014Self-talk_as_a_regulatory_mechanism_How_you_do_it_matters.pdf)). In proof-of-principle studies, "using non-first-person pronouns and one's own name (rather than first-person pronouns) during introspection enhances self-distancing" ([Kross et al., 2014](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/emotion-selfcontrol-psych/wp-content/uploads/sites/1322/2024/07/KrossJ_Pers_Soc_Psychol2014Self-talk_as_a_regulatory_mechanism_How_you_do_it_matters.pdf)). In studies of first impressions and public speaking, "the non-first-person group performed better according to objective raters" and "displayed less distress" than the first-person group in Studies 2 and 3 ([Kross et al., 2014](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/emotion-selfcontrol-psych/wp-content/uploads/sites/1322/2024/07/KrossJ_Pers_Soc_Psychol2014Self-talk_as_a_regulatory_mechanism_How_you_do_it_matters.pdf)). The paper's summary: "small shifts in the language people use to refer to the self during introspection consequentially influence their ability to regulate their thoughts, feelings, and behavior under social stress" ([Kross et al., 2014](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/emotion-selfcontrol-psych/wp-content/uploads/sites/1322/2024/07/KrossJ_Pers_Soc_Psychol2014Self-talk_as_a_regulatory_mechanism_How_you_do_it_matters.pdf)). The exercise below tries the pronoun shift.

> They demonstrated that using non-first-person pronouns and one's own name (rather than first-person pronouns) during introspection enhances self-distancing.
>
> Kross, Bruehlman-Senecal, Park, Burson, Dougherty, Shablack, Bremner, Moser and Ayduk (2014), "Self-talk as a regulatory mechanism: how you do it matters," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 106(2), 304-324, [paper PDF](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/emotion-selfcontrol-psych/wp-content/uploads/sites/1322/2024/07/KrossJ_Pers_Soc_Psychol2014Self-talk_as_a_regulatory_mechanism_How_you_do_it_matters.pdf).

> Together, these findings demonstrate that small shifts in the language people use to refer to the self during introspection consequentially influence their ability to regulate their thoughts, feelings, and behavior under social stress, even for vulnerable individuals.
>
> Kross et al. (2014), same paper, [paper PDF](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/emotion-selfcontrol-psych/wp-content/uploads/sites/1322/2024/07/KrossJ_Pers_Soc_Psychol2014Self-talk_as_a_regulatory_mechanism_How_you_do_it_matters.pdf).

(Kross et al., 2014, JPSP 106(2): 304-324.)

### Plain words

The tested change was in how participants referred to themselves during reflection ([Kross et al., 2014](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/emotion-selfcontrol-psych/wp-content/uploads/sites/1322/2024/07/KrossJ_Pers_Soc_Psychol2014Self-talk_as_a_regulatory_mechanism_How_you_do_it_matters.pdf)). For reflection, describe the same worry using "I" and then your own name; record any difference, including none.

For example: the sentence "I'm dreading the meeting" rewritten as "Sarah is dreading the meeting"; the exercise is writing both sentences and noting any difference. (An invented illustration.)
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Rumination|Self-distancing reduces rumination]] and emotional reactivity**, viewing experiences from a third-person or distanced perspective reduces negative affect and rumination (Kross & Ayduk, 2011)
- **Distanced self-talk reduces emotional reactivity**, using your own name or "you" rather than "I" reduces reactivity in social anxiety and other contexts (Kross et al., 2014)

## Practice

- When you are emotionally activated, try describing the situation in the third person ("Sarah is feeling anxious about the meeting") or from a temporal distance ("a year from now, how much will this matter?").
