> **Principle 147** — Do not seek pleasure as the highest good

Seneca's Letter 59 draws a line between pleasure and joy. The letter reports the Stoic thesis bluntly, "we Stoics hold that pleasure is a vice", while noting that the word is commonly used more loosely ([Letters to Lucilius 59, Gummere translation; opened text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_59)). His working distinction: pleasure is "inspired by an opinion concerning a spurious good" and is "likely soon to take another direction", while joy "never ceases, and never changes into its opposite":

> For "joy" is an elation of spirit,—of a spirit which trusts in the goodness and truth of its own possessions.

(Seneca, Letters to Lucilius 59, trans. Richard M. Gummere; [opened text, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_59).)

"This joy springs only from the knowledge that you possess the virtues. None but the brave, the just, the self-restrained, can rejoice" (Seneca, [Letter 59, opened text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_59)). The arrangement the letter points to: build the life around what you are, and let pleasure follow or not.

### Plain words

Pleasure is not evil on this account, but it is unreliable: it follows from things going well, and things change. Joy, in Seneca's sense, follows from what you are, and luck does not move it.

### For example

Take two people at the same dinner. One needs the evening to be excellent to call the week a success; the other came for the company. The same meal costs the first more than the second.
