> **Principle 49** — Build social support as a buffer

Cohen and Wills's review set the question up properly: is support good for you regardless of stress (a main effect), or does it protect specifically against stressors (buffering)? Their answer was that the evidence supports both, and the patterns differ by the type of measure. Buffering shows up when the support measure assesses perceived availability of interpersonal resources; main effects show up when the measure assesses integration in a large social network.

> Evidence for the buffering model is found when the social support measure assesses the perceived availability of interpersonal resources that are responsive to the needs elicited by stressful events.
> (Cohen and Wills 1985, Psychological Bulletin 98(2))

Holt-Lunstad, Smith and Layton's meta-analysis of 148 studies covering 308,849 participants reported a 50% increased likelihood of survival for participants with stronger social relationships (OR 1.50), and concluded that the influence of social relationships on mortality risk is comparable with well-established risk factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption.

> The influence of social relationships on risk for mortality is comparable with well-established risk factors for mortality.
> (Holt-Lunstad, Smith and Layton 2010, PLoS Medicine)

The compendium's exercise: identify whom you could approach for different kinds of help, and discuss those expectations with them.

**Plain words** Make a list of the kinds of help you might need: someone to listen, someone to drive you somewhere, someone who knows the medical system, someone who will tell you the truth. For each, name a person you could ask. The exercise is the naming and, where it makes sense, the telling, so the expectations exist before the crisis.

*For example:* Facing a diagnosis feels different when you know exactly who you'd call first.
