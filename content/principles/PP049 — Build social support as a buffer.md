> **Principle 49** — Build social support as a buffer

Cohen and Wills's review set the question up properly: is support good for you regardless of stress (a main effect), or does it protect specifically against stressors (buffering)? Their answer was that the evidence supports both, and the type of measure decides which you find. Buffering shows up when the support measure assesses perceived availability of interpersonal resources; main effects show up when the measure assesses integration in a large social network.

> Evidence for the buffering model is found when the social support measure assesses the perceived availability of interpersonal resources that are responsive to the needs elicited by stressful events.
> (Cohen and Wills 1985, Psychological Bulletin 98(2))

The stakes are not just comfort. Holt-Lunstad, Smith and Layton's meta-analysis of 148 studies covering 308,849 participants found a 50% increased likelihood of survival for participants with stronger social relationships, and concluded that the influence of social relationships on mortality risk is comparable with well-established risk factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption.

> The influence of social relationships on risk for mortality is comparable with well-established risk factors for mortality.
> (Holt-Lunstad, Smith and Layton 2010, PLoS Medicine)

The compendium's application: build the support before the crisis. Perceived availability is the operative thing, so the work is two-sided, being reachable and reaching, keeping the friendships in repair before you need them as infrastructure.

**Plain words** The helpful part of support is mostly knowing it is there. The problem has not shrunk; you are calmer about it because someone would come if you called. Two people can face the same diagnosis differently: one has a name ready for who comes, the other is working that out mid-crisis. Build the roster in peacetime. Call people when nothing is wrong, which is how they learn to pick up when something is.

*For example:* Facing a diagnosis feels different when you know exactly who you'd call first.
