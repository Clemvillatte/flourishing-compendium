> **Principle 458** — Practise the will to meaning

Frankl built logotherapy on a claim about what people want most:

> "According to logotherapy, the striving to find a meaning in one's life is the primary motivational force in man."

He names the rivals directly: the will to pleasure of Freudian psychoanalysis and the [[Will to power|will to power (Adler)]] of Adlerian psychology. Practising the will to meaning starts with taking that striving seriously in yourself.

### What happens when it is blocked

Frankl says the will to meaning can be frustrated, and that the substitutes then move in:

> "Sometimes the frustrated will to meaning is vicariously compensated for by a will to power, including the most primitive form of the will to power, the will to money. In other cases, the place of frustrated will to meaning is taken by the will to pleasure."

He ties this to the "existential vacuum" and to the loss of tradition: "No instinct tells him what he has to do, and no tradition tells him what he ought to do; soon he will not know what he wants to do." The drive can be neglected by a culture that leaves people guessing what to live for.

(Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning, Part Two, "Basic Concepts of Logotherapy", pp. 153, 167, 169, Beacon Press edition as scanned at the Internet Archive.)

**Plain words** Most people assume that underneath, we all want to feel good or to come out on top. Frankl argues that the first thing people need is a reason: something or someone worth living for. Pleasure and status are what they reach for when that reason has gone missing. To act on this, ask what your days are for, and notice when you are reaching for comfort or status because that question has gone unanswered.

For example: Frankl reports a poll in France in which 89% of those asked admitted that man needs "something" for the sake of which to live, and 61% said there was something or someone in their own lives for whose sake they were ready to die. He repeated it at his Vienna clinic and found the outcome practically the same (p. 154).
