> **Principle 393** — Practise the phenomenological reduction

This page relies on Dan Zahavi's account of Husserl in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, quoted directly.

### The natural attitude

The [SEP entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/) puts the starting point this way: "In ordinary life we are so absorbed in the world that we simply take its reality for granted; it is simply there waiting to be discovered and investigated." Husserl calls this the natural attitude. The alternative is to attend to "what it means for something to be given as real in the first place", to "the how of its givenness."

### What the epoché does

The [[Epoché|epoché]] works like this: "We keep it, in order to be able to investigate it, but we bracket its validity." The aim is not doubt: "The purpose of the epoché is not to doubt, neglect, abandon, or exclude reality from our research; rather its aim is to suspend or neutralize a certain dogmatic attitude towards reality."

### What is left

Husserl: "'The' world is not lost as a result of the epoché. The epoché is by no means a suspension of the being of the world and of every world-oriented judgment ..." Nor is it a turn inwards. The SEP entry says "we do not turn the gaze inwards in order to examine the happenings in a private interior sphere. Rather we look at how the world shows up to the subject."

### Scope

In Husserl the epoché is a first step towards the transcendental reduction, "a more prolonged analysis" of how subject and world are correlated. The practice here is an everyday version.

(Husserl, Ideas I §§31-33; Cartesian Meditations §§8-11; Crisis §§35-38. Quoted via SEP.)

**Plain words** You normally treat things as simply there. The epoché asks you to set that assumption aside, without denying the world, and look at how things show up and what you bring to it.

*For example:* Imagine a conversation where you feel the other person is hostile. Ask how the hostility shows up: in a tone, a word, a look. Part of it may be your own reading. This is an everyday use of the idea, not Husserl's example.
