> **Principle 18** — Label fiction as fiction

Sutra 1.9 defines the fluctuation that Woods calls predicate-relation (vikalpa): "The predicate-relation (vikalpa) is without any [corresponding perceptible] object and follows as a result of perceptions or of words" (trans. Woods, p. 26). The principle calls it imagination or construction.

### What the commentary says

"This [predicate-relation] does not amount to a source-of-valid-ideas, nor does it amount to a misconception." Although there is no corresponding object, "something is evidently said", because the idea depends on "the authority of perceptions or of words" (p. 26). Vyasa's examples are about language: when it is said that "The true nature of the Self is intelligence", the Self is itself nothing but intelligence, so one asks what is related to what. He also cites "Chaitra's cow", where the expression works through a relation, and the phrase "The arrow comes to a standstill" (pp. 26-27).

### Where the principle goes further

Woods says the idea follows from "perceptions or of words", not from words alone. The text does not say that planning and empathy are vikalpa, or tell the reader to find a sentence of word-spin in rumination and label it. The commentary's own examples are philosophical statements about the Self and ordinary grammar. The labelling practice is the principle's adaptation.

**Plain words** Patanjali names a kind of mental event that speaks of something without a matching perceived object. It is neither a valid piece of knowledge nor a plain mistake. The text treats it as a feature of how language works. Marking your own rumination sentences as constructions is a modern, practical use of that idea, not part of the sutra.

*For example:* Imagined example: you think "everyone thinks I got that wrong". You notice there is no particular person or comment behind the sentence, and you write beside it: no evidence, just words.

(Yoga Sutra 1.9 with Vyasa's Bhasya and Vacaspati Misra's gloss, trans. James Haughton Woods, The Yoga-System of Patanjali (1914), pp. 26-27, Internet Archive, opened 2026-10-21.)
