> **Principle 109** — Start moral education early

Aristotle's argument runs through habit: "states of character arise out of like activities", and he emphasizes the importance of habits formed in youth:

> "It makes no small difference, then, whether we form habits of one kind or of another from our very youth; it makes a very great difference, or rather all the difference." (NE II.1)

The habit that matters is liking the right things: "we ought to have been brought up in a particular way from our very youth, as Plato says, so as both to delight in and to be pained by the things that we ought; for this is the right education" (II.3).

Book X connects receptiveness to argument with prior habituation: "the soul of the student must first have been cultivated by means of habits for noble joy and noble hatred, like earth which is to nourish the seed", because "he who lives as passion directs will not hear argument that dissuades him, nor understand it if he does" (X.9).

(NE II.1, II.3, X.9, W. D. Ross translation.)

**Plain words** A child can learn what to do while still needing help learning what to enjoy or dislike. Aristotle includes both in education, and he says habituation should continue during adult life.

*For example:* Imagined example: a parent helps a child thank someone for a kindness and asks what that person did for them.
