> **Principle 28** — Use social support as a two-way practice

The evidence for receiving and for giving is not symmetrical. Cohen and Wills's review found "evidence consistent with both" a buffering model and a main-effect model. Buffering appeared when support meant the perceived availability of "interpersonal resources that are responsive to the needs elicited by stressful events", and a main effect appeared when support meant integration in a large social network ([Cohen and Wills 1985, summary at CMU](https://kilthub.cmu.edu/articles/journal_contribution/Stress_social_support_and_the_buffering_hypothesis/6618335)). So the buffering result concerns perceived availability, not help actually received.

For giving, Brown and colleagues followed older married adults for five years. Mortality "was significantly reduced for individuals who reported providing instrumental support to friends, relatives, and neighbors", while "receiving support had no effect on mortality once giving support was taken into consideration" ([Brown et al. 2003, Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/1467-9280.14461)). In a Detroit-area sample (n = 846), stress predicted mortality among people who had not helped others in the past year (HR = 1.30) but not among those who had (HR = 0.96) ([Poulin et al. 2013, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23327269/)). Both studies are observational.



### Scope

I did not open Cutrona and Russell 1990 or the full texts of these studies. The seed's claim that receiving support buffers stress holds only for perceived availability, and Brown et al. found receiving had no independent effect on mortality. Reciprocity as an "adaptive stance" is the compendium's, and the sources do not test it. I found nothing on refusing help.

**Plain words**
Knowing support is available protects against stress. In two studies, helping others, not being helped, predicted longer life. Both directions matter, but the evidence differs.

**For example**
Once a week, offer practical help to one person, and tell one person what help you could use.

## Practice

- When in need, ask for help directly. When able, give help without expectation of return. Balance the two over time, not at every moment.
