> **Principle 18** — Practise deliberate distraction management: know what pulls your attention

Duckworth, Gendler and Gross argue that self-control does not have to be a fight in the moment. It can take the form of "proactively choosing or changing situations in ways that weaken undesirable impulses or potentiate desirable ones", for example "the student who goes to the library without a cell phone". They add that "impulses tend to grow stronger over time", so situational strategies "which can nip a tempting impulse in the bud" may be especially effective ([Duckworth et al., 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26817725/)).

Distractibility also varies between people. Forster and Lavie compared 61 people on a laboratory task:

> individuals reporting high levels of distractibility [...] experienced greater distractor interference than did individuals reporting low levels.

That gap existed only under low perceptual load, and they suggest "high-load modifications of daily tasks may prove useful" ([Forster & Lavie, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576274/)).

(Duckworth et al., 2016; Forster & Lavie, 2007.)

**Plain words** Your list of attention traps is personal: a certain app, a topic, a person who drops by. Knowing the list lets you act before the pull starts, by moving the phone, closing the tab or choosing a seat away from the door, which is easier than resisting once it has begun.

For example (imagined): if checking sports scores always costs you an hour, put the app on a second screen of your phone and check it at set times. The effort goes into the setup once and not into resisting every time.

## Psychology parallels

- **Individual differences in distractibility**, people differ in what captures their attention and how resistant they are to distraction (Forster & Lavie, 2008; Sörqvist et al., 2013)
- **A phone's mere presence may drain cognitive capacity, but this is contested**, a meta-analysis preprint of 56 studies found a negative effect only on working memory capacity, with null results for the other functions tested (Parry, 2022, PsyArXiv; the original "brain drain" hypothesis is Ward et al., 2017)

## Philosophy parallels

- **Know thyself** (Delphic maxim)
- **[[The examined life|The examined life]] includes examined distraction** (Socrates, Apology)
- **[[Environmental design|Environmental design over willpower]]** (Aristotle, Politics)

## Practice

- Keep a distraction log for a week. Note what pulled your attention and when. Identify your top 3 pullers and design around them (remove, block, or restructure).
