> **Principle 448** — Practise revolt

Camus does not use revolt as a mood or a slogan. In The Myth of Sisyphus he calls it "one of the only coherent philosophical positions": "a constant confrontation between man and his own obscurity" (p. 54, trans. O'Brien). It is not hope: "It is not aspiration, for it is devoid of hope. That revolt is the certainty of a crushing fate, without the resignation that ought to accompany it" (p. 54).

### Revolt against the absurd

Revolt is also what stops suicide from looking like a solution. Suicide, he says, "is acceptance at its extreme", so it is the contrary of revolt (p. 54). What revolt gives back is value: "That revolt gives life its value. Spread out over the whole length of a life, it restores its majesty to that life" (p. 55). He sums up the result as "three consequences, which are my revolt, my freedom, and my passion" (p. 64).

The image is Sisyphus, who "teaches the higher fidelity that negates the gods and raises rocks". The book ends: "The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy" (p. 123).

### Revolt in The Rebel

In The Rebel, Camus carries the idea from private meaning to shared life. The rebel is "a man who says no, but whose refusal does not imply a renunciation", and who is "also a man who says yes, from the moment he makes his first gesture of rebellion" (Part One). Rebellion's first value is shared: "I rebel—therefore we exist" (Part One).

**Plain words** Revolt, for Camus, is staying awake to how little the universe answers your questions, and not letting that end your life or pass into a flat resignation. It is the mind facing something it cannot explain and refusing to pretend, or to quit. He does not say it feels good all the time. He says it is where life gets its worth.

*For example:* Imagined example: a nurse on an underfunded ward knows one shift will not repair the system. She does the shift anyway, with care, and without telling herself that the system is fine.

(Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus (1942), "An Absurd Reasoning" and "The Myth of Sisyphus," pp. 54-55, 64, 123, trans. Justin O'Brien, Vintage 1991; The Rebel (1951), Part One, trans. Anthony Bower; both opened 2026-10-21.)
