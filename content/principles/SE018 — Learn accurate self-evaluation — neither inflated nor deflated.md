> **Principle 18** — Learn accurate self-evaluation: neither inflated nor deflated

Socrates' edge over the politician he examined was calibration, not knowledge:

> he knows nothing, and thinks that he knows. I neither know nor think that I know. In this latter particular, then, I seem to have slightly the advantage of him.

(Plato, Apology 21d, Jowett translation; [MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html))

Socrates' stated advantage is limited: he recognizes that he lacks the knowledge under discussion. The passage supplies an example of acknowledging a limit to one's knowledge.

**Plain words**
For one task, list what you can demonstrate and what remains uncertain. Identify evidence that would change your assessment, whether upward or downward.

*For example:* Imagine a new employee completing an analysis they have checked while asking a colleague to review an unfamiliar procedure.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Better-than-average effect / illusory superiority|Better-than-average effect / illusory superiority]]**, most people rate themselves above average (Svenson, 1981; Dunning et al., 1989)
- **[[Self-verification theory (Swann)|Self-verification vs self-enhancement]]**, people seek both accurate and flattering feedback; the balance matters (Swann, 1983; Sedikides et al., 2004)
- **Accurate self-assessment predicts better outcomes**, calibration (knowing what you know and do not know) improves decisions (Moore & Healy, 2008; Vazire, 2010)

## Philosophy parallels

- **Know thyself** (Delphic maxim)
- **[[Humility|Humility and accuracy]]** (Aquinas, Summa Theologica)
- **[[Socratic wisdom|Socratic wisdom]]** (Plato, Apology)

## Practice

Calibrate your self-evaluation: seek feedback, test your assumptions, and notice where you might be over- or under-estimating. Ask: what is the evidence?
