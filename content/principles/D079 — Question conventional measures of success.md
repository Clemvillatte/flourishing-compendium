> **Principle 79** — Question conventional measures of success

Zhuangzi's first chapter closes its catalogue of scaled beings with the line:

> "Therefore it is said, 'The Perfect man has no (thought of) self; the Spirit-like man, none of merit; the Sagely-minded man, none of fame.'" (Zhuangzi 1, Legge trans., [ETH Zurich PDF](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf))

The chapter opens with the cicada and the dove laughing at the Peng bird's flight: "Of what use is it for this (creature) to rise 90,000 li, and make for the South?" The small cannot judge the large; what looks like useless height to a dove is the flight itself to the Peng. Daodejing 20 shows the same divergence lived out in a crowd:

> "The multitude of men look satisfied and pleased; as if enjoying a full banquet, as if mounted on a tower in spring. I alone seem listless and still, my desires having as yet given no indication of their presence." (DDJ 20, Legge trans., [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

The speaker names the crowd's pleasures precisely, then names his own object of value: "(Thus) I alone am different from other men, but I value the nursing-mother (the Tao)."

(Zhuangzi 1; DDJ 20.)

**Plain words** The prizes are real: the banquet is real food and the spring tower has a real view. The question is whose satisfaction they carry. Zhuangzi's line names the three places people usually store their worth: self, merit, fame. A practical test: before defending an ambition, say whose life it improves, and whether that person asked for the improvement.

*For example:* Halfway through law school, someone realizes they're there because it was the family's dream — that's the moment to ask the question.
