> **Principle 171** — Nothing is beyond redemption

The Dao is the refuge of the imperfect: no one is so flawed that alignment is closed to them. Chapter 62 of the Daodejing says the Tao holds the most honoured place of all things, and then says what it does there:

> "Bad men it guards, and doth their ill efface."
> (DDJ 62, [Legge's translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/216/216-h/216-h.htm))

The chapter's closing argument is that the ancients prized the Tao because "it could be got by seeking for it, and the guilty could escape (from the stain of their guilt) by it". Legge also renders the plainer prose line: "Even men who are not good are not abandoned by it." The chapter's refusal is about abandonment: no one is outside what the Tao covers. (DDJ 62.)

**Plain words** After breaking a commitment, ask what you promised, what can still be done, and what repair you can offer the person affected. If the original promise can no longer be fulfilled, explain that and propose a realistic alternative. A final judgment about what kind of person you are does not answer any of those questions.

*For example:* Suppose you break a promise you had kept for months. The chapter's picture is that the broken promise does not move you into a category the Tao stopped covering; the question becomes what repair is still possible.
