> **Principle 26** — Bring the mind back, without drama

Krishna's instruction to Arjuna on meditation is precise about frequency and tone. Whenever the heart slips its control, the practitioner is to rein it back, again and again, without adding commentary or blame to the wandering itself.

> But, as often as the heart
> Breaks—wild and wavering—from control, so oft
> Let him re-curb it, let him rein it back
> To the soul's governance; for perfect bliss
> Grows only in the bosom tranquillised,
>
> — Bhagavad Gita 6.26 (Arnold, The Song Celestial)

**Plain words** The instruction is not "have an unwavering mind." It is "notice it left, bring it back, repeat." The wandering is treated as an ordinary fact of attention, not a moral failure; the return is where the training lives. Each return is a rep in the same sense a lift is a rep, and a session with fifty of them is fifty units of work done, not a list of failures to regret.

*For example:* A meditator catches themselves mentally rehearsing an argument instead of watching the breath, notices, and returns attention to the breath. There is no inner monologue about how bad they are at this; the return itself is the practice.

The same attitude applies outside formal sitting. Reading a page, driving, listening to someone talk: attention drifts, and the practitioner simply returns it to the task. What is dropped is the secondary story about the drift. The Gita's phrasing is worth noticing here: the text does not ask that the mind never break control, only that it be re-curbed, so often that re-curbing becomes the habit the chapter asks for.

(BG 6.26.)
