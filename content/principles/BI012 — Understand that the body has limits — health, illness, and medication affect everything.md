> **Principle 12** — Understand that the body has limits: health, illness, and medication affect everything

The body is the machinery that mood, motivation and judgement run on. Epictetus opens the Enchiridion with a sorting exercise: what you control, and what you do not. The body lands in the second column:

> "Things not in our control are body, property, reputation, command, and, in a word, whatever are not our own actions." (Epictetus, Enchiridion 1, [Carter's translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html))

That placement does the principle's work. If the body is not yours to command, then illness, pain, medication changes, hormonal shifts and ageing are not character defects arriving one after another; they are the conditions your mental life has to run on. Reading a bodily mood as a moral failure means blaming yourself for something that was never in your column. The medical literature keeps the connection modest: a meta-analysis of 25 studies covering 348,014 people found hypothyroidism associated with clinical depression at an odds ratio of 1.30, strongest for overt disease ([Bode et al., JAMA Psychiatry 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34524390/)). The compendium develops the body's limits over time in [[The seasons of illness]] and [[The seasons of the body]].

**Plain words**
Your mood has a body. When it drops, the search usually starts in your biography: what did I do wrong, what should I fix about my life? Run it the other way too: how did you sleep, is an illness coming on, did a medication change, where is the hormonal cycle, what decade are you in? None of these settles the question by itself; together they are the first place to look before you indict your character. When a mood change tracks a lab value, the lab value is the first thing to ask about.

*For example:* The "motivation problem" that tracked exactly with the thyroid levels — the willpower was fine; the metabolism wasn't.

## Psychology parallels

- **Chronic illness and depression**, chronic physical illness is associated with higher rates of depression (Barnett et al., 2012; Naylor et al., 2012)
- **Medication side effects on mood**, many medications affect mood and cognition (Preston et al., 2017; Haddad & Anderson, 2002)
- **Hormonal effects on mood**, thyroid, menstrual cycle, and menopause affect mood (Schmidt et al., 2015; Sundström-Poromaa, 2018)

## Practice

- When you notice a mood or cognitive change, check the body: illness, pain, medication changes, hormonal shifts, ageing. Address the physiological substrate before treating the mood as purely psychological.
