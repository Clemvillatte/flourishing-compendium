> **Principle 91** — Ask who is aware

The Kena Upanishad opens with the "who" questions, quoted below. The reply does not hand over an object: the sought one is "the ear of the ear, the mind of the mind" (Kena 1.2), and "The eye does not go thither, nor speech, nor mind. We do not know, we do not understand, how any one can teach it" (Kena 1.3). Ramana Maharshi made the question the core of a modern practice: in Nan Yar, "the Path of Knowledge, the enquiry in the form of 'Who am I?', is the principal means". The linked pages carry the standing dispute: Advaita's atman and Buddhism's anatman read the outcome differently, and this page's teaching is the inquiry.

(Ramana Maharshi, modern articulation of the Advaita line; Kena 1.1-1.4.)

> At whose wish does the mind sent forth proceed on its errand? At whose command does the first breath go forth? At whose wish do we utter this speech? What god directs the eye, or the ear?
>
> Kena Upanishad 1.1, translated by F. Max Müller, [online text](https://texts.wara.in/upanishads/kena/).

**Plain words**
Most of your attention sits on things: sounds, worries, the phone. This question points it the other way, at whatever is doing the noticing. The Kena's reply gives no object to find there. The exercise is to ask and to look; the page promises no result.

**For example**
In an imagined case, someone caught in a loop of worry asks "who is aware of this worry?" and looks back for the looker. The exercise is the looking itself.
