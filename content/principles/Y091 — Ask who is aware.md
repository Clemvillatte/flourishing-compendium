> **Principle 91** — Ask who is aware

The Kena Upanishad opens with the "who" questions: "At whose wish does the mind sent forth proceed on its errand? At whose command does the first breath go forth? At whose wish do we utter this speech? What god directs the eye, or the ear?" (Kena 1.1). The reply does not hand over an object: the sought one is "the ear of the ear, the mind of the mind" (Kena 1.2), and "the eye does not go thither, nor speech, nor mind. We do not know, we do not understand, how any one can teach it" (Kena 1.3). Ramana Maharshi made this question the core of a modern practice, atma-vichara or self-inquiry. The series keeps the standing dispute in view: Advaita's atman and Buddhism's anatman read the outcome differently, and the page teaches the inquiry, not the verdict.

(Ramana Maharshi, modern articulation of the Advaita line; Kena 1.1-1.4.)

> At whose wish does the mind sent forth proceed on its errand? At whose command does the first breath go forth? At whose wish do we utter this speech? What god directs the eye, or the ear?
>
> Kena Upanishad 1.1, translated by F. Max Müller, [online text](https://texts.wara.in/upanishads/kena/).

**Plain words**
Most of your attention sits on things: sounds, worries, the phone. This question points it the other way, at whatever is doing the noticing. You will not find an object there, and that is the point: the question loosens the assumption that everything worth finding is one more thing to see. Ask it when you are stuck inside a thought, and the thought starts losing the room.

**For example**
In an imagined case, someone caught in a loop of worry asks "who is aware of this worry?" and looks back for the looker. Nothing shows up as an object, and the loop has loosened by then.
