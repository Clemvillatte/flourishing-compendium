> **Principle 141** — Care for life as the root of all endeavor

Zhuangzi 3 opens with the danger of spending a limited life pursuing unlimited knowledge. Its cook offers an image of careful action: his knife passes through openings rather than cutting against the bones, and remains sharp after nineteen years. Even this experienced worker changes his pace at a difficult joint:

> Nevertheless, whenever I come to a complicated joint, and see that there will be some difficulty, I proceed anxiously and with caution, not allowing my eyes to wander from the place, and moving my hand slowly.
> (Zhuangzi 3 §2, Legge, [METIS English Classics edition](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf))

Afterwards he wipes the knife and puts it away. The ruler hears a lesson in [[Yangsheng (cultivating vitality)|yangsheng]], nourishing life:

> Excellent! I have heard the words of my cook, and learned from them the nourishment of (our) life.
> (Zhuangzi 3 §2, Legge, [METIS English Classics edition](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf))

There is also a harder question about the work itself. Chris Fraser asks whether the cook should be doing this task at all: skill at cutting up an ox does not settle whether it is fitting work ([Applied Emptiness in the Zhuangzi, manuscript p. 24](http://cjfraser.net/images/2013/08/Fraser_Heart-FastingForgetting.pdf)). I use the care taken with the knife as an image for preserving the person doing the work, while keeping that further question open: what does the work cost other lives? This reading makes no promise of longevity.

(Zhuangzi 3 §2.)

### Plain words

Care for the lives affected by your work, including your own.

### For example

An editor considering an extra assignment could first check what would happen to meal breaks and existing promises. If there is room to negotiate, propose less work or a later deadline. Name the cost rather than promising the same output from a day that has no space left.
