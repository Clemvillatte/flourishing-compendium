> **Principle 2** — Know that attention has three networks: alerting, orienting, and executive control

Fan, McCandliss, Sommer, Raz and Posner built on Posner's account of attention as a set of brain systems. They describe three networks "defined in anatomical and functional terms": alerting, orienting and executive attention. Their Attention Network Test measures each from reaction times in a single 30-minute session ([Fan et al., 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11970796/)).

> A study with 40 normal adult subjects indicates that the ANT produces reliable single subject estimates of alerting, orienting, and executive function, and further suggests that the efficiencies of these three networks are uncorrelated.
>
> Fan et al., Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience (2002)

The authors also report "some interactions in which alerting and orienting can modulate the degree of interference from flankers", so the networks are not fully isolated. The count of three is a framework that has grown. Petersen and Posner's 2012 update says research on orienting and executive functions "has supported the addition of new networks of brain regions" ([Petersen & Posner, 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22524787/)).

(Posner & Petersen, 1990; Fan et al., 2002; Petersen & Posner, 2012.)

**Plain words** "Paying attention" covers at least three jobs. One is getting ready and staying alert. One is pointing your focus at the right place. One is choosing the right response when something competes with it. A person can be strong at one and weaker at another, since the test finds them uncorrelated. Asking which job a situation needs is more useful than telling yourself to concentrate.

For example (imagined): a long night drive needs alertness. Finding a name on a crowded noticeboard needs orienting. Ignoring a chatty colleague while you finish a report needs executive control.

## Practice

- Diagnose which network is the bottleneck. If you are sleepy and cannot stay alert, that is alerting. If you cannot find the right information, that is orienting. If you are distracted and cannot suppress interference, that is executive control. Each requires different remedies.
