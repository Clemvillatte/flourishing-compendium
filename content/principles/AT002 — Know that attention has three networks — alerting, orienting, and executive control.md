> **Principle 2** — Know that attention has three networks: alerting, orienting, and executive control

Attention is not one thing. It has at least three distinct networks: alerting (achieving and maintaining a state of vigilance), orienting (selecting information from a specific location or modality), and executive control (resolving conflict, planning, and overriding automatic responses). Each can be separately trained and separately impaired.

**Plain words** Attention isn't one muscle; it's three: the alarm (wake up, something's happening), the pointer (look over there), and the manager (hold the line, ignore the noise). Each can be strong or weak independently. Know which one your situation is calling for.

*For example:* The night-shift nurse has a trained alarm; the auditor has a trained manager. Both call it "paying attention" — they're playing different sports.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Posner's three-network model|Posner's three-network model]]**, alerting, orienting, and executive attention are anatomically and functionally distinct (Posner & Petersen, 1990; Fan et al., 2002; Petersen & Posner, 2012)
- **[[Attention Network Test (ANT)|Attention Network Test (ANT)]]**, the three networks can be measured separately and show distinct developmental trajectories (Fan et al., 2002; Rueda et al., 2004)

## Practice

- Diagnose which network is the bottleneck. If you are sleepy and cannot stay alert, that is alerting. If you cannot find the right information, that is orienting. If you are distracted and cannot suppress interference, that is executive control. Each requires different remedies.
