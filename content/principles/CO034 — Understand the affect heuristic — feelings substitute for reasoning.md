> **Principle 34** — Understand the affect heuristic: feelings substitute for reasoning

When making judgments about risk or benefit, you often rely on how you feel about the thing rather than on deliberate reasoning. If something feels good, you judge its risks as low and benefits as high; if it feels bad, the reverse.

**Plain words** The affect heuristic: when judging risk or benefit, you rely on how you feel about the thing rather than deliberate reasoning. Feels good = low risk, high benefit; feels bad = the reverse. Your feelings are doing statistics they're not qualified for.

*For example:* The person who fears flying (feels scary) and texts while driving (feels fine) — the affect is running the risk assessment. The numbers say flying is absurdly safe and the texting is absurdly lethal. The feeling is a fast, confident, terrible analyst.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Affect heuristic|Affect heuristic]]**, positive or negative feelings about an object drive judgments of its risk and benefit (Slovic et al., 2002; Finucane et al., 2000)
- **The affect heuristic explains risk perception gaps**, fears that feel vivid (nuclear, flying) are judged riskier than dull risks (cars, stairs) regardless of statistics (Slovic, 1987)

## Philosophy parallels

- **Reason vs passion** (Hume, Treatise: "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions")
- **[[Distinguishing feeling from fact|Distinguishing feeling from fact]]** (Stoics, Epictetus)

## Practice

- When judging risk or benefit, ask: "is this judgment based on how it feels or on actual data?" Deliberately separate the feeling from the statistic.
