> **Principle 112** — See action within rest and rest within action

Krishna's riddle in Chapter 4 is that action and inaction cannot be read off the outside of a life. Verse 4.18 puts it:

> Those who see action in inaction and inaction in action are truly wise amongst humans. Although performing all kinds of actions, they are yogis and masters of all their actions.

(Bhagavad Gita 4.18, trans. Swami Mukundananda, [holy-bhagavad-gita.org](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/4/verse/18/).)

Mukundananda's commentary reads both halves. On action in inaction, he describes someone who renounces social duties out of indolence while "their mind continues to contemplate upon the objects of the senses", and says that such idleness "is actually sinful action." On inaction in action, he points to those who "execute their social duties without attachment to results", and are therefore "not entangled in karmic reactions". Verse 4.19 describes the wise as those "whose every action is free from the desire for material pleasures and who have burnt the reactions of work in the fire of divine knowledge" ([BG 4.19](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/4/verse/19/)). Verse 4.20 adds, "Despite engaging in activities, they do not do anything at all" ([BG 4.20](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/4/verse/20/)).

What the text gives is a definition of the wise worker. It says nothing about rest as recovery. Reading "rest" as inner weather, and scheduling one restful action a week, is this page's own extension.

**Plain words**

Two people can be doing the same thing and be in very different states. One sits still while the mind runs after what it wants. The other works while the mind has put down its claim. The Gita calls the second one rested.

**For example**

A woman gardens on Sunday for two hours and notices the afternoon passed without a plan in her head. Her brother, on the next chair with nothing to do, is replaying an argument. Only one of them has stopped.
