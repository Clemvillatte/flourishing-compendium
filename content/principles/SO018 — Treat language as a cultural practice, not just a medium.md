> **Principle 18** — Treat language as a cultural practice, not just a medium

Boroditsky's 2011 article reviews evidence that languages can influence thought in particular domains, including space, time and causality. ([How Language Shapes Thought, Scientific American, February 2011](https://web.uvic.ca/~dbub/Cognition_Action/SpecialTopicsEssays_files/How%20Language%20Shapes%20Thought.pdf)) The compendium recommends examining the distinctions your wording makes available and the assumptions it carries.

(Boroditsky, "How Language Shapes Thought," Scientific American, February 2011; Lupyan et al., 2020.)

> "The answer, it turns out, is both — the way we think influences the way we speak, but the influence also goes the other way." (Lera Boroditsky, [How Language Shapes Thought, Scientific American, February 2011](https://web.uvic.ca/~dbub/Cognition_Action/SpecialTopicsEssays_files/How%20Language%20Shapes%20Thought.pdf))

Boroditsky's article traces the history: the notion that different languages impart different cognitive skills became associated with Sapir and Whorf, but by the 1970s many scientists had become disenchanted with the hypothesis, one problem being "a near complete lack of evidence to support their claims." ([How Language Shapes Thought, Scientific American, February 2011](https://web.uvic.ca/~dbub/Cognition_Action/SpecialTopicsEssays_files/How%20Language%20Shapes%20Thought.pdf)) Her report is that this has changed: "decades later, a solid body of empirical evidence showing how languages shape thinking has finally emerged." ([How Language Shapes Thought, Scientific American, February 2011](https://web.uvic.ca/~dbub/Cognition_Action/SpecialTopicsEssays_files/How%20Language%20Shapes%20Thought.pdf))

The article discusses examples in particular domains, including space, time and causality. ([How Language Shapes Thought, Scientific American, February 2011](https://web.uvic.ca/~dbub/Cognition_Action/SpecialTopicsEssays_files/How%20Language%20Shapes%20Thought.pdf)) The practical direction for a writer is attention: notice the concepts your language makes available and the ones it leaves unnamed, and treat other languages' distinctions as data about what can be noticed.

**Plain words**
Boroditsky describes influences in both directions: thought influences language, and language can influence thought. ([How Language Shapes Thought, Scientific American, February 2011](https://web.uvic.ca/~dbub/Cognition_Action/SpecialTopicsEssays_files/How%20Language%20Shapes%20Thought.pdf)) For a writer, this is a reason to examine the distinctions their wording makes.

*For example* (writing exercise): Describe the same event twice, first naming the actor and then leaving the actor unnamed. Ask what each wording makes explicit.
