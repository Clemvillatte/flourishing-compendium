> **Principle 18** — Treat language as a cultural practice, not just a medium

Treat language as a cultural practice that shapes thought, identity, and social relations, rather than a neutral medium of communication. The strong Sapir-Whorf hypothesis is false; the weak version — that language influences habitual thought and attention — has empirical support (Boroditsky, 2011; Lupyan et al., 2020). The adaptive stance is to attend to the language you use, the concepts it makes available, and the identities it constructs.

(Boroditsky, "How Language Shapes Thought," Scientific American, February 2011; Lupyan et al., 2020.)

> "The answer, it turns out, is both — the way we think influences the way we speak, but the influence also goes the other way." (Lera Boroditsky, [How Language Shapes Thought, Scientific American, February 2011](https://web.uvic.ca/~dbub/Cognition_Action/SpecialTopicsEssays_files/How%20Language%20Shapes%20Thought.pdf))

Boroditsky's article traces the history: the notion that different languages impart different cognitive skills became associated with Sapir and Whorf, but by the 1970s scientists had become disenchanted with the hypothesis, one problem being "a near complete lack of evidence to support their claims." Her report is that this has changed: "decades later, a solid body of empirical evidence showing how languages shape thinking has finally emerged."

The evidence she surveys is domain-specific, not a claim that language imprisons thought. Research in her lab and others, she writes, has been "uncovering how language shapes even the most fundamental dimensions of human experience: space, time, causality and relationships to others," and the influence runs in both directions, from thought to language and back. The practical direction for a writer is attention: notice the concepts your language makes available and the ones it leaves unnamed, and treat other languages' distinctions as data about what can be noticed.

**Plain words**
Language isn't a neutral pipe; it shapes thought, identity, and relations. The strong version is false; the weak version is true and matters. Your vocabulary is quietly steering what you notice.

*For example:* The language with seven words for snow and the one with one — both populations see the same field, but one of them sees distinctions the other literally has no hooks for. Vocabulary is attention.
