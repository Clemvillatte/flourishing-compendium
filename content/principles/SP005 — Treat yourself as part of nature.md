> **Principle 5** — Treat yourself as part of nature

The preface to Part III of the Ethics opens with a complaint about other writers: they treat human beings as exempt from the order of nature.

> Indeed they seem to think of man in Nature as a kingdom within a kingdom. (Spinoza, Ethics III, Preface; Bennett translation)

The preface then draws the consequence for the affects:

> So the affects of hate, anger, envy etc., considered in themselves, follow from the same necessity and force of Nature as other particular things. (Spinoza, Ethics III, Preface; Bennett translation)

The program that follows treats the affects as objects of study rather than targets of sermon: "approaching human actions and appetites in the way I would approach questions about lines, planes, and bodies" (Spinoza, Ethics III, Preface; Bennett translation). Part I states: "Whatever exists is in God, and nothing can exist or be conceived without God" (Spinoza, Ethics I, P15; Bennett translation). This principle applies that approach to self-examination: ask what preceded a fear or jealousy and what conditions might help explain it, rather than treating the feeling as a scandal.

**Plain words** Spinoza treats fear and appetite as natural things, subject to causes like anything else. That is a way to examine them: look for what comes before a feeling and what changes with it. The preface itself states the approach; it does not report evidence about outcomes.

*For example (imagined):* A man who has hated himself for years for being "so emotional" starts keeping notes on when the emotion rises and what comes before it, treating it as information about his own system.
