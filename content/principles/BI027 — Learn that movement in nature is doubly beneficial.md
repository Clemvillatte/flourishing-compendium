> **Principle 27** — Learn that movement in nature is doubly beneficial

"Doubly beneficial" bundles two bodies of evidence, and neither study adds the two together. A pooled analysis of ten UK studies with 1252 participants found that green exercise improved self-esteem (d = 0.46) and mood (d = 0.54), with large benefits from short engagements and greater effects when water was present ([Barton & Pretty, 2010](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/683/)). A systematic review found lower anger, fatigue and sadness after activity in natural environments than in synthetic ones, though its authors called the overall evidence suggestive rather than settled ([Bowler et al., 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2924288/)).

A controlled experiment, with walking in both conditions, found that a 90-minute walk through a natural setting decreased self-reported rumination and subgenual prefrontal cortex activity, while the same-length walk through an urban setting did not ([Bratman et al., 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4507237/)). One statistical qualification from the paper's Results section: the self-reported rumination difference between the groups did not reach the conventional .05 threshold (time-by-environment interaction P = 0.07); the nature group decreased significantly within itself while the urban group showed no statistically significant change, and the subgenual-prefrontal-cortex difference was significant ([Results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4507237/)):

> "We show in healthy participants that a brief nature experience, a 90-min walk in a natural setting, decreases both self-reported rumination and neural activity in the subgenual prefrontal cortex (sgPFC), whereas a 90-min walk in an urban setting has no such effects on self-reported rumination or neural activity." (Abstract; Bratman et al., 2015, PNAS 112(28): 8567-72, [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4507237/))

**Plain words**
When choosing a walking route, consider a park or waterside path ([Barton & Pretty, 2010](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/683/); [Bowler et al., 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2924288/)). The research gives reasons to try natural surroundings, without promising that every outing will improve your mood ([Barton & Pretty, 2010](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/683/); [Bowler et al., 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2924288/)). It does not establish a doubling of benefits ([Bratman et al., 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4507237/)).

**For example**
(Imagined) Two colleagues take the same 30-minute lunchtime walk, one along a canal path, one through a car park and arcade. The legs do identical work; each can notice what the afternoon's mood does afterward.

## Practice

- Exercise in nature: walk, run, or cycle in parks, forests, or by water. The pooled studies found benefits from short engagements ([Barton & Pretty, 2010](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/683/)), and the natural-versus-synthetic comparison is suggestive rather than settled ([Bowler et al., 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2924288/)).
