> **Principle 7** — Recognise affective forecasting errors; expect hedonic adaptation

Wilson and Gilbert, who have reviewed this area, summarise it this way: "People base many decisions on affective forecasts, predictions about their emotional reactions to future events. They often display an impact bias, overestimating the intensity and duration of their emotional reactions to such events." The effect holds "even when they know what the future event is likely to entail and they are not in a particularly 'hot' or 'cold' emotional state at the time of making their forecast" ([Wilson and Gilbert, 2005](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/wilson-gilbert-cdips2005.pdf)).

### Why it happens

They name two causes. One is focalism, "the tendency to overestimate how much we will think about the event in the future and to underestimate the extent to which other events will influence our thoughts and feelings". The other is "that forecasters fail to recognize how readily they will make sense of novel or unexpected events once they happen." For negative events, they call that last oversight immune neglect.

### Both directions

In their longer review, the error is described as covering "overestimating initial intensity and overestimating duration" ([Wilson and Gilbert, 2003](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Wilson%20&%20Gilbert%20(Advances).pdf)). A later paper notes that the bias "has been found in predictions for both positive and negative events", though it tends to be "somewhat greater for distressing events" ([Cognitive determinants of affective forecasting errors](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3170528/)). People predict the direction of a reaction accurately but not its intensity or duration.

### Not settled

The page's claim that this is "one of the most robust findings" goes beyond what these sources say. Wilson and Gilbert published a 2013 comment titled "The Impact Bias Is Alive and Well", which suggests the finding has been challenged ([Wilson and Gilbert, 2013](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/WIlson_Gilbert_2013.pdf)).

**Plain words** People tend to expect a big event to affect their mood more strongly and for longer than it does, whether good or bad. You adjust faster than you predict, partly because you will have other things on your mind and partly because you will make sense of what happened.

*For example:* Imagine dreading a difficult conversation for three weeks, and then it takes eight minutes and goes fine. That is a forecast of intensity and duration that did not come true.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Impact bias|Impact bias]]**, people overestimate the intensity and duration of future emotional reactions (Wilson & Gilbert, 2003)
- **[[Hedonic adaptation|Hedonic adaptation]]**, gains and losses both fade toward baseline, and the rate of adaptation is often underestimated (Lyubomirsky, 2011)
- **Durability bias is not fully eliminated by awareness**, knowing about the bias reduces but does not eliminate it (Wilson et al., 2000)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Nothing in excess|Nothing in excess]]** (Delphic maxim, inscribed at Delphi)
- **[[The vanity of desire|The vanity of desire]]** (Schopenhauer, World as Will and Representation)

## Practice

- Before making a decision based on how you think you will feel, discount the predicted intensity by roughly half, especially for its duration. Ask: "how did I actually feel three months after the last similar event?"
