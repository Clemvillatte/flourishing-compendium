> **Principle 7** — Recognise affective forecasting errors; expect hedonic adaptation

You overestimate how long and how intense your future feelings will be, both positive and negative. The new job, the rejection, the move, the illness — each will feel less intense, sooner, than predicted. This is one of the most robust findings in affect science and has direct implications for how you make decisions.

**Plain words** You overestimate how long and how intense your future feelings will be — both good and bad. The new job, the rejection, the illness: each will feel less intense, sooner, than predicted. Expect hedonic adaptation, and stop paying the premium for forecasts.

*For example:* The person who spends three miserable weeks dreading the conversation that turns out to be eight minutes and fine — and the buyer's high that fades by February. The forecasting error runs in both directions; budget for it.
## Psychology parallels

- **[[Impact bias|Impact bias]]**, people overestimate the intensity and duration of future emotional reactions (Wilson & Gilbert, 2003)
- **[[Hedonic adaptation|Hedonic adaptation]]**, gains and losses both fade toward baseline, and the rate of adaptation is often underestimated (Lyubomirsky, 2011)
- **Durability bias is not fully eliminated by awareness**, knowing about the bias reduces but does not eliminate it (Wilson et al., 2000)

## Philosophy parallels

- **[[Nothing in excess|Nothing in excess]]** (Delphic maxim, inscribed at Delphi)
- **[[The vanity of desire|The vanity of desire]]** (Schopenhauer, World as Will and Representation)

## Practice

- Before making a decision based on how you think you will feel, discount the predicted intensity by roughly half, especially for its duration. Ask: "how did I actually feel three months after the last similar event?"
