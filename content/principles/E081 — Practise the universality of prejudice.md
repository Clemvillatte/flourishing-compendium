> **Principle 473** — Practise the universality of prejudice

Gadamer defends a claim that sounds odd at first: you cannot understand anything without prejudgments. "A person who is trying to understand a text is always projecting," he writes, reading it "with particular expectations in regard to a certain meaning" (Truth and Method, p. 269, trans. Weinsheimer and Marshall). He turns the Enlightenment's ideal on its head: "the fundamental prejudice of the Enlightenment is the prejudice against prejudice itself, which denies tradition its power" (p. 273).

### What he asks of you

He does not tell you to keep every prejudice. The aim is to "fundamentally rehabilitate the concept of prejudice" (p. 278), and then to notice which ones are working in you: "the fore-meanings that determine my own understanding can go entirely unnoticed" (p. 271). A trained reader is "sensitive to the text's alterity", which "involves neither 'neutrality' with respect to content nor the extinction of one's self, but the foregrounding and appropriation of one's own fore-meanings and prejudices" (p. 271). The text itself corrects you: the fore-projection is "constantly revised in terms of what emerges as he penetrates into the meaning" (p. 269).

### The limit

The prejudices "that occupy the interpreter's consciousness are not at his free disposal" (p. 295). You cannot lift them off in one move. You can notice them when something surprises you.

**Plain words** You never come to a person or a text with an empty head. You come with expectations, and they are what let you understand anything at all. The risk is not having them but not seeing them. Gadamer's answer is to let the other person or text push back and to change your reading when it does.

*For example:* Imagined example: you read a letter from a relative and hear it as cold. A second reading, with the thought that you expected coldness, lets you see a nervous attempt to be careful. Neither reading was prejudice-free; the second one knew what it was bringing.

(Gadamer, Truth and Method, 2nd rev. ed. (Continuum, 2004), pp. 269-273, 278, 295, trans. Weinsheimer and Marshall. Quotations and page numbers are taken from the excerpt compiled by Pierre Legrand, which cites this edition; opened 2026-10-21.)
