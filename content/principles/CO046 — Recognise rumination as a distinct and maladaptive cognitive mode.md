> **Principle 46** — Recognise rumination as a distinct and maladaptive cognitive mode

> I propose that the ways people respond to their own symptoms of depression influence the duration of these symptoms. People who engage in ruminative responses to depression, focusing on their symptoms and the possible causes and consequences of their symptoms, will show longer depressions than people who take action to distract themselves from their symptoms.

(Nolen-Hoeksema, "Responses to depression and their effects on the duration of depressive episodes," Journal of Abnormal Psychology 100(4), 1991, abstract; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1757671/).)

Rumination also predicted new depressive episodes: "rumination also predicted depressive disorders, including new onsets of depressive episodes," and it "may be particularly characteristic of people with mixed anxiety/depressive symptoms" (Nolen-Hoeksema, Journal of Abnormal Psychology 109(3), 2000, abstract; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11016119/)). The 2000 abstract says rumination did not predict chronicity once baseline symptoms were accounted for. A later review adds that rumination "appears to more consistently predict the onset of depression rather than the duration" (Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco and Lyubomirsky, "Rethinking Rumination," Perspectives on Psychological Science 3(5), 2008; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26158958/)).

The line between harmful and useful repetition comes from Treynor, Gonzalez and Nolen-Hoeksema (Cognitive Therapy and Research 27, 2003). They found two components: the reflection items "suggest a purposeful turning inward to engage in cognitive problem solving to alleviate one's depressive symptoms," while "the brooding factor of rumination was associated with more depression both concurrently and in the longitudinal analyses, suggesting it is not adaptive" ([University of Michigan copy](https://websites.umich.edu/~gonzo/papers/treynor-rumination.pdf)). That split is not clean in everyone: in currently depressed people the brooding and reflection factors blurred together ([Whitmer and Gotlib, 2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10608-011-9361-3), abstract only).

On treatment, a randomised trial of 42 people with medication-refractory residual depression found that adding rumination-focused CBT to treatment as usual "significantly improved residual symptoms and remission rates," with effects "mediated by change in rumination." The authors note it lacked an attention-control group (Watkins et al., British Journal of Psychiatry 199(4), 2011; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21778171/)).

**Plain words** Rumination means going around the same distress, its causes and what it might lead to, without getting anywhere. Studies link it to longer and new episodes of depression. A thinking style closer to purposeful problem solving looked less harmful than gloomy brooding, though that split is blurrier in people who are already depressed. Therapy aimed at rumination helped in a small early trial.

*For example (imagined):* Someone replays a difficult conversation for the tenth night. On the eleventh they write down one thing they will say differently next time and then do something else, which is the move from brooding toward problem solving.

## Psychology parallels

- **Rumination (brooding) predicts depression**, the brooding subcomponent of rumination is a strong prospective predictor of depressive episodes (Nolen-Hoeksema, 2000; Treynor et al., 2003)
- **Reflective pondering is less maladaptive than brooding**, the reflective component is less strongly associated with depression (Treynor et al., 2003)
- **Rumination-focused CBT (RFCBT) reduces rumination and depression**, interventions that specifically target rumination are effective (Watkins et al., 2011)

## Practice

- When you notice repetitive negative thinking, ask: "is this moving me toward understanding or solution, or am I just cycling?" If cycling, shift to action, distraction, or a different cognitive mode.
