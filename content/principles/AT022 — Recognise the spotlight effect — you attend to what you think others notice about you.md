> **Principle 22** — Recognise the spotlight effect: you attend to what you think others notice about you

In the study that named the effect, Gilovich, Medvec and Savitsky sent participants into a room wearing a T-shirt bearing a large picture of Barry Manilow, a singer the participants found embarrassing. The shirt-wearers estimated that roughly twice as many observers would notice and identify the picture as actually did: the average estimate was exactly twice the observers' actual accuracy rate, an average discrepancy of 23 percentage points. A companion study found the same overestimate for speech: discussion participants overestimated how prominent their positive and negative utterances were to fellow discussants.

The account the authors give is anchoring with insufficient adjustment: you stand in your own experience, where the shirt feels enormous, and adjust only part of the way toward what the room actually sees.

> "This research provides evidence that people overestimate the extent to which their actions and appearance are noted by others, a phenomenon dubbed the spotlight effect." (Gilovich, Medvec & Savitsky, 2000 — [full text](https://www.psychologyib.com/uploads/1/1/7/5/11758934/the_spotlight_effect_-_ib_psychology.pdf) — J Pers Soc Psychol 78(2), p. 211)

**Plain words**
Your own experience is the anchor: the stain feels enormous from the inside. Everyone else is anchored in their own experience, busy with their own stains. The gap between those two viewpoints is the bias, and knowing it exists lets you discount your first estimate of how many eyes are on you.

**For example**
Replaying the joke that bombed for a week: ask anyone from that dinner what they remember, and you will hear about their own evening, including their own joke that also bombed.

## Practice

- When you feel scrutinised, remember the spotlight effect: others are mostly attending to themselves, not to you. This reduces social anxiety and self-consciousness.
