> **Principle 72** — Practice letting go (vossagga)

Vossagga appears in the Ānāpānasati Sutta as the last of sixteen steps of breath meditation, where Thanissaro's translation renders it "relinquishment". The training sequence runs through inconstancy, dispassion and cessation before it reaches this final step (MN 118, [text](https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.118.than.html)). The Dhammapada verse the page cites applies the same release to time: "Let go of the past, let go of the future, let go of the present" (Dhammapada 348, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita, [text](https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/dhp/dhp.24.budd.html)).

> "He trains himself, 'I will breathe in focusing on relinquishment.' He trains himself, 'I will breathe out focusing on relinquishment.'" (MN 118, step 16 of 16, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, [text](https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.118.than.html))

Neither passage presents relinquishment as a method for making thoughts dissolve, and neither promises relief. MN 118 places it at the end of a graduated training carried by the breath; Dhammapada 348 frames it as part of crossing to the farther shore of existence. The compendium's exercise is narrower: when a thought, mood or tension is noticed, allow it to run its course without adding effort either to hold it or to expel it.

### Plain words

Noticing where your effort is going is the exercise: the plan you keep re-running, the argument you keep re-arguing. Letting go means you stop servicing it with attention on demand; it does not require forcing it out of mind or deciding to stop caring. The breath gives the pacing: in MN 118 relinquishment is practiced one breath at a time, at the end of a longer sequence, not achieved in a single decision.

### For example

Imagine re-reading an email you already sent, rewording it in your head. Letting go is noticing that you are doing this and returning to what you are actually doing now. The rewording may start again later; the exercise is the returning.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Nonattachment|Nonattachment]]**: Sahdra, Shaver and Brown define the Buddhist notion as "release from mental fixations", related to but distinguishable from the Western construct of attachment, and present evidence consistent with Buddhist theory that it is "psychologically and socially adaptive" ([2010, J Pers Assess 92(2)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20155561/))
