> **Principle 353** — Practise the word that is spoken

> If we could take an inventory of all the physical and psychic phenomena to be found within a dialogical event, there would still remain outside something sui generis that could not be included — and that is just what does not allow itself to be understood as the sum of the speech of two or more speakers, together with all the accidental circumstances. This something sui generis is their dialogue.

(Buber, "The Word That Is Spoken," in The Knowledge of Man: Selected Essays, trans. Maurice Friedman and Ronald Gregor Smith, Harper & Row, 1965, p. 112. Quoted as given, with page, in Niels Wilde, "Buoyant Ontologies: The Roots and Ramifications of Dialogue in Buber and Heidegger," Religions 12(9), 2021, 778; [open-access PDF](https://www.wisdomlib.org/uploads/journals/mdpi-relig/2021-volume-12-issue-9--2077-1444-12-9-778-.pdf). Buber's own book was not available to read in full, so this passage is second-hand.)

The same article quotes Buber on origin: "Language never existed before address; it could become monologue only after dialogue broke off or broke down" (p. 115). Together the two passages carry the entry's idea. Language lives in being spoken to someone, and a conversation has a reality that is not reducible to two people's separate speeches.

The phrase "creates a shared reality" is the compendium's wording, not Buber's. Buber's term for the space where this happens is the between, and the passages above do not say that people should or can produce it by effort. The advice not to use words "as tools or weapons" is the compendium's gloss on his contrast between speaking to someone and speaking about them, and was not checked against Buber's own text.

The psychology parallel is shared reality theory. Echterhoff, Higgins and Levine define it as involving "a (subjectively perceived) commonality of individuals' inner states" about a target, and they treat the saying-is-believing effect, where people tune a message to an audience and then come to believe it, as an example of how communication creates shared reality (Perspectives on Psychological Science 4(5), 2009, abstract; [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26162223/)). That research describes how agreement is made, and it does not by itself describe Buber's I-Thou speech. Hardin and Higgins (1996) was not opened.

**Plain words** A conversation can be more than two people exchanging messages. For Buber, what happens in the space between speaker and listener belongs to neither of them alone. Speaking to someone, rather than at them or about them, is where the relation lives.

*For example (imagined):* Two former partners can trade tidy, accurate messages for weeks. The one conversation in which each says what is true and listens to the other is a different kind of event.
