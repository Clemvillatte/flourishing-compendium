> **Principle 79** — Build financial security, not financial excess

Two findings frame this principle, and the second changes the first. Kahneman and Deaton analysed more than 450,000 US survey responses and found that emotional well-being "rises with log income, but there is no further progress beyond an annual income of ~$75,000", while, in their words, "Low income exacerbates the emotional pain associated with such misfortunes as divorce, ill health, and being alone" ([PNAS 107, 2010](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1011492107)).

(Kahneman and Deaton, 2010; Killingsworth et al., 2023, with the caveat that the exact plateau is contested.)

### The plateau did not hold up

The 2023 adversarial reanalysis by Killingsworth, Kahneman and Mellers "confirmed the flattening pattern only for the least happy people", and found that "Happiness increases steadily with log(income) among happier people, and even accelerates in the happiest group" ([PNAS 120, 2023, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36857342/)). So the claim that extra wealth does nothing for well-being is not supported. What the data support is that the cost of having too little is real and sharp.

The principle therefore rests on two things the evidence handles well: low income makes misfortune hurt more, and a buffer removes that. Whether you should want more than a buffer is a question about values, and the studies do not settle it.

**Plain words**
Money protects you from some kinds of suffering, and having too little makes every other problem worse. The research does not say more money stops helping at a fixed number. The principle is a choice: make sure the basics and a cushion are covered first, and then ask what extra money would actually be for.

**For example**
Work out the monthly cost of rent, food, transport and health, then count the months of that you have saved. Treat getting that number to a level you can live with as the first goal, and write down what any money beyond it is for before chasing it.
