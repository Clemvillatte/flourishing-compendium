> **Principle 73** — Learn co-regulation: affect is regulated with others, not just within

Butler and Randall give the term a precise definition rather than a loose one. They propose coregulation as the process by which relationship partners form a dyadic emotional system involving an oscillating pattern of affective arousal and dampening. They note the term has been used loosely in the literature, and propose fixing it with this operational definition ([Butler & Randall, 2013, Emotion Review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1754073912451630)).

> Coregulation refers to the process by which relationship partners form a dyadic emotional system involving an oscillating pattern of affective arousal and dampening that dynamically maintains an optimal emotional state.
>
> — Butler and Randall, 2013, Emotion Review 5(2) ([Sage](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1754073912451630))

**Plain words** The definition treats emotional regulation as something that happens between people, not only inside one person. What a person does in interaction changes what the other person's affect does, and the definition describes the two as forming one system.

For example (imagined): a panicked caller and a friend who says, slowly, that they should work out exactly what is happening. That is an attempt at interpersonal regulation. Whether it becomes the bidirectional process the definition describes would depend on both partners adjusting.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Co-regulation of affect in relationships|Co-regulation of affect in relationships]]**, partners and close others regulate each other's affect through attunement, responsiveness, and synchronisation (Butler & Gross, 2009; English & Eldreda, 2020)
- **Social support as affect regulation**, perceived support reduces affective reactivity to stressors (Cohen & Wills, 1985)

## Practice

- When you are emotionally activated, one thing that can help is a calm, attuned person. Do not treat needing co-regulation as a failure of self-regulation.
