> **Principle 35** — Recognise and work with mind-wandering: it is not the enemy

Mind-wandering — attention drifting from the task to unrelated thoughts — is normal and universal (roughly 50% of waking hours). It has costs (reduced performance, lower mood) but also benefits (creativity, planning, memory consolidation). The goal is not elimination but skilful management.

**Plain words** Minds wander — roughly half of waking life, by the research. It's not a defect; it's a mode. The goal isn't zero wandering, it's knowing when you want it and when you don't. Treat it like daydreaming's engine, not your enemy.

*For example:* The student who condemns themselves for drifting during the lecture — everyone drifts. The ones who do well just notice the drift and come back, forty times an hour. That's the whole class.
## Psychology parallels

- **Mind-wandering occupies roughly half of waking hours**, experience-sampling studies show people mind-wander about 47% of the time (Killingsworth & Gilbert, 2010; Kane et al., 2007)
- **[[Mind wandering|Mind-wandering has both costs and benefits]]**, costs: distraction, reduced performance, lower mood; benefits: creativity, planning, prospective memory (Mooneyham & Schooler, 2013; Baird et al., 2012)
- **A wandering mind is an unhappy mind (contested magnitude)**, people are less happy when mind-wandering, but the effect size and causal direction are debated (Killingsworth & Gilbert, 2010; 2019 replication)

## Practice

- Do not fight mind-wandering. Notice it. If it is productive (planning, creativity), allow it. If it is rumination or distraction, gently redirect. Use "productive mind-wandering" times (walks, showers) deliberately.
