> **Principle 7** — Grade your effort honestly

Patanjali notes that results come at different speeds and gives a scale for why. Sutra 1.21 says:

> For the keenly intense, [concentration] is near.

(Yoga Sutras 1.21, trans. James Haughton Woods, *The Yoga-System of Patanjali*, Harvard Oriental Series 17, 1914, pp. 47-48, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/yogasystemofpata00wooduoft).)

The next sutra refines it: "Because [this keenness] is gentle or moderate or keen, there is a [concentration] superior even to this [near kind]" (1.22). The commentary explains that attainment is "near to him who follows the vehement method and is of mildly keen intensity; still more near to him who is of moderately keen intensity; and most near to him who is of vehemently keen intensity". It uses the scale to answer an objection: if the means are the same, why do yogins succeed at different speeds? The reply is that the methods are gentle, moderate or vehement, "through the force of subliminal-impressions and the invisible-influences (adrda) of previous births".

So the text grades the intensity of method and ties the speed of results to it, with the cause placed partly in past conditioning. Two points go past it. The text does not say to assess yourself, and "accurate self-assessment" as a practice is this page's own inference. Matching practice intensity to the difficulty of your current mind is also this page's own suggestion, and the tradition's scale is not about difficulty.

**Plain words**

Some people get there sooner, and the text says it depends on how intense the practice is. You can use that by counting honestly what you do, with neither pride nor scolding, and then deciding whether to do more.

**For example**

A man says he meditates daily. He counts his calendar and finds eight sittings in the last month. He calls it gentle practice, which is true, and picks four sittings a week to run as a month-long trial.
