> **Principle 14** — Preserve your childlike vitality

Daodejing 55 names the infant as the model of vitality at its fullest: "He who has in himself abundantly the attributes (of the Tao) is like an infant." The chapter's evidence is bodily:

> "(The infant's) bones are weak and its sinews soft, but yet its grasp is firm. It knows not yet the union of male and female, and yet its virile member may be excited;--showing the perfection of its physical essence. All day long it will cry without its throat becoming hoarse;--showing the harmony (in its constitution)." (Daodejing 55, [Legge](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

The harmony is the point: to know it, the chapter says, is to know the unchanging Tao. Chapter 10 gives the practitioner's training: "When one gives undivided attention to the (vital) breath, and brings it to the utmost degree of pliancy, he can become as a (tender) babe." Chapter 28 gives the return: "The simple child again, free from all stains." The warning in chapter 55 runs against adult force: "Where the mind makes the vital breath to burn, (False) is the strength, (and o'er it we should mourn.)" When things have become strong, they "become old, which may be said to be contrary to the Tao."

(DDJ 10, 28, 55.)

**Plain words** The chapter's picture of vitality is a baby who spends its voice all day and ends the day none the worse. Your own version of the question is about cost. Somewhere in your week there is an activity you do by pushing: you finish it depleted and need to recover from it. The chapter's alternative is not rest but rhythm, doing the thing at a pace that does not collect that debt.

*For example:* Give one hour this week to something with no outcome attached: a walk with no destination, a game, building something useless. Watch what your attention does when nothing is being collected from it.
