> **Principle 49** — Learn to use breath as an attentional anchor: always available, always stabilising

The breath is the standard anchor in attention training for a practical reason: it is always happening, so it can be found at any moment without equipment. The opened review of slow-breathing studies summarized the physiological side: slow breathing practices modify autonomic and cerebral variables, with associated psychological outputs including increased comfort and relaxation. (Zaccaro et al., 2018.)

> "Slow breathing may be a self-regulation tool with a role in enhancing autonomic, cerebral, and psychological flexibility." (Zaccaro et al., 2018, [Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 12:353](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353/full))

The review covers slow breathing techniques specifically, not every use of the breath as an anchor. The stabilising claim in this principle's title is the compendium's reading, grounded in the practice traditions cited below rather than in a controlled trial of anchoring. What the opened source supports is narrower: deliberate slow breathing is a technique with measurable physiological correlates, and the breath itself is the most portable object to attend to. (Zaccaro et al., 2018.)

**Plain words**
An anchor is something you can return to when the mind moves. The breath qualifies because it goes with you: no app, no prop, no special place. Feeling one breath does not stop the storm; it gives you a place to stand while it blows.

*For example:* Before a difficult phone call, three slow breaths with attention on the feeling of the air at the nostrils. The call is still difficult; the anchor is there for the duration of it.

## Psychology parallels

- **Breath-focused attention stabilises and calms**, attending to breath reduces mind-wandering and activates parasympathetic regulation (Tang et al., 2015; Zaccaro et al., 2018)
- **Slow breathing regulates autonomic nervous system**, deliberate slow breathing activates parasympathetic and reduces arousal (Lehrer & Gevirtz, 2014; Zaccaro et al., 2018)

- **Breath as life force** (Yoga, pranayama)

## Practice

- Use breath as your primary attentional anchor. In meditation, during work, before difficult tasks: attend to the breath. Notice its natural rhythm. Use slow, deliberate breathing to calm and focus.
