> **Principle 49** — Learn to use breath as an attentional anchor: always available, always stabilising

Zaccaro and colleagues reviewed slow-breathing techniques and their physiological and psychological correlates. This evidence concerns deliberately changing breathing rate. (Zaccaro et al., 2018.)

> "Slow breathing techniques promote autonomic changes increasing Heart Rate Variability and Respiratory Sinus Arrhythmia paralleled by Central Nervous System (CNS) activity modifications." (Zaccaro et al., 2018, [Frontiers in Human Neuroscience 12:353](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2018.00353/full))

The compendium's anchoring exercise uses sensations of ordinary breathing as an object of attention. Effects reported for paced breathing should not be assumed for simply noticing an unaltered breath.

**Plain words**
Choose a sensation of ordinary breathing, such as air moving at the nostrils. If attention wanders, return to that sensation. Observe your experience without forcing a slower rhythm or requiring yourself to become calm.

*For example:* Imagine pausing before a call to notice one comfortable breath, then making the call without expecting the exercise to change how it goes.

## Psychology parallels

- **Deliberately slowed breathing has reported autonomic correlates**, the review reports changes in heart rate variability and respiratory sinus arrhythmia during slow breathing techniques (Zaccaro et al., 2018)
- **Reported psychological outputs during paced breathing**, the studies reviewed report increased comfort and relaxation and reduced symptoms of arousal and anxiety during paced breathing (Zaccaro et al., 2018)

## Practice

- As a compendium exercise, use the sensation of ordinary breathing as the object of attention, in meditation, during work, or before difficult tasks. The exercise is to notice the breath as it is and return to it when attention moves; it is distinct from deliberately slowing the breath.
