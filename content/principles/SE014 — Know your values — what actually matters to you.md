> **Principle 14** — Know your values: what actually matters to you

Socrates puts the daily examination of virtue at the center of the good life, and makes the alternative explicit:

> the greatest good of man is daily to converse about virtue, and all that concerning which you hear me examining myself and others, and that the life which is unexamined is not worth living

(Plato, Apology 38a, Jowett translation; [MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html))

Socrates connects a worthwhile life with continuing discussion of virtue and examination of oneself and others. One application is to name a priority and ask why you consider it worth pursuing.

**Plain words**
Pick something you say matters to you. Write down your reasons for pursuing it and a reason someone might question it. Consider whether you still endorse that priority, including priorities learned from family or community.

*For example:* Imagine considering a promotion. You list the reasons for accepting or declining, then ask which priorities you are willing to defend.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[Values clarification|Values clarification in ACT]]**, identifying and clarifying values is a core ACT process (Hayes et al., 2011; Wilson et al., 2010)
- **Values and well-being**, living in accord with values predicts well-being and life satisfaction (Sheldon & Elliott, 1999; Sheldon et al., 2004)
- **[[Self-concordance theory|Self-concordance theory]]**, goals aligned with values are pursued more effectively and predict well-being (Sheldon & Elliot, 1999)

## Philosophy parallels

- **Know what you value** (Socrates, Apology)
- **[[The examined life|The examined life]] requires examined values** (Socrates)
- **[[Eudaimonia|Eudaimonia as living in accord with virtue]]** (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics)

## Practice

Examine competing priorities: for each, write your reasons for pursuing it and a reason someone might question it, and consider whether you still endorse it.
