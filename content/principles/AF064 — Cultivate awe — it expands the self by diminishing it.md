> **Principle 64** — Cultivate awe: it expands the self by diminishing it

Keltner and Haidt hold that two appraisals are central to every clear case of awe: "perceived vastness, and a need for accommodation, defined as an inability to assimilate an experience into current mental structures" ([Keltner & Haidt, 2003](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29715721/)). Piff and colleagues add a claim about the self:

> Awe is an emotional response to perceptually vast stimuli that transcend current frames of reference.
>
> Piff et al., Journal of Personality and Social Psychology (2015)

Across five studies (N = 2,078), people prone to awe were more generous in an economic game, and inducing awe increased ethical decision-making, generosity and prosocial values. In a naturalistic test, standing in a grove of towering trees "enhanced prosocial helping behavior and decreased entitlement". The effects were explained in part by feelings of a "small self" ([Piff et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25984788/)).

The evidence for lasting benefit is narrower. In an eight-week randomized study of 60 older adults, weekly awe walks raised daily prosocial positive emotions and lowered daily distress, yet "postintervention anxiety, depression, and life satisfaction did not change from baseline in either group" ([Sturm et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32955293/)).

(Keltner & Haidt, 2003; Piff et al., 2015; Sturm et al., 2022.)

**Plain words** Awe is the feeling of meeting something so big that your usual way of making sense of things does not quite hold. In that moment your own worries shrink, and studies find people then act more generously. It is a short-term shift, and the walks study did not find it changed clinical measures of mood.

For example (imagined): you spend ten minutes outdoors looking at the night sky, or standing under old trees, and notice how small your to-do list feels. Treat it as a reset of perspective, and do not expect it to treat anxiety or depression.

## Psychology parallels

- **Awe reduces self-focus and increases prosocial behaviour**, awe is associated with small-self effect and increased generosity (Piff et al., 2015)
- **Awe walks raised daily prosocial emotions and lowered daily distress in older adults**, though anxiety, depression and life satisfaction did not change from baseline (Sturm et al., 2022)
- **Awe promotes humility**, awe-prone people were rated as more humble by friends (Stellar et al., 2018)

## Practice

- Seek out experiences of vastness — nature, art, music, the night sky, extraordinary human achievement. Let yourself feel small in a way that is liberating rather than diminishing.
