> **Principle 99** — Recognise political justice (politikon dikaion)

Aristotle's term for justice inside a political community is politikon dikaion. He splits it in two:

> "Of political justice part is natural, part legal, natural, that which everywhere has the same force and does not exist by people's thinking this or that; legal, that which is originally indifferent, but when it has been laid down is not indifferent"

His examples of the legal part are a prisoner's ransom set at a mina and the rule that a goat, not two sheep, be sacrificed (NE V.7). He also says who the term covers: people who are "free and either proportionately or arithmetically equal" and who share a life aimed at self-sufficiency (NE V.6).

### Why ethics reaches into politics

In the opening chapter of the Ethics, Aristotle says politics "legislates as to what we are to do and what we are to abstain from", so its end "must include those of the others, so that this end must be the good for man" (NE I.2). Read with V.7, this puts the laws a community has laid down inside the question of how its members can live well.

(NE I.2, V.6, V.7, W. D. Ross translation.)

**Plain words** A community's rules come in two kinds. Some track something that holds whatever people happen to think. Others are agreements that could have gone another way, like a ransom price or the side of the road you drive on, and they bind once they are set. Either way, the rules around you shape how hard a good life is to reach. So asking how you are doing includes asking under which rules you are doing it, and what part of them you could change.

For example: Imagined: two towns with equally decent residents, one where a reliable bus runs past the clinic and the school, one where it does not. The first town's residents find it easier to get care and education, whatever their personal virtue.
