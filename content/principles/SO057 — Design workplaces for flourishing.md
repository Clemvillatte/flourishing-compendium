> **Principle 57** — Design workplaces for flourishing

> Results of a study of 51 work teams in a manufacturing company [...] show that team psychological safety is associated with learning behavior, but team efficacy is not, when controlling for team psychological safety. As predicted, learning behavior mediates between team psychological safety and team performance.

(Edmondson, "Psychological Safety and Learning Behavior in Work Teams," Administrative Science Quarterly 44(2), 1999, 350-383, abstract; [JSTOR reprint copy](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Group_Performance/Edmondson%20Psychological%20safety.pdf).)

Edmondson defines the term as "a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking" (abstract; also p. 354). In her sample the route to better performance ran through learning behavior, which she describes as "asking questions, seeking feedback, experimenting, reflecting on results, and discussing errors or unexpected outcomes of actions." This supports the entry's example of a team where saying "I don't know" is safe, though the study measured teams in one manufacturing firm and did not test other workplaces.

The other claims in this entry were not checked. These are that autonomy support, work-life balance and fair pay "predict well-being, productivity, and retention," and that high demand with low control and low support is the harmful combination (Karasek, Deci and Ryan, Warr and the job demands-resources model). Treat them as the entry's inherited claims.

**Plain words** People learn more in a team where speaking up is safe. A team where admitting an error costs you will hide errors. That is a design choice a manager can make, and in this study it showed up in how the teams learned and performed.

*For example:* One team lets a new member say "I don't know how this works" and gets help. In another, the same sentence marks that person as weak, and they stop asking.

## Practice

- In management, provide autonomy, clear expectations, and support. In job design, match demands to resources. In career choice, weigh autonomy and purpose alongside pay.
