> **Principle 73** — Guard the free person's cheerfulness under loss

In the note to IV.73, Elwes's Spinoza lists what strength looks like: "he that is strong hates no man, is angry with no man, envies no man, is indignant with no man, despises no man, and least of all things is proud." ([Ethics IV, P73 note](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3800/pg3800.txt)) Envy itself is defined sharply in Part III: "Envy is hatred, in so far as it induces a man to be pained by another's good fortune, and to rejoice in another's evil fortune." ([Ethics III, Definitions of the Emotions, XXIII](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3800/pg3800.txt)) Both directions of the comparison reflex — the sting at their windfall, the spark at their faceplant — are the one error. And envy makes a person dangerous to be around, Spinoza adds: those in its grip "are at variance, and are therefore to be feared in proportion, as they are more powerful than their fellows." ([Ethics IV, Appendix X](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3800/pg3800.txt))

When comparison bites, convert it once into a question: what specifically do I want to build, and does their having theirs block it?

**Plain words** The sting at a friend's windfall and the spark at a rival's stumble are the same mistake pointed two ways. Their outcome is not a measurement of you. Ask what you actually want to build — then check whether their having theirs stops you.

*For example:* A friend's funding gets announced the same week your application fails. Notice the pull toward both bitterness and pretend-generosity. One question dissolves most of it: did their grant remove anything from your life, or just from your ranking?
