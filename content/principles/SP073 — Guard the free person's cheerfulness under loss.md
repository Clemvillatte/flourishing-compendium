> **Principle 73** — Guard the free person's cheerfulness under loss

Among the appendix's signs is one of the most quietly radical claims in practical philosophy: the free person is not made sad by another's good fortune, nor made glad by another's loss. Envy and schadenfreude are the same error in two directions, reading another's power as a subtraction from one's own. The remedy is the monism applied socially: in one system, another's increase of power is a potential ally, not a threat. Practically, when comparison bites, convert it once into a question: what specifically do I want to build, and does their having theirs block it?

(Ethics IV, Appendix, the envy and gloating signs; Part III, the definitions of envy and mockery.)

**Plain words** The free person is not made sad by another's good fortune, nor made glad by another's loss — envy and schadenfreude are the same error in opposite directions: rating yourself against weather. Guard the cheerfulness under loss; the other person's outcome is not a fact about you.

*For example:* The friend's windfall that used to sting and the rival's faceplant that used to delight — and the year someone trains both reflexes out. Not suppression; the correction of a measurement error. Their fortune was never data about your life.
