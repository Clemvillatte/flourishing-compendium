> **Principle 18** — Practice practical wisdom: judgment over rules

Aristotle's account of practical wisdom (phronesis) begins with its definition: "Practical wisdom, then, must be a reasoned and true state of capacity to act with regard to human goods." Its mark is deliberation: "Now it is thought to be the mark of a man of practical wisdom to be able to deliberate well about what is good and expedient for himself, not in some particular respect, e.g. about what sorts of thing conduce to health or to strength, but about what sorts of thing conduce to the good life in general."

> Practical wisdom, then, must be a reasoned and true state of capacity to act with regard to human goods. ... Now it is thought to be the mark of a man of practical wisdom to be able to deliberate well about what is good and expedient for himself, not in some particular respect, e.g. about what sorts of thing conduce to health or to strength, but about what sorts of thing conduce to the good life in general.
>
> (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book VI, trans. Ross)

The scope line carries the principle: rules address the particulars (health, strength, what conduces), while judgment addresses the whole. A rule cannot see the situation it lands in; that seeing is the trained capacity. The compendium's reading is to cultivate the deliberation and keep the rules as its instruments.

(Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book VI.)

**Plain words**
Rules are written for the average case, and the actual case never quite is the average one. The capacity pointed at here is reading the situation in front of you and choosing. The practice: when you follow a rule, notice what the rule is for. The noticing is the part that transfers.

*For example:* The veteran nurse who ignores the protocol's letter and catches the sepsis the checklist missed — that's judgment, and it's why she's allowed near the checklist.
