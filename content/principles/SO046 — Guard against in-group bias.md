> **Principle 46** — Guard against in-group bias

Guard against in-group bias — the tendency to favor one's own group and derogate others. The minimal-group paradigm (Tajfel et al., 1971) showed that even arbitrary group assignments produce bias. The bias operates through perception (in-group homogeneity effect), evaluation (in-group favoritism), and behavior (discrimination). The adaptive stance is deliberate debiasing.

The original report states the finding plainly:

> "In the first series of experiments, it was found that the subjects favoured their own group in the distribution of real rewards and penalities in a situation in which nothing but the variable of fairly irrelevant classification distinguished between the ingroup and the outgroup." (Tajfel, Billig, Bundy & Flament, 1971, [abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.2420010202))

The sharper result was what the boys gave up for the difference itself: "the clearest effect on the distribution of rewards was due to the subjects' attempt to achieve a maximum difference between the ingroup and the outgroup even at the price of sacrificing other 'objective' advantages" (same abstract). Bias for its own sake is the finding; that is why the guard has to be deliberate.

**Plain words** In-group bias fires even for arbitrary groups — random assignment is enough; the experiments prove it. The bias operates through perception, not malice: your group's flaws look situational, theirs look essential. Knowing the mechanism is most of the defence.

*For example:* "We're under pressure" versus "they're always sloppy" — the same missed deadline, two different explanations, assigned by team colours. That's the bias, running quietly. Everyone's doing it, which is why you check yourself first.

## Practice
