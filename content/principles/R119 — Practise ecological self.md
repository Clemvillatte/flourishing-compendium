> **Principle 381** — Practise ecological self

Arne Naess set out the ecological self in a 1986 lecture, "Self-realization: an ecological approach to being in the world". He begins: "We underestimate ourself" and "tend to confuse our 'self' with the narrow ego". With "sufficient comprehensive (all-sided) maturity, we cannot help but 'identify' our self with all living beings". His one-sentence definition is "The ecological self of a person is that with which this person identifies" (pp. 225-227). The self is relational from the start: "We may be said to be in, and of, Nature from the very beginning of our selves" (p. 226).

His illustrations are plain. Watching a flea die in acid under his microscope, he says his empathy "was a process of identification: that 'I saw myself in the flea'". In court, a Sami man called a river "part of himself" (pp. 227, 231). On enrichment, he writes: "You are much greater, deeper, generous and capable of more dignity and joy than you think!" (p. 235). He adds that "The requisite care flows naturally if the 'self' is widened and deepened so that protection of free nature is felt and conceived as protection of ourselves" (p. 236). (all quotations from [Naess 1995](https://scispace.com/pdf/self-realization-an-ecological-approach-to-being-in-the-jarqxwpglv.pdf)). I did not open the 1989 or 2008 works listed in the principle.

**Plain words**
Ask what you identify with: a street, a river, a landscape, other living things. What you identify with is part of your wider self, and harm to it can feel like harm to you. Naess argues that care follows from that wider identification without needing to be forced.

**For example**
You have walked the same stretch of creek for years. When a developer proposes clearing it, you notice you are reacting as if something of yours is at stake, and you take that feeling seriously when you write to the council.
