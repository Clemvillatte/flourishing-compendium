> **Principle 83** — Recognise affect's sociocultural interfaces: culture shapes what you feel and how

The principle says culture "determines" which emotions are idealised and stigmatised. The research supports a more specific claim.

### Ideal affect

Tsai (2007) distinguishes actual affect, "the affective states that people actually feel", from ideal affect, "the affective states that people ideally want to feel". She compares American and East Asian cultures, "which differ in their valuation of high-arousal positive affective states (e.g., excitement, enthusiasm) and low-arousal positive affective states (e.g., calm, peace-fulness)" ([abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1745-6916.2007.00043.x)). A later paper summarising Tsai, Knutson and Fung (2006) adds the key qualifier: "temperament and personality traits, not cultural values, predicted actual affect better than cultural values, whereas cultural values predicted ideal affect better" ([review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/00220221221084236)). So culture shapes what people want to feel more than what they feel.

### Expressing and suppressing

Butler, Lee and Gross (2007) found that suppression "was associated with increased negative emotions and hostile behaviors in European Americans", while it "reduced or reversed negativity and hostility in Asian Americans who held Asian values" (as described in a [review of coping and emotion regulation](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6628463/)). The picture is not settled: another paper notes that Roberts et al. (2008) found that "culture failed to moderate the effect of emotion suppression" ([Universality vs. cultural specificity](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6379289/)).

### What the sources do not show

The sources describe group averages, so they do not license assumptions about an individual. The Wierzbicka (1999), Butler and Gross (2009) and Soto et al. (2011) citations on this page have not been checked. The Butler study described above is the 2007 paper with Lee. "Treating them as universal leads to misjudgement" is a reasonable inference, not a finding.

**Plain words** Cultures differ in which feelings people want to have, and in what it means to show or hold back a feeling. Calm may be prized in one place and excitement in another. Holding back anger may damage a relationship in one setting and protect it in another, though not every study agrees. Before you judge a feeling or its display, ask what norm you are applying.

*For example:* Imagine a father whose family reads his calm as coldness. If his culture treats quiet provision as care, then his family and he may be using different yardsticks for the same behaviour. This is a possibility the research makes plausible, not a documented case.

## Psychology parallels

- **Ideal affect varies across cultures**, European Americans idealise high-arousal positive affect; East Asians idealise low-arousal positive affect (Tsai, 2007)
- **Emotion norms and suppression's costs vary across cultures**, suppression is maladaptive in Western but not necessarily East Asian contexts (Butler & Gross, 2009; Butler, Lee & Gross, 2007; Soto et al., 2011)
- **Affective concepts vary across languages and cultures**, the emotion vocabulary and categories available differ (Wierzbicka, 1999)

## Practice

- When you judge your own or others' affect as "wrong" or "too much," check whether you are applying a culturally specific norm. What is "appropriate" varies.
