> **Principle 83** — Recognise affect's sociocultural interfaces: culture shapes what you feel and how

Culture determines which emotions are idealised, which are stigmatised, how they are expressed, and what they mean. Your culture's affective norms are not universal; treating them as such leads to misjudgement of self and others.

**Plain words** Culture determines which emotions are idealised, stigmatised, expressed, and what they mean. Your culture's affective norms are not universal. Read affect through the local lens — including your own family's culture — before grading it.

*For example:* The stoic father whose family reads his calm as coldness — and the discovery that in his culture, quiet provision IS the love language. Both were decoding correctly by different rubrics. The translation, not the feeling, was missing.
## Psychology parallels

- **Ideal affect varies across cultures**, European Americans idealise high-arousal positive affect; East Asians idealise low-arousal positive affect (Tsai, 2007)
- **Emotion norms and suppression's costs vary across cultures**, suppression is maladaptive in Western but not necessarily East Asian contexts (Butler & Gross, 2009; Soto et al., 2011)
- **Affective concepts vary across languages and cultures**, the emotion vocabulary and categories available differ (Wierzbicka, 1999)

## Practice

- When you judge your own or others' affect as "wrong" or "too much," check whether you are applying a culturally specific norm. What is "appropriate" varies.
