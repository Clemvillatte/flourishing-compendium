> **Principle 48** — Learn open monitoring meditation: awareness without narrow focus

Lutz, Slagter, Dunne and Davidson separate two styles of meditation. Focused attention "entails the voluntary focusing of attention on a chosen object", while open monitoring "involves non-reactive monitoring of the content of experience from moment to moment" ([Lutz et al., 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2693206/)).

> One aims to remain only in the monitoring state, attentive moment by moment to anything that occurs in experience without focusing on any explicit object.
>
> Lutz et al., Trends in Cognitive Sciences (2008), Box 2

They also describe the order of learning: open monitoring practices share "the initial use of FA training to calm the mind and reduce distractions". The central aim is "to gain a clear reflexive awareness of the usually implicit features of one's mental life". The neuroscience is early. In their words, "little is known about the neurophysiological processes involved in meditation", and findings on open monitoring "provide preliminary support" for their ideas. A later review agrees that "more methodologically rigorous studies are required" ([Tang et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25783612/)).

(Lutz et al., 2008.)

**Plain words** Most meditation starts by holding attention on one thing, such as the breath. Open monitoring is the next stage. You stop holding on to any one thing and notice whatever comes up: a sound, a worry, a mood. The goal is to see the workings of your own mind more clearly, and not to empty it. It is usually learned after some focused practice has steadied the mind.

For example (imagined): after several weeks of breath-focus practice, you sit for ten minutes with no anchor, noticing thoughts and sounds as they arise and, when you catch yourself carried away by one, simply noticing that too.

## Psychology parallels

- **Open monitoring cultivates a reflexive awareness of mental life**, the capacity to notice the contents of experience without a narrow focus (Lutz et al., 2008)
- **Open monitoring may reduce "getting stuck" on a target**, an early finding in studies of intensive OM training (Lutz et al., 2008); a two-week general mindfulness course reduced mind wandering and raised working memory capacity (Mrazek et al., 2013)

## Practice

- Practise open monitoring: 10-20 minutes, sitting with open awareness. Notice whatever arises (thought, sensation, sound, emotion) without following or judging. Let it pass. Notice the noticing.
