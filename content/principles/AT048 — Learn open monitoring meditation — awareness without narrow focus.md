> **Principle 48** — Learn open monitoring meditation: awareness without narrow focus

Lutz, Slagter, Dunne and Davidson separate two styles of meditation. Focused attention "entails the voluntary focusing of attention on a chosen object", while open monitoring "involves non-reactive monitoring of the content of experience from moment to moment" ([Lutz et al., 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2693206/)).

> One aims to remain only in the monitoring state, attentive moment by moment to anything that occurs in experience without focusing on any explicit object.
>
> Lutz et al., Trends in Cognitive Sciences (2008), Box 2

They also describe the order of learning: open monitoring practices share "the initial use of FA training to calm the mind and reduce distractions". The central aim is "to gain a clear reflexive awareness of the usually implicit features of one's mental life". The neuroscience is early. In their words, "little is known about the neurophysiological processes involved in meditation", and findings on open monitoring "provide preliminary support" for a specific prediction about its attentional pattern. A later review agrees that "more methodologically rigorous studies are required" ([Tang et al., 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25783612/)).

(Lutz et al., 2008.)

**Plain words** In the practices described by this review, focused attention can prepare the way for open monitoring. The emphasis shifts from returning to one chosen object to noticing experience without making any object the centre. A sound, a worry or a mood can be noticed in that way. This describes a practice and its aim; it does not establish one learning sequence or timetable for every meditation tradition.

For example: a practitioner familiar with focused attention notices a sound and then a thought, without deliberately selecting either as the next object to hold. The review gives no fixed number of weeks after which this transition must happen.

## Psychology parallels

- **Open monitoring cultivates a reflexive awareness of mental life**, the capacity to notice the contents of experience without a narrow focus (Lutz et al., 2008)
- **Open monitoring may reduce "getting stuck" on a target**, an early finding in studies of intensive OM training (Lutz et al., 2008); a two-week general mindfulness course reduced mind wandering and raised working memory capacity (Mrazek et al., 2013)

## Practice

- Practise open monitoring: 10-20 minutes, sitting with open awareness. Notice whatever arises (thought, sensation, sound, emotion) without following or judging. Let it pass. Notice the noticing.
