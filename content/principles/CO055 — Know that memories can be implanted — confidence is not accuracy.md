> **Principle 55** — Know that memories can be implanted: confidence is not accuracy

In the study that made this point famous, Loftus and Pickrell gave participants three true childhood events and one invented one, a story of being lost in a shopping mall. After a survey and two interviews, 25% of participants (n = 6) reported a false memory of getting lost. The authors treated that as an existence proof: "people can be led to believe that entire events happened to them after suggestions to that effect" (Loftus and Pickrell, 1995, p. 723, as quoted in [Murphy et al., 2023](https://cora.ucc.ie/server/api/core/bitstreams/075c236a-356f-4694-94c5-9500b6c148d3/content)).

A preregistered replication with five times the sample (N = 123) coded 35% of participants as reporting a false memory, most of them partial (27%) and some full (8%). In the extension, mock jurors "were also highly likely to believe the fabricated event had occurred and that the participant was truly remembering the event" (Murphy et al., 2023, abstract and discussion, [Memory 31(6), 818-830](https://cora.ucc.ie/server/api/core/bitstreams/075c236a-356f-4694-94c5-9500b6c148d3/content)).

The slogan "confidence is not accuracy" needs care. Wixted and Wells found that under "pristine testing conditions" (an initial test, a fair lineup, no influence from the administrator), "high-confidence suspect identifications are remarkably accurate." When conditions are poorer, "the accuracy of even a high-confidence suspect ID is seriously compromised" (Wixted and Wells, 2017, abstract, [Psychological Science in the Public Interest 18](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28395650/)). So confidence is a decent guide to a fresh, uncontaminated memory and a poor one after suggestion.

**Plain words**
A vivid, confident memory can still be wrong, especially if it has been rehearsed, discussed or suggested to you. Be slow to act on such a memory against someone, and look for records.

**For example**
A family story says you once got lost in a department store. Relatives tell it every holiday, and you start to "see" the aisles. The vividness may only reflect how often the story has been told.

## Psychology parallels

- **[[False memory implantation (Loftus)|False memory implantation (Loftus)]]**, suggestive techniques can create detailed false memories (Loftus & Pickrell, 1995; Shaw & Porter, 2015)
- **[[Misinformation effect|Misinformation effect]]**, exposure to misleading information after an event alters memory for the event (Loftus & Palmer, 1974; Loftus, 2005)
- **Confidence does not equal accuracy**, memory confidence is weakly related to memory accuracy (Wells et al., 2000)

## Practice

- Do not treat vividness or confidence as evidence of accuracy. When a memory is important (for a decision, a judgment, a legal matter), seek external corroboration.
