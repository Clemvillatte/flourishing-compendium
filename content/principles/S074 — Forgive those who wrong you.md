> **Principle 196** — Forgive those who wrong you

Marcus begins the day by expecting the ungrateful, the arrogant, the deceitful, and the envious, and explains them by one fact: all of it happens to them "by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil" ([Meditations II.1, George Long translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt)). Epictetus draws the consequence for how to respond, in the quotation below.

> They are mistaken about good and evil. Ought we then to be angry with them, or to pity them? But show them their error, and you will see how they desist from their errors.

Epictetus, Discourses I.18, [George Long translation](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/epictetus/discourses/george-long/text/book-1).

The same teaching runs through the Meditations. Marcus calls it peculiar to man to love even those who do wrong, since they are kinsmen who "do wrong through ignorance and unintentionally" and have "not made thy ruling faculty worse than it was before" ([Meditations VII.22](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt)). When able, the instruction is to "correct by teaching those who do wrong"; when not, to remember "that indulgence is given to thee for this purpose" ([Meditations IX.11](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt)). Every soul is "involuntarily deprived" of truth, a point to bear "constantly in mind", "for thus thou wilt be more gentle towards all" ([Meditations VII.63](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt)).

(Med. II.1, VII.22, VII.63, IX.11; Disc. I.18.)

### Plain words

When someone wrongs you, the Stoic instruction is to look first at what they believed was good. Both texts describe wrongdoing as a mistake about good and evil rather than a free choice of evil; Epictetus puts the response as a choice between anger and pity ([Discourses I.18](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/epictetus/discourses/george-long/text/book-1)). The wrong is not thereby excused: the instruction to correct it by teaching assumes it was a wrong ([Meditations IX.11](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt)).

### For example

As a modern application, suppose a colleague takes credit for your work. The passages' questions: what did they believe they needed, and are you able to show them the error? If yes, the instruction is to teach; if not, the instruction is indulgence. Neither response requires pretending the act was fair ([Meditations IX.11](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt)).
