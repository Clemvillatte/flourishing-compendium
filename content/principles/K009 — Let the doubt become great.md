> **Principle 9** — Let the doubt become great

Chan master Sheng Yen describes "great doubt" (dayiqing) as "an inner uneasiness or anxiety", a "deep tension caused by the feeling that there is something essential that we need to know—that we must know—but that we don't know". He says it is "not simple agnosticism or skepticism" but "a state of all-consuming questioning that, at its deepest, is irresistible and relentless, admitting no solution other than one that totally gets to the bottom of the matter" (Sheng Yen, Hoofprint of the Ox, p. 118). A small doubt passes quickly, but in major doubt "the doubt mass will continue to expand, consuming everything until there is nothing left but total doubt". The saying he cites is "Small doubt, shallow enlightenment; great doubt, deep enlightenment; no doubt, no enlightenment" (same page).

Bret Davis, writing on Rinzai kōan practice, makes the same point: "The Great Doubt is not a skeptical stance taken toward an object or doctrine; it is rather a matter of being wholeheartedly plunged into the issue we are trying to resolve at the very heart of our practice" (Davis, Zen Pathways, p. 296). His advice is to "put it in the pit of your stomach and sit with it, meditate on it with your whole being and not just with your head" (p. 297). I found no "ball of doubt" wording in the Nishitani chapters I opened, so I do not attribute it to him.

**Plain words**
Great doubt is not indecision. It is a question about your life that you cannot put down, and you keep it with you through the day instead of rushing to settle it.

**For example**
Suppose a long-held belief about yourself ("I am the reliable one") stops fitting. Rather than adopting a new label by Friday, you carry the question for weeks.
