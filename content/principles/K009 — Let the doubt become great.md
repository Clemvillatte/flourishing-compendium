> **Principle 9** — Let the doubt become great

The Zen tradition's "great doubt" (daigi) is not ordinary hesitation but a systematic doubt about the self and its certainties, cultivated until it becomes, in Nishitani's phrase, a ball of doubt that consumes everything else. The instruction is counterintuitive for anyone trained to resolve doubt quickly: in this tradition, doubt is the engine, and a comfortable certainty is the obstacle. Practically, when a settled conviction about yourself comes loose ("I am a person who..."), let it stay loose for a while before replacing it; the replacement chosen in haste is usually the old one in new clothes.

(Nishitani, Religion and Nothingness; the Zen tradition of the koan as doubt-generator.)

**Plain words** In Zen, great doubt isn't being unsure about lunch. It's a deep questioning of the self and everything you take for certain, kept alive until it swallows everything else. Doubt here is fuel, and comfortable certainty is what gets in the way.

*For example:* At forty, someone who has always known what they wanted asks, with no drama, "What if I never knew?" and doesn't hurry to answer.
