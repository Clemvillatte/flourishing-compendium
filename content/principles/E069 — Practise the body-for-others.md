> **Principle 461** — Practise the body-for-others

Sartre gives the body three dimensions. "I exist my body: this is its first dimension of being. My body is utilized and known by the Other: this is its second dimension." The third is: "I exist for myself as a body known by the Other" (Being and Nothingness, Part III, ch. 2, p. 351, trans. Barnes). This third body is out of reach: you can feel it but not handle it.

### Sartre on shyness

His example is shyness. The shy person is "vividly and constantly conscious of his body not as it is for him but as it is for the Other". Sartre corrects the usual wording: "I can not be embarrassed by my own body as I exist it. It is my body as it is for the Other which may embarrass me" (p. 353). When the shy man longs "not to have a body anymore", Sartre says, it is this alienated dimension he wants to destroy, and the attempts are useless (p. 353).

### Two other voices

Merleau-Ponty says the encounter need not turn me into an object: "the positing of the other does not reduce me to the status of an object in his field" (Phenomenology of Perception, p. 411, trans. Smith). De Beauvoir shows how the body-for-others can be unequal. Man "thinks of his body as a direct and normal connection with the world", yet "regards the body of woman as a hindrance, a prison" (The Second Sex, Introduction, trans. Parshley).

**Plain words** You have two bodies at once: the one you live in, and the one other people see. You cannot get rid of the second or fully control it, which is why being watched can feel like having something taken from you. Sartre's account suggests that hiding will not work. The task is to hold both: this is what I do and feel, and this is how I appear, without letting either swallow the other.

*For example:* Imagined example: a student stands to give a talk and feels every part of the body as a thing for the room to judge. The practice is to bring attention back to the feet on the floor and the breath, while accepting that the audience's view exists too.

(Sartre, Being and Nothingness, Part III, ch. 2, pp. 351-353, trans. Barnes; Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, trans. Colin Smith (Routledge Classics, 2002), pp. 410-411; de Beauvoir, The Second Sex (1949), Introduction, trans. H. M. Parshley; all opened 2026-10-21.)
