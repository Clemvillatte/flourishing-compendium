Edmund Husserl (born 8 April 1859, Proßnitz, Moravia; died 27 April 1938) founded phenomenology, the disciplined description of experience from the first-person point of view ([SEP, Edmund Husserl](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). His watchword set the school's whole temper: "We can absolutely not rest content with 'mere words' […]. Meanings inspired only by remote, confused, inauthentic intuitions—if by any intuitions at all—are not enough: we must go back to the 'things themselves'" ([SEP, Edmund Husserl](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)).

## Personal story / context

Husserl began as a mathematician: he studied in Leipzig and Berlin under Weierstrass and Kronecker, took his doctorate in 1883 on the calculus of variations, and became Weierstrass's assistant ([SEP, Edmund Husserl](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). Franz Brentano's Vienna lectures turned him to philosophy, and he habilitated at Halle in 1887 with work on the concept of number ([SEP, Edmund Husserl](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). The *Logical Investigations* (1900-01) secured him a chair at Göttingen; in 1916 he moved to Freiburg, retiring in 1928 in favor of Martin Heidegger ([SEP, Edmund Husserl](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). The Nazis then undid the career: barred from teaching and publishing for his Jewish ancestry, stripped of his citizenship, he wrote in a 1933 letter of "the erection of a spiritual ghetto, into which I and my children … are to be driven" ([SEP, Edmund Husserl](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). He died weeks before the war, and a young Franciscan, Hermann Leo Van Breda, smuggled his roughly 45,000 pages of research manuscripts to Leuven, where the Husserl Archives still hold them ([SEP, Edmund Husserl](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)).

## Their why

Husserl's founding complaint was that science had grown exact while losing contact with the experience it describes. Mathematics had taught him precision; Brentano had taught him that mind is always mind *of* something. His method, the reduction, was built to return philosophy to that ground: examine not what the world is made of but how it is given, before theories take over.

## Their position

Intentionality is the baseline: every act of consciousness is directed toward an object, and phenomenology maps both the act and the object-as-meant ([SEP, Edmund Husserl](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). The epoché brackets the natural attitude, suspending the dogmatic assumption of the world's reality so that its givenness can be studied; the reduction then leads from the natural sphere back to its transcendental foundation ([SEP, Edmund Husserl](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). His late work, the *Crisis of European Sciences* (1936), located the trouble precisely: physics draws its predicates from the things it handles, and yet science floats on the lifeworld, the subject-relative, bodily, culturally shaped world of everyday experience ([SEP, Edmund Husserl](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). In the Crisis's words, "scientifically valid knowledge has first-person plural intentionality as its precondition. Science is a theoretical attitude *we* can adopt upon the world. It is an intersubjective achievement with its own presuppositions and genesis" ([SEP, Edmund Husserl](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)).

## Core ideas

- **Intentionality** — consciousness as always consciousness of something. See [[Intentionality]].
- **Epoché and reduction** — the suspension of the natural attitude and the return to how things are given. See [[Epoché]], [[Bracketing]], [[The transcendental reduction]].
- **The lifeworld** — the everyday world of lived experience as the ground science forgets. See [[The lifeworld]], [[Phenomenology of the lifeworld]].
- **Eidetic seeing** — grasping the essential structure of an experience through varied imagination. See [[Eidos]], [[Phenomenological seeing]].
- **Temporality** — consciousness as retaining, attending, anticipating; the structure of inner time. See [[Retention and protention]], [[Temporality (Zeitlichkeit)]].
