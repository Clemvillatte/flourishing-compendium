Maurice Merleau-Ponty (born 14 March 1908, Rochefort-sur-Mer; died 3 May 1961, Paris) made the body the subject of philosophy: perception, he argued, is not a mental picture of the world but the living action of an embodied being already caught up in it ([SEP, Maurice Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)). The entry on his death reports that he left the world "with Descartes' *Optics* open on his desk" ([SEP, Maurice Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)).

## Personal story / context

Merleau-Ponty trained in the Parisian lycées and the École Normale Supérieure (1926-30), where his friends included Simone de Beauvoir and Claude Lévi-Strauss, and took the agrégation in 1930, ranked second ([SEP, Maurice Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)). He defended his two dissertations in July 1945: *The Structure of Behavior* and *Phenomenology of Perception*, the books on which his fame rests ([SEP, Maurice Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)). With Sartre and Beauvoir he founded *Les Temps Modernes* in 1945 and served as its political editor, until the Korean War opened a rift; he resigned in December 1952 and in *Adventures of the Dialectic* (1955) criticized Sartre's "ultrabolshevism" ([SEP, Maurice Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)). In 1952 he was elected to the Chair of Philosophy at the Collège de France, the youngest person ever elected at forty-four ([SEP, Maurice Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)). He died of a heart attack at fifty-three; the unfinished *Visible and Invisible*, drafted 1959-61, appeared after his death ([SEP, Maurice Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)).

## Their why

Merleau-Ponty's complaint was that philosophy had forced a choice between the body as a machine and the mind as a pure spectator. His alternative began from the fact that we perceive before we theorize: sensing is "a living communication with the world that makes it present to us as the familiar place of our life" ([SEP, Maurice Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)). His first essay on painting, on Cézanne, already carried the point: art struggling to capture the birth of perception in the visible ([SEP, Maurice Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)).

## Their position

The body's typical mode of existence is "being-toward-the-world," which he describes as "a pre-objective orientation toward a vital situation that is explicable neither in terms of third-person causal interactions nor by explicit judgments or representations" ([SEP, Maurice Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)). Its relation to space is "intentional, although as an 'I can' rather than an 'I think'"; the body "is not 'in' space but lives or inhabits it" ([SEP, Maurice Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)). An "intentional arc" ties the whole of a life together: it "projects around us our past, our future, our human milieu, our physical situation, our ideological situation, and our moral situation" ([SEP, Maurice Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)). The late ontology generalizes this into flesh: carnal being, "a being of depths, of several leaves or several faces," is "a prototype of Being, of which our body, the sensible sentient, is a very remarkable variant" ([SEP, Maurice Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)). Chiasm names the crisscrossing this makes possible, "a kinship between the sensing body and sensed things that makes their communication possible" ([SEP, Maurice Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)).
