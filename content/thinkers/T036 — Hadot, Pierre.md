Pierre Hadot (February 21, 1922, Reims, France – April 24, 2010, Orsay) was a French historian of philosophy who recovered the ancient idea that philosophy is a *bios*, a way of life whose theories served its practice ([Wikipedia, Pierre Hadot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Hadot)). He spent his career as a director of studies at the École pratique des hautes études and then as professor of the History of Hellenistic and Roman Thought at the Collège de France (1983-1991).

## Personal story / context

He began as a Catholic priest, ordained in 1944, and left the priesthood after Pope Pius XII's 1950 encyclical *Humani generis* ([Wikipedia, Pierre Hadot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Hadot)). His scholarship started in patristics and Neoplatonism: a Sorbonne doctorate on Porphyry and Marius Victorinus (1968), translations and commentaries on Porphyry, Ambrose, Plotinus and Marcus Aurelius. From that textual work came his most famous finding: ancient philosophy was a practice before it was a literature. His wife, the historian of philosophy Ilsetraut Hadot, assisted him in formulating and extending the account of spiritual exercises; toward the end of his life he said Epicureanism and Stoicism nourished his own life and that of modern society ([Wikipedia, Pierre Hadot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Hadot)). His books include *Exercices spirituels et philosophie antique* (1981), *Philosophy as a Way of Life* (English, 1995), *The Inner Citadel* (1998) and *What Is Ancient Philosophy?* (2002).

## Their why

The question underneath all his work: what was philosophy actually for, for the people who lived it? He found the answer in the schools' practices rather than their textbooks: exercises intended to transform the practitioner's perception and, through it, the practitioner's life. In 1994 he published an article with a title that states his complaint about the modern university plainly: "There Are Nowadays Professors of Philosophy, but not Philosophers" ([Wikipedia, Pierre Hadot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Hadot)).

## Their position

Ancient philosophy was a training, and its texts were instruments of that training. His definition of the spiritual exercises at its center:

> "practices ... intended to effect a modification and a transformation in the subjects who practice them" ([Hadot, Philosophy as a Way of Life, quoted in Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Hadot))

He argued philosophy is best pursued in real conversation rather than only through written texts and lectures, and that philosophy as taught in universities today is, for the most part, a distortion of its original therapeutic impulse ([Wikipedia, Pierre Hadot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Hadot)). The thought reaches beyond antiquity: his analysis of the exercises influenced the late Michel Foucault, in the second and third volumes of *The History of Sexuality* ([Wikipedia, Pierre Hadot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Hadot)).

## Core ideas

- **Philosophy as a way of life** — ancient philosophy as a *bios* to be lived, with theory serving practice ([Wikipedia, Pierre Hadot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Hadot)). See [[Self-transformation over information]], [[Therapeutic philosophy]], [[B008 — Treat the truths as tasks, not beliefs|Treat the truths as tasks, not beliefs]].
- **Spiritual exercises** — "practices ... intended to effect a modification and a transformation in the subjects who practice them": from "practices of attention (*prosoche*), meditations (*meletai*), memorizations of *dogmata*, self-mastery (*enkrateia*), the therapy of the passions," demanding "daily or continuous repetition"; attention to the present is "in a sense, the key to spiritual exercises" ([IEP, Pierre Hadot](https://iep.utm.edu/hadot/)). See [[Spiritual exercises]], [[The three disciplines]].
- **The inner citadel** — the title of his 1992 book on Marcus Aurelius's *Meditations* (English, *The Inner Citadel*, 1998), where the text's repetitions read as the record of the exercises themselves ([Wikipedia, Pierre Hadot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Hadot)). See [[The inner citadel]], [[ST206 — The Inner Citadel|The Inner Citadel]], [[D096 — Don't let the outer displace the inner|Don't let the outer displace the inner]].
- **The view from above** — the exercise he argued was practiced across all the ancient schools: students reconsider how small their lives and actions appear from an enlarged, cosmic perspective, to combat the significance self-interest and passions assign to particular episodes ([IEP, Pierre Hadot](https://iep.utm.edu/hadot/)). See [[The view from above]], [[D166 — The farther out you go, the less you know|The farther out you go, the less you know]].
- **The Meditations as a kept notebook** — the repetitions are not morbidity but the work of a man "trying to do what, in the last analysis, we are all trying to do: to live in complete consciousness and lucidity, to give to each of our instants its full intensity, and to give meaning to our entire life" ([IEP, Pierre Hadot](https://iep.utm.edu/hadot/)). See [[B192 — Begin the day with intention; end it with review|Begin the day with intention; end it with review]], [[D054 — Practice daily — small consistent effort compounds|Practice daily: small consistent effort compounds]].
- **Attention, or vigilance** — *prosoche*, the constant practice of attention that heads the exercise list ([IEP, Pierre Hadot](https://iep.utm.edu/hadot/)). See [[Spiritual vigilance]], [[B017 — Practice heedfulness (appamada)|Practice heedfulness (appamada)]], [[Technologies of the self]].
