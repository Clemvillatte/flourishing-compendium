Martin E. P. Seligman (born August 12, 1942, Albany, New York) is the Zellerbach Family Professor of Psychology at the University of Pennsylvania and director of its Positive Psychology Center ([Wikipedia, Martin Seligman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Seligman)). He began as a researcher of learned helplessness, and in 1998, as president of the American Psychological Association, he turned the field's attention toward what makes life worth living. His Tanner Lecture at Michigan in 2010 sets out the view in his own words: "psychology is just as concerned with strength as it is with weakness. It is just as interested in building what makes life worth living as it is with repairing pathology" ([Seligman, Tanner Lecture 2010](https://tannerlectures.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Seligman_10.pdf)).

## Personal story / context

Seligman took a philosophy degree at Princeton in 1964, summa cum laude, and a psychology PhD at Penn in 1967 ([Wikipedia, Martin Seligman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Seligman)). His first major work was the laboratory research on dogs that, having received shocks they could not escape, later failed to use opportunities to escape; he read the result as a model of depression, and the theory that grew from it, learned helplessness, became his first book ([Wikipedia, Martin Seligman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Seligman)). The puzzle of the subjects who never became helpless, about one-third of the people in his laboratory by his count, moved him toward the study of optimism as a habit of mind. He describes himself plainly: "I am a pessimist and a depressive. I take my own medicine" ([Seligman, Tanner Lecture 2010](https://tannerlectures.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Seligman_10.pdf)).

## Their why

Seligman's stated motivation is the gap he saw between relieving misery and building a life. As a therapist he found that removing a patient's sadness, anxiety and anger did not produce a happy person. In his words: "What I got was an empty person" ([Seligman, Tanner Lecture 2010](https://tannerlectures.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Seligman_10.pdf)). The question that drives him is what fills that emptiness: the skills of relationships, meaning, engagement and positive emotion, which he came to see as different skills from those taught by therapy for suffering.

## Their position

Seligman holds that the target of positive psychology should be well-being rather than happiness or life satisfaction. Reviewing his own Authentic Happiness theory of 2002, he changed his mind: "positive emotion, engagement, and meaning did not exhaust what people valued for their own sake", so the model grew to five elements — PERMA: positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning, and accomplishment ([Seligman, Tanner Lecture 2010](https://tannerlectures.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Seligman_10.pdf)). The change was deliberate, driven by his concern that life-satisfaction answers track current mood too closely to serve as the field's target measure — a point he credits to Ruut Veenhoven's work ([Seligman, Tanner Lecture 2010](https://tannerlectures.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Seligman_10.pdf)). Positive psychology, on his account, is a supplement to psychology-as-usual: "Removing the disabling conditions of life is laudable, but it is not the same endeavor as building the enabling conditions of life" ([Seligman, Tanner Lecture 2010](https://tannerlectures.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Seligman_10.pdf)).

## Core ideas

- **Learned helplessness** — people who experience uncontrollable bad events become passive, not trying to do anything about their future; the 1967 experiments with Maier and Overmier that opened his career ([Wikipedia, Martin Seligman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Seligman)). See [[Learned Helplessness Theory]].
- **Learned optimism** — the habit of mind that reads setbacks as temporary, specific and changeable; his name for what he saw in the people he could not make helpless in his own laboratory ([Seligman, Tanner Lecture 2010](https://tannerlectures.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Seligman_10.pdf)). See [[Learned optimism]], [[Optimistic explanatory style]].
- **Positive psychology** — the field he convened as APA president around the question "What makes life worth living, and how can we build it?" ([Seligman, Tanner Lecture 2010](https://tannerlectures.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Seligman_10.pdf)). See [[Positive psychology]].
- **PERMA and well-being theory** — well-being with five elements: positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning, and accomplishment ([Seligman, Tanner Lecture 2010](https://tannerlectures.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Seligman_10.pdf)). See [[PERMA model]].
- **Character strengths and virtues** — with Christopher Peterson, the 2004 classification of six virtues (wisdom/knowledge, courage, humanity, justice, temperance, transcendence) and the strengths that express them ([Wikipedia, Martin Seligman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Seligman)). See [[Character strengths (Peterson & Seligman)]], [[VIA classification]].
