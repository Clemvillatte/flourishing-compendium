Immanuel Kant (April 22, 1724, Königsberg — February 12, 1804) "is the central figure in modern philosophy," whose critical philosophy "set the terms for much of nineteenth and twentieth century philosophy" ([SEP, Kant](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/)).

## Personal story / context

Kant was born in Königsberg, the capital of East Prussia, "a major commercial center, an important military port, and a relatively cosmopolitan university town." After college he "spent six years as a private tutor to young children outside Königsberg," returning in 1754 and beginning to teach at the University of Königsberg (the Albertina) the following year; "For the next four decades Kant taught philosophy there, until his retirement from teaching in 1796 at the age of seventy-two" ([SEP, Kant](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/)).

## Their why

The fundamental idea of Kant's critical philosophy — "especially in his three Critiques: the Critique of Pure Reason (1781, 1787), the Critique of Practical Reason (1788), and the Critique of the Power of Judgment (1790) — is human autonomy" ([SEP, Kant](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/)). The Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785) is his "main work on the fundamental principle of morality"; Toward Perpetual Peace (1795) is among his "main works in political philosophy" ([SEP, Kant](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/)).

## Their position

The Groundwork's humanity formulation, quoted in the MIT course PDF:

> "So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only." (Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, trans. T. K. Abbott, [MIT course asset PDF](https://openlearninglibrary.mit.edu/assets/courseware/v1/d724068ab9d302a3eba40f625520f510/asset-v1:MITx+24.02x+2T2020+type@asset+block@L11_Kant_Groundwork.pdf))

In Perpetual Peace, the third definitive article states: "The rights of men, as citizens of the world, shall be limited to the conditions of universal hospitality." The article continues: "We are speaking here... not of philanthropy, but of right; and in this sphere hospitality signifies the claim of a stranger entering foreign territory to be treated by its owner without hostility" (Kant, Perpetual Peace, [Gutenberg #50922](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/50922/50922-h/50922-h.htm)).

## Core ideas

- **The categorical imperative** — the moral law stated as a universal test: what makes an act right is that its maxim could hold as a universal law, and what gives that law its authority is reason itself. The doctrine at the center of the three Critiques "is human autonomy": "human reason gives itself the moral law" ([SEP, Kant](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/)). See [[The categorical imperative]], [[Moral obligation]].
- **Persons as ends** — the humanity formulation: "So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only," because "rational nature exists as an end in itself" ([Groundwork, Abbott](https://openlearninglibrary.mit.edu/assets/courseware/v1/d724068ab9d302a3eba40f625520f510/asset-v1:MITx+24.02x+2T2020+type@asset+block@L11_Kant_Groundwork.pdf)). See [[Persons as ends]], [[Dignity]], [[Respect]].
- **Autonomy as the critical idea** — the same self-legislation extended to knowledge: "the human understanding is the source of the general laws of nature that structure all our experience" ([SEP, Kant](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/)). See [[Autonomy]], [[Self-legislation]].
- **Cosmopolitan right and hospitality** — a right of strangers, not benevolence: "hospitality signifies the claim of a stranger entering foreign territory to be treated by its owner without hostility" ([Perpetual Peace](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/50922/50922-h/50922-h.htm)). See [[Cosmopolitanism]], [[Hospitality]], [[Cosmopolitan care]].
- **The sublime and aesthetic judgement** — the Critique of the Power of Judgment (1790) closes the critical system, carrying the account of disinterested pleasure by which I treat beauty and the sublime ([SEP, Kant](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant/)). See [[The sublime]], [[Disinterested aesthetic pleasure]].
