Albert Bandura (December 4, 1925 – July 26, 2021) was a Canadian-American psychologist and professor of social science in psychology at Stanford University, known as the originator of social learning theory, social cognitive theory, and the theoretical construct of self-efficacy ([Wikipedia, Albert Bandura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Bandura)). His 1977 Psychological Review paper, "Self-efficacy: Toward a Unifying Theory of Behavioral Change," states the theory in its abstract: "This theory states that psychological procedures, whatever their form, alter the level and strength of self-efficacy" ([Bandura, 1977](https://www.susana.org/_resources/documents/default/3-4524-7-1640787326.pdf)).

## Personal story / context

Bandura was born in Mundare, Alberta, Canada, and took his doctorate at the University of Iowa under Arthur L. Benton before joining Stanford, where he spent his career. He was responsible for the theoretically influential Bobo doll experiment (1961), which demonstrated the conceptual validity of observational learning: children who watched an adult act aggressively toward a doll were more likely to also beat the doll, having learned through observation ([Wikipedia, Albert Bandura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Bandura)). The Wikipedia biography credits him with influencing the transition between behaviorism and cognitive psychology ([Wikipedia, Albert Bandura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_Bandura)).

## Their why

The 1977 paper's abstract states the core hypothesis:

> "It is hypothesized that expectations of personal efficacy determine whether coping behavior will be initiated, how much effort will be expended, and how long it will be sustained in the face of obstacles and aversive experiences." ([Bandura, 1977](https://www.susana.org/_resources/documents/default/3-4524-7-1640787326.pdf))

## Their position

The paper's core distinction is between two kinds of expectation: "An outcome expectancy is defined as a person's estimate that a given behavior will lead to certain outcomes. An efficacy expectation is the conviction that one can successfully execute the behavior required to produce the outcomes" ([Bandura, 1977](https://www.susana.org/_resources/documents/default/3-4524-7-1640787326.pdf)). Expectations also "vary on several dimensions that have important performance implications": magnitude (which difficulty levels the expectation covers), generality (how far it extends beyond the training situation), and strength (whether disconfirming experiences extinguish it) ([Bandura, 1977](https://www.susana.org/_resources/documents/default/3-4524-7-1640787326.pdf)).

The model states where the expectation comes from: "In the proposed model, expectations of personal efficacy are derived from four principal sources of information: performance accomplishments, vicarious experience, verbal persuasion, and physiological states. The more dependable the experiential sources, the greater are the changes in perceived self-efficacy" ([Bandura, 1977](https://www.susana.org/_resources/documents/default/3-4524-7-1640787326.pdf)). The paper also proposes the cognitive-mediator account: "cognitive processes mediate change but... cognitive events are induced and altered most readily by experience of mastery arising from effective performance" ([Bandura, 1977](https://www.susana.org/_resources/documents/default/3-4524-7-1640787326.pdf)).
