Erich Seligmann Fromm (born March 23, 1900, Frankfurt am Main; died March 18, 1980, Muralto, Switzerland) was a psychoanalyst and social theorist of the Frankfurt School who made love and freedom matters of character rather than fate ([Wikipedia, Erich Fromm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Fromm)). His 1941 answer to why people flee their own freedom reads: "There is only one possible, productive solution for the relationship of individualized man with the world: his active solidarity with all men and his spontaneous activity, love and work, which unite him again with the world, not by primary ties but as a free and independent individual" ([Wikipedia, Erich Fromm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Fromm)).

## Personal story / context

Fromm was the only child of a Frankfurt family of rabbis, and his dissertation at Heidelberg (1922) was a sociology of the Jewish diaspora ([Wikipedia, Erich Fromm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Fromm)). He trained in analysis at Frieda Reichmann's Heidelberg sanatorium, married her in 1926, and joined the Frankfurt Institute for Social Research in 1930 ([Wikipedia, Erich Fromm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Fromm)). When the Nazis made Germany impossible he moved through Geneva to Columbia University in New York, and in 1949 settled in Mexico City as a professor at UNAM, where he built a psychoanalytic section at the medical school; he finished his career in Switzerland ([Wikipedia, Erich Fromm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Fromm)). His books ran from *Escape from Freedom* (1941) through *The Art of Loving* (1956), his bestseller, to *To Have or To Be?* (1976) ([Wikipedia, Erich Fromm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Fromm)).

## Their why

Fromm watched ordinary people surrender to authoritarianism and saw a psychological event. Freedom had cut the old ties that gave life security, and for many it became "an unbearable burden," so that "powerful tendencies arise to escape from this kind of freedom into submission" ([Wikipedia, Erich Fromm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Fromm)). His question was what a free person could do with that isolation, and his answer was character: the productive person relates to the world through love and work instead of submission or possession.

## Their position

The escape mechanisms he mapped were authoritarianism, destructiveness and automaton conformity, in which a person reshapes himself to fit what society prefers ([Wikipedia, Erich Fromm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Fromm)). Against escape he set productiveness, writing: "It is the paradox of human existence that man must simultaneously seek for closeness and for independence; for oneness with others and at the same time for the preservation of his uniqueness and particularity. ...the answer to this paradox – and to the moral problems of man – is productiveness" ([Wikipedia, Erich Fromm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Fromm)). Love, on this account, is an art and a capacity: "Love is a human activity, not passivity (*to be* in love, rather than *to fall* in love)" ([Fromm, Marx's Contribution to the Knowledge of Man, 1968](https://www.marxists.org/archive/fromm/works/1968/marx.htm)). His late summary reads: "I believe that the man choosing progress can find a new unity through the development of all his human forces, which are produced in three orientations. These can be presented separately or together: biophilia, love for humanity and nature, and independence and freedom" ([Wikipedia, Erich Fromm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Fromm)).

## Core ideas

- **Escape from freedom** — the mechanisms by which people flee the isolation freedom brings: submission, destructiveness, conformity. See [[Escape from freedom]], [[Conformity]], [[The dizziness of freedom]].
- **Having versus being** — two modes of existence, one organized around possessing, the other around alive activity. See [[The having mode]].
- **Productive love** — love as an art with elements of care, responsibility, respect and knowledge, rather than a feeling one falls into. See [[Love]], [[Relational psychoanalysis]].
- **Biophilia** — the love of life as a character orientation, set against necrophilia, the love of death. See [[Love]], [[Loneliness]].
- **Social character** — the shared character structure a society shapes in its members, the bridge he built between psychoanalysis and sociology. See [[Domination critique]].
