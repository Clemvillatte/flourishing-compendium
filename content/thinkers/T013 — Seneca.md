> **Thinker T013** — Lucius Annaeus Seneca (c. 1 BCE–65 CE), Roman Stoic philosopher, playwright and statesman; tutor and then advisor to Nero, whom he served until retirement and, charged with treason, was compelled to execute himself.

## Personal story / context

Seneca was born in Corduba, Spain, and educated in rhetoric and philosophy in Rome ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/)). In 41 he was accused of adultery with Caligula's sister and exiled to Corsica; he consoled his mother Helvia from there in one of his earliest surviving works. Recalled to become tutor to the young Nero, he stayed on as advisor after Nero's accession in 54, writing his major works amid increasingly difficult circumstances until he asked permission to retire ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/)). In 65, charged with complicity in the Pisonian conspiracy to murder Nero, he was compelled to commit suicide. The Moral Letters to Lucilius and the Natural Questions belong to his last years, 62–65 ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/)).

## Their why

Seneca writes for people he portrays as suffering from a sickness of the soul, and he means philosophy as a salutary practice, not a lecture: readers should actively work through ideas and learn to live by them ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/)). The urgency behind the Letters is the sense that life is being wasted while it is being postponed:

> "While we are postponing, life speeds by. Nothing, Lucilius, is ours, except time." (Letter 1, trans. Gummere, [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_1))

## Their position

Anger, grief and fear are not to be moderated but replaced: he treats them as irrational responses involving judgment and assent, and the ideal agent defends others because it is appropriate, not out of rage ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/)). The study of nature serves the same therapy, teaching readers to see their local lives from the perspective of the stars, where riches and borders shrink ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/)). A life lived well is complete in itself: in Letter 12 he compares it to a day that contains everything, "light and darkness", so that further days are more of the same ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/)). What one person can do is bounded and real:

> "What is required, you see, of any man is that he should be of use to other men—if possible, to many; failing that, to a few; failing that, to those nearest him; failing that, to himself." (On the Private Life 3.5, as quoted in [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/seneca/))

## Core ideas

- **Premeditation of evils** — rehearse what can go wrong before it does. See [[Premeditatio malorum]], [[Negative visualisation]], [[Hope tempered by prudence]], [[ST221 — The Premeditation of Evils|221. The Premeditation of Evils]].
- **Anger examined early** — anger as judgment, answered with delay and reappraisal. See [[Anger examined early]], [[Anger reappraisal]], [[Delay plus reappraisal]], [[Anger]], [[ST205 — Seneca on Anger|205. Seneca on Anger]].
- **The evening review** — close the day by examining it. See [[The evening review]], [[Daily self-examination]], [[K274 — Evening examination|K274 Evening examination]], [[ST220 — The Evening Review|220. The Evening Review]].
- **The shortness of life** — time is the only thing that is ours; guard it. See [[ST213 — The Shortness of Life|213. The Shortness of Life]], [[Memento mori]], [[Attention management]], [[Daily architecture]].
- **Emotions as judgments** — passions live in assent, not in events. See [[Emotions as judgments]], [[Cognitive reappraisal]], [[Accept loss without devastation]].
- **Voluntary discomfort** — practice want before want practices you. See [[Voluntary discomfort]], [[Negative visualisation]], [[Sufficiency]].
- **Use to others** — benefit many, or few, or the nearest, or yourself. See [[Care]], [[Generosity]], [[Friendship]], [[Dignity]].
- **Contempt for fame** — reputation is not the self. See [[Contempt for fame]], [[The wise man's indifference to reputation]], [[Humility]].
