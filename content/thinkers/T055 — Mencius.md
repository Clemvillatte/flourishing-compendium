Mencius (孟子, Mengzi, "Master Meng"; Latinized by Jesuit missionaries; full name Meng Ke) was a Confucian philosopher of the fourth century BCE, born about a century after Confucius's death in the state of Zou, in what is now Shandong Province ([SEP, Mencius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/)). The *Mengzi*, a collection of dialogues and debates probably compiled by his disciples, is the main source for his thinking.

## Personal story / context

He is said to have studied under Confucius's grandson Zisi ([SEP, Mencius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/)). As an adult he traveled among states advising rulers to govern through benevolence rather than force, held office in Qi, and resigned when the ruler would not enact his reforms ([SEP, Mencius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/)). He self-identified as a follower of Confucius: "Ever since man came into this world, there has never been one greater than Confucius" (Mengzi 2A2, Lau translation, quoted in [SEP, Mencius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/)).

## Their why

The question underneath the *Mengzi*: why do people do evil, if the tendencies toward virtue are innate? His answer, argued against a rival who held that human nature is neither good nor bad, is that the environment and failure to reflect can spoil what is originally there — the sprouts must be cultivated, not presumed.

## Their position

Human nature is good (*xing shan*), in the form of four sprouts. The claim, in Van Norden's translation of 6A6:

> "As for what they are inherently [*qing*], they can become good. This is what I mean by calling their natures [*xing*] good. As for their becoming not good, this is not the fault of their potential... Benevolence, righteousness, propriety, and wisdom are not welded to us externally. We inherently have them. It is simply that we do not reflect upon [*si*] them." (Mengzi 6A6, quoted in [SEP, Mencius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/))

The evidence anyone can check is the reaction to a child in danger:

> "Suppose someone suddenly saw a child about to fall into a well: anyone in such a situation would have a feeling of alarm and compassion—not because one sought to get in good with the child's parents, not because one wanted fame among one's neighbors and friends, and not because one would dislike the sound of the child's cries. From this we can see that if one is without the feeling of compassion, one is not human." (Mengzi 2A6, quoted in [SEP, Mencius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/))

## Core ideas

- **The four sprouts** — compassion (benevolence, *ren*), disdain and shame (righteousness, *yi*), respect (propriety, *li*), approval and disapproval (wisdom, *zhi*): "The feeling of compassion is benevolence. The feeling of disdain is righteousness. The feeling of respect is propriety. The feeling of approval and disapproval is wisdom" (Mengzi 6A6, quoted in [SEP, Mencius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/)). See [[R059 — Practise the Mencian sprouts|Practise the Mencian sprouts]], [[Ren]], [[Righteousness]], [[Natural virtue]].
- **Human nature is good** — the sprouts are tendencies to develop, not finished virtues; becoming good or bad depends on environment and reflection ([SEP, Mencius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/)). See [[Natural virtue]], [[The exemplary person]].
- **Extension** — the moral task is to extend or fill out the nascent inclinations, from the spontaneous reaction to the child to the whole of life ([SEP, Mencius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/)). See [[Effortless action through long effort]], [[ST241 — The Child at the Well|The Child at the Well]].
- **Benevolent government** — rulers should govern through benevolence and moral persuasion rather than brute force; he resigned office rather than stay where his reforms were refused ([SEP, Mencius](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/)). See [[M048 — Be a torch and a mirror|Be a torch and a mirror]], [[The mirror for others]].
- **The five relationships** — the classical Confucian web of family and social duties his teaching presupposes. See [[R057 — Practise the five relationships|Practise the five relationships]], [[R058 — Practise filial piety (xiao)|Practise filial piety (xiao)]], [[ST243 — The Five Relationships|The Five Relationships]].
