Carol Susan Dweck (born October 17, 1946, in New York) is an American psychologist who built the mindset research program: the study of what people believe about the malleability of their own abilities, and what those beliefs do to motivation ([Wikipedia, Carol Dweck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Dweck)). She studied at Barnard College (B.A. 1967) and took her Ph.D. at Yale in 1972, and since 2004 has been the Lewis and Virginia Eaton Professor of Psychology at Stanford.

## Personal story / context

Her research began with learned helplessness. Her Yale dissertation was titled *The Role of Expectations and Attributions in the Alleviation of Learned Helplessness in a Problem-Solving Situation* (1972), a study of why people give up after failure and what restores their persistence ([Wikipedia, Carol Dweck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Dweck)). She taught at Illinois, Harvard and Columbia before moving to Stanford in 2004. The through-line of her work, from learned helplessness to mindsets, runs from that research question to beliefs about ability. She introduced the implicit-theories research program in a 1988 paper, popularized it in her 2006 book *Mindset: The New Psychology of Success*, and was elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 2012 and named the inaugural laureate of the Yidan Prize for Education Research in 2017 ([Wikipedia, Carol Dweck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Dweck)).

## Their why

Her dissertation asked the research question directly: after unsolvable problems, people stop trying on solvable ones; what expectations and attributions alleviate that helplessness? The mindset work turned that question toward beliefs: if the difference between collapse and persistence lies in what a person believes about ability, then beliefs themselves become the thing to study and to teach.

## Their position

Ability beliefs come in two forms. In her own words:

> "In a fixed mindset students believe their basic abilities, their intelligence, their talents, are just fixed traits. They have a certain amount and that's that, and then their goal becomes to look smart all the time and never look dumb. In a growth mindset students understand that their talents and abilities can be developed through effort, good teaching and persistence." ([Wikipedia, Carol Dweck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Dweck))

In the academic literature she defines implicit theories as "core assumptions about the malleability of personal attributes" ([Wikipedia, Carol Dweck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Dweck)). Her 1988 *Psychological Review* paper with Ellen L. Leggett, "A Social-Cognitive Approach to Motivation and Personality," specifies how implicit theories orient people toward particular goals, which then set up the different behavior patterns ([Dweck & Leggett, 1988](https://mathedseminar.pbworks.com/f/Dweck+&+Leggett+(1988)+A+social-cognitive+approach+to+motivation+and+personality.pdf)). Against a common misreading, she insists the growth mindset is not empty encouragement: "The growth mindset was intended to help close achievement gaps, not hide them. It is about telling the truth about a student's current achievement and then, together, doing something about it" ([Wikipedia, Carol Dweck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Dweck)).

## Core ideas

- **Fixed and growth mindsets** — the two implicit theories: ability as a fixed quantity to protect, or as something developable through effort, teaching and persistence ([Wikipedia, Carol Dweck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Dweck)). See [[Growth mindset]], [[Fixed views of self]], [[Implicit theories of intelligence]], [[SE062 — Cultivate growth orientation — see self as developable|Cultivate growth orientation: see self as developable]].
- **Mastery versus performance goals** — in the 1988 paper's own words: "performance goals (in which individuals are concerned with gaining favorable judgments of their competence) and learning goals (in which individuals are concerned with increasing their competence)" ([Dweck & Leggett, 1988](https://mathedseminar.pbworks.com/f/Dweck+&+Leggett+(1988)+A+social-cognitive+approach+to+motivation+and+personality.pdf)). See [[Achievement goal theory (Dweck, Elliot)]], [[Mastery orientation]], [[MO020 — Recognise mastery vs performance goals — learning vs proving|Recognise mastery vs performance goals: learning vs proving]].
- **The helplessness question** — her dissertation on alleviating learned helplessness through expectations and attributions ([Wikipedia, Carol Dweck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Dweck)). See [[S117 — Handle failure without despair|Handle failure without despair]], [[Achievement motivation theory]].
- **What praise teaches** — her advice to parents: "teach their children to love challenges, be intrigued by mistakes, enjoy effort, and keep on learning... they will have a lifelong way to build and repair their own confidence" ([Wikipedia, Carol Dweck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Dweck)). See [[P088 — Use praise and blame as instruments, not metaphysics|Use praise and blame as instruments, not metaphysics]].
- **Evidence and its limits** — her teams' interventions were reported in *Psychological Science* (2015) and *Nature* (2019, a national experiment showing where growth-mindset effects appear); critics note the research can be difficult to replicate ([Wikipedia, Carol Dweck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Dweck)). See [[Growth mindset]], [[MO008 — Know that motivation is trainable — it can be built and shaped|Know that motivation is trainable: it can be built and shaped]].
- **Motivation, personality and development unified** — her 2017 theory placing goals, beliefs and representations at the heart of personality development ([Wikipedia, Carol Dweck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carol_Dweck)). See [[Intrinsic motivation]], [[Competence]].
