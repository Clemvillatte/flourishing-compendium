Richard M. Ryan and Edward L. Deci are American psychologists whose collaboration shaped self-determination theory (SDT), one of the most-cited research programmes in motivation and well-being. They were both at the University of Rochester when their 2000 American Psychologist article, "Self-Determination Theory and the Facilitation of Intrinsic Motivation, Social Development, and Well-Being," consolidated the theory's programme of research into a single review. Their work underpins the eudaimonic well-being literature in psychology.

The article opens by contrasting the human tendency toward agency and growth with the capacity of social conditions to diminish or crush it: "The fullest representations of humanity show people to be curious, vital, and self-motivated," while "Yet, it is also clear that the human spirit can be diminished or crushed and that individuals sometimes reject growth and responsibility" ([Ryan & Deci 2000](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf)). It then proposes three innate psychological needs (competence, autonomy, and relatedness) as the conditions under which that tendency either flourishes or withers.

## Their why

They kept a single question: what conditions let human beings be curious, vital, and self-motivated rather than passive and alienated? They answered it empirically, by manipulating social contexts rather than assuming traits. The article's own words: "Research guided by self-determination theory has had an ongoing concern with precisely these issues" ([Ryan & Deci 2000](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf)).

## Their position

The three needs are treated as innate, not learned: "we have identified three such needs--the needs for competence... relatedness... and autonomy--that appear to be essential for facilitating optimal functioning of the natural propensities for growth and integration, as well as for constructive social development and personal well-being" ([Ryan & Deci 2000](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf)). Motivation is not a single quantity: SDT distinguishes autonomous from controlled motivation, and treats intrinsic motivation as "the inherent tendency to seek out novelty and challenges, to extend and exercise one's capacities, to explore, and to learn" ([Ryan & Deci 2000](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf)). Extrinsic rewards that are contingent on task performance reliably undermine intrinsic motivation.

## Core ideas

- **Intrinsic motivation and autonomy** — the prototypic manifestation of growth; supported by autonomy and competence, undermined by control. See [[Intrinsic motivation]], [[Autonomous motivation]], [[Controlled motivation]].
- **Three basic psychological needs** — competence, autonomy, relatedness. See [[Autonomy]], [[Competence]], [[Relatedness]], [[Psychological needs theory]].
- **Internalization of extrinsic motivation** — people can take in external demands and make them self-endorsed. See [[Identified versus introjected motivation]], [[Extrinsic motivation]].
- **Eudaimonic versus hedonic well-being** — the article ties need satisfaction to "an ongoing sense of integrity and well-being or 'eudaimonia'"; need satisfaction is theorized invariant across cultures even where specific goals vary. See [[Eudaimonic well-being]], [[Vitality]].
- **Goal contents** — intrinsic versus extrinsic aspirations. See [[Intrinsic versus extrinsic goals]], [[Extrinsic aspirations]], [[Aspiration index]].
