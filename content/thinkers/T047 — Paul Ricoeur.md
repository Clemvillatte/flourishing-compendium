Paul Ricoeur (1913–2005) is the French philosopher who made interpretation the road to the self. Across more than thirty books he argued that human beings are not transparent to themselves: they understand their lives, their symbols, and their history through texts and stories that must be interpreted — and that the self this interpretation yields is a narrative self, one self among others, attested rather than proven ([SEP, Paul Ricoeur](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ricoeur/)). His phrase for the hinge of his whole project is "the symbol gives rise to thought" ([SEP, Paul Ricoeur](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ricoeur/)).

## Personal story / context

Born in Valence in 1913, he lost his mother shortly after his birth and his father at the Battle of the Marne in 1915; raised in Rennes by devout French Reformed Protestant grandparents, he described his faith as "an accident transformed into a destiny through an ongoing choice, while scrupulously respecting other choices" ([SEP, Paul Ricoeur](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ricoeur/)). Called up in 1939, he spent most of World War II in German prison camps, where he studied Jaspers and prepared a translation of Husserl's *Ideas I* in the margins of a book hidden from his jailers ([SEP, Paul Ricoeur](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ricoeur/)). He taught at Strasbourg, the Sorbonne and Nanterre, served a difficult year as dean after the 1968 uprising, and from 1970 taught part of each year at the University of Chicago; his late honors included the Kyoto Prize (2000) and the Kluge Prize (2004) ([SEP, Paul Ricoeur](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ricoeur/)).

## Their why

Ricoeur kept one question in view: what can be attributed to a human being, given that the self is neither immediately certain of itself nor a mere illusion? His major work on the self responds to readings of the Cartesian cogito that make it too strong, too weak, or nonexistent, and proposes instead a hermeneutics of a "wounded cogito" — a self that attests to itself, acts, and takes responsibility, but is never fully master of itself ([SEP, Paul Ricoeur](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ricoeur/)). Self-understanding therefore runs through interpretation, of symbols, narratives, memory and institutions, and accepts the resulting self as fallible and finite.

## Their position

*The Symbolism of Evil* (1960) argued that symbolic language conveys more than one meaning and that philosophy must learn to make sense of symbolic language and to think from it, a requirement he compresses into the formula "the symbol gives rise to thought" ([SEP, Paul Ricoeur](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ricoeur/)). Interpretation combines objective analysis with recognition that discourse carries a surplus of meaning; "one then seeks to explain more in order to understand better" ([SEP, Paul Ricoeur](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ricoeur/)). *Time and Narrative* (1984–88) shows that stories configure action and time into followable wholes, producing a "discordant concordance," and concludes with narrative identity: personal identity can itself be considered narratively, through the story a person tells about their life or that others tell about it ([SEP, Paul Ricoeur](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ricoeur/)). *Oneself as Another* (1992) replaces the shattered cogito with a "wounded cogito" — a person capable of attesting to their existence, acting, recounting and taking responsibility — and distinguishes *idem* identity (sameness) from *ipse* identity (selfhood through change), so that "I am and am not the person I was ten years ago" ([SEP, Paul Ricoeur](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ricoeur/)). His "little ethics" states the aim as "aiming at a good life lived with and for others in just institutions," with solicitude governing intimate relations and justice governing distant ones, both resting on the golden rule: "one ought not do to others what one does not want done to him- or herself" ([SEP, Paul Ricoeur](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ricoeur/)). Late works, *Memory, History, Forgetting* (2004) and *The Course of Recognition* (2005), treat the abuses of memory and the difficult possibility of forgiveness and mutual recognition ([SEP, Paul Ricoeur](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ricoeur/)).
