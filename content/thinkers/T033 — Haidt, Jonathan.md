Jonathan David Haidt (born October 19, 1963, in New York City) is an American social psychologist who rebuilt the study of moral judgment around intuition rather than reasoning ([Wikipedia, Jonathan Haidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Haidt)). He studied philosophy at Yale (B.A. 1985, magna cum laude) and psychology at the University of Pennsylvania (M.A. 1988, Ph.D. 1992), spent his formative research years at the University of Virginia, and has taught at NYU's Stern School of Business since 2011, where he holds the Thomas Cooley Chair in Ethical Leadership.

## Personal story / context

He was raised in a secular Jewish family in Scarsdale, New York, and identified as an atheist by fifteen; he calls his upbringing "very assimilated" ([Wikipedia, Jonathan Haidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Haidt)). Two encounters shaped his career. His doctoral dissertation at Penn asked, in its own words, whether it is wrong to eat your dog, a study of how moral judgment, affect and culture interact. Then a postdoctoral fellowship at the University of Chicago under cultural psychologist Richard Shweder, whom Haidt calls "the teacher that most affected me," pulled his attention to how morality differs across cultures rather than developing within one mind ([Wikipedia, Jonathan Haidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Haidt)). He wrote three books for general readers: The Happiness Hypothesis (2006), The Righteous Mind (2012) and The Coddling of the American Mind (2018, with Greg Lukianoff), and most recently The Anxious Generation (2024) on smartphones and childhood.

## Their why

The puzzle that runs through his work: people are certain, fast and emotional about right and wrong, and only afterwards produce reasons. His dissertation put the intuition first: what people feel about eating a dog, not what they can argue about it. The cross-cultural work with Shweder kept the question alive: if reasoning were the engine of morality, moral codes would not look so different from place to place.

## Their position

Moral judgment is intuition first, reasoning second. His 2001 paper "The Emotional Dog and Its Rational Tail" states the hypothesis in the abstract itself:

> "The author gives 4 reasons for considering the hypothesis that moral reasoning does not cause moral judgment; rather, moral reasoning is usually a post hoc construction, generated after a judgment has been reached." ([Haidt, 2001, Psychological Review 108(4), PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11699120/))

The model's core sentence: "moral judgment is generally the result of quick, automatic evaluations (intuitions)" ([Haidt, 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11699120/)). His later metaphor divides the mind into a rider (conscious reasoning) on an elephant (automatic processes), with the rider serving the elephant more than steering it ([Wikipedia, Jonathan Haidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Haidt)).

## Core ideas

- **The social intuitionist model** — moral judgment as "quick, automatic evaluations (intuitions)," with reasoning as post hoc construction; a social model that emphasizes social and cultural influences over private reasoning ([Haidt 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11699120/)). See [[Social intuitionist model (Haidt)]], [[Moral intuition research]], [[CO075 — Recognise moral cognition — intuition comes first, reasoning second|Recognise moral cognition: intuition comes first, reasoning second]].
- **Moral foundations theory** — morality rests on intuitions in several categories: care/harm, fairness/cheating, loyalty/betrayal, authority/subversion and sanctity/degradation, with liberty/oppression added in The Righteous Mind; co-developed with Craig Joseph and Jesse Graham, partly from Shweder's writings ([Wikipedia, Jonathan Haidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Haidt)). See [[Moral foundations]], [[Moral development research]].
- **Disgust as guardian** — with Paul Rozin and Clark McCauley he built the Disgust Scale; they describe disgust as beginning as a guardian of the mouth against pathogens, then expanding through biological and cultural evolution to guard the body, the social order and the moral order ([Wikipedia, Jonathan Haidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Haidt)). See [[Moral emotions]], [[AF056 — Recognise disgust's function — it protects from contamination|Recognise disgust's function: it protects from contamination]].
- **Moral elevation** — with Sara Algoe, the study of responses to moral beauty: warm, loving feelings, calmness, and a desire to become a better person ([Wikipedia, Jonathan Haidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Haidt)). See [[Moral emotions]], [[Self-transcendence]].
- **Awe and the diminishment of self** — with Dacher Keltner, the prototype analysis of awe: "two appraisals are central and are present in all clear cases of awe: perceived vastness, and a need for accommodation, defined as an inability to assimilate an experience into current mental structures" ([Keltner & Haidt, 2003, Cognition and Emotion 17(2), PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29715721/)). See [[Awe]], [[Self-transcendent experiences]], [[K228 — Awe practice|Awe practice]].
- **Morality beyond the WEIRD** — his research program tests whether morality's structure varies across cultures, in a 2023 study of the nomological network of morality across cultures ([Wikipedia, Jonathan Haidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonathan_Haidt)). See [[Moral foundations]], [[Moral dilemma research]].
