Keiji Nishitani (西谷啓治, February 27, 1900 – November 24, 1990) was a Japanese philosopher of the Kyoto School, a disciple of Kitarō Nishida, whose central problem was the overcoming of nihilism through nihilism ([Wikipedia, Keiji Nishitani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keiji_Nishitani)).

## Personal story / context

His life as a young man, in his own words:

> "My life as a young man can be described in a single phrase: it was a period absolutely without hope... My life at the time lay entirely in the grips of nihility and despair... My decision then, to study philosophy was in fact – melodramatic as it might sound – a matter of life and death." (quoted in [Wikipedia, Keiji Nishitani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keiji_Nishitani))

He received his doctorate from Kyoto Imperial University in 1924 for a dissertation on Schelling and Bergson, studied under Martin Heidegger, and became interested in Zen through D. T. Suzuki's interpretation ([Wikipedia, Keiji Nishitani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keiji_Nishitani)). His major work is *Religion and Nothingness*, with *The Self-Overcoming of Nihilism* alongside it.

## Their why

The problem of his life, verbatim: "The fundamental problem of my life ... has always been, to put it simply, the overcoming of nihilism through nihilism" (quoted in Heisig's *Philosophers of Nothingness*, via [Wikipedia, Keiji Nishitani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keiji_Nishitani)). The European death of God had reached Japan as meaninglessness; his question was whether that collapse could be passed through rather than merely treated.

## Their position

Nihility is not to be avoided but traversed: the ego's groundlessness, seen fully, turns out to be its nature — and on the far side of that seeing, emptiness (*śūnyatā*) appears not as vacancy but as the field where things are what they are. His statement of interdependence, verbatim:

> "All things that are in the world are linked together, one way or the other. Not a single thing comes into being without some relationship to every other thing." (*Religion and Nothingness*, quoted in [Wikipedia, Keiji Nishitani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keiji_Nishitani))

## Core ideas

- **Nihility and its traversal** — the collapse of meaning as the passage, not the obstacle ([Wikipedia, Keiji Nishitani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keiji_Nishitani)). See [[K008 — Expect the collapse of meaning; do not waste it|Expect the collapse of meaning; do not waste it]], [[K009 — Let the doubt become great|Let the doubt become great]].
- **The ego's groundlessness as nature, not failure** — seeing through the self is the breakthrough ([Wikipedia, Keiji Nishitani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keiji_Nishitani)). See [[K011 — See the ego's groundlessness as its nature, not its failure|See the ego's groundlessness as its nature, not its failure]], [[K010 — Step back from the ego to the field|Step back from the ego to the field]].
- **Śūnyatā as true self-nature** — emptiness reached in the traversal of nihility ([Wikipedia, Keiji Nishitani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keiji_Nishitani)). See [[Buddhist emptiness of inherent nature]], [[K014 — Let nothingness work as the place of things|Let nothingness work as the place of things]].
- **Interpenetration** — all things linked, nothing without relationship ([Wikipedia, Keiji Nishitani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keiji_Nishitani)). See [[K018 — Take interpenetration literally|Take interpenetration literally]], [[Things thinging]].
- **The return to the ordinary** — the completion of the passage in everyday things ([Wikipedia, Keiji Nishitani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keiji_Nishitani)). See [[K023 — Practice the return to the ordinary as the completion|Practice the return to the ordinary as the completion]], [[K015 — Let things be what they are, on their own bottom|Let things be what they are, on their own bottom]].
