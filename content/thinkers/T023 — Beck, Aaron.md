Aaron T. Beck (July 18, 1921 – November 1, 2021) was an American psychiatrist and professor in the department of psychiatry at the University of Pennsylvania, best known as the father of cognitive therapy ([Wikipedia, Aaron Beck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaron_Beck)). He is the co-author, with Disner, Beevers and Haigh, of the 2011 Nature Reviews Neuroscience review of the cognitive model of depression: "In the 40 years since Aaron Beck first proposed his cognitive model of depression, the elements of this model--biased attention, biased processing, biased thoughts and rumination, biased memory, and dysfunctional attitudes and schemas--have been consistently linked with the onset and maintenance of depression" ([Disner et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21731066/)). The review's abstract states the authors' purpose: "In this Review, we identify the functional and structural neurobiological architecture of Beck's cognitive model of depression."

## Personal story / context

Beck was born in Providence, Rhode Island, on July 18, 1921, the youngest of four children of Elizabeth Temkin and Harry Beck, Jewish immigrants from Ukraine; Harry worked as a printer, and the family lived what the biography calls a "comfortable, lower-middle class lifestyle" in the city's Blackstone neighborhood ([Wikipedia, Aaron Beck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaron_Beck)). The American Psychologist named him, in July 1989, one of the five most influential psychotherapists of all time, and he joined the University of Pennsylvania in 1954, where he spent the rest of his career ([Wikipedia, Aaron Beck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaron_Beck)). He died on November 1, 2021.

## Their why

The model's elements, as the review lists them, are the biased attention, biased processing, biased thoughts and rumination, biased memory, and dysfunctional attitudes and schemas that have been "consistently linked with the onset and maintenance of depression" ([Disner et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21731066/)). The review also states the mechanism finding: "in general the negative cognitive biases in depression are facilitated by increased influence from subcortical emotion processing regions combined with attenuated top-down cognitive control" ([Disner et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21731066/)). The 2011 review is the framing this page carries; it is a review of the model, not the model's first statement.

## Their position

The review's framing: "Although numerous studies have examined the neural mechanisms that underlie the cognitive aspects of depression, their findings have not been integrated with Beck's cognitive model. In this Review, we identify the functional and structural neurobiological architecture of Beck's cognitive model of depression" ([Disner et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21731066/)). Its neural reading:

> "Although the mechanisms underlying each element of the model differ, in general the negative cognitive biases in depression are facilitated by increased influence from subcortical emotion processing regions combined with attenuated top-down cognitive control." (Disner, Beevers, Haigh & Beck, 2011, [Nature Reviews Neuroscience, PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21731066/))

## Core ideas

- **The cognitive model** — the claim that depression is maintained by biased attention, processing, memory and schemas rather than by circumstances alone: the model's elements "have been consistently linked with the onset and maintenance of depression" ([Disner et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21731066/)). See [[Beck's cognitive model]], [[Cognitive-behavioural model]].
- **Automatic thoughts and the cognitive triad** — Beck "termed these cognitions 'automatic thoughts'" and found their content "fell into three categories: negative ideas about oneself, the world, and the future," interrelated as the cognitive triad ([Wikipedia, Aaron Beck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaron_Beck)). See [[Beck's cognitive model]].
- **Cognitive distortions** — the systematic errors of depressed thinking the therapy teaches patients to catch ([Wikipedia, Aaron Beck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aaron_Beck)). See [[Cognitive distortions]], [[Cognitive distortions research]].
- **Cognitive restructuring** — testing and revising beliefs as the therapy's core move. See [[Cognitive restructuring]], [[Belief evaluation in CBT]], [[Behavioral experiments in CBT]].
- **Behavioral activation** — re-engaging depressed patients in scheduled, graded activity. See [[Behavioral activation]], [[Activity scheduling (BA)]], [[Graded task assignment (Beck, BA)]].
- **Differential activation**. See [[Differential Activation Hypothesis of depression]].
