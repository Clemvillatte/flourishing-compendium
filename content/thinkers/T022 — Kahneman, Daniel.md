Daniel Kahneman (5 March 1934, Tel Aviv — 27 March 2024) received the 2002 Sveriges Riksbank Prize in Economic Sciences "for having integrated insights from psychological research into economic science, especially concerning human judgment and decision-making under uncertainty" ([Nobel Prize facts page](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2002/kahneman/facts/)). The Nobel page states: "His research on decision-making under uncertainty resulted in the formulation of a new branch of economics, prospect theory" ([Nobel Prize facts page](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2002/kahneman/facts/)).

## Personal story / context

Kahneman was born on March 5, 1934, in Tel Aviv, to Lithuanian Jewish parents who had emigrated to France in the early 1920s; he spent his childhood in Paris, where the family was living when the city fell to Nazi Germany in 1940. His father was picked up in the first major round-up of French Jews and released after six weeks. He became an Israeli-American psychologist, shared the 2002 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Vernon L. Smith, and became known as the "grandfather of behavioral economics"; much of the prize-winning work was the long collaboration with Amos Tversky. He died on March 27, 2024 ([Wikipedia, Daniel Kahneman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Kahneman)).

## Their why

The prize motivation names the question that organized his career: "for having integrated insights from psychological research into economic science, especially concerning human judgment and decision-making under uncertainty" ([Nobel Prize facts page](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2002/kahneman/facts/)). Late in that career the question turned to how experience is remembered, the ground of the peak-end finding. The book's metaphor, from its text as circulated in the IIT Kanpur course handout:

> "I describe mental life by the metaphor of two agents, called System 1 and System 2, which respectively produce fast and slow thinking." (Kahneman, Thinking, Fast and Slow, [ch. 0 handout PDF](https://www.cse.iitk.ac.in/users/se367/14/Readings/kahneman_TFTS-handout-ch-0-1-3_sml.pdf))

## Their position

Kahneman's framing is a division of labor, not brain parts: "I adopt terms originally proposed by the psychologists Keith Stanovich and Richard West, and will refer to two systems in the mind, System 1 and System 2" — "System 1 operates automatically and quickly, with little or no effort and no sense of voluntary control"; "System 2 allocates attention to the effortful mental activities that demand it, including complex computations" ([Thinking, Fast and Slow, ch. 1, IIT Kanpur handout PDF](https://www.cse.iitk.ac.in/users/se367/14/Readings/kahneman_TFTS-handout-ch-0-1-3_sml.pdf)). The peak-end experiments added a memory finding: duration "plays a small role in retrospective evaluations of aversive experiences; such evaluations are often dominated by the discomfort at the worst and at the final moments of episodes" ([Kahneman et al. 1993](https://www.ius.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:5ae9adc9-61ec-4174-b37c-4b752f36c23b/Kahnemann%20et%20al.%20-%20When%20More%20Pain%20is%20Preferred%20to%20Less%20(1993).pdf)).

## Core ideas

- **System 1 and System 2** — the two agents of the title: one fast, automatic and effortless, the other slow, effortful and deliberate; both definitions are quoted in Their position above ([Thinking, Fast and Slow, ch. 1, IIT Kanpur handout PDF](https://www.cse.iitk.ac.in/users/se367/14/Readings/kahneman_TFTS-handout-ch-0-1-3_sml.pdf)). See [[System 1 and System 2]], [[Dual-process theory]].
- **Heuristics and biases** — with Amos Tversky, the program that showed judgment under uncertainty runs on shortcuts rather than statistics: anchoring, availability, representativeness ([Wikipedia, Daniel Kahneman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Kahneman); [Nobel Prize facts page](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2002/kahneman/facts/)). See [[Anchoring effect]], [[Availability heuristic]], [[Representativeness heuristic]], [[Cognitive biases]].
- **Prospect theory** — "His research on decision-making under uncertainty resulted in the formulation of a new branch of economics, prospect theory" ([Nobel Prize facts page](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2002/kahneman/facts/)). See [[Prospect theory]], [[Framing effects]].
- **Peak-end rule and duration neglect** — "such evaluations are often dominated by the discomfort at the worst and at the final moments of episodes" ([Kahneman et al. 1993](https://www.ius.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:5ae9adc9-61ec-4174-b37c-4b752f36c23b/Kahnemann%20et%20al.%20-%20When%20More%20Pain%20is%20Preferred%20to%20Less%20(1993).pdf)). See [[Peak-end rule]], [[Duration neglect]].
- **The planning fallacy and the outside view** — the finding that forecasters inside a project underestimate time, cost and risk, and the reference-class cure. See [[Planning fallacy]], [[Outside view / reference class forecasting]].
