Shantideva (Śāntideva) was an 8th-century CE Indian philosopher, Buddhist monk, poet and scholar at the great monastery-university of Nalanda, and the author of the *Bodhicaryavatara*, "a long poem describing the process of enlightenment from the first thought to full buddhahood" ([Wikipedia, Śāntideva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9A%C4%81ntideva)). Its guard-the-mind chapter carries his most quoted counsel: "By simple binding of this mind alone, / All these things are likewise bound. / By simple taming of this mind alone, / All these things are likewise tamed" ([Way of the Bodhisattva, Padmakara trans., quoted at Terebess](https://terebess.hu/english/oxherd27a.html)).

## Personal story / context

His life is known through legend and late biography. One account makes him a prince of the Saurashtra region in modern-day Gujarat, son of a King Kalyanavarman, going by the name Śantivarman; the earliest extant biography, by Vibhūticandra, instead has him born in southern India to a King Mañjuśrīvarman ([Wikipedia, Śāntideva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9A%C4%81ntideva)). The famous Nalanda legend has his fellow monks goading him into giving a talk before the whole university, seated on a large lion throne, hoping he would be exposed as unable to recite scripture and leave of his own accord; instead "Shantideva easily climbed the throne and recited stanza 9.35 of *The Way of the Bodhisattva*," after which the bodhisattva Manjushri appeared and then suddenly disappeared together with Shantideva ([Wikipedia, Śāntideva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9A%C4%81ntideva)). Two works survive, both written as training manuals for one who follows the bodhisattva path: the *Bodhicaryavatara* and the *Śikṣāsamuccaya*, the latter a nineteen-chapter compendium organized as a commentary on twenty-seven mnemonic verses ([Wikipedia, Śāntideva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9A%C4%81ntideva)).

## Their why

The poem exists because suffering has an address. Shantideva's teaching begins in the observation that all fear and suffering are mind-made at the root, and that a mind left unguarded can undo every discipline built around it. His remedy is not suppression but training: patience rehearsed on enemies, anger dissected while it rises, joy taken in others' happiness, and the deliberate exchange of one's own interests for theirs.

## Their position

The training is graded and practical. First the mind is bound: "Those who wish to keep a rule of life / Must guard their minds in perfect self-possession. / Without this guard upon the mind, / No discipline can ever be maintained" ([Way of the Bodhisattva, Padmakara trans., quoted at Terebess](https://terebess.hu/english/oxherd27a.html)). The unguarded mind is the elephant that "will bring us down to pains of deepest hell," while with the rope of mindfulness "Our fears will come to nothing, / Every virtue drop into our hands" ([Way of the Bodhisattva, Padmakara trans., quoted at Terebess](https://terebess.hu/english/oxherd27a.html)). The poem's later chapters turn this training toward others: anger is answered by tracing harm to conditions rather than persons, patience is learned from those who test it, and the self-cherishing habit is exchanged for the wish for others' happiness.

## Core ideas

- **Guarding the mind** — all discipline hangs on watchfulness over the mind. See [[K006 — Guard the mind as the root of everything downstream|Guard the mind as the root of everything downstream]].
- **Patience as a trained capacity** — anger dissected, conditions blamed rather than persons, enemies as teachers. See [[B104 — Practice patience (kshanti)|Practice patience (kshanti)]], [[B106 — Blame conditions, not persons|Blame conditions, not persons]], [[K039 — Treat the enemy as the teacher of patience|Treat the enemy as the teacher of patience]].

See [[K046 — Practice the exchange deliberately, as a skill|Practice the exchange deliberately, as a skill]], [[K045 — Understand self-cherishing as the root error|Understand self-cherishing as the root error]].
