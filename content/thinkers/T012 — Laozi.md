> **Thinker T012** — Laozi, "the Old Master," the figure behind the Daodejing; by the traditional account a keeper of archives at the Zhou court and a senior contemporary of Confucius, though the book itself is likely a composite of sayings compiled over centuries.

## Personal story / context

The biography that starts everything is the Han historian Sima Qian's, and even Sima Qian treated it cautiously. In his account Laozi was a native of the southern state of Chu, family name Li, given name Er, also styled Dan, and he kept the archival records at the Zhou court ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/laozi/)). Confucius reportedly consulted him about ritual and praised him afterward. Seeing the Zhou house decline, Laozi left; at the northwest border the customs official Yin Xi asked him to write down his teachings, and the resulting book, about five thousand characters in two parts on Dao and virtue, is the Daodejing. Then he departed, and no one knows where he went ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/laozi/)). Modern scholarship divides on whether any such person existed: some treat Laozi as entirely legendary, and the Daodejing as an anthology of sayings from many hands that reached stable form by the mid-third century BCE ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/laozi/)).

## Their why

The book is written against a diagnosis: disorder traced to striving, desire and the exaltation of the rare over the commonplace. Distinctions become harmful when they harden into hierarchies of value, and desire warps both judgment and experience ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/laozi/)). The question underneath its eighty-one chapters is practical: how to live, and how to govern, without forcing.

## Their position

The opening chapter distinguishes the dao that can be trodden or named from the enduring Dao:

> "The Tao that can be trodden is not the enduring and unchanging Tao. The name that can be named is not the enduring and unchanging name." (Daodejing 1, Legge's translation, [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/216))

Wu wei, the signature doctrine, is not inaction: the entry stresses that it contrasts action driven by self-serving desire with a mode of action aligned with ziran, what is so of itself. Its emblem is water, which benefits all things and takes the low place men dislike (ch. 8), and which defeats the hard (ch. 78). Reversal is the book's logic of change: the Dao moves by return, extremes turn into their opposites, and the sage keeps to the low, the soft and the insufficient:

> "I have three precious things which I prize and hold fast. The first is gentleness; the second is economy; and the third is shrinking from taking precedence of others." (Daodejing 67, Legge's translation, [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/216))

De is what things obtain from the Dao: "All things are produced by the Tao, and nourished by its outflowing operation" (Daodejing 51, Legge's translation, [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/216)). Governance follows the same grain: ruling a large state is like cooking a small fish, and a ruler free of desire lets the world come to peace of its own accord ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/laozi/)).

## Core ideas

- **Dao — the way that cannot be named** — the source and pattern of things. See [[D001 — Align your life with the Dao|D001]], [[Dao]], [[D016 — Distrust names and labels|D016]], [[Distrust names and labels]], [[The ten thousand things]].
- **De — embodied character-power** — what a being obtains from Dao and lives out. See [[D004 — Cultivate de — your embodied character-power|D004]], [[De]], [[A042 — Recognise that virtue is a hexis, not a feeling|A042]].
- **Wu wei — non-forcing** — action without self-serving striving. See [[D003 — Act through wu wei — non-forcing|D003]], [[D055 — Don't force|D055]], [[Wu wei]], [[Non-striving]], [[Non-contention]].
- **Ziran — naturalness** — what things are of themselves. See [[D056 — Follow the natural grain|D056]], [[Ziran (naturalness)]], [[D099 — Let things happen of themselves|D099]].
- **Reversal and return (fu)** — extremes turn; the Dao moves by return. See [[D005 — Expect reversal — extremes turn into their opposites|D005]], [[D006 — Move by return (fu) — everything renews in cycles|D006]], [[Reversal (fu)]], [[Fu (return)]], [[The Dao moves by reversal]].
- **Water and yielding** — the soft overcomes the hard. See [[D069 — Don't meet force with force|D069]], [[The water of life]], [[The soft overcomes the hard]], [[Stillness under turbulence]].
- **The empty hub and the uncarved block** — usefulness of what is not there; simplicity before carving. See [[The empty hub of the wheel]], [[Pu (the uncarved block)]], [[D045 — Keep the empty cup|D045]], [[Wu (non-being)]].
- **Knowing sufficiency (zhi zu)** — contentment as a skill. See [[D019 — Know what is enough (zhizu)|D019]], [[Zhi zu (knowing sufficiency)]], [[Sufficiency]], [[Y036 — Train contentment as a skill|Y036]].
- **Sparse speech, plain words** — [[D135 — Teach without words|D135]], [[Sparse speech]], [[Plain words over beautiful]], [[The sage teaches without words]].
- **Small country, simple life** — the governance ideal. See [[D134 — Govern like cooking a small fish|D134]], [[Small country, simple life]], [[Domination critique]], [[Spontaneous order]].
- **The three treasures** — gentleness, economy, not presuming precedence. See [[D031 — Treasure 1 — compassion (ci)|D031]], [[D032 — Treasure 2 — frugality (jian)|D032]], [[D033 — Treasure 3 — not daring to be first|D033]].
