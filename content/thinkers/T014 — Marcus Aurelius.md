Marcus Aurelius (born April 26, 121 CE as Marcus Annius Verus; died March 17, 180 CE, in a military camp in Pannonia — Sirmium per one authority, Vienna per another). He kept the Meditations in Greek, as private notes to himself, never intended for an audience. His introduction, written by the translator of the 1634/1635 comparison edition republished by Project Gutenberg (Casaubon's), notes that "he extenuates nothing, but nothing sets down in malice." He owed his Stoicism to Q. Junius Rusticus, "whom also I must thank that ever I lighted upon Epictetus his _Hypomnemata_, or moral commentaries" ([Meditations I.7](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2680/pg2680.txt)).

The opening of Book II is one continuous self-address: "Remember how long thou hast already put off these things... It is high time for thee to understand the true nature both of the world, whereof thou art a part; and of that Lord and Governor of the world, from whom, as a channel from the spring, thou thyself didst flow." ([Meditations II.1, Casaubon 1634/1635 comparison text, Project Gutenberg 2680](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2680/pg2680.txt))

## The book and its stance

The Meditations is a notebook, not a treatise. It records practice notes kept during campaigns on the Danube frontier, and opens with thanks to his family and teachers for specific virtues. Two aims recur: honest self-review, and rehearsing difficulty before it arrives.

## Philosophy of the Meditations

The work is Stoic in content. Three themes carry most of it: the dichotomy of control (some things are up to us, others are not); the discipline of desire (wanting what happens, rather than wanting reality to change); and duty to others, framed as natural kinship among rational beings.
