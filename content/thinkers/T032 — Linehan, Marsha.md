Marsha M. Linehan (born May 5, 1943, in Tulsa, Oklahoma) is an American psychologist who built dialectical behavior therapy, the first treatment shown to work for chronically suicidal people diagnosed with borderline personality disorder ([Wikipedia, Marsha Linehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsha_Linehan)). She spent her career at the University of Washington, where she was professor of psychology and of psychiatry and behavioral sciences until retiring in 2019.

## Personal story / context

Her own life is the reason the therapy exists. As a teenager she was admitted to the Institute of Living in Hartford, Connecticut, diagnosed with schizophrenia, and spent more than two years there through electroconvulsive therapy, seclusion and cold-pack treatments while struggling with self-harm and suicidal behavior; she left at twenty and returned to Tulsa, where she studied suicide at night school before taking her psychology degrees at Loyola University Chicago (B.Sc. 1968, M.A. 1970, Ph.D. 1971) ([Wikipedia, Marsha Linehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsha_Linehan)). She later trained in Zen meditation, becoming a Zen teacher, and folded what she learned there into the therapy. Looking back, she said:

> "I know what hell feels like, but even now I can't find words to describe it. Every word that comes to mind is so utterly inadequate to describe how terrible hell is. Even saying it is terrible communicates nothing about the experience. When I reflect on my life, I often realize that there is no amount of happiness in the universe that could ever balance the searing, excruciating emotional pain I experienced those many years ago." ([Wikipedia, Marsha Linehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsha_Linehan))

## Their why

The vow she made as a patient, in her own words:

> "The day when I was sitting in the piano room by myself, a lonely soul in the midst of other lonely souls in the unit, I am not sure what made me do what I did next. Whatever it was, there and then I made a vow to God that I would get myself out of hell and that, once I did, I would go back into hell and get others out. That vow has guided and controlled most of my life since then." ([Wikipedia, Marsha Linehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsha_Linehan))

Every part of DBT answers that vow: a therapy built by someone who had been the patient, for the patients everyone else had given up on.

## Their position

Linehan's position is that chronically suicidal people can be treated, and that treating them demands holding two opposites at once: complete acceptance of the person as they are, and relentless change of the behaviors that are destroying them. DBT, in her description of its skills, splits exactly that way: "The first two offer the path to acceptance of reality as it is, while the last two, taken together, are change skills that help clients embrace the changes they need to make in their lives" ([Wikipedia, Marsha Linehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsha_Linehan)). The treatment grew from trial and error with real suicidal clients, and she describes its development as having "involved much trial and error, false starts, unexpected insights, and lucky breaks as the many different components of the treatment steadily coalesced into a coherent therapy. Ultimately, I was able to conduct a strictly controlled clinical trial that demonstrated that DBT is effective in helping highly suicidal people live lives experienced as worth living, the results of which I published in 1991. Until this point there had been no effective therapy for this population; now there was" ([Wikipedia, Marsha Linehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsha_Linehan)). Her manual's skills, she says plainly, are "psychological and behavioral translations of meditation practices from eastern spiritual training" ([Wikipedia, Marsha Linehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsha_Linehan)).

## Core ideas

- **Dialectics, or "a synthesis or integration of opposites"** — the therapy's spine: accept the client fully, and push for change, without letting either cancel the other; the four skills modules are grouped the same way, mindfulness and distress tolerance for acceptance, emotion regulation and interpersonal effectiveness for change ([Wikipedia, Dialectical behavior therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectical_behavior_therapy)). See [[DBT Biosocial Theory]], [[Radical acceptance]].
- **Distress tolerance** — the capacity to survive unbearable emotion without making it worse, taught as concrete skills rather than willpower. See [[Distress tolerance (DBT)]], [[TIPP skills (DBT)]], [[Dive reflex (Linehan, Khalsa)]], [[Grounding techniques]], [[Urge surfing]].
- **Opposite action** — act against the emotion's urge: shame says hide, so show up; fear says flee, so approach. See [[Opposite action (Linehan)]], [[Facial feedback]], [[Shame]], [[The courage to act against impulse]].
- **Behaviour is learned, so it can be relearned** — skills training as the heart of treatment: rehearse, practice, acquire. See [[Skills training (DBT, CBT)]], [[Behavioural rehearsal role-play (DBT, social skills training)]], [[Value-based living]], [[Experiential avoidance theory]].
- **Wise mind and mindfulness** — the Zen inheritance: skills as translations of contemplative practice, with "wise mind" naming the meeting of emotional mind and rational mind. See [[Radical acceptance]], [[K123 — Radical acceptance|Radical acceptance]], [[K249 — Half-smile|Half-smile]].
- **Emotion dysregulation and its biosocial roots** — the theory that invalidating environments and biological vulnerability interact to produce the disorder. See [[DBT Biosocial Theory]], [[SE069 — Recognise identity disturbances — when self is fragmented|Recognise identity disturbances: when self is fragmented]], [[SE076 — Recognise clinical self-symptoms — when self-problems require evaluation|Recognise clinical self-symptoms: when self-problems require evaluation]].
