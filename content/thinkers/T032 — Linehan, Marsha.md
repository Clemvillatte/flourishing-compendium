Marsha M. Linehan (born May 5, 1943, in Tulsa, Oklahoma) is an American psychologist who developed dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) at the University of Washington, described in detail in her 1993 book on treating borderline personality disorder ([Wikipedia, Dialectical behavior therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectical_behavior_therapy); [Wikipedia, Marsha Linehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsha_Linehan)). She spent her career there as professor of psychology and of psychiatry and behavioral sciences until retiring in 2019.

## Personal story / context

As a teenager she was admitted to the Institute of Living in Hartford, Connecticut, diagnosed with schizophrenia, and spent more than two years there through electroconvulsive therapy, seclusion and cold-pack treatments while struggling with self-harm and suicidal behavior; she left at twenty, returned to Tulsa, studied suicide at night school, and took her psychology degrees at Loyola University Chicago (B.Sc. 1968, M.A. 1970, Ph.D. 1971) ([Wikipedia, Marsha Linehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsha_Linehan)). She later trained in Zen meditation and became a Zen teacher. Looking back on those years, she said:

> "I know what hell feels like, but even now I can't find words to describe it. Every word that comes to mind is so utterly inadequate to describe how terrible hell is. Even saying it is terrible communicates nothing about the experience. When I reflect on my life, I often realize that there is no amount of happiness in the universe that could ever balance the searing, excruciating emotional pain I experienced those many years ago." ([Wikipedia, Marsha Linehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsha_Linehan))

## Their why

The vow she made as a patient, in her own words:

> "The day when I was sitting in the piano room by myself, a lonely soul in the midst of other lonely souls in the unit, I am not sure what made me do what I did next. Whatever it was, there and then I made a vow to God that I would get myself out of hell and that, once I did, I would go back into hell and get others out. That vow has guided and controlled most of my life since then." ([Wikipedia, Marsha Linehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsha_Linehan))

Her research program began with hospitals and suicidal patients, and because the research grant required a formal diagnosis, the study centered on borderline personality disorder ([Wikipedia, Marsha Linehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsha_Linehan)).

## Their position

DBT holds acceptance and change together. Linehan defines the therapy as "a synthesis or integration of opposites" ([Wikipedia, Dialectical behavior therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectical_behavior_therapy)), and her own grouping of the skills follows the same split: "The first two offer the path to acceptance of reality as it is, while the last two, taken together, are change skills that help clients embrace the changes they need to make in their lives" ([Wikipedia, Marsha Linehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsha_Linehan)). The treatment, she writes, "involved much trial and error, false starts, unexpected insights, and lucky breaks," and its 1991 controlled trial "demonstrated that DBT is effective in helping highly suicidal people live lives experienced as worth living" — until then, in her words, "there had been no effective therapy for this population; now there was" ([Wikipedia, Marsha Linehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsha_Linehan)).

## Core ideas

- **Dialectics** — the therapy defined as "a synthesis or integration of opposites": therapist and client balance acceptance-oriented and change-oriented strategies, in a process compared to thesis, antithesis, synthesis ([Wikipedia, Dialectical behavior therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectical_behavior_therapy)). See [[DBT Biosocial Theory]], [[Radical acceptance]].
- **Distress tolerance** — the skills module for bearing acute pain without making it worse, one of the four modules (mindfulness, distress tolerance, emotion regulation, interpersonal effectiveness). See [[Distress tolerance (DBT)]], [[TIPP skills (DBT)]], [[Dive reflex (Linehan, Khalsa)]], [[Grounding techniques]], [[Urge surfing]].
- **Opposite action** — "Opposite action is acting opposite to the emotional urge to do or say something": when fear is unjustified, "approach what you fear rather than avoid it"; for unjustified shame, "stop hiding your behavior with people who you know won't reject you" ([Linehan, DBT Skills Training Manual, 2nd ed., reproducible teaching notes](https://www.guilford.com/add/linehan7_old/lin-p-3teaching.pdf?t=1)). See [[Opposite action (Linehan)]], [[Facial feedback]], [[Shame]], [[The courage to act against impulse]].
- **Skills training** — behaviour is learned and retrainable: the skills are "designed to solve different set of problems," and skills-training groups run weekly from her manual ([Wikipedia, Marsha Linehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsha_Linehan); [Linehan teaching notes](https://www.guilford.com/add/linehan7_old/lin-p-3teaching.pdf?t=1)). See [[Skills training (DBT, CBT)]], [[Behavioural rehearsal role-play (DBT, social skills training)]], [[BE004 — Understand that behaviour is learned — skills can be acquired|Understand that behaviour is learned: skills can be acquired]], [[Value-based living]], [[Experiential avoidance theory]].
- **Wise mind and the Zen inheritance** — the core skills are "psychological and behavioral translations of meditation practices from eastern spiritual training," and she used these in developing the term "wise mind" ([Wikipedia, Marsha Linehan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marsha_Linehan)). See [[Radical acceptance]], [[K123 — Radical acceptance|Radical acceptance]], [[K249 — Half-smile|Half-smile]].
- **A biosocial theory of the disorder** — DBT "is based upon the biosocial theory of mental illness" ([Wikipedia, Dialectical behavior therapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectical_behavior_therapy)). See [[DBT Biosocial Theory]], [[SE069 — Recognise identity disturbances — when self is fragmented|Recognise identity disturbances: when self is fragmented]], [[SE076 — Recognise clinical self-symptoms — when self-problems require evaluation|Recognise clinical self-symptoms: when self-problems require evaluation]].
