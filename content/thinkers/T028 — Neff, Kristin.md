Kristin Neff is an associate professor of educational psychology at the University of Texas at Austin who gave self-compassion its first academic studies and its standard measure, the 2003 Self-Compassion Scale ([Wikipedia, Kristin Neff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristin_Neff)). She defines the construct plainly: "Self-compassion simply involves doing a U-turn and giving yourself the same compassion you'd naturally show a friend when *you're* struggling or feeling badly about yourself" ([Neff, self-compassion.org](https://self-compassion.org/what-is-self-compassion/)).

## Personal story / context

Neff studied communication as an undergraduate at UCLA and moral development for her doctorate at Berkeley, then spent two postdoctoral years in Denver studying self-concept ([Wikipedia, Kristin Neff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristin_Neff)). Her interest began in her last year of graduate school, when she took up Buddhist meditation ([Wikipedia, Kristin Neff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristin_Neff)). In her own account: "I was going through a very messy divorce, and I was stressed about finishing my dissertation and finding a job... the lady teaching the meditation class talked a lot about self-compassion, and I just thought, 'Oh my god... this makes so much sense. Why has no one ever just said before, "You really should be kind to yourself, and it'll actually be really healthy if you are?"'" ([Neff, Greater Good interview](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/the_power_of_self_compassion)). The practice was tested when her son Rowan was diagnosed with autism: "when it really saved me was when my son was diagnosed with autism in 2004" ([Neff, Greater Good interview](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/the_power_of_self_compassion)). The family's journey with Rowan became the book and documentary *The Horse Boy* ([Wikipedia, Kristin Neff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristin_Neff)).

## Their why

Neff's motivation is the observation that people reserve their kindness for others and spend their failures on self-criticism. Her research question was whether treating oneself as one treats a friend could be measured and taught. At UT Austin she found that "no one knew how to even define self-compassion, let alone conduct research on it in academia," and decided: "I just knew that this was so important, I wanted it to be my life's work" ([Neff, Greater Good interview](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/the_power_of_self_compassion)).

## Their position

Self-compassion has three components in her model: self-kindness versus self-judgment, common humanity versus isolation, and mindfulness versus over-identification ([Wikipedia, Kristin Neff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristin_Neff)). In her words: "When we are self-compassionate, we recognize that our suffering connects us rather than separates us from others" ([Neff, self-compassion.org](https://self-compassion.org/the-three-elements-of-self-compassion-2/)). She argues the construct beats self-esteem, which carries pitfalls "such as narcissism and social comparison" and needs constant evaluation ([Wikipedia, Kristin Neff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kristin_Neff)). She insists it is not self-pity, telling the park-bench story: watching her son stim, she caught the thought "Why am I the only one who's having such a hard time?", and "my feelings of intense isolation turned into feelings of deep connection with the other mothers at the park, and with all parents everywhere" ([Neff, Five Myths](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/the_five_myths_of_self_compassion)). On a transatlantic flight, mid-tantrum, she told herself "This is so hard for you, darling... I'm sorry this is happening. I'm here for you," and found that "As I soothed myself, he was soothed as well" ([Neff, Five Myths](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/article/item/the_five_myths_of_self_compassion)).

## Core ideas

- **Self-compassion** — treating yourself with the kindness you would show a friend; the construct she defined and measured. See [[Self-compassion]].
- **Three components** — self-kindness, common humanity, and mindfulness, each defined against its failure mode (judgment, isolation, over-identification). See [[Self-compassion]], [[Mindfulness]].
- **Self-compassion over self-esteem** — a way to feel worthy without ranking yourself against others. See [[Amor sui (right self-love)]].
- **Mindful Self-Compassion (MSC)** — the eight-week training program she built with Chris Germer, and the nonprofit Center for Mindful Self-Compassion behind it. See [[Mindful self-compassion programme]].
- **Self-compassion practice** — the daily exercises her research drew from, including the self-soothing phrases above. See [[K162 — Self-compassion practice|Self-compassion practice]].
