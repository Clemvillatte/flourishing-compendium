> **Thinker T017** — James J. Gross, American psychologist at Stanford University, developer of the process model of emotion regulation and the field's most-cited researcher on emotion regulation; known for the reappraisal-versus-suppression comparison and the extended process model.

## Personal story / context

Gross is a psychologist in the Department of Psychology at Stanford University, Stanford, California ([Gross 2002, author affiliation](https://www.psychiatry.wisc.edu/courses/Nitschke/seminar/gross_2002_psychophys.pdf)). The field's organizing framework is his. Emotion regulation is now an organized research area with a standard textbook, journals and measures, and the process model is what organizes it. The 2002 Psychophysiology article carrying his early-career award statement, "Emotion regulation: Affective, cognitive, and social consequences," is the article the field cites for the definition of the field ([Gross 2002](https://www.psychiatry.wisc.edu/courses/Nitschke/seminar/gross_2002_psychophys.pdf)).

## Their why

Gross's program answers a practical question. Emotions sometimes serve us badly, and people try to regulate them. His model maps where in the emotion-generative process a strategy acts, and uses that location to predict its costs and benefits. As the 2002 article puts it, emotion regulation "refers to the processes by which we influence which emotions we have, when we have them, and how we experience and express them" ([Gross 2002](https://www.psychiatry.wisc.edu/courses/Nitschke/seminar/gross_2002_psychophys.pdf)). The model is an organizing frame, not a tested law: it "differentiates regulatory strategies by the stage of the emotion-generative process that they primarily target" ([Sheppes, Suri & Gross 2015, Annual Review of Clinical Psychology](https://people.socsci.tau.ac.il/mu/galsheppes/files/2014/10/Sheppes-Suri-Gross-2015-Annual-Review.pdf)).

## Their position

The model's core move is a location, not a content, distinction. In the 2002 article: "According to this model, emotion may be regulated at five points in the emotion generative process: (1) selection of the situation; (2) modification of the situation; (3) deployment of attention; (4) change of cognitions; and (5) modulation of experiential, behavioral, or physiological responses. The first four of these are antecedent focused, the fifth is response focused" ([Gross 2002, Figure 1 caption](https://www.psychiatry.wisc.edu/courses/Nitschke/seminar/gross_2002_psychophys.pdf)). Suppression, the late-stage strategy, is costly: it "leads to decreased positive but not negative emotion experience", increases sympathetic activation and worsens memory, while reappraisal, an earlier strategy, is not ([Gross 2015, Psychological Inquiry](http://www.johnnietfeld.com/uploads/2/2/6/0/22606800/gross_2015.pdf)). Gross later extended the model: emotion regulation as one type of valuation, with identification, selection and implementation stages before strategy choice; Sheppes and colleagues caution that "at present, there is relatively modest empirical support for the extended process model of emotion regulation" (2015).

- **The process model of emotion regulation** — five families by location in the emotion-generative process. See [[Process model of emotion regulation Gross]], [[Situation selection]], [[Attentional deployment theory]].
- **Reappraisal versus suppression** — the canonical comparison of an antecedent-focused and a response-focused strategy. See [[Expressive suppression versus reappraisal]], [[Cognitive reappraisal]], [[Anger regulation research]].
