Nāgārjuna (c. 150 – c. 250 CE) was a Buddhist monk of southern India who founded the Madhyamaka school, the "philosophy of the middle way" centered on emptiness (*śūnyatā*) ([Wikipedia, Nagarjuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarjuna); [SEP, Nagarjuna](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)). His most important work, the *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* ("Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way"), is the central text of the tradition.

## Personal story / context

Little of his life is reliably known: surviving accounts were written centuries after his death and are mostly hagiographical ([Wikipedia, Nagarjuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarjuna)). Most scholars place him somewhere in South India between the first and third centuries CE; traditional sources place him in Vidarbha, the Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang in South Kosala, and Walser argues he may have lived and worked at Amarāvatī ([Wikipedia, Nagarjuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarjuna)). A Sātavāhana king may have been his patron, which is the basis of the conventional dating ([Wikipedia, Nagarjuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarjuna)). The *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* runs about 450 stanzas expounding his whole system ([SEP, Nagarjuna](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)).

## Their why

Every way of carving the world into independent, self-existing things breaks down under analysis; cause and effect cannot be mutually independent substances, because the relation itself is conceptually constructed ([SEP, Nagarjuna](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)). His question is what remains of "intrinsic nature" (*svabhāva*) once dependence is seen everywhere — and his answer is: nothing that could anchor craving or a fixed self.

## Their position

Emptiness means the absence of *svabhāva*, an independent ontological foundation; his main target is the substance-view, not the mere existence of things ([SEP, Nagarjuna](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)). The teaching rests on two truths, and the failure to distinguish them is the failure to understand the Buddha's reality:

> "8. The Dharma teaching of the Buddha rests on two truths: conventional truth and ultimate truth. 9. Who do not know the distinction between the two truths, they do not understand reality in accordance with the profound teachings of the Buddha. 10. The ultimate truth is not taught independently of customary ways of talking and thinking. Not having acquired the ultimate truth, nirvana is not attained." (*Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* 24.8-10, quoted in [Wikipedia, Nagarjuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarjuna))

Emptiness is not a doctrine that disables the world but the condition of its working:

> "All is possible when emptiness is possible. Nothing is possible when emptiness is impossible." (*Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* 24.14, quoted in [Wikipedia, Nagarjuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarjuna))

## Core ideas

- **Emptiness (*śūnyatā*)** — the absence of inherent existence (*svabhāva*): "The Tathagata is devoid of intrinsic nature; this world is devoid of intrinsic nature" ([SEP, Nagarjuna](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/); [Wikipedia, Nagarjuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarjuna)). See [[Shunyata]], [[B010 — See emptiness — nothing has a fixed essence (shunyata)|See emptiness: nothing has a fixed essence (shunyata)]].
- **Dependent origination** — cause and effect are mutually dependent, not independently existing; the dependence analysis grounds the emptiness claim ([SEP, Nagarjuna](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)). See [[Dependent origination]], [[B004 — See conditions, not essences (paticca-samuppada)|See conditions, not essences (paticca-samuppada)]].
- **The two truths** — conventional and ultimate; nirvana is not attained without the distinction (*MMK* 24.8-10) ([Wikipedia, Nagarjuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarjuna)). See [[B122 — Use two truths (conventional and ultimate)|Use two truths (conventional and ultimate)]], [[The middle path]].
- **Samsara and nirvana not distinct** — "What is the limit of nirvana, that is the limit of samsara. There is not even the finest gap to be found between the two" (*MMK* 22.19-20) ([Wikipedia, Nagarjuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarjuna)). See [[B135 — Samsara and nirvana are one mind|Samsara and nirvana are one mind]].
- **The middle way** — Madhyamaka itself, the refusal of both extremes of permanence and annihilation ([SEP, Nagarjuna](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)). See [[B009 — Walk the Middle Way|Walk the Middle Way]], [[B123 — Refuse both extremes — nothing is permanent, nothing is nothing|Refuse both extremes: nothing is permanent, nothing is nothing]].
