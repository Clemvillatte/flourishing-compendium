Simone Adolphine Weil (3 February 1909, Paris – 24 August 1943, Ashford, England) was a French philosopher and mystic who made attention the center of moral and spiritual life ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil)). She wrote little that she published in her lifetime; her major works, including *Waiting for God* and *The Need for Roots*, appeared after her death at thirty-four.

## Personal story / context

She studied under Alain (Émile Chartier) at the Lycée Henri IV and at the École Normale Supérieure, then taught philosophy in secondary schools from 1931 ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil)). In 1934 she took a year's leave to work incognito on the assembly lines at Alstom and Renault, believing she had to know factory labour from the inside; the experience gave her the concept of affliction, *malheur* ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil)). In 1936 she joined the Republican side in the Spanish Civil War, burned herself stepping into a pot of boiling oil, and left the front ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil)). Religious experiences followed: prayer for the first time at Assisi in 1937, and in 1938 the certainty that "Christ himself came down and took possession of me" ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil)). She was drawn to Catholicism but declined baptism. She reached London in 1943 to work for the Free French; diagnosed with tuberculosis, she restricted her food to what occupied France had and died at Ashford ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil)).

## Their why

Factory labour, war and her own chronic pain taught her that suffering does not merely hurt: it crushes thought itself, since "thought flies from affliction as promptly and irresistibly as an animal flees from death" ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil)). Her question became what a person of goodwill can actually do for the afflicted, and her answer was not theory but a way of looking.

## Their position

Attention, for Weil, is the moral act itself. Her most quoted sentence states it: "Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity" ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil)). In the school-studies essay she ties study to compassion:

> "The soul empties itself of all its own contents in order to receive into itself the being it is looking at, just as he is, in all his truth." ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil))

Love of neighbour, in her formula, comes to this: asking the sufferer "What are you going through?" ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil)). Attention is also prayer, its very "substance" ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil)).

## Core ideas

- **Attention as generosity** — "Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity": the foundation of love and justice ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil)). See [[The discipline of attention]], [[Attention]], [[Attentiveness]].
- **Attention as prayer and as study** — attention is the "substance" of prayer; her essay *Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies with a View to the Love of God* is one of her clearest formulations of attention as a suspension of self so the reality of the other may come forward in its own truth ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil)). See [[Attention]], [[Knowledge by acquaintance]], [[The craft of attention]].
- **Affliction (malheur)** — suffering that destroys the very capacity to think and ask; it "constrains a man to ask continually 'why' - the question to which there is essentially no reply" ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil)). See [[Empathy]], [[Flow]].
- **The sufferer's question** — love of neighbour as asking "What are you going through?" and receiving the answer in its truth ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil)). See [[Empathy]], [[Attentiveness]].
- **Rootedness** — her 1943 book *The Need for Roots*: obligations and needs over a system of rights, the spiritual conditions of a people's recovery ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil)). See [[Rootedness]], [[D035 — Live within your ecosystem's bounds|Live within your ecosystem's bounds]].
- **Every separation is a link** — from *Gravity and Grace*: "Two prisoners whose cells adjoin communicate with each other by knocking on the wall. The wall is the thing which separates them but it is also their means of communication... Every separation is a link" ([Wikipedia, Simone Weil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simone_Weil)). See [[ST023 — Simone Weil on Attention|Simone Weil on Attention]].
