Treatment kernels are the working units of process-based therapy: small, empirically grounded change methods aimed at specific processes of change, fitted to an individual's network rather than delivered as a fixed protocol. The manual that carries the model defines them:

> "The basic elements of these strategies are treatment kernels—specific change methods designed to modify processes of change." (Hofmann, Hayes & Lorscheid, 2021, Learning Process-Based Therapy, Chapter 10)

The book's metaphor makes the design logic plain: "Metaphorically, treatment kernels can be thought of as construction elements" (Chapter 10). A builder does not accept only one brand of windows or doors; a therapist, on this model, does not accept only one school's methods. The book's examples — exposure among them — sit in the same toolbox, each proposed insofar as it is an empirically based kernel properly linked to an evidence-based process of change.

### Where the idea sits

Kernels belong to a wider shift that Hayes and Hofmann describe in World Psychiatry: "a transition in evidence-based care toward a process-based field that seeks to integrate the full range of psychosocial and contextual biological processes". They call for a turn toward process-based therapy, "in which processes, procedures and their linkage are evidence-based, and are used to alleviate the problems and promote the prosperity of people" (Hayes & Hofmann, 2017, [World Psychiatry, via PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5608815/)). A kernel is the "procedure" half of that linkage, and a process of change is the other half.

### What it is not

A kernel is not a full treatment package, and not a technique used because it is familiar. Its justification is the process it moves in the person in front of the clinician.

### Here

My practice kernels (K-prefixed entries) are written in this spirit: each names a small change method and the process it moves.
