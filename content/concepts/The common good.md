The common good names what a community owes to all its members. The Stanford Encyclopedia's working definition: "the 'common good' refers to those facilities—whether material, cultural or institutional—that the members of a community provide to all members in order to fulfill a relational obligation they all have to care for certain interests that they have in common," and the term itself "may refer either to the interests that members have in common or to the facilities that serve common interests" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, The Common Good](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/common-good/)).

### Aristotle's ground

The oldest argument for the concept is in Politics I: the city is not a convenience but the natural home of the human being. "The city-state is prior in nature to the household and to each of us individually. For the whole must necessarily be prior to the part" ([Aristotle, Politics 1253a, Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0058%3Abook%3D1%3Asection%3D1253a)). Aristotle's premise is that "man alone of the animals possesses speech," and speech exists to indicate "the advantageous and the harmful, and therefore also the right and the wrong," so that "it is partnership in these things that makes a household and a city-state" ([Politics 1253a, Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0058%3Abook%3D1%3Asection%3D1253a)). A being that reasons about right and wrong with others is, by nature, a citizen.

### Two readings of "common"

The encyclopedia splits the concept along a live divide. On a "communal" conception, citizens' common interests are interests they have as citizens, prior to their private and sectional interests, and deliberation should abstract from the latter. On a "distributive" conception, citizens belong to groups with distinct sectional interests that "make partly competing claims on the material, cultural and institutional facilities in a community," and the common good is a set of facilities that answers to everyone's interests as a distributive principle prescribes ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/common-good/)). The first reads the common good as what we share; the second, as what fairly divides.

Sandel's civic version belongs to the joint-activity family: he "described the civic conception of the common good as a process of reflecting on citizens' preferences and improving them through deliberation with fellow citizens," on which "a civic culture is one that provides spaces that cultivate civic virtue and opportunities to reason together about the kind of political community we want to create" (Sandel 2020: 236, as characterized in [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/common-good/)). The common good is not a fixed list of goods on this view; it is the ongoing practice of reasoning about it together.
