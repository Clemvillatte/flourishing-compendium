“The desert before the promised land” — Hebrew Bible, Numbers, as cultural parallel.

### What it is

In the book of Numbers, the Israelites stand at the border of Canaan, the land promised to them, and lose their nerve. The whole community demands to undo the journey: "If only we had died in the land of Egypt... or if only we might die in this wilderness!... It would be better for us to go back to Egypt!" (Numbers 14:2-3, Revised Jewish Publication Society translation (2023), [Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Numbers.14?lang=bi)). The reply sets the pattern the concept names: the generation that refused to enter is made to wander, and "your children roam the wilderness for forty years, suffering for your faithlessness" (Numbers 14:33, Revised JPS, [Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Numbers.14?lang=bi)).

### What it carries

The desert is the stretch between deliverance and fulfillment. The people's discomfort is real, and their instinct is to flee backward, to the known misery of Egypt rather than the unknown risk of the land. Numbers itself presents the forty years as punishment for faithlessness rather than as a curriculum, and my comparison to M007 keeps that difference in view. What the narrative supplies is the psychology of the middle passage: the pull to retreat is strongest exactly when arrival has become conceivable, and the demand to go back is answered, in the story, only by the next generation entering instead.
