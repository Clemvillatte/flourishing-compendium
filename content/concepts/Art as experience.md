Dewey's 1934 book on aesthetics opens by naming its task: to restore continuity between art and ordinary experience. ([Dewey, Art as Experience, 1934](https://archive.org/details/deweyjohnartasanexperience))

> "This task is to restore continuity between the refined and intensified forms of experience that are works of art and the everyday events, doings, and sufferings that are universally recognized to constitute experience." (Dewey, Art as Experience, ch. 1, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/deweyjohnartasanexperience))

The book's next sentence makes the point in an image: mountain peaks do not float unsupported. Works of art are the refined and intensified forms of what everyday life already contains. ([Dewey, Art as Experience, 1934](https://archive.org/details/deweyjohnartasanexperience))

> "Mountain peaks do not float unsupported; they do not even just rest upon the earth. They are the earth in one of its manifest operations." (Dewey, Art as Experience, ch. 1, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/deweyjohnartasanexperience))

The image carries a claim about where art gets its substance. Dewey's argument runs that a wall has been built around art by treating the painting or book as a physical object apart from experience, and that the work of art is what the product does with and in experience. To understand the aesthetic in its approved forms, he says, one must begin with it in the raw: the events and scenes that hold the attentive eye and ear of a person, the sights that hold a crowd, the mechanic absorbed in doing the job well ([Dewey, Art as Experience, ch. 1, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/deweyjohnartasanexperience)]). The concept puts the burden on attention rather than on the museum: what makes an experience aesthetic is a way of having it, not the prestige of the thing had.
