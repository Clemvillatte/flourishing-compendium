The dichotomy of control is Epictetus's division, stated in the first line of the Enchiridion: some things are up to us and some are not. Up to us are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, our own actions; not up to us are body, property, reputation, command (Enchiridion 1, [Carter translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html)).

> "Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions. Things not in our control are body, property, reputation, command, and, in one word, whatever are not our own actions." (Epictetus, Enchiridion 1, Elizabeth Carter translation, [MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html))

### What the division does

The same chapter explains the division's stakes: things in our control are "by nature free, unrestrained, unhindered," while things not in our control are "weak, slavish, restrained, belonging to others" (Enchiridion 1, [Carter translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html)). Mistaking the slavish for free has consequences: "You will lament, you will be disturbed, and you will find fault both with gods and men" (Enchiridion 1, [Carter translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html)). The chapter also turns the division into a test for appearances: every "harsh appearance" is to be examined first by "whether it concerns the things which are in our own control, or those which are not" (Enchiridion 1, [Carter translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html)). If an appearance concerns something outside our control, Epictetus says to "be prepared to say that it is nothing to you" (Enchiridion 1, [Carter translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html)).

### The compendium's refinement

The chapter sorts into two piles; the compendium's own comparison sorts into three. S053 distinguishes what you control (judgements, choices, effort), what you influence but do not control (other people's behaviour, outcomes of projects), and what you cannot control at all (the weather, the past); S053's own page cites the same Discourses passages and Enchiridion 1. The acceptance-and-commitment principle P157 sorts the same field into its two halves: contacting what is without defense, and persisting in the service of chosen values. These are the compendium's editorial comparisons, not Epictetus's own terms.
