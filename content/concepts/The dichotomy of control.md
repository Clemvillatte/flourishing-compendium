The dichotomy of control is Epictetus's division, stated in the first line of the Enchiridion: some things are up to us and some are not. Up to us are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, our own actions; not up to us are body, property, reputation, command.

> "Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions. Things not in our control are body, property, reputation, command, and, in one word, whatever are not our own actions." (Epictetus, Enchiridion 1, Long translation, [MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html))

### What the division does

The chapter's second movement gives the logic: things in our control are "by nature free, unrestrained, unhindered," while things not in our control are "weak, slavish, restrained, belonging to others." Misjudge which is which and you get the complete symptom list: "You will lament, you will be disturbed, and you will find fault both with gods and men." The division is also diagnostic equipment. Every "harsh appearance" is to be examined first by whether it concerns what is ours; if not, "be prepared to say that it is nothing to you."

### The coarse edge

The dichotomy is deliberately blunt, and later pages sharpen it. Much of life sits between the two piles: health is body, yet habits influence it; other people's choices are theirs, yet our conduct sways them. The compendium's control-versus-influence principle (S053) works that refinement, and the serenity-prayer parallel (P157) carries the same sorting into a modern form. Chapter 1 closes the point: aim at both the great things and at power and riches and you gain neither, "by which alone happiness and freedom are achieved."
