Mentalization-based treatment (MBT), developed by Anthony Bateman and Peter Fonagy, is "an evidence-based treatment for borderline personality disorder (BPD)" that focuses "specifically on the mentalizing vulnerabilities of the patient in the context of an understanding of attachment process" ([Bateman and Fonagy, MBT chapter, UCL repository](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1489633/3/Fonagy_MBT%20chapter%20for%20Kaplan%20and%20Sadock%20complete%20071015.pdf)). The early partial-hospital programme worked "in the context of group and individual psychotherapy" on "increasing the patient's capacity for mentalization" ([Mentalization-based treatment of BPD, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15061343/)). See [[Mentalization theory]] for the concept itself.

### How sessions work

Assessment covers the patient's "mentalizing vulnerabilities and mentalizing profile", "non-mentalizing cycles", and "a shared formulation, which includes specific detail of attachment patterns and areas of vulnerability to emotional dysregulation" (UCL chapter). Fonagy and Luyten describe the therapist's task: "to promote curiosity about the way mental states motivate and explain the actions of the self and others", through "an inquisitive stance" and by "qualifying their own understanding and inferences (in that mental states are opaque)" ([Attachment, Mentalization, and the Self, UCL repository](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1535395/1/Fonagy%20Luyten_with%20figures.pdf)). A paper on psychosis lists four principles: "a 'not-knowing' therapeutic stance, unassuming interventions, a focus on currently felt affect, and careful adjustment to the patient's level of mentalization and arousal" ([Mentalization and Psychosis, Springer](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10879-019-09449-0.pdf)).

### Trial evidence

The original partial-hospital trial led to follow-ups. The MBT chapter reports an 8-year follow-up in which, relative to treatment as usual, suicidality was 23% versus 74% and diagnostic status 13% versus 87%. It also says the long-term follow-up shows lower social and relational satisfaction "than expected, although the benefit of the therapy remains possible to detect". A systematic review of 14 papers found MBT achieved "either superior or equal reductions in psychiatric symptoms" compared with other treatments, including supportive group therapy, usual psychiatric care and structured clinical management ([Is mentalization-based therapy effective in treating the symptoms of BPD?, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6900007/)).

An outpatient trial of 134 patients found "substantial improvements" in both MBT and structured clinical management ([Mentalization based treatment for borderline personality disorder, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2816926/)).

### Where results are mixed

An Australian trial of 72 participants compared 18 months of MBT with enhanced case management. "Both groups showed a significant reduction", and the authors report differences between groups that "will require further research" ([Randomized-Controlled Trial of MBT, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33262710/)). Fonagy and Luyten note that the model "makes the strong and so far almost unwarranted assumption" that rising mentalizing capacity drives symptom improvement, and the MBT chapter says "the evidence for mentalizing mediating therapeutic change in MBT is currently limited". A trial of MBT for comorbid BPD and antisocial personality disorder states that it is "not adequately powered to demonstrate real differences" and limited to "feasibility rather than a demonstration of effectiveness" ([PMC5006360](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5006360/)).

### Scope

MBT is a clinical treatment delivered by trained therapists, and the evidence above concerns personality disorder. The wiki should not present it as self-help.
