Forgiveness is the release of a debt: the decision and the slow work of ending resentment and the claim to repayment, without erasing the wrong or excusing it. The word's two philosophical landmarks are Hannah Arendt's The Human Condition (1958), where forgiveness remedies the irreversibility of action, and Jacques Derrida's On Cosmopolitanism and Forgiveness (2001), where it must remain impossible. Psychology adds a working distinction between deciding to forgive and emotionally replacing resentment.

### Arendt's remedy

Arendt's setting is action itself. Acts cannot be undone, and "without being forgiven, released from the consequences of what we have done, our capacity to act would, as it were, be confined to one single deed from which we could never recover" ([Arendt, The Human Condition, p. 237](https://dl1.cuni.cz/pluginfile.php/1188468/mod_resource/content/1/Arendt_Human_Condition_Forgiveness.pdf)). Forgiveness, in her words, "acts anew and unexpectedly, unconditioned by the act which provoked it and therefore freeing from its consequences both the one who forgives and the one who is forgiven."

It depends on plurality, "for no one can forgive himself," and its alternative is punishment, "but by no means its opposite": both attempt "to put an end to something that without interference could go on endlessly." What we cannot punish, she argues, we also cannot forgive; that is her mark of radical evil.

### Derrida's impossible

Derrida radicalizes the limit. "If there is forgiveness, it must forgive the unforgivable"; "if one had to forgive only what is forgivable... then one would not forgive" ([Derrida, quoted in Ivic 2021](https://www.scielo.br/j/fun/a/zNpGfVzwpX36jBKp78xdvfH/?lang=en)). Pure forgiveness must remain "exceptional and extraordinary, in the face of the impossible," outside any exchange of apology for absolution. The scandal he presses on is that this pure forgiveness and the ordinary, conditional work of reconciliation are irreducible to each other and yet indissociable: a forgiveness that changed nothing would not be forgiveness at all.

### What the interventions show

The clinical literature is more modest and testable. A review of five meta-analyses of forgiveness interventions found they raise forgiveness relative to no treatment, with more hope and less depression and anxiety; process-based programmes beat shorter decision-based ones, and effective components include recalling the offense, empathizing with the offender, committing to forgive and overcoming unforgiveness ([Recine 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25487180/)). The reviewers flag inconsistent measures and no agreed definition of forgiveness, which keeps the evidence practical rather than settled.
