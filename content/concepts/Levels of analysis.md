“Levels of analysis” — Marr, 1982.

David Marr's Vision set the framework cognitive science still uses: an information-processing device is not understood at one level but three. His summary passage:

> "At one extreme, the top level, is the abstract computational theory of the device, in which the performance of the device is characterized as a mapping from one kind of information to another, the abstract properties of this mapping are defined precisely, and its appropriateness and adequacy for the task at hand are demonstrated. In the center is the choice of representation for the input and output and the algorithm to be used to transform one into the other. And at the other extreme are the details of how the algorithm and representation are realized physically—the detailed computer architecture, so to speak. These three levels are coupled, but only loosely." ([Marr, Vision, 1982, ch. 1](https://qiongzhang.github.io/Marr.pdf))

### Why the looseness matters

The three levels answer different questions: what the system is for, how it does it, and what it is made of. Because the coupling is loose, a change at one level does not dictate the others, and a full account requires all three. The same cash-register algorithm can live in wires or in a child's pencil work, Marr notes, while the computational theory of a process is determined by the task, not by the hardware.

The compendium's parallels put the framework to contemplative use: the ontological difference between levels of being, the two truths that hold conventional and ultimate descriptions side by side, and the study of the commanding self as a specific opponent each rely on keeping levels distinct instead of letting one collapse into another.
