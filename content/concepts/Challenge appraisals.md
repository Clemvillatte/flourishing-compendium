Challenge appraisals are the interpretation of a stressor as something one can grow through rather than be harmed by. The concept's modern evidence base is the stress-mindset research of Crum, Salovey and Achor, which distinguishes two beliefs about the same pressure:

> "In the context of stress, one's stress mindset can be conceptualized as the extent to which one holds the belief that stress has enhancing consequences for various stress-related outcomes such as performance and productivity, health and wellbeing, and learning and growth (referred to as a 'stress-is-enhancing mindset')" ([Crum, Salovey & Achor, 2013](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/crum_salovey_anchor_2013_-_rethinking_stress.pdf))

### What the appraisal carries

The findings run through belief, feeling and body. Those holding the enhancing mindset "reported having better health than those who endorse a stress-is-debilitating mindset: specifically, respondents reported fewer symptoms of depression and anxiety while also reporting higher levels of energy." The mindset is also movable: "stress mindsets can be altered by watching short, multimedia film clips presenting factual information biased toward defining the nature of stress in 1 of 2 ways." The appraisal is therefore not positive thinking about outcomes but a judgement about the nature of the demand: high enough stakes to matter, and resources equal to it. My principle P046 carries the applied reading: treat problems as invitations, since the challenge framing changes what the pressure produces. A compendium exercise drawn from the appraisal is to take one current stressor and write, first, what it threatens, and second, what it trains. What the appraisal does not give: a denial of overload; a challenge appraisal of a genuinely excessive demand does not shrink the demand, and the enhancing mindset is a claim about stress, not about everything.
