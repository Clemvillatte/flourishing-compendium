“Distinguishing practice from theory” — Buddhism: raft parable.

The Buddha's raft simile, from the Alagaddūpama Sutta (MN 22), draws the line between a practice and the theory carried around with it. The Dhamma is introduced as a crossing device: "Monks, I will teach you the Dhamma compared to a raft, for the purpose of crossing over, not for the purpose of holding onto" ([Thanissaro translation, MN 22](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.022.than.html)). In the simile a traveler sees "a great expanse of water, with the near shore dubious & risky, the further shore secure & free from risk", gathers "grass, twigs, branches, & leaves", and crosses over on the raft he binds from them.

The mistake is what happens after crossing. The man who asks "Why don't I, having hoisted it on my head or carrying it on my back, go wherever I like?" is told he is not doing "what should be done with the raft"; the right response is to leave it, "having dragged it on dry land or sinking it in the water". The closing instruction carries the distinction to the teachings themselves:

> In the same way, monks, I have taught the Dhamma compared to a raft, for the purpose of crossing over, not for the purpose of holding onto. Understanding the Dhamma as taught compared to a raft, you should let go even of Dhammas, to say nothing of non-Dhammas. ([Thanissaro translation, MN 22](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.022.than.html))

The compendium reads the parable as a test to apply to its own material: a teaching is for crossing over, and its worth shows in the crossing, not in the carrying. Modern parallels include the case of practices that work while their popular theory remains contested, and the recognition that some things are not fixable by any method.
