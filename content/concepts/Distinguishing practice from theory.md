The raft simile in the Alagaddūpama Sutta (MN 22) compares the Dhamma to a means of crossing that should not be carried after its purpose has been fulfilled ([Thanissaro translation, MN 22](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.022.than.html)). The Dhamma is introduced as a crossing device: "Monks, I will teach you the Dhamma compared to a raft, for the purpose of crossing over, not for the purpose of holding onto" ([Thanissaro translation, MN 22](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.022.than.html)). In the simile a traveler sees "a great expanse of water, with the near shore dubious & risky, the further shore secure & free from risk", gathers "grass, twigs, branches, & leaves", and crosses over on the raft he binds from them ([Thanissaro translation, MN 22](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.022.than.html)).

The mistake is what happens after crossing. The man who asks "Why don't I, having hoisted it on my head or carrying it on my back, go wherever I like?" is told he is not doing "what should be done with the raft"; the right response is to leave it, "having dragged it on dry land or sinking it in the water" ([Thanissaro translation, MN 22](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.022.than.html)). The closing instruction carries the distinction to the teachings themselves:

> In the same way, monks, I have taught the Dhamma compared to a raft, for the purpose of crossing over, not for the purpose of holding onto. Understanding the Dhamma as taught compared to a raft, you should let go even of Dhammas, to say nothing of non-Dhammas. ([Thanissaro translation, MN 22](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.022.than.html))

The compendium applies the simile as an analogy to the practice/theory distinction: a teaching is a crossing device, and its worth shows in the crossing, not in the carrying. That application is the compendium's, not the sutta's stated distinction.
