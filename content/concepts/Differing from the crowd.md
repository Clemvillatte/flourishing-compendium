Chapter 20 of the Daodejing is a first-person portrait of someone who feels out of step with everyone around them and values what they hold anyway. In James Legge's 1891 translation the speaker says: "The multitude of men look satisfied and pleased; as if enjoying a full banquet" ([Legge, Daodejing 20](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/216/216-h/216-h.htm)).

### What the chapter says

The speaker sets themselves against the crowd: "I alone seem listless and still, my desires having as yet given no indication of their presence. I am like an infant which has not yet smiled." Other people have "enough and to spare", while "I alone seem to have lost everything." The chapter ends with the line "(Thus) I alone am different from other men, but I value the nursing-mother (the Tao)" ([Legge, Daodejing 20](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/216/216-h/216-h.htm)). The chapter opens with "When we renounce learning we have no troubles", a line that sets up the contrast between conventional cleverness and the speaker's plainness.

### How to read it

The speaker is not boasting. The description is of discomfort, "dejected and forlorn", and of difference that is accepted, with attention fixed on the Tao as the source that nourishes. Translators differ on tone and word choice, so the wording here is Legge's alone.

### Scope

Chapter 20 describes a feeling and a value. It does not give advice on when to follow a crowd and when not to, and it does not say that being different is a mark of wisdom. Applying it to modern pressure to conform is the reader's inference.
