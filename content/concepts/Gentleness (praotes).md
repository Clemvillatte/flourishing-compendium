“Gentleness (praotes)” — [[T002 — Aristotle|Aristotle]], NE IV.5.

Praotēs is Aristotle's name for the mean concerned with anger, discussed in Book IV of the Nicomachean Ethics ([Aristotle, NE IV, Ross translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.4.iv.html)). Ross's translation gives the calibration:

> "The man who is angry at the right things and with the right people, and, further, as he ought, when he ought, and as long as he ought, is praised. This will be the good-tempered man, then, since good temper is praised." (Aristotle, [Nicomachean Ethics IV.5, translated by W. D. Ross, Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.4.iv.html))

### The extremes around the mean

The chapter locates gentleness between two failures, both blamed. The deficiency reads as a lack of feeling for one's own case:

> "The deficiency, whether it is a sort of 'inirascibility' or whatever it is, is blamed. For those who are not angry at the things they should be angry at are thought to be fools, and so are those who are not angry in the right way, at the right time, or with the right persons; for such a man is thought not to feel things nor to be pained by them, and, since he does not get angry, he is thought unlikely to defend himself; and to endure being insulted and put up with insult to one's friends is slavish." (Aristotle, [Nicomachean Ethics IV.5, translated by W. D. Ross, Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.4.iv.html))

The excess comes in two tempers:

> "Now hot-tempered people get angry quickly and with the wrong persons and at the wrong things and more than is right, but their anger ceases quickly-which is the best point about them. By reason of excess choleric people are quick-tempered and ready to be angry with everything and on every occasion; whence their name." (Aristotle, [Nicomachean Ethics IV.5, translated by W. D. Ross, Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.4.iv.html))

Both extremes fail at the same job, knowing what is worth defending and when: the slavish person cannot defend it, the choleric defends everything at once, and the good-tempered person is praised for the fit of the anger to its occasion rather than for its smallness. The chapter's hesitation about the deficiency's name is itself instructive. The excess had a ready name in choleric, while the absence of anger at insults was so far from a virtue that the vocabulary barely covered it, and the slavish tag does the moral work the missing name would have done.
