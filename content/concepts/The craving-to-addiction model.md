### What it is

The craving-to-addiction model is the account of addictive behavior developed by the psychiatrist Judson Brewer and colleagues from their work on mindfulness training for smoking cessation. Addiction here is a learned loop: a cue triggers a feeling, the feeling triggers craving, and acting on the craving relieves the feeling, which reinforces the association. Brewer, Elwafi and Davis note that ancient Buddhist models of suffering and modern addiction science converge: "these early models show remarkable similarity to current models of the addictive process, especially in their overlap with operant conditioning (positive and negative reinforcement)" (Brewer, Elwafi & Davis, 2013, p. 366, [author manuscript, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3434285/)).

### Where it comes from

The model was articulated in the 2013 review "Craving to Quit", which compared Buddhist analyses of craving with behavioral and neurobiological research on addiction. The authors name the pattern directly: "resulting in what, building on the work of Baker, Curtin and others... for convenience we term the 'addictive loop'" (Brewer, Elwafi & Davis, 2013, [author manuscript, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3434285/)).

### Core claims

The loop is assembled by ordinary learning: smoking becomes tied to positive states, such as feeling relaxed after a good meal, and to negative ones, such as stress relief, so cues for either state trigger craving on their own. Their illustrative smoker finds "himself with a half-smoked cigarette sitting between his fingers" (Brewer, Elwafi & Davis, 2013, Text Box 1, [author manuscript, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3434285/)).

Repetition then removes the loop from view: "through repeated smoking, this addictive loop may become automated or habitual, leading to cue-induced behavior that is largely outside of consciousness, let alone conscious control" (Brewer, Elwafi & Davis, 2013, [author manuscript, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3434285/)).

Avoidance of cues dampens input into the loop, and substitute behaviors such as candy or a walk sidestep the behavior, yet "neither of these strategies dismantles the addictive loop" durably (Brewer, Elwafi & Davis, 2013, Figure 1 caption, [author manuscript, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3434285/)).

### How it explains the Buddhist parallel

The model gives B006 a mechanism in the language of learning theory. Craving here is an acquired pattern, strengthened each time acting on it brings relief. Mindfulness intervenes by letting the craving be felt without being obeyed, so the reinforcement stops and the association fades. In their trial data, the correlation between daily cigarette use and self-reported craving fell from r = 0.58 at the start of training to r = 0.13, statistically insignificant, at week four (Brewer, Elwafi & Davis, 2013, Table 1, [author manuscript, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3434285/)). As the authors put it, "mindfulness training may have decoupled the relationship between craving and smoking during treatment," and over time, "without continued sustenance (smoking), the fire burns out by itself" (Brewer, Elwafi & Davis, 2013, [author manuscript, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3434285/)).

### An everyday example

A person reaches for the phone after every difficult email. The notification is the cue, the buzz of relief is the reward, and the reaching becomes automatic. The model recommends noticing the itch without scratching it, repeatedly.
