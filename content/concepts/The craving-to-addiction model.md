craving is a learned trigger-response loop that mindfulness can unwind (Brewer, 2013).

### What it is

I use this title for the "addictive loop" of Brewer, Elwafi and Davis's review of mindfulness training for addictions. Addiction here is a learned loop: a cue triggers a state, the state triggers craving, and acting on the craving maintains or improves positive affect or reduces negative affect, reinforcing the association. The authors note that ancient Buddhist models of suffering and modern addiction science converge: "these early models show remarkable similarity to current models of the addictive process, especially in their overlap with operant conditioning (positive and negative reinforcement)" (Brewer, Elwafi & Davis, 2013, Abstract, [author manuscript, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3434285/)).

### Where it comes from

The account was articulated in the 2013 review "Craving to Quit", which compared Buddhist analyses of craving with behavioral and neurobiological research. The authors name the pattern directly: "for convenience we term the 'addictive loop'" (Brewer, Elwafi & Davis, 2013, [author manuscript, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3434285/)).

### Core claims

The loop is assembled by ordinary learning: smoking becomes tied to positive states, such as feeling relaxed after a good meal, and to negative ones, such as stress, so cues for either state trigger craving on their own. Repetition then removes the loop from view: "Through repeated smoking, this addictive loop may become automated or habitual, leading to cue-induced behavior that is largely outside of consciousness, let alone conscious control" (Brewer, Elwafi & Davis, 2013, [author manuscript, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3434285/)).

Avoidance of cues dampens input into the loop, and substitute behaviors such as candy or a walk sidestep the behavior, yet "neither of these strategies dismantles the addictive loop" (Brewer, Elwafi & Davis, 2013, Figure 1 caption, [author manuscript, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3434285/)).

### How it explains the Buddhist parallel

The model gives B006, the principle that craving drives suffering, a psychological parallel in the language of learning theory. On the authors' account, craving is an acquired pattern, strengthened each time acting on it brings relief; mindfulness intervenes by letting it be felt without being obeyed, so the reinforcement stops. Their evidence comes from a mindfulness training trial for smokers: at the start of training, daily cigarette use and self-reported craving correlated at r = 0.58; at week four the correlation was r = 0.13, statistically insignificant; correlations reappeared at follow-ups as quitters' craving fell and continuing smokers' rose. The authors read this cautiously: "One interpretation of this is that mindfulness training may have decoupled the relationship between craving and smoking during treatment." Over time, "without continued sustenance (smoking), the fire burns out by itself" (Brewer, Elwafi & Davis, 2013, [author manuscript, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3434285/)).

### An everyday example

The authors' case is a smoker who lights up after being yelled at by his boss: the scolding is the cue, the stress the state, the cigarette the relief, and the relief reinforces the reaching. His smoking becomes so automatic that he finds "himself with a half-smoked cigarette sitting between his fingers" (Brewer, Elwafi & Davis, 2013, Text Box 1, [author manuscript, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3434285/)).
