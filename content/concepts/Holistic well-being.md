“Holistic well-being” — Ryff, 1989.

Carol Ryff's 1989 paper asked what psychological well-being actually is, and found that the reigning measures had little theoretical grounding, despite an extensive literature on the contours of positive functioning. Her opening move:

> "Reigning measures of psychological well-being have little theoretical grounding, despite an extensive literature on the contours of positive functioning." ([Ryff, 1989, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 57(6)](https://thefpr.org/wp-content/uploads/Ryff1989_happiness.pdf))

### Six aspects, one person

Ryff drew on that literature of positive functioning and operationalized its aspects: "self-acceptance, positive relations with others, autonomy, environmental mastery, purpose in life, and personal growth." The design gives the concept its holistic character: well-being is measured as a profile across the facets of a life, how one stands toward oneself, toward others, toward environments, and toward one's own future. Three hundred and twenty-one men and women, "divided among young, middle-aged, and older adults," rated themselves on these measures along with six earlier instruments, including affect balance, life satisfaction and depression.

### The eudaimonic thread

The theory beneath the dimensions is, in Ryff's reading, Aristotelian in spirit. She argues that [[T002 — Aristotle|Aristotle]]'s eudaimonia might better have been translated as realization of one's true potential rather than as happiness, and that "Had Aristotle's view of eudaimonia as the highest of all good been translated as realization of one's true potential rather than as happiness, the past 20 years of research on psychological well-being might well have taken different directions." On this view, feeling good is not the target; functioning well is. Personal growth makes the point sharply, since Ryff frames it as a requirement that runs beyond any achieved state of functioning, a matter of continuing "to develop one's potential, to grow and expand as a person."

### Here

The idea crosses my tracks: a principle on connection to mind and emotions picks up the inside of the profile, a principle on Te Whare Tapa Whā offers a Māori model of well-being, and a principle on the sociocultural level treats the settings in which wellness is defined.
