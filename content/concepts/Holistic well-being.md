“Holistic well-being” — Ryff, 1989.

Carol Ryff's 1989 paper asked what psychological well-being actually is, and answered that the reigning measures had no theory behind them. Her opening move:

> "Reigning measures of psychological well-being have little theoretical grounding, despite an extensive literature on the contours of positive functioning." ([Ryff, 1989, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 57(6)](https://thefpr.org/wp-content/uploads/Ryff1989_happiness.pdf))

### Six aspects, one person

Ryff drew on that literature of positive functioning and operationalized its aspects: "self-acceptance, positive relations with others, autonomy, environmental mastery, purpose in life, and personal growth." The design gives the concept its holistic character. Well-being is not a single score but a profile across the facets of a life: how one stands toward oneself, toward others, toward environments, and toward one's own future. The study behind the paper rated 321 men and women, "divided among young, middle-aged, and older adults," on these measures alongside six earlier instruments, including affect balance, life satisfaction and depression.

### The eudaimonic thread

The theory beneath the dimensions is Aristotelian in spirit. Ryff notes that Aristotle's eudaimonia might better have been translated as realization of one's true potential rather than as happiness, and that "Had Aristotle's view of eudaimonia as the highest of all good been translated as realization of one's true potential rather than as happiness, the past 20 years of research on psychological well-being might well have taken different directions." On this view, feeling good is not the target; functioning well is. Personal growth makes the point sharply, since Ryff frames it as a requirement that runs beyond any achieved state of functioning, a matter of continuing "to develop one's potential, to grow and expand as a person."

### In the compendium

The idea crosses the compendium's tracks: a principle on connection to mind and emotions picks up the inside of the profile, the Te Whare Tapa Whā principle offers a Māori model that widens the frame further than Ryff, from body to spirit to land, and a principle on the sociocultural level notes that cultures disagree about which aspects of a life count as wellness.
