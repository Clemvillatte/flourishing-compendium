subjective time dilates with novelty and attention (Wittmann, 2016).

## What the research shows

Subjective duration is not a readout of clock time. In Marc Wittmann's review of the field, two factors reliably stretch or shrink it: attention paid to time, and physiological arousal.

> “In summary, increased attention to time (such as in waiting situations) and an increase in physiological arousal (such as under stress) can lead to longer time estimates when judging intervals in the range of milliseconds to seconds and minutes.”
>
> — Marc Wittmann, [The inner experience of time](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2685813/), 2009

## Novelty and fear

The dilation extends to novelty. The duration of a deviant stimulus within a stream of standard events tends to be systematically overestimated, though Wittmann and colleagues found it is not oddball-ness alone that carries the effect, but the looming signal moving toward the viewer. Fear works on the same lever: novice skydivers who reported stronger fear during their first tandem jump experienced its duration as lasting longer, with subjective estimates of roughly thirty minutes. The reverse also holds: “Time speeds up when we are involved in pleasant activities, but it drags during periods of boredom.”

## The embodied self

Wittmann's interpretation ties the whole field to the body. There is no sense organ for time; duration is felt through the bodily self, with the insular cortex, the region that integrates body signals such as the heartbeat, active during timing tasks. In his words, “We don't perceive time in the outside world but through our sense of self,” and “The more we feel ourselves, the slower felt passage of time.”
