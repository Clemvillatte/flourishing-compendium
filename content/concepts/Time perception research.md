## What the research shows

Subjective duration is not a readout of clock time. In Wittmann's (2009) review of the field, attention and arousal are discussed as influences on duration judgments, and the review also records paradoxical underestimation for some emotional stimuli.

> “In summary, increased attention to time (such as in waiting situations) and an increase in physiological arousal (such as under stress) can lead to longer time estimates when judging intervals in the range of milliseconds to seconds and minutes.”
>
> — Marc Wittmann, [The inner experience of time](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2685813/), Phil Trans R Soc B 364 (2009), section 4, “The emotional experience of time”

## Novelty and dilation

Time dilation has been shown for stimuli that differ from standard events: the duration of a deviant stimulus within a stream of standard events tends to be systematically overestimated. In one imaging experiment, Wittmann and colleagues found a dilation effect only for looming targets moving toward the viewer, and not for receding ones, suggesting it is not oddball-ness alone that carries the effect (Wittmann et al., 2010).

Duration judgments also track mood: the review's introduction notes that time speeds up during pleasant activities and drags during boredom (Wittmann, 2009). In the review's section on emotional experience, novice skydivers who reported stronger fear during a first tandem jump judged its duration as longer than did less fearful novices (Wittmann, 2009).

## Wittmann's account

Wittmann's own interpretation is embodied. There is no sense organ for time, and he relates the felt passage of longer durations to the insular cortex, the primary brain region for processing body signals. As he put it in an interview: “We don't perceive time in the outside world but through our sense of self,” and “The more we feel ourselves, the slower felt passage of time.”
