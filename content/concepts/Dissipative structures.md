Dissipative structures are ordered patterns that exist only while energy or matter flows through them. The term is Ilya Prigogine's. The 1977 Nobel Prize in Chemistry cited Prigogine "for his contributions to non-equilibrium thermodynamics, particularly the theory of dissipative structures" ([Nobel Prize summary](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1977/summary/)). His one-sentence version of the discovery:

> "It is shown that non-equilibrium may become a source of order and that irreversible processes may lead to a new type of dynamic states of matter called 'dissipative structures'." (Prigogine, [Nobel Lecture, 1977](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/prigogine-lecture.pdf), abstract)

### Order fed by flow

Prigogine's examples do not behave like equilibrium order. A fluid heated from below stays at rest until a critical temperature gradient is crossed; then convection begins and a pattern of rolling cells appears, in which "a macroscopic number of molecules have to move in a coherent fashion over macroscopic times to realize the flow pattern." In his words, "above some critical value certain fluctuations are amplified and give rise to a macroscopic current. A new supermolecular order appears which corresponds basically to a giant fluctuation stabilized by exchanges of energy with the outside world. This is the order characterized by the occurrence of 'dissipative structures'" ([Nobel Lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/prigogine-lecture.pdf)). In the heated-fluid example, the organized flow depends on the maintained temperature gradient and the energy exchange with the outside world.

His compact statement of the machinery: "There are three aspects which are always linked in dissipative structures: the function as expressed by the chemical equations, the space-time structure, which results from the instabilities, and the fluctuations, which trigger the instabilities" (same text).
