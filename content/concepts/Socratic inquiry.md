Socratic inquiry is the question-driven search Plato dramatizes, and its home text is the Theaetetus, where Socrates explains his method to a young mathematician by way of the midwife. The midwife delivers children; his art delivers thoughts:

> "And therefore I am not myself at all wise, nor have I anything to show which is the invention or birth of my own soul, but those who converse with me profit." (Plato, [Theaetetus](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/theatu.html), 150c, translated by Benjamin Jowett)

### How the inquiry runs

The pains belong to the inquirer, not to Socrates. "Dire are the pangs which my art is able to arouse and to allay in those who consort with me, just like the pangs of women in childbirth," he says, and he suspects Theaetetus has a thought in him ready to be delivered. The progress, when it comes, belongs to the inquirer too: "the many fine discoveries to which they cling are of their own making. But to me and the god they owe their delivery."

The dialogue then runs the method to its end. Socrates asks what knowledge is, each answer Theaetetus offers is examined and found wanting, and the inquiry turns back on itself: "Once more we shall have to begin, and ask 'What is knowledge?' and yet, Theaetetus, what are we going to do?" The unresolved ending is the lesson. A Socratic inquiry is not a lecture one receives but a labor one undergoes, and its result is a better question plus a person who has felt the difference between a settled opinion and an examined one.
