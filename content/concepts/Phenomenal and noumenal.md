"Phenomenal and noumenal" — Kant, Critique of Pure Reason.

Kant's distinction marks the boundary of knowledge. Space and time are the forms of our intuition, so the objects we experience are appearances, phenomena; the thing as it is apart from our intuition, the noumenon, cannot be presented. The noumenon survives in the system only as a limit: the marker of what the boundary excludes, not a second class of knowable objects.

> "The conception of a noumenon is therefore merely a limitative conception and therefore only of negative use. But it is not an arbitrary or fictitious notion, but is connected with the limitation of sensibility, without, however, being capable of presenting us with any positive datum beyond this sphere." (Kant, Critique of Pure Reason, [Meiklejohn translation, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/4280/4280-h/4280-h.htm))
>
> "It is therefore not merely possible or probable, but indubitably certain, that space and time, as the necessary conditions of all our external and internal experience, are merely subjective conditions of all our intuitions, in relation to which all objects are therefore mere phenomena, and not things in themselves, presented to us in this particular manner." (Kant, Critique of Pure Reason, [Meiklejohn translation, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/4280/4280-h/4280-h.htm))
