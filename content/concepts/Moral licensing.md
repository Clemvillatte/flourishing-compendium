Moral licensing names the finding that a person's own record of good behavior can make later questionable behavior more likely: past virtue is experienced as a credential that licenses transgression. The founding study is Monin and Miller's 2001 paper on moral credentials and the expression of prejudice, whose abstract states the hypothesis:

> "Three experiments supported the hypothesis that people are more willing to express attitudes that could be viewed as prejudiced when their past behavior has established their credentials as nonprejudiced persons." ([Monin & Miller, 2001, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11474723/))

In the studies, participants who had disagreed with blatantly sexist statements were later more willing to favor a man for a stereotypically male job, and those who had selected a member of a stereotyped group for one job were more likely to reject a member of that group for another ([2001 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11474723/)).

### Replication status

The effect has not held up cleanly. A 2024 direct replication of Study 2 with over nine hundred participants reported:

> "We found no support for a consistent moral credential effect: the effect was close to zero in a scenario where participants indicated their preferences to hire from different ethnicities (d = 0.02 to 0.08, depending on inclusion criteria), and was in the opposite direction in a scenario where they indicated preferences for different genders (d = –0.50 to –0.38)." ([Xiao et al., 2024, Replication Registered Report](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12372676/))

My use is cautionary rather than diagnostic: D184 (Don't over-cultivate the appearance of virtue) treats the advertised-virtue pattern as a hazard regardless of the debate over effect sizes, and K036 lists the concept among its psychology parallels.
