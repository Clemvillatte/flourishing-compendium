Multicultural identity theory studies how people hold more than one cultural identity at once, and whether those identities live together or fight. The reference point is John W. Berry's acculturation framework, set out in his 1997 Applied Psychology paper "Immigration, Acculturation, and Adaptation" and extended in his 2005 review "Acculturation: Living successfully in two cultures."

Berry's four strategies fall out of two questions: keep the heritage culture? engage the wider society? His own statement of the two poles that matter here:

> When there is an interest in both maintaining one's heritage culture while in daily interactions with other groups, integration is the option. In this case, there is some degree of cultural integrity maintained, and at the same time seeking, as a member of an ethnocultural group, to participate as an integral part of the larger social network.

(Berry, "Acculturation: Living successfully in two cultures," Applied Psychology 29 (2005), [PDF](https://dl.icdst.org/pdfs/files/6bb45352e822a3d8be6ac473b7232639.pdf))

The empirical payoff, in the same paper: "Generally, those pursuing the integration strategy experience less stress, and achieve better adaptations than those pursuing marginalisation; the outcomes for those pursuing assimilation and separation experience intermediate levels of stress and adaptation." Integration is not assimilation by a longer name; as Berry puts it, "assimilation and integration are distinct concepts, involving differing attitudes and behaviors."
