"Zu den Sachen selbst," to the things themselves, is Edmund Husserl's maxim for what philosophy should do: stop trading in inherited concepts and return to what is actually given in experience. The formulation stands in the introduction to the second part of the Logical Investigations (1900-01), where Husserl rejects meanings fed by "remote, confused, inauthentic intuitions" and insists that "we must go back to the 'things themselves'" ([SEP, Dan Zahavi](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). The Sachen are the matters under investigation, taken as they present themselves rather than as prior theory has filed them.

### What the return asks

The method behind the maxim is description. Husserl brackets the "natural attitude," the everyday taking-for-granted of the world, not to doubt the world but to examine how anything shows up in it; in the SEP's summary, "we do not lose the world as a result of the epoché, but from now on it must be considered solely as an intentional correlate" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)).

What remains for study is the correlation between experienced objects and their manners of givenness, which Husserl called the correlational a priori and, per the entry, described as the breakthrough that dominated his life's work. Consciousness is always consciousness of something; describing an experience means describing what it is of, its object as given. Ideas I (1913) adds the principle Husserl called the "principle of all principles": everything originarily offered to us in intuition is to be accepted as what it presents itself as being, but only within the limits in which it is presented.

### From things to the life-world

The return has a late destination. In his final period Husserl treated the life-world, in the entry's phrase "the world we live and feel at home in," as the basis of science. The entry summarizes the point: "the world as described by science is not an autonomous world, a world behind or below the manifest world. Rather, it is the same world as that of everyday experience...but now enriched theoretically." The maxim therefore does not attack science; it demands that theory stay answerable to the experience it enriches.

### In the compendium

E011 "Practise phenomenological description" cites the maxim directly: describe experience as it is given, before theorising about it. P004 "Start from experience, not from theory" carries the same rule with James and Dewey, and Y026 and A050 train the attention it needs.

The compendium also pairs the return with contemplative cousins: K015 and B124 meet suchness, B048 keeps beginner's mind, and D010, D013 and D099 receive things as they are in the Daoist register. ST259, the apple story, gives the method its front door: the apple's far side is not seen and yet is given as there.
