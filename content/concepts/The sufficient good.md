The compendium's heading "the sufficient good" points here to Aristotle's account of happiness as final and self-sufficient. The seed names the Nicomachean Ethics.

### Aristotle's ground

The argument runs through what "final" means. Aristotle grades ends: some are pursued for the sake of something else, some for their own sake, and "by the term 'absolutely final,' we denote that which is an object of choice always in itself, and never with a view to any other" (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, book I, ch. VII, trans. D. P. Chase, [Project Gutenberg #8438](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt)). His test case:

> "honour, pleasure, intellect, in fact every excellence we choose for their own sakes, it is true (because we would choose each of these even if no result were to follow), but we choose them also with a view to happiness, conceiving that through their instrumentality we shall be happy: but no man chooses happiness with a view to them, nor in fact with a view to any other thing whatsoever." (same work)

Self-sufficiency then makes the same point about wanting:

> "for the present we define that to be self-sufficient "which taken alone makes life choice-worthy, and to be in want of nothing;" now of such kind we think Happiness to be: and further, to be most choice-worthy of all things; not being reckoned with any other thing, for if it were so reckoned, it is plain we must then allow it, with the addition of ever so small a good, to be more choice-worthy than it was before" (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, book I, ch. VII, trans. D. P. Chase, [Project Gutenberg #8438](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt))

The addition argument is doing the work: if happiness were one good among others, then happiness plus any extra good would beat happiness alone, and the final end would not be final. The conclusion drawn from it:

> "So then Happiness is manifestly something final and self-sufficient, being the end of all things which are and may be done." (same work)

Two boundaries Aristotle draws are worth keeping. Self-sufficiency is not self-isolation: by "sufficient for Self" he means not "for a single individual living a solitary life, but for his parents also and children and wife, and, in general, friends and countrymen; for man is by nature adapted to a social existence" (same work). And the standard is not abundance: the sufficient good is the one that leaves nothing needed, not the one that outdoes every rival.
