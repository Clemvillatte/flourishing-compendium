Achievement goal theory is the social-cognitive framework that asks which goal a person adopts in an achievement situation, and treats that choice as the key to how they meet effort, difficulty and failure. Dweck's 1986 account distinguishes learning goals, focused on developing competence, from performance goals, focused on obtaining favorable judgments of competence or avoiding unfavorable judgments ([Dweck, Motivational Processes Affecting Learning, 1986](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/1986-dweck.pdf)). Elliot and Church (1997) extended the framework by dividing performance goals into an approach and an avoidance form ([Elliot and Church, 1997](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/a4482ae1eeaaaed5df0d72b14e93f484104ddda4)).

### Two kinds of goals

Dweck's original distinction, in her words:

> "Achievement motivation involves a particular class of goals--those involving competence--and these goals appear to fall into two classes: (a) learning goals, in which individuals seek to increase their competence, to understand or master something new, and (b) performance goals, in which individuals seek to gain favorable judgments of their competence or avoid negative judgments of their competence"

The article pairs the two goals with two behaviour patterns. Of the adaptive pattern she writes that it "is characterized by challenge seeking and high, effective persistence in the face of obstacles," while the maladaptive "helpless" pattern shows "challenge avoidance and low persistence in the face of difficulty." Dweck reports that the goals are not adopted at random: in her summary, children who believe intelligence is a fixed trait tend to orient toward performance goals, while children who believe it is malleable tend to orient toward learning goals. That link connects this theory to the compendium's pages on implicit theories of intelligence and growth mindset.

### Approach and avoidance

Elliot and Church kept the mastery goal and split the performance category in two, producing a trichotomous framework of mastery, performance-approach and performance-avoidance goals, embedded in a hierarchical model of approach and avoidance motivation. Elliot later defined the underlying distinction this way:

> "Approach motivation is the energization of behavior by, or the direction of behavior toward, positive stimuli (objects, events, possibilities), whereas avoidance motivation is the energization of behavior by, or the direction of behavior away from, negative stimuli (objects, events, possibilities)"

([Elliot, The Hierarchical Model of Approach-Avoidance Motivation, 2006](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11031-006-9028-7.pdf))

In their classroom study, mastery goals were grounded in achievement motivation and high competence expectancies, performance-approach goals in a combination of achievement motivation, fear of failure and high expectancies, and performance-avoidance goals in fear of failure and low expectancies. The study reported that mastery goals facilitated intrinsic motivation, performance-approach goals enhanced graded performance, and performance-avoidance goals proved inimical to both. Later work elaborated the framework into a 2 by 2 model that crosses the mastery and performance distinction with the approach and avoidance distinction (Cury, Elliot, Da Fonseca and Moller, 2006).

### Evidence and limits

The 1997 findings come from a single classroom study, so they are associations between goal adoptions and outcomes rather than demonstrations that changing a goal changes a life. The same study found performance-approach goals were not uniformly harmful, since they went with better grades. The theory's claim is narrower than "mastery is good and performance is bad": it concerns what each kind of goal does to how a person reads failure and effort.
