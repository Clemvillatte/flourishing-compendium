Deep listening is listening with the sole intention of understanding — attention as generosity, in the ear's register. Its psychological statement is Carl Rogers' empathic understanding: sensing the other's private world "as if" it were one's own, without ever losing the "as if" — the check that keeps understanding from becoming takeover ([Rogers, "The Necessary and Sufficient Conditions of Therapeutic Personality Change" (1957)](https://garfield.library.upenn.edu/classics1978/A1978FD05200002.pdf)). Its contemplative statement is Thich Nhat Hanh's: deep listening "is the kind of listening that can help relieve the suffering of another person" — listening with one purpose, to help them empty their heart, without interrupting or arguing, because "one hour like that can bring transformation and healing" ([Thich Nhat Hanh, in conversation](https://plumvillage.org/about/thich-nhat-hanh/interviews-with-thich-nhat-hanh/oprah-talks-to-thich-nhat-hanh)).

The mechanism: what makes listening deep is what it withholds. Advice, rebuttal, and one-upping each replace the speaker's experience with the listener's; deep listening holds those moves in abeyance so that the speaker can hear themselves — often for the first time ([[K207 — Deep listening]]; [[K373 — Notice the fixing reflex]]).

The distinction to hold: deep listening is not agreement and not passivity. The listener's full attention is an act, and understanding a view is not endorsing it — the compassion is in the receiving, not in the verdict.

An example at its smallest: twenty minutes, one person, no advice, no rebuttal — the sole intention, understanding.

Its contemplative cognates are Buber's I–Thou meeting ([[I and Thou]]) and the Buddhist practice of listening deeply ([[B147 — Listen deeply]]).
