The portrait of the sage is the Bhagavad Gita's description, given by Shree Krishna in the twelfth chapter, of the person who is dear to him: a working checklist of dispositions rather than a mystical vision. The verses:

> "Those devotees are very dear to Me who are free from malice toward all living beings, who are friendly, and compassionate. They are free from attachment to possessions and egotism, equipoised in happiness and distress, and ever-forgiving. They are ever-content, steadily united with Me in devotion, self-controlled, of firm resolve, and dedicated to Me in mind and intellect." (Bhagavad Gita 12.13–14, trans. Swami Mukundananda, [Holy Bhagavad Gita](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/12/verse/13))

### What the portrait carries

The qualities named are mostly relational and ordinary: freedom from malice, friendliness, compassion, forgiveness, contentment, self-control, firmness of resolve. The Sanskrit of the first line runs "adveṣhṭā sarva-bhūtānāṁ maitraḥ karuṇa eva cha nirmamo nirahankāraḥ sama-duḥkha-sukhaḥ kṣhamī": without enmity toward all beings, friendly, compassionate, without possessiveness, without egoism, even in suffering and happiness, forbearing. The Stoics drew the same kind of portrait for their own tradition, and I keep the two side by side: the inner citadel is Marcus Aurelius's version of the unconquerable mind, and the sameness of the sage is the ideal of the person unmoved by fortune. My principle SP072 puts the portrait to work: take the free person's signs as a monthly audit, reading the list as questions rather than as claims. A compendium exercise drawn from the verses is to take one quality from the list, say the freedom from malice, and audit the month's dealings against it. What the portrait does not give: evidence that anyone meets it fully; it is a regulative ideal, an image to steer by.
