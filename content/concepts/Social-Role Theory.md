Social-Role Theory is Alice Eagly's account of sex differences and similarities in social behaviour. Its key principle, in Eagly and Wood's summary:

> "Social role theory is a social psychological theory that pertains to sex differences and similarities in social behavior. Its key principle is that differences and similarities arise primarily from the distribution of men and women into social roles within their society. Through socialization and the formation of gender roles, the behaviors of men and women generally support and sustain the division of labor." ([Eagly and Wood, 2016](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/9781118663219.wbegss183))

### Origin

Eagly formulated the theory in her 1987 book Sex Differences in Social Behavior: A Social-Role Interpretation, building on her earlier work on gender stereotypes as inferences from social roles. The book applied social-role thinking to meta-analyses of aggression, helping, social influence and group behaviour ([Eagly and Wood, 1991](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED303721.pdf)).

### Core claims

The theory's working unit is the gender role, defined as "those shared expectations about appropriate conduct that apply to individuals solely on the basis of their socially identified sex" ([Eagly and Wood, 1991](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED303721.pdf)). The content of those expectations is summarised in two clusters:

> "Women are expected to be communal--that is, friendly, unselfish, and concerned with others. Men are expected to be agentic--that is, independent, masterful, and competent." ([Eagly and Wood, 1991](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED303721.pdf))

These expectations "arise from the distribution of women and men into different social roles in society--in particular, the assignment of child-rearing and other domestic work to women and the tendency for women and men to carry out different types of paid employment" ([Eagly and Wood, 1991](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED303721.pdf)). People then tend to behave consistently with their roles, and their histories of enacting roles build skills and attitudes that carry forward.

### What the theory explains

The theory gives a structural reading of gender differences: the division of labour comes first, and psychological differences follow as people adjust to their roles. In industrialized economies, roles are organized so that women are more likely to be homemakers and caretakers, and men primary providers in full-time paid work ([Eagly and Wood, 2016](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/9781118663219.wbegss183)). Change the roles, the theory predicts, and the behaviour follows. Later versions of the theory, developed with Wendy Wood, add a biosocial layer: physical differences such as gestation and upper-body strength shaped the historical division of labour, which then does the psychological work.
