George Loewenstein proposed the information-gap account in "The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation" (Psychological Bulletin 116(1), 75-98, 1994). The abstract says it "interprets curiosity as a form of cognitively induced deprivation that arises from the perception of a gap in knowledge or understanding" ([SciSpace record of the paper](https://scispace.com/papers/the-psychology-of-curiosity-a-review-and-reinterpretation-2bj8cmzgxx)).

### What the account set out to explain

Loewenstein argued that earlier theories "did not offer an adequate explanation for why people voluntarily seek out curiosity, and they failed to delineate situational determinants of curiosity". They also left out four features: curiosity's "intensity, transience, association with impulsivity, and tendency to disappoint when satisfied" (abstract, same record).

### How the gap works

Golman and Loewenstein restate the idea: "a gap opens when a question becomes activated but the answer is not known with certainty". They name three factors that raise curiosity. Importance: "curiosity tends to be stronger about questions that are more important". Salience: "curiosity tends to be an increasing function of the salience of the information gap". Epiphany: "people are especially motivated to acquire information that has the potential to fill multiple information gaps at once". An answer known with certainty leaves "nothing... at stake" ([Golman and Loewenstein, Curiosity, Information Gaps, and the Utility of Knowledge, CMU PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/Curiosity,%20Information%20Gaps,%20and%20the%20Utility%20of%20Knowledge%20Golman_Loewenstein%20April%202015.pdf)).

An academic article describing the 1994 model says curiosity depends on prior knowledge: a learner first attends to what they know, and "as one gains information about the topic, the likelihood of the learner shifting focus to what information he or she is missing increases", which is where curiosity begins ([Just Curious, University of Guelph journal](https://journal.lib.uoguelph.ca/index.php/perj/article/download/3954/4033/21533)). Without an existing knowledge base, curiosity is unlikely to arise.

### Evidence and its limits

An educational review reports experiments in which people were asked to produce words from definitions. Those who felt sure of the word did not want to be told, and those with the word "on the tip of their tongue" became very curious ([Pluck and Johnson, Stimulating curiosity to enhance learning, White Rose PDF](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/74470/1/Pluck_and_Johnson_2011_Curiosity.pdf)). The same authors say the broader claims about puzzles and cliffhangers "remain anecdotal".

A practitioner source adds that gaps need to be manageable: if the gap seems too large, closing it "will seem overwhelming and will discourage learning", and if too small, "not worth the effort" ([Borowske, library journal PDF](https://alair.ala.org/server/api/core/bitstreams/af89247f-ba69-4040-a0ef-0c2433a86c19/content)). I have not found that the 1994 paper states the inverted-U shape in those words, so treat it as an application, not a direct finding.

### For the essays

The theory fits writing about wisdom and learning in one specific way: curiosity is felt as a mild lack, and it is shaped by what a person already knows. The abstract also records curiosity's tendency to disappoint when satisfied, so the theory does not promise that filling a gap feels good.
