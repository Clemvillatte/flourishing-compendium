Restorative justice is the family of practices the review tested: "face-to-face meetings among all parties connected to a crime, including victims, offenders, their families and friends, and court-ordered financial restitution" ([Sherman & Strang 2007](https://www.iirp.edu/images/pdf/RJ_full_report.pdf)). The 2007 review of the evidence, by Sherman and Strang for the Smith Institute, states:

> "A review of research on restorative justice (RJ) in the UK and abroad shows that across 36 direct comparisons to conventional criminal justice (CJ), RJ has, in at least two tests each:
> - substantially reduced repeat offending for some offenders, but not all;
> - doubled (or more) the offences brought to justice as diversion from CJ;
> - reduced crime victims’ post-traumatic stress symptoms and related costs;
> - provided both victims and offenders with more satisfaction with justice than CJ;
> …
> — [Lawrence W. Sherman & Heather Strang, Restorative Justice: The Evidence, Smith Institute 2007 (IIRP full PDF)](https://www.iirp.edu/images/pdf/RJ_full_report.pdf) (first four of seven findings; three further findings omitted)

The report's own framing is relational: "Restorative justice is a way of thinking about what is best for the many connections among crime victims, their offenders and the criminal justice process" ([Sherman & Strang 2007](https://www.iirp.edu/images/pdf/RJ_full_report.pdf)). The direct forms vary in scope: "Another 'direct' form of restorative justice is victim-offender mediation, at which both victim and offender are present, often without other people affected by the crime, and where a mediator negotiates between them" ([Sherman & Strang 2007](https://www.iirp.edu/images/pdf/RJ_full_report.pdf)). The evidence is candid about limits: "The most important conclusion is that RJ works differently on different kinds of people" ([Sherman & Strang 2007](https://www.iirp.edu/images/pdf/RJ_full_report.pdf)) — a caution against prescribing the conference as a uniform fix for every case.
