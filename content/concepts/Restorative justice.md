Restorative justice is the family of practices that bring offender, victim, family and community into a structured face-to-face meeting rather than sending the case through court alone. Sherman and Strang's 2007 review of the evidence from the UK and abroad is the canonical summary. The abstract begins:

> "A review of research on restorative justice (RJ) in the UK and abroad shows that across 36 direct comparisons to conventional criminal justice (CJ), RJ has, in at least two tests each:
> - substantially reduced repeat offending for some offenders, but not all;
> - doubled (or more) the offences brought to justice as diversion from CJ;
> - reduced crime victims' post-traumatic stress symptoms and related costs;
> - provided both victims and offenders with more satisfaction with justice than CJ;"
> — [Sherman, Strang, Barnes, Bennett, Angel, Newbury-Birch, Woods & Gill 2007, Restorative Justice: The Evidence, Smith Institute](https://www.iirp.edu/images/pdf/RJ_full_report.pdf)

The mechanism the report points to is interaction: when the offender meets the person they harmed, the apology and its sincerity do work that sentencing does not ([Sherman and Strang 2007](https://www.iirp.edu/images/pdf/RJ_full_report.pdf)).
