“The expansive mind” — Zhuangzi, Inner Chapters.

### What it is

The compendium uses this title for the scale of mind Zhuangzi celebrates in the opening chapter of the Inner Chapters. The cicada and the dove laugh at the great Peng bird's ninety-thousand-li flight because their own world ends at the nearest tree, and the narrator states the point as a fact of scale: "The knowledge of that which is small does not reach to that which is great; (the experience of) a few years does not reach to that of many" (Zhuangzi, Inner Chapters 1, trans. Legge, [ctext.org](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/enjoyment-in-untroubled-ease)). The morning mushroom does not know the month; the cicada does not know spring from autumn.

### What it carries

The chapter then distinguishes grades of freedom. Liezi rides the wind "with an admirable indifference (to all external things)" yet still has something to wait for; the expansive mind goes further: "suppose one who mounts on (the ether of) heaven and earth in its normal operation, and drives along the six elemental energies of the changing (seasons), thus enjoying himself in the illimitable - what has he to wait for?" (Zhuangzi, Inner Chapters 1, trans. Legge, [ctext.org](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/enjoyment-in-untroubled-ease)). Its summary sentence names what the scale is for: "The Perfect man has no (thought of) self; the Spirit-like man, none of merit; the Sagely-minded man, none of fame" (Zhuangzi, Inner Chapters 1, trans. Legge, [ctext.org](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/enjoyment-in-untroubled-ease)).

The CO013 principle takes the psychological reading: thought has a direction, and it can be expansive or contracted. The contracted mind is not wrong at its own scale, the cicada's flight is real flight; it is simply a mind that mistakes its tree for the sky. The practice is asking, in a fixed frame of mind, what a ninety-thousand-li view of this same situation would show.
