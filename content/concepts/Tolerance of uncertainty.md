In their analysis of medical practice, Reis-Dennis, Gerrity and Geller discuss tolerance for uncertainty in terms of a clinician's comfort with incomplete information. Dugas and Carleton examine intolerance of uncertainty in anxiety research. In Dugas and colleagues' model of generalized anxiety disorder, intolerance of uncertainty is one of four process variables alongside beliefs about worry, poor problem orientation and cognitive avoidance. In their preliminary test with 24 people with generalized anxiety disorder and 20 nonclinical controls, a discriminant function using all four process variables correctly classified 82% of participants.

> IU represents, at its core, fear of the unknown – a long-recognized, deep-seated fear identified in normative and pathological samples. (Carleton, "The intolerance of uncertainty construct in the context of anxiety disorders: theoretical and practical perspectives", Expert Review of Neurotherapeutics 12(8), 2012, [abstract](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1586/ern.12.82))

A normative analysis in medicine defines the positive side directly:

> In making this case, we characterize tolerance for uncertainty as comfort in the face of one’s subjective perception of one’s own ignorance about some aspect of a clinical situation. (Reis-Dennis, Gerrity and Geller, "Tolerance for Uncertainty and Professional Development: a Normative Analysis", Journal of General Internal Medicine 36(8), 2021, [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7853704/))

The same authors argue that neither tolerance nor intolerance of uncertainty is necessarily a good or bad trait: both can give a clinician advantages while exposing them to pitfalls, and they propose cultivating virtues such as courage, diligence and curiosity to avoid the extremes. Their analysis is normative philosophy of medicine, not intervention evidence.
