Grief for what passes is the reading of Zhuangzi's drumming-on-the-tub story as a lesson in grieving well, not in feeling nothing. The story is in chapter 18 of the Zhuangzi. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says the chapter "centers around the theme of the acceptance of death as part of the natural processes of Heaven and Earth" and contains this narrative about Huishi's visit ([SEP, Zhuangzi](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2012/entries/zhuangzi/)).

### The story

When Zhuangzi's wife died, his friend Huizi came to condole and found him squatting on the ground, drumming on a basin and singing. Huizi objected that this went too far. Zhuangzi's reply, in Legge's translation:

> When she first died, was it possible for me to be singular and not affected by the event? But I reflected on the commencement of her being. She had not yet been born to life; not only had she no life, but she had no bodily form; not only had she no bodily form, but she had no breath.

(Zhuangzi 18, "Perfect Enjoyment", trans. James Legge, [Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/perfect-enjoyment).) He goes on to describe change after change, from breath to form to birth to death, "like the procession of the four seasons". If he were to wail now, he says, "I should think that I did not understand what was appointed (for all). I therefore restrained myself!" (same source).

Giles renders the same moment as: "When she died, I could not help being affected by her death. Soon, however, I remembered that she had already existed in a previous state before birth." He ends: "Therefore I refrain" ([Giles, Chuang Tzŭ, ch. 18, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Chuang_Tz%C5%AD_(Giles)/Chapter_18)).

### What the text says and does not say

The sequence in the text is clear: he was affected first, then reflected, then stopped. The text does not tell us that he never felt loss, and a reading of it as emotional detachment skips his own first sentence. It also does not tell us how long the reflection took or what he felt afterwards. Translators disagree on small matters: tub, basin and bowl for the same vessel, and "singing" in all three.

The reason he gives is about the natural order of change. Whether this counts as grief proper is a question for interpreters; the compendium's title "Grief for what passes" is its own framing, and the wording of this page keeps the claim to what the translations say.
