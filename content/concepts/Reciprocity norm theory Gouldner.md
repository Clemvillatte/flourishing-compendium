Gouldner's theory that a generalized moral norm of reciprocity is a universal part of moral codes. The norm has two minimal demands (repay help, and do not injure helpers), and, in Gouldner's account, it does real work holding social systems together.

> "I suggest that a norm of reciprocity, in its universal form, makes two interrelated, minimal demands: (1) people should help those who have helped them, and (2) people should not injure those who have helped them." (Gouldner, "The Norm of Reciprocity: A Preliminary Statement", 1960, p. 171, [scanned article](http://kpolanyi.scoolaid.net:8080/xmlui/handle/10694/347))

### Origin
Alvin W. Gouldner, then at Washington University at St. Louis, published the theory as "The Norm of Reciprocity: A Preliminary Statement" in the American Sociological Review 25(2), April 1960, pp. 161-178. The article presents itself as a contribution to functional theory, and it separates three things often conflated: reciprocity as a pattern of mutually contingent exchange, the folk belief in reciprocity, and the generalized moral norm of reciprocity.

### Core claims
On universality, Gouldner offers a comparison marked as suspicion rather than demonstration:

> "A norm of reciprocity is, I suspect, no less universal and important an element of culture than the incest taboo, although, similarly, its concrete formulations may vary with time and place." (same article, p. 171)

The article's abstract frames the universality as a hypothesis — that the moral norm is one of the universal "principal components" of moral codes — not as a settled finding.

### What the norm does
The theory is a middle-range account of a moral norm, and its explanatory work is at the system level: the article examines how the norm is implicated in the maintenance of stable social systems. A norm that obliges repayment lets help begin without a contract, and it stabilizes relationships once begun; Gouldner quotes L. T. Hobhouse's dictum that "reciprocity. . . is the vital principle of society". One scope limit Gouldner himself flags: whether the norm also requires returns to be "roughly equivalent" to what was received he leaves as an open empirical question, noting that the equivalence reading belongs to Homans, Thurnwald, Simmel and Malinowski rather than to his own formulation.

### In everyday life
Illustrative scenario, not a reported study result: a colleague spends an afternoon reviewing a draft. No invoice follows, but the debt is felt on both sides: the reviewer expects eventual help in kind, and the recipient who never offers any finds the relationship cooling. The norm is doing the accounting invisibly.
