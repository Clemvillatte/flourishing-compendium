Dichotomous thinking is the habit of sorting events into two opposite boxes: all or nothing, right or wrong, safe or dangerous. A 2022 scoping review defines it as "a propensity for individuals to understand events as opposites, in a binary way" ([Bonfá-Araujo, Oshio and Hauck-Filho, 2022](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jpr.12328)).

### Ordinary and extreme forms

The review does not treat the habit as a defect in itself. "Albeit perceiving events through a dichotomized lens is a universal experience of human nature, more extreme manifestations have been associated with psychopathology." Sorting can help, since "such categorizations can indeed help us in making decisions or choosing something", although "understanding events as only having two outputs may result in incompatibilities with the reality of facts." The authors also note that "dichotomizing and hierarchizing facts simplifies the processes involved when an individual has to choose, generating less anxiety" ([review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jpr.12328)).

### What the evidence shows

The review gathered 19 studies, with 8,289 participants in total, that used one of two questionnaires, the Dichotomous Thinking Inventory (Oshio) or a scale for eating disorders. It concludes that the style "occurs, in general, in the presence of maladaptive traits of personality" and is "mainly associated with variables related to pathological functioning", among them aggressiveness, eating disorders, intolerance of ambiguity, narcissism, antisocial personality, perfectionism and the Dark Triad. Its summary of interpersonal findings is that people with this style "tend to be hostile, antagonistic, rigid, and have conflicting interpersonal relationships" ([review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jpr.12328)).

The coefficients are modest. Associations with the Dark Triad ranged from .12 to .30 ([review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jpr.12328)), a range that suggests a tendency running through these traits and not a strong rule.

### How it may work

For depression the authors suggest that the style "may so intensify the experience of negative but ordinary situations that they become unbearable", and in eating disorders it "might lead an individual to think that binge eating and complete food restriction are the only options available" ([review](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/jpr.12328)). These are interpretations, not tested mechanisms.

### Scope

These findings are correlations from questionnaires, mostly in Japan and Australia and with university students. The phrase "predicts distress and interpersonal dysfunction" goes further than the review, which did not combine the effect sizes and calls for studies in Western samples. It also notes one exception to the pattern, a link with emotional intelligence that disappeared once coping was included.
