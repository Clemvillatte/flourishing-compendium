Side-Bet Theory is Howard Becker's 1960 account of commitment: people stay with a line of activity because they have staked other interests on it, and those stakes would be forfeited if they stopped. His abstract states the mechanism.

> "Commitments come into being when a person, by making a side bet, links extraneous interests with a consistent line of activity. Side bets are often a consequence of the person's participation in social organizations." (Becker, [Notes on the Concept of Commitment](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/222820), American Journal of Sociology 66(1), 1960, abstract)

### Origin

The theory appeared in that 1960 American Journal of Sociology paper. It was later applied to organizations, where the consistent line of activity is staying employed in the organization.

### Core claims

The theory predicts commitment from the cost of exit. A study of substance abuse counselors states its use:

> "According to Becker's (1960) side-bet theory, there is a strong link between an employee's commitment to an organization and/or occupation and the perceived 'penalty' that he/she would incur as a result of leaving an organization or occupation." (Rothrauff et al., [Occupational turnover intentions among substance abuse counselors](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2997936/), Journal of Substance Abuse Treatment 40(1), 2011)

Rothrauff and colleagues' illustration of the theory: "changing occupations (main bet) could entail decreased income, a move away from family, and loss of friendships (i.e., side-bets)" ([same study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2997936/)). The greater the perceived penalties, the greater the commitment.

### In everyday life

That example is the theory in ordinary terms: an employee stays because leaving would forfeit the pension, the seniority and the workplace friendships already banked.
