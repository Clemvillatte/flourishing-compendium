“I and Thou” (*Ich und Du*, 1923) is Martin Buber's name for the two basic attitudes a person can take: the relation of *I–Thou* — meeting another being in their wholeness, present to them without using them — and the mode of *I–It*, in which the other becomes an object of experience, classification, and use ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Martin Buber”](https://iep.utm.edu/martin-buber/); [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Martin Buber”](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2016/entries/buber/)). The claim is not that the I–It mode is evil — science, administration, and much of daily life run on it — but that a life conducted entirely in I–It, including with people, thins into management; the Thou is *met*, not known, and only in meeting does the I itself become real.

The concept grounds a practice of presence rather than a theory: to listen to someone without simultaneously preparing the reply, formulating them, or finishing their sentence is to treat them as a Thou for a moment — which is why the compendium pairs it with attentional presence in relationship ([[AT057 — Cultivate attentional presence in relationships — attend to others]]) and [[Deep listening|deep listening]] ([[B147 — Listen deeply]]), and against the framing and possessing moves that quietly convert persons into objects ([[D082 — Don't impose your frame on others]]; [[D104 — Love without possessing]]).

The distinction to hold: I–Thou is not intimacy or agreement. One can meet an opponent as a Thou (the meeting is the mode, not the warmth — [[R081 — Practise I-Thou]] is the compendium's training form), and one can use a lover as an It. Buber's late extension — that every particular Thou "glimpses" the eternal Thou — is what keeps the concept from being mere interpersonal technique.

An example at its smallest: in the next conversation, one full minute of listening with nothing prepared — no question held in reserve, no lesson drawn — then noticing what changed in the speaker.

The Confucian cousin is [[Ren]] — relation, not the isolated individual, as the unit of ethics.
