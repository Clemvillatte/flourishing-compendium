The matching law, formulated by R. J. Herrnstein in 1961, holds that "relative response rates will match relative reinforcement rates produced by that responding" ([Further analysis of the matching law, ERIC EJ767623](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ767623.pdf)). Herrnstein's original finding came from pigeons: "when animals are presented with two opportunities to respond (pecking key X or key Y), each of which delivers reinforcers... on its own variable interval (VI) schedule, they allocate their responses on X and Y in proportion to the rates of reward available" ([The behavioral economics of consumer brand choice, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167487003000084)).

### The equation and its extension

In strict form, the share of responses to one option equals the share of reinforcement it produces. Baum (1974) "showed systematic deviations from strict matching between relative response and relative reinforcer frequencies and proposed a power function alternative, the generalized matching law", with two parameters: "bias (c)" and "sensitivity to reinforcement (a)" ([A Model for Residence Time in Concurrent Variable Interval Performance, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1790879/)). Bias is "a preference for one alternative over the other independent of relative reinforcement rate" (ERIC EJ767623). A sensitivity below 1.0 is called undermatching and is "now considered the normative finding" on concurrent VI schedules (PMC1790879).

### How well it fits

Baum's 1979 review found that "almost all of 103 sets of data from 23 different studies of choice conformed closely to the equation", though "the best-fitting values of a frequently deviated from" 1.0, and for response counts "a tended to fall short of 1.0 (undermatching)" ([Baum, 1979, Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1901/jeab.1979.32-269)). In rhesus monkeys, steady-state data "were well characterized by the generalized matching law", with response ratios that "undermatched reinforcer frequency ratios but matched reinforcer magnitude ratios" ([Dynamic Response-by-Response Models of Matching Behavior in Rhesus Monkeys, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1389781/)). The law has also been tested with other rewards, such as wheel-running in rats, where undermatching was typical ([PMC1284819](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC1284819/pdf/11453620.pdf)).

### Humans and social settings

A study of social dynamics reports that the relation between relative response rate and relative reinforcer rate "is known as the matching law", and that data "seemed to support the notion that matching is a steady-state phenomenon (e.g., Baum, 1974) that requires some exposure to experimental contingencies before it occurs" ([An application of the matching law to social dynamics, ERIC EJ780540](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ780540.pdf)). Another ERIC paper applies the same equation to the number of shots attempted and scored, which shows the idea being carried into everyday choices.

### Limits for the essays

The law describes average allocation of behaviour across concurrent options, not a rule for how a person should choose. The ratio form does not say why a reinforcer matters to someone. Statements about meaning, suffering or habit in this wiki rest on other pages, not on this one.
