Social epistemology is the field that studies truth-seeking as a social activity rather than a lone mind's achievement. Where traditional epistemology asks how an individual should determine what is true, it asks how people and groups do so together.

> "Social epistemology is concerned with how people can best pursue the truth with the help of, or sometimes in the face of, other people or relevant social practices and institutions." (O'Connor, Goldberg & Goldman, [Social Epistemology, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemology-social/))

### Goldman's veritistic program

Of Alvin Goldman's Knowledge in a Social World (1999) the entry says: "The seminal defense for this sort of approach to social epistemology is Alvin Goldman (1999)", a book that "developed Goldman's 'veritistic' approach, which focuses on the reliability with which various social practices produce true beliefs" ([entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemology-social/)). Philip Kitcher's paper shows the field's style of question: "Philip Kitcher's 1990 paper 'The Division of Cognitive Labor' argues that scientists divide labor more effectively when they are motivated by credit" ([entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemology-social/)) — truth seekers "might all herd onto the most promising problem in science, while credit seekers will choose less popular topics where they are more likely to be the one generating a finding".

### What the field studies

The entry's working topics are testimony, peer disagreement, judgment aggregation, and epistemic systems. On peer disagreement, the entry presents "Conciliationism" as "the view that in peer disagreement (some degree of) modification in one's confidence is rationally required" ([entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemology-social/)). Judgment aggregation raises the "doctrinal paradox": a court of three judges can each hold a logically self-consistent set of beliefs while a majority aggregation function delivers a group judgment that is jointly inconsistent ([entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemology-social/)). And for epistemic systems — juries, democracies, and online platforms — the question becomes "how best to design systems whose goals include epistemic goods such as the production or distribution of knowledge or true belief" ([entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epistemology-social/)).
