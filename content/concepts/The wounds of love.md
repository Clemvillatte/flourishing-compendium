"The wounds of love" belongs to Alcibiades' entrance in Plato's *Symposium*, the one speech in the dialogue that praises love by describing an injury. Alcibiades arrives drunk, sits with Socrates, and announces the terms of his testimony: "I have felt the serpent's sting; and he who has suffered, as they say, is willing to tell his fellow-sufferers only, as they alone will be likely to understand him." The wound has a source and a name: "I have been bitten by a more than viper's tooth; I have known in my soul, or in my heart, or in some other part, that worst of pangs, more violent in ingenuous youth than any serpent's tooth, the pang of philosophy, which will make a man say or do anything" (Plato, *Symposium*, Alcibiades' speech, trans. Jowett; [Project Gutenberg, eBook 1600](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1600)). The speech then shows the wound's structure. Alcibiades recounts his seduction attempt, and the failure: "I fancied that he was smitten, and that the words which I had uttered like arrows had wounded him," but Socrates was not wounded, and Alcibiades concludes, "no one was ever more hopelessly enslaved by another." The wound is not the injury love does to the beloved; it is the bond formed around an unavailable figure, the attachment that cannot be either satisfied or renounced. Alcibiades' own summary is the clinical picture: "I could not be angry with him or renounce his company, any more than I could hope to win him." What the dialogue puts beside the wound is Socrates' own account of love as the child of plenty and want, desire that lacks what it reaches for; Alcibiades lives that definition as an affliction. The teaching for attachment is direct: the wound that binds a person to another is often not what the other gives but what the other's unavailability keeps alive, and seeing the structure, the one bitten speaking to fellow sufferers, is the beginning of release.
