"The wounds of love" belongs to Alcibiades' entrance in Plato's *Symposium*, the speech in which he praises Socrates by describing an injury. Alcibiades arrives drunk, sits with Socrates, and announces the terms of his testimony: "I have felt the serpent's sting; and he who has suffered, as they say, is willing to tell his fellow-sufferers only, as they alone will be likely to understand him, and will not be extreme in judging of the sayings or doings which have been wrung from his agony" (Plato, *Symposium*, Alcibiades' speech, trans. Jowett; [Project Gutenberg, eBook 1600](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1600)). The wound has a source and a name: "I have been bitten by a more than viper's tooth; I have known in my soul, or in my heart, or in some other part, that worst of pangs, more violent in ingenuous youth than any serpent's tooth, the pang of philosophy, which will make a man say or do anything." The speech then shows the wound's structure. Alcibiades recounts his seduction attempt, and the failure: "I fancied that he was smitten, and that the words which I had uttered like arrows had wounded him," but Socrates was not wounded, and Alcibiades concludes, "no one was ever more hopelessly enslaved by another." His own summary of the bind: "I could not be angry with him or renounce his company, any more than I could hope to win him." What the dialogue puts beside the wound is Socrates' own account of love as the child of Plenty and Poverty, desire that lacks what it reaches for; in the same translation Alcibiades lives that account as an affliction. Read as the companion's comparison with attachment patterns, the scene shows a bond formed around a figure who neither yields nor departs: the wound Alcibiades names is the pang of philosophy, not an attachment diagnosis, but the structure he describes, unable to win, unable to leave, unable to stay angry, is the structure the attachment materials examine, and the speech's own frame, one sufferer telling fellow sufferers, is where the comparison begins.
