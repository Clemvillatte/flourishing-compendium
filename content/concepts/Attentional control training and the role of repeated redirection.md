Attentional control training names practices that exercise attention, and the movement most of them repeat is the return of attention to a chosen object. The framework behind the name comes from Posner and Petersen's 1990 review "The Attention System of the Human Brain" ([Annual Review of Neuroscience 13, pp. 25-42](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev.ne.13.030190.000325)), which treated attention as a system of anatomically distinct networks rather than a single faculty. The mindfulness tradition supplies the method: notice that the mind has left the object, and bring it back.

### The three networks

Posner and Petersen divided attention into three networks: alerting, which achieves and maintains an alert state; orienting, which selects information from sensory input; and executive control, which resolves conflict among responses. The executive network includes midline frontal and anterior cingulate cortex, regions reliably activated when responses conflict and since extended to regulating both cognition and emotion ([Petersen & Posner 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3413263/)). On this framework the return from distraction can be read as conflict resolution: something else has captured the system, and the goal is restored.

### What one repetition looks like

Wendy Hasenkamp and colleagues (2012) scanned 14 experienced practitioners during 20 minutes of breath-focused meditation ([author PDF](https://wendyhasenkamp.net/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/hasenkamp-neuroimage-2012.pdf)). Their model has four intervals in a cycle: mind wandering, awareness of wandering, shifting attention back, and sustained attention. Each interval was associated with activity in different networks: default mode regions during wandering, the salience network (anterior insula, dorsal anterior cingulate) at the moment of noticing, and frontoparietal executive regions, including right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, during the shift back and the return to focus. The study mapped activity during the practice, not improvements caused by it.

### Evidence and limits

A 2020 review by Ruchika Prakash and colleagues covered 57 studies of mindfulness training measured against the three networks ([review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8011594/)). The most consistent finding was improvement in executive control; alerting results were mixed and orienting support weak. The review also gave reasons for caution: benefits appeared in 64 percent of studies with inactive controls against 54 percent with active controls, and only 10 of the 57 addressed demand characteristics. The defensible summary is promising but inconsistent evidence for better conflict monitoring, not a proven general sharpening of attention.

### In the compendium

The concept backs AT006 "Recognise that attention is trainable: it can be cultivated" and AT055 "Recognise and work with attentional conditioning: what you practise becomes default." Y026 "Bring the mind back, without drama" quotes the Gita's instruction (Bhagavad Gita 6.26) and supplies the rep reading: fifty returns in a session are fifty reps, not a failure. AT050 applies the cycle to the body scan and AT062 to feeds engineered for capture, AT070 names anxiety's threat bias as trainable, and AT042 and AT044 treat sleep and exercise as supports for attentional capacity. The Yoga tradition offers a comparison in abhyasa, the sustained effort to rest in stillness that Patanjali pairs with vairagya (Yoga Sutra 1.12-13), a structural parallel rather than a claim of influence.
