abstract, verbal "problem-solving" without action maintains anxiety (Borkovec, 1983).

The research program of Thomas Borkovec and colleagues mapped what worry actually is: a chain of negatively charged verbal thought, abstract rather than imagistic, that feels like preparation while functioning as avoidance. The 1998 synthesis of fifteen years of findings states it in one sentence:

> "Worry involves a predominance of verbal thought activity, functions as a type of cognitive avoidance, and inhibits emotional processing." (Borkovec, Ray & Stöber 1998, Cognitive Therapy and Research 22(6), p. 561)

The mechanism explains the apparent paradox. Worry runs in words, and verbal-linguistic activity produces far less physiological arousal than vivid imagery of the same feared event, so each worry session dampens the fear response in the short term. That relief reinforces the worrying, while the avoidance it buys blocks the emotional processing that would actually resolve the fear. Worry feels like working on the problem; functionally it is a way of not looking at it, and the anxiety is maintained rather than discharged. The research connects to [[P024 — Let doubt be genuine]]: genuine doubt risks contact with the feared thing, while worry rehearses it safely in the abstract, which is why the one changes a life and the other circulates.
