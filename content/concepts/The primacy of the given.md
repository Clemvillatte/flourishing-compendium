The primacy of the given is Husserl's rule that what shows itself in experience is to be taken as it shows itself: before any theory about experience, the presentation itself is the authority. The rule is stated in Ideas I as the principle of all principles:

> "That every originary presentive intuition is a legitimizing source of cognition, that everything originarily (so to speak, in its 'personal' actuality) offered to us in 'intuition' is to be accepted simply as what it is presented as being, but also only within the limits in which it is presented there." (Husserl, Ideas I, in [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/), Hua 3/51)

### What the principle carries

Two halves matter equally. The first half gives intuition its authority: what is originarily given is a legitimizing source of cognition. The second half cuts the authority down to size: accepted "only within the limits in which it is presented there," so that an appearance is trusted as an appearance and not mistaken for more than it gives. Behind the principle stands Husserl's dictum to go back to "the things themselves" rather than rest with "mere words," and his later formulation of method: "The true method follows the nature of the things to be investigated and not our prejudices and preconceptions" (Hua 25/26, in [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). I pair this with CO018's warning about anchoring, initial values distorting subsequent judgment: the practice of returning to what is actually given, within its limits, is the phenomenological corrective for the anchored judgment. A compendium exercise drawn from the principle is to state a difficulty as what is given, what is actually showing up here and now, before any story about what it means. What the principle does not give: a guarantee against illusion; it rules on how appearances are to be taken, not on which appearances are veridical, and the limits-clause is where the illusion is caught.
