“Mind wandering” — Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010.

## Facets

- **unhappiness** — Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010
- **both costs and benefits**
- **the default mode network**

The finding that named the field came from a smartphone study. Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert sampled 2250 adults at random moments, asking what they were doing, whether they were thinking about something else, and how they felt ([Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/A_Wandering_Mind_Is_an_Unhappy_Mind.pdf)):

> Mind wandering occurred in 46.9% of the samples and in at least 30% of the samples taken during every activity except making love.

The mood result was blunt: "people were less happy when their minds were wandering than when they were not", across every activity, including the least enjoyable. And the direction ran the interesting way: time-lag analyses "strongly suggested that mind wandering in our sample was generally the cause, and not merely the consequence, of unhappiness". Their closing lines:

> In conclusion, a human mind is a wandering mind, and a wandering mind is an unhappy mind. The ability to think about what is not happening is a cognitive achievement that comes at an emotional cost.

### The costs, and the benefits

Benjamin Mooneyham and Jonathan Schooler's review keeps both halves in view. On the cost side, "mind-wandering typically occurs at a significant cost to performance", with deficits in reading, sustained attention, and aptitude tests ([Mooneyham and Schooler, 2013](https://metalab.psych.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/publications/2013/2013-The-costs-and-benefits-of-mind-wandering-a-review.pdf)). On the benefit side, they credit mind-wandering with a role in both autobiographical planning and creative problem solving, and propose three further functions: "attentional cycling, dishabituation, and relief from boredom". The boredom finding is the review's most disarming: in one tedious-task study, "mind-wandering appeared to partially insulate people against the mood costs of engaging in a particularly tedious task". The reviewers' summary guard is that wandering "does not typically provide affective relief" even where it relieves tedium.

### The practice effect

The neuroscience gives the contemplative claim a handle. In experienced meditators, "the main nodes of the default-mode network (medial prefrontal and posterior cingulate cortices) were relatively deactivated... across all meditation types", with the differences "consistent with decreased mind-wandering" ([Brewer et al., 2011](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1112029108)). The wandering mind is the brain's default, and the practice effect shows the default is trainable.
