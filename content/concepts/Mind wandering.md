“Mind wandering” — Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010.

## Facets

- **unhappiness** — Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010
- **both costs and benefits**
- **the default mode network**

In a 2010 smartphone study, Matthew Killingsworth and Daniel Gilbert sampled 2250 adults at random moments, asking what they were doing, whether they were thinking about something else, and how they felt ([Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/A_Wandering_Mind_Is_an_Unhappy_Mind.pdf)):

> Mind wandering occurred in 46.9% of the samples and in at least 30% of the samples taken during every activity except making love.

They report two further findings ([Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/A_Wandering_Mind_Is_an_Unhappy_Mind.pdf)). On mood:

> ...multilevel regression revealed that people were less happy when their minds were wandering than when they were not [slope (b) = –8.79, P < 0.001], and this was true during all activities, including the least enjoyable.

On direction, the authors read their time-lag analyses this way ([Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/A_Wandering_Mind_Is_an_Unhappy_Mind.pdf)):

> Although negative moods are known to cause mind wandering (13), time-lag analyses strongly suggested that mind wandering in our sample was generally the cause, and not merely the consequence, of unhappiness (12).

Their closing lines ([Killingsworth and Gilbert, 2010](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/A_Wandering_Mind_Is_an_Unhappy_Mind.pdf)):

> In conclusion, a human mind is a wandering mind, and a wandering mind is an unhappy mind. The ability to think about what is not happening is a cognitive achievement that comes at an emotional cost.

### The costs, and the possible benefits

Benjamin Mooneyham and Jonathan Schooler's review keeps both halves in view ([Mooneyham and Schooler, 2013](https://metalab.psych.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/publications/2013/2013-The-costs-and-benefits-of-mind-wandering-a-review.pdf)). On the cost side:

> Substantial evidence suggests that mind-wandering typically occurs at a significant cost to performance. Mind-wandering–related deficits in performance have been observed in many contexts, most notably reading, tests of sustained attention, and tests of aptitude.

On the benefit side, they reviewed evidence for possible roles in autobiographical planning and creative problem solving, and proposed three further candidate functions ([Mooneyham and Schooler, 2013](https://metalab.psych.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/publications/2013/2013-The-costs-and-benefits-of-mind-wandering-a-review.pdf)):

> ...attentional cycling, dishabituation, and relief from boredom.

Their most disarming result concerns tedium ([Mooneyham and Schooler, 2013](https://metalab.psych.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/publications/2013/2013-The-costs-and-benefits-of-mind-wandering-a-review.pdf)):

> In short, mind-wandering appeared to partially insulate people against the mood costs of engaging in a particularly tedious task.

Their summary guard ([Mooneyham and Schooler, 2013](https://metalab.psych.ucsb.edu/sites/default/files/publications/2013/2013-The-costs-and-benefits-of-mind-wandering-a-review.pdf)):

> ...mind-wandering does not typically provide affective relief, and indeed imposes significant costs to mood as well as performance.

### Meditation findings

Judson Brewer's group compared experienced meditators with matched controls across three meditation types ([Brewer et al., 2011](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1112029108)):

> We found that the main nodes of the default-mode network (medial prefrontal and posterior cingulate cortices) were relatively deactivated in experienced meditators across all meditation types.

The same scans showed stronger coupling in the meditators between control-related regions, and the authors read the differences as "consistent with decreased mind-wandering" ([Brewer et al., 2011](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1112029108)). The study reports differences between groups, not training effects: what practice does to the default is a question it frames rather than one it answers ([Brewer et al., 2011](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1112029108)).
