The illusory truth effect is the finding that repetition makes a statement feel truer. The mechanism is processing fluency: a repeated claim is easier for the mind to process than a novel one, and that ease is mistaken for evidence. Felt certainty tracks ease of processing, not accuracy.

> "Repetition increases belief in false statements. This illusory truth effect occurs with many different types of statements (e.g., trivia facts, news headlines, advertisements), and even occurs when the false statement contradicts participants' prior knowledge." — [Fazio, Collabra: Psychology 6(1) (2020), Article 38, full text](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/4661/2c2131dcec0324ad7cb147974ce8f9453e80.pdf)

### What makes it dangerous

The follow-up sentence is the one worth sitting with: "We again find that prior knowledge does not protect against the illusory truth effect. Repeated false statements were given higher truth ratings than novel statements, even when they contradicted participants' prior knowledge." Knowing better is not a vaccine. The compendium's principles on certainty and testing what arises both draw on this: fluency is a property of the channel, not of the message, and a claim felt twice is not a claim checked twice.
