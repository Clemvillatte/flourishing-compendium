Correspondence is Tim Ingold's name for the way living beings go on together. His premise is that every living being should be envisaged "not as a blob but as a bundle of lines," and that in answering to one another these lifelines co-respond. The term carries the old sense of exchanging letters: lives correspond the way correspondents do, over time, each reply bending the next.

> "Correspondence, in this sense, is the process by which beings or things literally answer to one another over time, for example in the exchange of letters or words in conversation, or indeed in holding hands." (Ingold, ["On human correspondence", JRAI 2017](https://aura.abdn.ac.uk/bitstreams/992285bd-a678-4ced-83a9-475a9e9a3413/download))

### Lines, not points

The contrast Ingold draws is with interaction. Interaction joins finished parties, each with interests already fixed; correspondence is what happens while lives are still forming. In his words, "The prosecution of interests, in interaction, is like an oscillation between two points. But correspondence is not a connection of points but a binding of lines. It is not interactive but multilinear. And these lines, as we have seen, join not at the ends but in the middle" ([same text](https://aura.abdn.ac.uk/bitstreams/992285bd-a678-4ced-83a9-475a9e9a3413/download)). He grounds this in three principles: "of habit (rather than volition), 'agencing' (rather than agency) and attentionality (rather than intentionality)" (same text).

The 2020 book Correspondences (Polity) gathers this as a practice of attention. As its publisher's description puts it, to correspond is "to join our lives with those of the beings, matters and elements with whom, and with which, we dwell upon the earth" ([Wiley/Polity page](https://www.wiley.com/en-us/Correspondences-p-9781509544103)).
