The TRACE model of speech perception is James McClelland and Jeffrey Elman's 1986 account of how the mind recognises speech: not by matching sound to stored word-shapes one at a time, but by letting small hypotheses about features, phonemes and words excite and inhibit each other as the sound unfolds. Their own summary of the program:

> "We describe a model called the TRACE model of speech perception. The model is based on the principles of interactive activation. Information processing takes place through the excitatory and inhibitory interactions of a large number of simple processing units, each working continuously to update its own activation on the basis of the activations of other units to which it is connected." ([McClelland & Elman, 1986, abstract](https://stanford.edu/~jlmcc/papers/McClellandElman86.pdf))

### Origin

The model appeared in Cognitive Psychology in 1986 (vol. 18, pp. 1-86). The paper's own note on where it comes from: "This framework is the interactive activation framework (McClelland & Rumelhart, 1981; Rumelhart & McClelland, 1981, 1982)" ([McClelland & Elman, 1986](https://stanford.edu/~jlmcc/papers/McClellandElman86.pdf)). A later retrospective by McClelland and colleagues describes the model as providing "a concrete instantiation of the interactive perspective" ([McClelland, Mirman & Holt, 2006](https://web.stanford.edu/~jlmcc/papers/McCMirmanHolt06_TiCS.pdf)).

### Core claims

The paper's abstract states what the model was meant to do:

> "TRACE II, the focus of this article, simulates a large number of empirical findings on the perception of phonemes and words and on the interactions of phoneme and word perception." ([McClelland & Elman, 1986, abstract](https://stanford.edu/~jlmcc/papers/McClellandElman86.pdf))

> "At the word level, the model captures the major positive feature of Marslen-Wilson COHORT model of speech perception, in that it shows immediate sensitivity to information favoring one word or set of words over others. At the same time, it overcomes a difficulty with the COHORT model: it can recover from underspecification or mispronunciation of a word's beginning." ([McClelland & Elman, 1986, abstract](https://stanford.edu/~jlmcc/papers/McClellandElman86.pdf))

The authors also state their scope honestly: "The TRACE model has some limitations, but we believe it is a step toward a psychologically and computationally adequate model of the process of speech perception" ([McClelland & Elman, 1986, abstract](https://stanford.edu/~jlmcc/papers/McClellandElman86.pdf)).

### How the model works

The model is mechanistic, and its parts are the explanation. A later summary by McClelland and colleagues gives the architecture: "The model (Figure I) consists of a feature layer, a phonemic layer and a lexical layer", and "Mutually consistent units on different levels (/k/ as the first phoneme in a spoken word, 'kiss' as the identity of the word) activate each other via excitatory connections, whereas mutually inconsistent units within the same level (/k/ and /g/ as the first phoneme) compete through mutually inhibitory connections" ([McClelland, Mirman & Holt, 2006](https://web.stanford.edu/~jlmcc/papers/McCMirmanHolt06_TiCS.pdf)). excitation flow is "bi-directional: both bottom-up (features to phonemes to words) and top-down (words to phonemes to features)" ([McClelland, Mirman & Holt, 2006](https://web.stanford.edu/~jlmcc/papers/McCMirmanHolt06_TiCS.pdf)). The name comes from the network itself: "The model is called the TRACE model because the network of units forms a dynamic processing structure called 'the Trace,' which serves at once as the perceptual processing mechanism and as the system's working memory" ([McClelland & Elman, 1986](https://stanford.edu/~jlmcc/papers/McClellandElman86.pdf)).

The 2006 retrospective adds the authors' own caution about what the model is: "we see the TRACE model not as the brain's implementation of this process, but as characterizing at a conceptual level a process that is implemented at a neural level using a much more distributed form of representation" ([McClelland, Mirman & Holt, 2006](https://web.stanford.edu/~jlmcc/papers/McCMirmanHolt06_TiCS.pdf)).

### In everyday life

Hearing someone speak in a noisy room is what the model is about: a cough can swallow a whole sound of the word "gift", and the listener still hears the word, because on this account the word level fills in for the missing phoneme level. (Illustrative scenario, not a reported simulation from the papers.)
