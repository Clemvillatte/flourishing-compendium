“Consequential evaluation” — Dewey, Theory of Valuation.

Consequential evaluation is the account of value worked out in John Dewey's Theory of Valuation (1939): valuing is not a flash of feeling but a judgment about what will follow from what. To value something is to appraise it as a means to ends, and the appraisal is open to evidence in the same way other empirical judgments are:

> "Examination of these appraisals discloses that they have to do with things as they sustain to each other the relation of means to ends or consequences. Wherever there is an appraisal involving a rule as to better or as to needed action, there is an end to be reached: the appraisal is a valuation of things with respect to their serviceability or needfulness." ([Dewey, Theory of Valuation, International Encyclopedia of Unified Science II(4), open full text](https://archive.org/stream/theoryofvaluatio032168mbp/theoryofvaluatio032168mbp_djvu.txt))

The test is behavioral before it is verbal, and Dewey puts it in one of his sharpest sentences:

> "In empirical fact, the measure of the value a person attaches to a given end is not what he says about its preciousness but the care he devotes to obtaining and using the means without which it cannot be attained." (same text)

What makes the theory consequential rather than merely instrumental is that ends get the same treatment as means. Ends-in-view are themselves appraised, and the appraisal is checkable:

> "(2) The existential relation in question is that of means-ends or means-consequences. (3) These propositions in their generalized form may rest upon scientifically warranted empirical propositions and are themselves capable of being tested by observation of results actually attained as compared with those intended."

> "Since desire and valuation of objects proposed as ends are inherently connected, and since desire and ends-in-view need to be appraised as means to ends (an appraisal made on the basis of warranted physical generalizations) the valuation of ends-in-view is tested by consequences that actually ensue." (same text)

The payoff for a life is a habit: when you want something, ask what would actually have to happen, what it would cost, and what the attainment will then make possible or block. That question is the working core of [[P079 — Choose acts by their consequences]], and it belongs to the same family as [[Valuation as inquiry]] and [[Pragmatism]].
