Benign versus malicious envy is the distinction van de Ven, Zeelenberg and Pieters (2009) tested between two qualitatively different types of envy: one that moves the envier up, and one that pulls the envied person down ([van de Ven et al., 2009, Emotion 9(3):419-429, PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19485619/)).

> "Envy is the painful emotion caused by the good fortune of others. This research empirically supports the distinction between two qualitatively different types of envy, namely benign and malicious envy." — [van de Ven, Zeelenberg & Pieters 2009, abstract, PubMed 19485619](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19485619/)

### Two motivations

The two types share the sting and part ways at what to do next: benign envy motivates improving one's own position; malicious envy motivates damaging the position of the superior other ([van de Ven et al., 2009, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19485619/)).

> "the experience of benign envy leads to a moving-up motivation aimed at improving one's own position, whereas the experience of malicious envy leads to a pulling-down motivation aimed at damaging the position of the superior other." — [van de Ven, Zeelenberg & Pieters 2009, abstract, PubMed 19485619](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19485619/)

### Evidence across languages

Study 1 used guided recall in the Netherlands, a culture with separate words for the two envy types, and found the predicted experiential differences; Studies 2 and 3, in the United States and Spain, used latent class analysis to confirm separate experiences of benign and malicious envy in cultures that have a single word for both ([van de Ven et al., 2009, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19485619/)).
