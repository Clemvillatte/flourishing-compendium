The wise man's indifference to reputation is the Stoic claim that what others say about a person is not among the things worth governing a life by. The judgment of the crowd is fickle, poorly informed and aimed at appearances; the wise man keeps his attention on the deed itself. Seneca's Letter 76 draws the line inside the good man's own task:

> "A good man will do what he thinks it will be honourable for him to do, even if it involves toil; he will do it even if it involves harm to him; he will do it even if it involves peril; again, he will not do that which will be base, even if it brings him money, or pleasure, or power."
> — [Seneca, Letters 76, trans. John Gummere](http://www.rockyrook.com/2021/06/letters-from-stoic-76-on-learning.html)

The passage rules the standard of action: honour, not reward. Reputation — applause, thanks, the memory others keep — belongs to the class of things that happen to a deed from outside, and Seneca's good man answers that these "lie outside my task":

> "All these matters lie outside my task. My thoughts are on the deed itself. I know that this is honourable. Therefore, whithersoever I am led and summoned by honour, I will go."
> — [Seneca, Letters 76, trans. John Gummere](http://www.rockyrook.com/2021/06/letters-from-stoic-76-on-learning.html)

Indifference here is Stoic indifference: not contempt, but refusal to count reputation as a good. It is classed with money, pleasure and power — things that can accompany a good life or a bad one, and so cannot be its measure. The practical corollary is a division of attention: the wise man does not consult the audience before acting, and does not audit its verdict afterward.
