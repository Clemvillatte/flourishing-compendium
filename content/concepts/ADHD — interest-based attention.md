ADHD attention is interest-based: driven by strong personal interest, or by imminent unpleasantness, rather than by the importance of the task. Thomas Brown, summarizing the evidence behind his 2013 book A New Understanding of ADHD in Children and Adults: Executive Function Impairments, describes the pattern:

> Every child or adult with ADHD whom I have ever evaluated has a few favored activities in which they have no problem focusing, sustaining effort, and utilizing working memory. But they do have such difficulties in almost everything else they do.
>
> (Thomas E. Brown, "The Truth about ADHD", [Psychology Today, April 25, 2013](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/kids-and-adhd/201304/the-truth-about-adhd))

The impairment varies with context: "a fundamental characteristic of ADHD is that it varies according to the context in which the person is functioning" (Brown 2013).

> They perform well if the task is something that actually interests them—something that really turns them on—they're up for it. If it doesn't turn them on, they cannot get up for it—they cannot adequately perform.
>
> (Brown 2013)

The contrast with importance is Brown's own framing of the puzzle: "Why do people with ADHD focus very well on a few things they really like to do, but they can't focus well on other tasks that they know are important?" (Brown 2013). On his account the brain's chemical messaging carries the load "unless the task is especially interesting or scary" (Brown 2013), which is why the capacity looks like laziness or weak willpower from outside and is neither.
