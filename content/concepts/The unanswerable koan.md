"The unanswerable koan" is my heading, illustrated here by case 1 of the Gateless Gate, the thirteenth-century collection of Mumon. The original entry names the Zen tradition and that collection.

### Joshu's dog

> A monk asked Joshu, a Chinese Zen master: "Has a dog Buddha-nature or not?" Joshu answered: "Mu." (Mumon, The Gateless Gate, case 1, trans. Nyogen Senzaki and Paul Reps, 1934, [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Gateless_Gate/Joshu%27s_Dog))

Mumon's own comment connects realization with passing the barrier of the patriarchs and with "the road of thinking" being blocked:

> "To realize Zen one has to pass through the barrier of the patriarchs. Enlightenment always comes after the road of thinking is blocked." (same page)

The comment also guards against reading Mu as a mere negative: "Do not believe it is the common negative symbol meaning nothing. It is not nothingness, the opposite of existence" (same page). The work it asks for is described without comfort:

> "If you really want to pass this barrier, you should feel like drinking a hot iron ball that you can neither swallow nor spit out." (Mumon's comment, same page)

Mumon's verse sets the trap the whole case turns on:

> "Has a dog Buddha-nature? / This is the most serious question of all. / If you say yes or no, / You lose your own Buddha-nature." (Mumon's verse, same page)

The instruction that accompanies it: "Just concentrate your whole energy into this Mu, and do not allow any discontinuation" (same page). Mumon's verse warns that saying yes or no means losing one's own Buddha-nature.
