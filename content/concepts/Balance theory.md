## In plain words

Balance theory is Fritz Heider's account of why our likes and dislikes want to fit together. It is "a theory of attitude change, proposed by Fritz Heider," which "conceptualizes the cognitive consistency motive as a drive toward psychological balance" ([Wikipedia, Balance theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balance_theory)). When our feelings multiply out to a negative, the theory predicts a pull to change one of them.

## Where it comes from

Heider set out the theory in The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations (1958), the same book that founded attribution theory. Dorwin Cartwright and Frank Harary recast the triads as signed graphs in a 1956 paper that built on Heider's earlier work, in which "a balanced signed graph has only cycles of positive sign" ([Wikipedia, Balance theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balance_theory)).

## How it works

The unit is the P-O-X triad: a person, another person, and a thing. If P likes object X but dislikes person O, learning that O created X leaves P with an imbalanced triad: "Multiplying the signs shows that the person will perceive imbalance (a negative multiplicative product) in this relationship, and will be motivated to correct the imbalance somehow" ([Wikipedia, Balance theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balance_theory)). The exits are the theory's predictions: P may "decide that O isn't so bad after all," "decide that X isn't as great as originally thought," or "conclude that O couldn't really have made X" ([Wikipedia, Balance theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balance_theory)). Balance is achieved "when there are three positive links or two negatives with one positive"; two positive links and one negative "creates imbalance or cognitive dissonance" ([Wikipedia, Balance theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balance_theory)).

## What it explains

The theory explains the quiet edits we make to our opinions to keep the set consistent: liking a friend's admired enemy, or revising a taste after learning who made the thing. Its formal descendants include structural balance in social networks, where, within the signed-graph model, a balanced graph splits into two camps.

## Everyday life

You admire a writer, then learn the writer despises a teacher you love. The triangle itches, and over the following weeks you may notice one of the three feelings quietly rewriting itself. Balance theory names the itch.
