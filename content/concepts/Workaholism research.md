“Workaholism research” — Ng et al., 2007.

Workaholism research studies heavy work investment that has stopped being a choice. The term comes from Wayne Oates, who created it in 1971 to describe the "uncontrollable need to work incessantly" and defined the workaholic as a "person whose need for work has become so excessive that it creates noticeable disturbance or interference with his bodily health, personal happiness, and interpersonal relations, and with his smooth social functioning" ([Sussman 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3835604/)). The field's central review states its own problem:

> "While the concept of workaholism has received a good deal of attention in the popular press, theoretical and empirical research have lagged behind. In part, the lack of a formal, agreed upon definition of the construct and its dimensions has hampered research on this topic. The purpose of this review is to offer a cohesive definition of workaholism, discuss its underlying dimensions, and identify its key antecedents and consequences." (Ng, Sorensen & Feldman 2007, abstract, [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/job.424))

### The model and its measures

Ng, Sorensen and Feldman's integration runs antecedents through to behavior: dispositions (achievement orientation, desire for higher self-esteem), socio-cultural experiences (a stressful childhood work can be an escape from, vicarious learning of workaholism at home and at work, peer competition, self-efficacy drawn mostly from work) and behavioral reinforcements (tangible and intangible rewards, winner-takes-all systems, organizational environments that drive overwork) feed immediate precursors: joy in working, guilt and anxiety when not working, obsession with working, and self-imposed long hours that spill into personal life ([Sussman 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3835604/), describing the model). Measurement most often uses the Workaholism Battery, whose items mark three dimensions, work involvement, work drive and work enjoyment, though studies repeatedly find only the drive and enjoyment factors ([Sussman 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3835604/)).

My principle D029 (stop before the cup overflows) is the practice-side reading: the hours are not the problem, the inability to stop is.
