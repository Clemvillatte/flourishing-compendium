Habit discontinuity is the idea that a major change in life circumstances loosens old habits and makes people more open to new behaviour. The field experiment that tested it states the hypothesis as: "behaviour change interventions are more effective when delivered in the context of life course changes". The assumption is that "when habits are (temporarily) disturbed, people are more sensitive to new information and adopt a mind-set that is conducive to behaviour change" ([Verplanken and Roy, 2016, Journal of Environmental Psychology 45, 127-134, QUB repository PDF](https://pureadmin.qub.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/180563797/1_s2.0_S0272494415300487_main.pdf)).

### What counts as a discontinuity

Verplanken and Roy list "transitions to new phases in life (e.g., from education to a job), geographical or physical changes (e.g., residential or work-related relocations), or changes in the environment where habits are executed (e.g., infrastructural changes)". Such changes "may force people to renegotiate ways of doing things, create a need for information to make the new choices, and a mind-set of being 'in the mood for change'" ([ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0272494415300487)).

### Why habits give way

The mechanism comes from earlier work by Wood, Tam and Witt. Their abstract says that "when usual contexts for performance change, habits cannot be cued by recurring stimuli, and performance should be disrupted". Among students transferring to a new university, exercising, newspaper-reading and TV-watching habits survived the move "only when aspects of the performance context did not change". They add that in some cases "the disruption in habits also placed behavior under intentional control so that participants acted on their current intentions" ([Wood, Tam and Witt, 2005, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15982113/)).

### The field test

Verplanken and Roy ran an experiment with 800 participants. Half of the households had recently relocated and half had not, and each group was split between an intervention promoting sustainable behaviours and a no-intervention control. Eight weeks later, "the intervention was more effective among recently relocated participants". "The results suggested that the duration of the 'window of opportunity' was three months after relocation."

### Limits

- The effect was small. The project summary reports "a small but statistically significant increase in self-reported sustainable behaviour" and warns of "no blanket roll-outs" ([HABiT project summary](https://timjackson.org.uk/sustainablelifestyles.ac.uk/projects/change-processes/habits.html)).
- The outcome was self-reported behaviour over eight weeks.
- Verplanken and Roy write in the paper that "a disruption is a temporary condition, and once a person has settled into the new situation, old habits may easily be re-activated".
- In their later chapter on consumer habits, they say the three-month window "is by no means set in stone, and may be different in other contexts" ([Consumer habits and sustainable consumption](https://pureadmin.qub.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/202925916/016_CHAPTER_15_Verplanken_Roy_1_2_.pdf)).
