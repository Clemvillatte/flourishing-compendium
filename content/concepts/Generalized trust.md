Generalized trust is trust in strangers, or in "most people in society", rather than trust in people you already know. A paper on trust and mental health defines it as "the trust in strangers or most people in society, which reflects the individual's belief that most people can be trusted" ([Taylor & Francis, 2025](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/21515581.2025.2456270)). An economics paper draws the same line: "The former entails trusting people you know, the latter trusting people you do not know" ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11482-023-10163-2)).

### What the evidence links it to

Helliwell and Wang's paper, which uses Gallup World Poll and Canadian survey data, reports that "those who feel themselves to be living in a trustworthy environment have much higher levels of subjective well-being". A 2016 follow-up using three large international surveys estimates the income-equivalent value of social trust, "with a likely lower bound equivalent to a doubling of household income" ([Helliwell, Huang and Wang, NBER](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22450/w22450.pdf)). A meta-analysis reports that "both general and specific trust were positively related to well-being" ([The Relationship Between Trust and Well-Being: A Meta-Analysis](https://ir.psych.ac.cn/bitstream/311026/47813/1/The%20Relationship%20Between%20Trust%20and%20Well-Being%EF%BC%9A%20A%20Meta-Analysis.pdf)).

These are associations from survey data. They do not on their own show that trust causes well-being.

### Trust as resilience

The 2016 paper also finds that "living in a high-trust environment makes people more resilient to adversity". Discrimination, ill-health or unemployment, "although always damaging to subjective well-being", is "much less damaging to those living in trustworthy environments" ([Helliwell, Huang and Wang, NBER](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w22450/w22450.pdf)).

### Is trusting naive?

The seed note says vulnerability to exploitation depends on context. One piece of evidence is the dropped-wallet experiments in Helliwell and Wang's work, which show that "wallets are far more likely to be returned, even by strangers in large cities, than people expect" ([Helliwell and Wang, CRDCN abstract](https://crdcn.ca/publication/trust-and-well-being-2/)). People under-estimate how trustworthy others are, at least in that setting.

### Where it comes from

A study of perceived inequality found, in a mediation analysis, that "perceptions of inequality diminish generalized trust, and diminished generalized trust, in turn, lowers life satisfaction" ([Wiley, Asian Journal of Social Psychology](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ajsp.12617)). Trust is therefore a feature of the social environment as well as of the person.
