The heading names the Plotinian arc: the soul comes from the intelligible realm, enters a body, and its fulfilment lies in turning back toward where it came from. Plotinus records the return in a first-person passage that opens the treatise On the Descent of the Soul into Bodies:

> "Often I have woken up out of the body to my self and have entered into myself, going out from all other things; I have seen a beauty wonderfully great and felt assurance that then most of all I belonged to the better part; I have actually lived the best life and come to identity with the divine [...] Then after that rest in the divine, when I have come down from Intellect to discursive reasoning, I am puzzled how I ever came down, and how my soul has come to be in the body when it is what it has shown itself to be by itself, even when it is in the body." (Plotinus, Ennead IV.8.1, Armstrong translation; passage abbreviated)

The descent in the same treatise is described as willing rather than as mere punishment: "the soul, though it is divine and comes from above, enters into the body and, though it is a god of the lowest rank, comes to this world by a spontaneous inclination". Armstrong's introductory note adds that "we do not altogether descend", since the highest part of the soul "remains in the intelligible, though we are not always aware of it". The return, on this account, is less travel than re-orientation: attention turning back to what was there all along.
