“The relaxation response” — Benson, 1975.

The relaxation response is Herbert Benson's name for the body's counterpart to the stress response, elicited by a simple mental practice. The seed cites 1975, which is the year of the popular book by Benson and Miriam Klipper. Benson, Beary and Carol had already published an article with the same title in Psychiatry in [1974](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/4810622/). The page rests on a PubMed record for that article (no abstract available), a PubMed abstract of a later study, and secondary descriptions of the book. The book itself was not opened.

### What it is

[Wikipedia's entry on the book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Relaxation_Response) describes the response as "an autonomic reaction elicited by a mental device and a passive attitude". It lists four components: a mental device, a passive attitude, a quiet environment and a comfortable position, and says that by 1996 only the first two were found to be essential. A behavioural-sleep-medicine chapter by Lichstein gives the same four elements and says Benson regarded the passive attitude as "the most important relaxation ingredient". ([Lichstein, Relaxation for Insomnia](https://www.med.upenn.edu/cbti/assets/user-content/documents/Lichstein_RelaxationforInsomnia-BTSD.pdf)) The practical instruction in the book, in outline, is to repeat a neutral word silently while breathing out, for ten to twenty minutes once or twice daily, according to a summary on [Superbook](https://superbook.ai/books/the-relaxation-response).

### Evidence at the molecular level

A later study from Benson's group measured gene expression in blood before and after a session. Both long-term and novice practitioners showed "significant temporal gene expression changes", with greater significance in the long-term group. Practice "enhanced expression of genes associated with energy metabolism, mitochondrial function, insulin secretion and telomere maintenance, and reduced expression of genes linked to inflammatory response and stress-related pathways". ([Bhasin et al. 2013, PLoS ONE, PubMed 23650531](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23650531/)) The study is small and the authors' interpretation of mitochondrial resilience is hypothetical, so this page treats it as suggestive, not conclusive.

### Limits

The seed compresses a book and a research programme into a date. The compendium links the response to stillness and calm in Daoist and Buddhist principles; the sources opened here do not support equating Benson's technique with samatha or passaddhi, and the link is the compendium's own analogy. This page does not summarise clinical trials on blood pressure or sleep.
