Chronobiology is the study of biological timing, and its cognitive branch documents what the original entry's carrier summarised: mental performance is not level across the day but waves with it. The review that anchored the original entry's claim opens:

> "Although peaks and troughs in cognitive performance characterize our daily functioning, time-of-day fluctuations remain marginally considered in the domain of cognitive psychology and neuropsychology." ([Schmidt, Collette, Cajochen & Peigneux, 2007](http://www.chronobiology.ch/wp-content/uploads/publications/2007_09.pdf))

### What the timing carries

The review's conclusion is twofold. First, generality: "beyond physiological variables, time-of-day modulations affect performance on a wide range of cognitive tasks measuring attentional capacities, executive functioning, and memory." Second, individuality: "These performance fluctuations are also contingent upon the chronotype, which reflects interindividual differences in circadian preference, and particularly upon the synchronicity between the individuals' peak periods of circadian arousal and the time of the day at which testing occurs." A morning person and an evening person measured at the same hour are not measured under the same conditions, and the synchrony between clock time and personal peak predicts who is at their best. My principles AT028 and D064 carry the applied reading: schedule attentionally demanding work to one's own peak, and act with the timing rather than against it. A compendium exercise drawn from the field is to rate one's clarity hourly for two ordinary days and see whether the schedule already matches the curve. What the field does not give: an excuse; the rhythms modulate capacity, they do not abolish responsibility for arranging the day.
