The social selves are William James's term, in The Principles of Psychology (1890), for the version of a person that exists in other people's minds. He opens the discussion with a one-line definition:

> "A man's Social Self is the recognition which he gets from his mates."
> — [James, Principles of Psychology, ch. X (1890)](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin10.htm)

James then notes that there is not one of them. There are as many social selves as there are people who carry an image of that person.

> "Properly speaking, a man has as many social selves as there are individuals who recognize him and carry an image of him in their mind. To wound any one of these his images is to wound him. But as the individuals who carry the images fall naturally into classes, we may practically say that he has as many different social selves as there are distinct groups of persons about whose opinion he cares."
> — [James, Principles of Psychology, ch. X (1890), p. 294](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin10.htm)

James adds that a person generally shows a different side of himself to each of these different groups. Many a youth who is demure enough before his parents and teachers, he writes, swears and swaggers like a pirate among his tough young friends.

### When the split hurts

James's own distinction at the end of the passage is between two ways the several selves can sit together. The division may be a discordant splitting, as where someone is afraid to let one set of his acquaintances know him as he is elsewhere. It may also be a perfectly harmonious division of labour, as where someone tender to his children is stern to the soldiers or prisoners under his command.
