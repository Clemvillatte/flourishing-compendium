The layered soul is Aristotle's account of what a soul is, set out in De Anima II.1-3: the soul is the form of a living body, not a separate thing inside it. The picture is layered because living functions come in levels — plants have nutrition, animals add perception and movement, humans add intellect — and the higher levels presuppose the lower ones. Aristotle is answering the question the tradition had left in confusion, "What is soul?", with a definition built from his hylomorphism: matter is potentiality, form is actuality, and the soul is the actuality of a living body.

### The definition

The core sentences of Book II, chapter 1, in J. A. Smith's translation:

> "That is why the soul is the first grade of actuality of a natural body having life potentially in it."

([De Anima II.1, 412a27-28, Smith translation](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Aristotle/De-anima/de-anima2.htm).)

Two sentences later the body is characterized as organized: "The parts of plants in spite of their extreme simplicity are 'organs'; e.g. the leaf serves to shelter the pericarp, the pericarp to shelter the fruit, while the roots of plants are analogous to the mouth of animals, both serving for the absorption of food" ([412b, Smith translation](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Aristotle/De-anima/de-anima2.htm)). The eye analogy makes the form-matter relation concrete:

> "Suppose that the eye were an animal-sight would have been its soul, for sight is the substance or essence of the eye which corresponds to the formula, the eye being merely the matter of seeing; when seeing is removed the eye is no longer an eye, except in name — it is no more a real eye than the eye of a statue or of a painted figure."

([De Anima II.1, Smith translation](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Aristotle/De-anima/de-anima2.htm).)

### Why the layers matter

The levels are not parts of one organ but capacities of one organized body: nutrition in plants, perception in animals, intellect in humans. Each level builds on the one below, since perceiving animals must also nourish themselves, and thinking humans must also perceive. Aristotle explicitly refuses the question of whether soul and body are one: "it is as meaningless as to ask whether the wax and the shape given to it by the stamp are one, or generally the matter of a thing and that of which it is the matter" ([412b6-9, Smith translation](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Aristotle/De-anima/de-anima2.htm)). The Stanford Encyclopedia summarizes the position: the soul is a "first actuality of a natural organic body" (412b5-6) and this applies "to plants, animals and humans alike" ([SEP, Aristotle's Psychology](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-psychology/)).

The concept belongs to the compendium's discrimination practices by analogy rather than by descent. Aristotle's levels are vital capacities, not layers of experience, and the passages opened here do not speak of body, energy, emotion or thought as objects of awareness. The analogy the compendium draws is with the Taittiriya Upanishad's sheaths, the map behind Test experience layer by layer (Y090); Distinguish the feeling from its interpretation (AF002) applies the same examining discipline to affect. The Aristotle page supplies the ontological ground for the analogy: a person is one organized body with ordered capacities, not a ghost piloting a machine.
