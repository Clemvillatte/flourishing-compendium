Environmental justice asks who bears the burdens of pollution and degradation, who is heard when decisions are made, and what a fair relationship with the natural world would look like. David Schlosberg's book on the subject says its task is "to explore what, exactly, is meant by 'justice' in definitions of environmental and ecological justice" ([Oxford Academic, abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/4798)).

### Schlosberg's argument

His central claim is that "a theory and practice of environmental justice necessarily includes distributive conceptions of justice, but must also embrace notions of justice based in recognition, capabilities, and participation." The aim is "a broad, multi-faceted, yet integrated notion of justice" that applies both to "environmental risks in human populations" and to "relations between human communities and non-human nature" ([abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/4798)).

The book's chapters show how the four ideas appear in practice. Chapter 3 describes "equity-based, recognition-focused, procedural, and capability-oriented frames of justice" within the United States movement ([chapter 3 abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/4798/chapter-abstract/147074953)). Chapter 4 finds "elements of equity and distribution, individual and cultural recognition, political participation, and individual and community functioning" in global movements such as indigenous rights, food security and climate justice ([chapter 4 abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/4798/chapter-abstract/147076194)).

### Justice to nature

Schlosberg also separates environmental justice, "justice among humans on environmental issues and risks", from ecological justice, "justice to non-human nature", and points to the gap between the two literatures ([chapter 1 abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/4798/chapter/147073476)). In chapter 6 he writes that "the point is not to develop a singular universal theory of ecological justice, but rather to illustrate the potential of various discourses, concepts and frames as they can be extended to animals, communities, and natural systems" ([chapter 6 abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/4798/chapter/147077912)). Chapter 7 argues for "a pluralist approach that allows for unity among different concerns and movements while avoiding the uniformity that is so often debilitating" ([chapter 7 abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/4798/chapter-abstract/147079490)).

### Health

Brulle and Pellow review "the dimensions of unequal exposures to environmental pollution (environmental inequality)", theories about its origins, and the response of the movement and of governments in the United States. They conclude "that more research is needed that links environmental inequalities with public health outcomes" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16533111/)). The link between unequal exposure and health is a case for study, which the review does not treat as settled.

### Scope

The Schlosberg material comes from publisher abstracts, so the page reports his argument in outline. The Brulle and Pellow review is also known only from its abstract.
