**Rational Emotive Behavior Theory** — Ellis — irrational beliefs, not events, disturb people; disputing them restores functioning.

Rational emotive behavior therapy (REBT) is Albert Ellis's theory of how suffering is made, and how it can be unmade. Ellis presented the therapy, first called rational therapy, in 1955, about a decade before Aaron Beck's cognitive model, and it is one of the founding forms of cognitive behavioral therapy. The APA Dictionary defines it this way:

> "a form of cognitive behavior therapy based on the concept that an individual's self-defeating beliefs influence and cause negative feelings and undesirable behaviors. Specifically, a person's problems are seen as the result of turning healthy desires for success, approval, and pleasure into demands (e.g., 'I must achieve outstandingly well in one or more important respects or I am an inadequate person')."
>
> (APA Dictionary of Psychology, "rational emotive behavior therapy (REBT)", developed in 1955 by Albert Ellis)

### Origin
Ellis, an American psychotherapist, was inspired by Asian, Greek, Roman and modern philosophers, and in his first major book he wrote that the central principle of his approach, that people are rarely emotionally affected by external events but rather by their thinking about such events, "was originally discovered and stated by the ancient Stoic philosophers" (Wikipedia, after Ellis). It is the clinical working-out of the old Stoic claim that people are disturbed not by things but by their views about things.

### Core claims
The model is usually stated as A-B-C:

> "The A-B-C model states that it is not an A, adversity (or activating event) that cause disturbed and dysfunctional emotional and behavioral Cs, consequences, but also what people B, irrationally believe about the A, adversity."
>
> (Wikipedia, "Rational emotive behavior therapy")

An activating event (A) meets a belief (B), and the belief, not the event, produces the emotional consequence (C). The beliefs that do the damage are the demands: rigid musts about achievement, approval and comfort. The therapy adds D and E, and the APA Dictionary gives the procedure: "an extension of ABC theory to include the procedure used in rational emotive behavior therapy of D isputing irrational beliefs to achieve several types of E ffects (e.g., self-enhancing beliefs, appropriate feelings, desirable behaviors). [devised by Albert Ellis]".

### How the theory explains suffering
This is a mechanistic theory of emotional disturbance. The machine is belief: an event arrives, the demand hidden in the interpretation fires, and the feeling follows the demand rather than the event. Because the belief is the movable part, the theory holds that disputing it, directly and emphatically, is the lever. Ellis's own technique was famously blunt: the therapist questions the client's beliefs head-on, causing them to rethink them, and disputing is a skill that can be learned over time to help people manage emotional responses and limit harmful beliefs (Simply Psychology). The theory also carries a limit worth knowing: it treats beliefs as the cause of disturbance, a claim the compendium's Stoic sources would state as a conditional, not a universal, and modern cognitive therapy has refined what counts as an irrational belief.

### In everyday life (illustrative scenario)
A man is not invited to a meeting he believes he should have been in. The event is thin: a missing email. The belief is heavy: if I am left out, I am disrespected, and if I am disrespected, I must be inadequate. The feeling follows the belief, not the calendar entry. REBT's move is to take the demand to court: must he be included? What would actually be true if he were not? The dispute does not deny the sting; it removes the "must" that turned a sting into a verdict on his worth.
