Rational emotive behavior therapy (REBT) is Albert Ellis's theory of how suffering is made, and how it can be unmade. Ellis presented the therapy, first called rational therapy, in 1955, about a decade before Aaron Beck's cognitive model, and it is one of the founding forms of cognitive behavioral therapy. The APA Dictionary defines it this way:

> "a form of cognitive behavior therapy based on the concept that an individual's self-defeating beliefs influence and cause negative feelings and undesirable behaviors. Specifically, a person's problems are seen as the result of turning healthy desires for success, approval, and pleasure into demands (e.g., 'I must achieve outstandingly well in one or more important respects or I am an inadequate person')."
>
> (APA Dictionary of Psychology, "rational emotive behavior therapy (REBT)", developed in 1955 by Albert Ellis)

### Origin
Ellis, an American psychotherapist, was inspired by Asian, Greek, Roman and modern philosophers. In his first major book on rational therapy, Ellis wrote that the central principle of his approach, that people are rarely emotionally affected by external events but rather by their thinking about such events, was not original to him:

> "was originally discovered and stated by the ancient Stoic philosophers."
>
> (Ellis, quoted in [Wikipedia, "Rational emotive behavior therapy"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_emotive_behavior_therapy))

The clinical working-out stands on that Stoic ground.

### Core claims
The model is usually stated as A-B-C:

> "The A-B-C model states that it is not an A, adversity (or activating event) that cause disturbed and dysfunctional emotional and behavioral Cs, consequences, but also what people B, irrationally believe about the A, adversity."
>
> (Wikipedia, "Rational emotive behavior therapy")

An activating event (A) meets a belief (B), and REBT proposes that the belief, particularly when a preference hardens into a rigid demand, helps shape the emotional consequence (C). The therapy then adds D and E: disputing the irrational beliefs to achieve new effects — self-enhancing beliefs, appropriate feelings, desirable behaviors — the procedure the APA Dictionary attributes to Ellis ([ABCDE technique](https://dictionary.apa.org/abcde-technique)).

### How the theory explains suffering
The theory treats belief as the movable part of emotional disturbance: an event arrives, an interpretation carries a demand, and the feeling follows the interpretation as much as the event. Disputing the demand, directly and emphatically, is the therapy's lever. In practice the therapist questions the client's beliefs head-on, causing them to rethink them, and disputing is a skill that can be learned over time to help people manage emotional responses and limit harmful beliefs ([Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/rational-emotive-behavior-therapy.html)).

### In everyday life
(illustrative scenario) A man is not invited to a meeting he believes he should have been in. The event is thin: a missing email. The belief is heavy: if I am left out, I am disrespected, and if I am disrespected, I must be inadequate. The feeling follows the belief as much as the calendar entry. REBT's move is to take the demand to court: must he be included? What would actually be true if he were not? The dispute does not deny the sting; it questions the "must" that turned a sting into a verdict on his worth.
