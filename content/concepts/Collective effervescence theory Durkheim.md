**Collective effervescence theory Durkheim** — Durkheim — shared ritual intensity produces fused emotion and group solidarity.

Collective effervescence is Émile Durkheim's term for what happens to feeling when people gather. In The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life (1912), studying the ceremonies of Australian Aboriginal clans, he describes a life in two phases: long stretches scattered in small family groups, and rare gatherings of the whole clan, where everything changes:

> "The very fact of the concentration acts as an exceptionally powerful stimulant. When they are once come together, a sort of electricity is formed by their collecting which quickly transports them to an extraordinary degree of exaltation."

> "Every sentiment expressed finds a place without resistance in all the minds, which are very open to outside impressions; each re-echoes the others, and is re-echoed by the others." ([Durkheim 1912, The Elementary Forms of the Religious Life, Swain translation, pp. 215-217](https://studfile.net/preview/16670754/page:12/))

Two things make the theory more than a description of crowds. First, the emotion is genuinely collective rather than merely parallel: each person's feeling amplifies the others', and the gathering finds rhythm by itself:

> "And since a collective sentiment cannot express itself collectively except on the condition of observing a certain order permitting co-operation and movements in unison, these gestures and cries naturally tend to become rhythmic and regular; hence come songs and dances." (same book)

Second, Durkheim makes the effervescence the birthplace of the sacred. The participant is taken so far outside ordinary life that the gathering itself comes to feel like another world, and the conviction settles that there are two worlds:

> "One is that where his daily life drags wearily along; but he cannot penetrate into the other without at once entering into relations with extraordinary powers that excite him to the point of frenzy. The first is the profane world, the second, that of sacred things."

> "So it is in the midst of these effervescent social environments and out of this effervescence itself that the religious idea seems to be born." (same book, pp. 217-219)

The theory is the ancestor of a whole research line on ritual and synchrony, from Randall Collins's interaction ritual chains to work on choral singing and marching together. Its relevance to flourishing is direct: some of the strongest self-transcendent experiences available to a person are not private at all, but made in company. The compendium's principle [[M015 — Let art be communal, not only private]] draws on exactly this, and [[K228 — Awe practice]] turns it into practice.
