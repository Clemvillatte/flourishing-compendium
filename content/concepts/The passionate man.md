The passionate man is Simone de Beauvoir's portrait, in The Ethics of Ambiguity (1947), of one of the forms freedom takes when it goes wrong: a subjectivity that chooses its object absolutely, and then tries to possess it. Her characterisation:

> "What characterizes the passionate man is that he sets up the object as an absolute, not, like the serious man, as a thing detached from himself, but as a thing disclosed by his subjectivity." (de Beauvoir, The Ethics of Ambiguity, [ch. II](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/ethics/de-beauvoir/ambiguity/ch02.htm))

### What the portrait carries

The failure is not in the caring but in the possession: "The passionate man seeks possession; he seeks to attain being... He causes certain rare treasures to appear in the world, but he also depopulates it. Nothing exists outside of his stubborn project... And having involved his whole life with an external object which can continually escape him, he tragically feels his dependence... And he remains at a distance; he is never fulfilled." The passage closes on the ethical edge: the passionate man "is on the way to tyranny," because "not intending his freedom for men, the passionate man does not recognize them as freedoms either. He will not hesitate to treat them as things. If the object of his passion concerns the world in general, this tyranny becomes fanaticism." The compendium principle E074 carries the practical reading: avoid the passionate man's stance in oneself, the absolutised object that depopulates the world around it. A compendium exercise drawn from the portrait is to notice when a valued thing has become an absolute, and to ask what other people have become in its service. What the portrait does not give: a dismissal of passion itself; de Beauvoir reserves generous passion as the ground of the ethical life, and it is the maniacal form, not the caring, that she condemns.
