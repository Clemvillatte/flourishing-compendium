Connectionism is the cognitive-science approach that models cognition as activity in networks of simple, neuron-like units: knowledge lives in the strengths of the connections between units, not in rules or sentences, and learning is the gradual adjustment of those strengths. The [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/connectionism/) defines it:

> "Connectionism is an approach to the study of human cognition that utilizes mathematical models, known as connectionist networks or artificial neural networks. Often, these come in the form of highly interconnected, neuron-like processing units." (IEP, Connectionism, Introduction)

### Origin

The approach's modern career began in 1986, when David Rumelhart and James McClelland's Parallel Distributed Processing research group published a two-volume compendium of modeling techniques and psychological models:

> "Connectionism sprang back onto the scene in 1986 with a monumental two-volume compendium of connectionist modeling techniques (volume 1) and models of psychological processes (volume 2) by David Rumelhart, James McClelland and their colleagues in the Parallel Distributed Processing (PDP) research group." (IEP, Connectionism, section 4)

The revival was powered by a learning algorithm, backpropagation, which let multi-layer networks learn from their errors.

### Core claims

The approach's bet about representation:

> "its proponents aimed to replace theoretical appeals to formal rules of inference and sentence-like cognitive representations with appeals to the parallel processing of diffuse patterns of neural activity." (IEP, Connectionism, Introduction)

And about processing:

> "they encode information through activation vectors (and weight vectors), and they process that information when activity propagates forward through many weighted connections." (IEP, Connectionism, section 2)

### How the approach explains

A network model explains cognition statistically rather than logically. Units stand for nothing in particular; a memory or a word's meaning is a pattern of activation spread across many units, and what a system knows is in its weights. Learning is error-driven weight change:

> "Connectionist systems generally learn by detecting complicated statistical patterns present in huge amounts of data." (IEP, Connectionism, section 5)

The famous early demonstration was the past-tense model: a network learned English verb forms and spontaneously reproduced children's u-shaped curve, over-applying "goed" mid-training, without any rule in the system:

> "all of the relevant information can be stored in superimposed fashion within the weights of a connectionist network" (IEP, Connectionism, section 6.a)

The standing objection comes from the classical camp: Fodor and Pylyshyn argued that connectionism cannot account for the systematicity and productivity of thought. As the IEP summarizes their critique, it was "largely aimed at showing that connectionism could not account for important characteristics of human thinking, such as its generally truth-preserving character, its productivity, and (most important of all) its systematicity" (section 6.d).

### In everyday life

The connectionist picture of skill is the everyday one of practice: nobody memorises a rule for riding a bicycle; thousands of small adjustments, each nudged by error, leave the balance knowledge in the "weights" of the body. Skill as accumulated error-correction, not stored instructions. (Illustrative scenario, not a reported model or study result.)
