Eudaimonia (εὐδαιμονία, “good spirit”) is Aristotle's term for the highest human good — a life of flourishing, of living well and doing well, rather than a passing state of mind. In the *Nicomachean Ethics* it is the one thing pursued for its own sake and not for anything beyond it: not pleasure, not contentment, not a feeling at all, but the *activity* of the soul in accordance with virtue, sustained across a complete life ([Britannica, “Eudaimonia”](https://www.britannica.com/topic/eudaimonia)). Because it is activity, it is acquired by practice, not by luck alone; because it spans a whole life, a flourishing youth can still be overturned — the argument of *Nicomachean Ethics* I.9–11 that the compendium keeps as A008 and A010 ([[A008 — Accept that flourishing takes a whole life]]; [[A010 — Recognise that fortune can overturn happiness]]).

The distinction that organises the whole library: eudaimonia is not [[Hedonic adaptation|hedonic]] pleasure. Hedonia is how experience feels; eudaimonia is what a life adds up to — meaning, growth, virtue, contribution ([[A001 — Make eudaimonia the organising end of your life]]). The positive psychologists' distinction between hedonic and eudaimonic well-being echoes this. The faculty that steers toward it is [[Phronesis]] — practical wisdom, the trained judgment about what the situation calls for.

The practical reading: because eudaimonia is activity, the question “is my life going well?” is answered by looking at what I repeatedly do, not at how I feel today ([[A006 — Expect flourishing to require external goods]] names the honest caveat — some flourishing needs resources; [[A026 — Practice patience with others' faults]] shows it is social). An example at its smallest: the quarterly hour — what did I do this quarter that expressed what I stand for?

Its great Greek rival is [[Ataraxia]] — tranquility as the goal rather than activity.
