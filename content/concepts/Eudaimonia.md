Eudaimonia (εὐδαιμονία, "good spirit") is Aristotle's term for the highest human good: a life of flourishing, of living well and doing well, rather than a passing state of mind.

> "Eudaimonia, in Aristotelian ethics, is the condition of human flourishing or living well. For Aristotle, eudaimonia is the highest human good, the only human good that is desirable for its own sake (as an end in itself) rather than for the sake of something else (as a means toward some other end)." ([Britannica, "Eudaimonia"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/eudaimonia))

In the *Nicomachean Ethics* it is not pleasure, not contentment, not a feeling at all, but the activity of the soul in accordance with virtue, sustained across a complete life:

> "'if the function of man is an activity of soul which follows or implies a rational principle,' and if the human good is the good performance of that function, then the 'human good turns out to be [rational] activity of soul in accordance with virtue.'" (*Nicomachean Ethics* I.7, quoted in [Britannica, "Eudaimonia"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/eudaimonia))

> "For one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day; and so too one day, or a short time, does not make a man blessed and happy." (*Nicomachean Ethics* I.7, quoted in [Britannica, "Eudaimonia"](https://www.britannica.com/topic/eudaimonia))

Because it is activity, it is acquired by practice, not by luck alone; because it spans a whole life, a flourishing youth can still be overturned, the argument of *Nicomachean Ethics* I.9–11 that the compendium keeps as A008 and A010.

### Eudaimonia versus hedonia

The distinction that organises the whole library: eudaimonia is not [[Hedonic adaptation|hedonic]] pleasure. Hedonia is how experience feels; eudaimonia is what a life adds up to: meaning, growth, virtue, contribution. The positive psychologists' distinction between hedonic and eudaimonic well-being echoes this. The faculty that steers toward it is [[Phronesis]], practical wisdom, the trained judgment about what the situation calls for.

### The practical reading

Because eudaimonia is activity, the question "is my life going well?" is answered by looking at what I repeatedly do, not at how I feel today. The honest caveat is that some flourishing needs resources, and the social side is real. Its great Greek rival is [[Ataraxia]], tranquility as the goal rather than activity.
