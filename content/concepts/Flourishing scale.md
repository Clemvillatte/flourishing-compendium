“Flourishing scale” — Huppert and So, 2013.

Huppert and So's scale grew from a question governments were starting to ask: if progress is to be measured by well-being, what exactly should be counted? Their answer starts from the mirror end of the diagnostic manuals. Taking the internationally agreed criteria for depression and anxiety, they defined the opposite of each symptom and arrived at ten features that combine feeling and functioning, in their words: competence, emotional stability, engagement, meaning, optimism, positive emotion, positive relationships, resilience, self-esteem, and vitality ([Social Indicators Research 110(3), 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3545194/)).

Their definition of what the scale measures:

> Flourishing refers to the experience of life going well. It is a combination of feeling good and functioning effectively. Flourishing is synonymous with a high level of mental well-being, and it epitomises mental health. (Huppert and So, "Flourishing Across Europe: Application of a New Conceptual Framework for Defining Well-Being," Social Indicators Research 110(3), 2013, pp. 837-861, [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3545194/))

### What it found

Applied to a representative sample of about 43,000 Europeans in 23 countries, the operational definition (all but one of the positive-characteristics features and all but one of the positive-functioning features, together with positive emotion) labelled 15.8 percent of the sample as flourishing. The spread between countries was large: Denmark topped the list at about 41 percent flourishing, with Slovakia, Russia and Portugal below 10 percent — a more than four-fold difference (ibid.). The scale's value for the compendium is structural: it shows flourishing is a multi-dimensional construct that a single life-satisfaction number would miss, the working premise of PP004.
