“Flourishing scale” — Huppert and So, 2013.

The compendium's inherited seed attributes the flourishing scale to Huppert and So (2013), and their framework is what this page documents — but a distinction matters. What Huppert and So built is a ten-feature conceptual framework and an operational definition of flourishing, not the eight-item questionnaire called the Flourishing Scale. That instrument belongs to Diener et al. (2009), and Huppert and So mention it by name when noting how recent scales economize: "It is interesting to note that two recently designed well-being scales, the Warwick-Edinburgh Mental Well-being Scale (Stewart-Brown et al. 2009; Tennant et al. 2007) and the Flourishing Scale of Diener et al. (2009) also use only one item per construct" ([Social Indicators Research 110(3), 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3545194/)).

Their framework starts from the mirror end of the diagnostic manuals: taking the internationally agreed criteria for depression and anxiety, they defined the opposite of each symptom and arrived at ten features. In their words: "These combine feeling and functioning, i.e. hedonic and eudaimonic aspects of well-being: competence, emotional stability, engagement, meaning, optimism, positive emotion, positive relationships, resilience, self esteem, and vitality" ([Social Indicators Research 110(3), 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3545194/)).

Their definition of what counts as flourishing:

> Flourishing refers to the experience of life going well. It is a combination of feeling good and functioning effectively. Flourishing is synonymous with a high level of mental well-being, and it epitomises mental health. (Huppert and So, "Flourishing Across Europe: Application of a New Conceptual Framework for Defining Well-Being," Social Indicators Research 110(3), 2013, pp. 837-861, [full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3545194/))

### What it found

Applied to a representative sample of about 43,000 Europeans in 23 countries, the operational definition (all but one of the positive-characteristics features and all but one of the positive-functioning features, together with positive emotion) labelled 15.8 percent of the sample as flourishing. The spread between countries was large: Denmark topped the list at about 41 percent flourishing, with Slovakia, Russia and Portugal below 10 percent — a more than four-fold difference (ibid.). The framework's value for the compendium is structural: it shows flourishing is a multi-dimensional construct that a single life-satisfaction number would miss, the working premise of PP004.
