Communitarianism is the view that the self is made, not merely accompanied, by its communities. Daniel Bell's SEP article defines it plainly: "Communitarianism is the idea that human identities are largely shaped by different kinds of constitutive communities (or social relations) and that this conception of human nature should inform our moral and political judgments as well as policies and institutions" ([SEP Communitarianism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/communitarianism/)). Those communities "shape, and ought to shape, our moral and political judgments," and we owe them support: without them we would be "disoriented, deeply lonely, and incapable of informed moral and political judgment" ([SEP Communitarianism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/communitarianism/)).

The movement began as a reaction to Rawls. Political philosophers "drawing primarily upon the insights of Aristotle and Hegel — Alasdair MacIntyre, Michael Sandel, Charles Taylor and Michael Walzer — disputed Rawls' assumption that the principal task of government is to secure and distribute fairly the liberties and economic resources individuals need to lead freely chosen lives" ([SEP Communitarianism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/communitarianism/)). In "Atomism," Taylor objected to the liberal view that "men are self-sufficient outside of society," defending instead the Aristotelian view that man "is not self-sufficient alone, and in an important sense is not self-sufficient outside a polis" (Taylor 1985, 190, as quoted in [SEP Communitarianism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/communitarianism/)).

The self at issue is attached rather than autonomous. Sandel: "We ordinarily think of ourselves... 'as members of this family or community or nation or people, as bearers of this history, as sons or daughters of that revolution, as citizens of this republic'" (Sandel 1981, 179, as quoted in [SEP Communitarianism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/communitarianism/)). Taylor's "higher, strongly evaluated goods" are "not somehow invented by individuals, but rather... located within the social world which provides one's framework of the lower and the higher" ([SEP Communitarianism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/communitarianism/)).

For the flourishing project, communitarianism is the structural counterweight to atomistic individualism: genuine flourishing runs through constitutive membership, not around it.
