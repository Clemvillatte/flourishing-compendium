Process-based therapy (PBT) is the framework Stefan G. Hofmann and Steven C. Hayes built to replace diagnosis-and-protocol psychotherapy: instead of asking which named treatment fits which named disorder, the clinician asks which processes of change are active in this particular person, and targets them directly. The book that carries the model announces it as a redefinition, not a new brand:

> "Process-based therapy (PBT) is a new definition of what evidence-based therapy even means. It's not a new treatment method—it's a new way of thinking about treatment methods." (Hofmann, Hayes & Lorscheid, Learning Process-Based Therapy (2021), Prologue)

### Origin

PBT emerged from the "protocols for syndromes" problem: decades of evidence-based therapy had produced treatment packages keyed to DSM diagnoses, which fit individuals poorly and multiplied faster than the evidence could sort them. The model was set out across a series of papers by Hayes and Hofmann from 2018 to 2019 and given its full clinical manual in Learning Process-Based Therapy. Its intellectual roots are evolutionary science, functional analysis, and network approaches to psychopathology.

> "PBT is not a new therapy—it is a new model of evidence-based therapy. The goal of PBT is to move toward understanding and targeting the processes of change in a given individual and to move away from nomothetic, group-based analyses that tend to miss important individual processes that may be vital for effective and efficient treatment." (Hofmann, Hayes & Lorscheid, Learning Process-Based Therapy (2021), Chapter 1)

### Core claims

The shift the model asks for runs from content to function and from groups to individuals:

> "PBT emphasizes the importance of function over content" (Hofmann, Hayes & Lorscheid, Learning Process-Based Therapy (2021), Chapter 1)

The aim, in the book's own summary:

> "The aim of PBT is to best understand which core biopsychological processes to target in an individual given their specific goals and stage of intervention, and how to best do so, using functional analysis, complex network approaches, and identification of core change processes developed from evidence-based treatments (Hayes & Hofmann, 2018)." (Hofmann, Hayes & Lorscheid, Learning Process-Based Therapy (2021), Chapter 1)

Three working parts carry the model. A network model maps the person's problem as interacting elements rather than a symptom of an underlying disease: "A network is made up of single parts that link together and influence one another." Functional analysis, done as a new ten-step form of diagnosis, identifies which processes maintain the problem. And treatment kernels, the smallest empirically grounded change methods, are fitted to the identified processes:

> "The basic elements of these strategies are treatment kernels—specific change methods designed to modify processes of change." (Hofmann, Hayes & Lorscheid, Learning Process-Based Therapy (2021), Chapter 10)

### How the model explains

PBT is a meta-framework: it organises rather than replaces other approaches. Any therapy tradition's methods survive as kernels if they can be linked to evidence about processes of change. The model's taxonomy of processes is the Extended Evolutionary Meta-Model (EEMM), which classifies change processes into six psychological dimensions (affect, cognition, attention, self, motivation, overt behavior) nested into two further levels (biophysiological and sociocultural), with variation, selection, retention and context operating in each. The diagnostic question becomes: which of these processes, in this person's network, is doing the maladaptive work, and what would make it adaptive?

### In everyday life

The model's stance generalises beyond the clinic. Someone whose low mood, isolation, doom-scrolling and poor sleep form a mutually reinforcing cluster is not treated as "having depression" in the protocol sense; the cluster is mapped, the process doing the heaviest lifting (say, attention rigidly fixed on threatening news) is identified, and a kernel targeting attentional flexibility is applied, then re-measured. (Illustrative scenario, not a reported client or study result.)

### In the compendium

The compendium's P156/PP081 psychological flexibility principles carry the ACT lineage PBT grew from; the EEMM's variation-selection-retention scaffold is the compendium's standing method for practice kernels.
