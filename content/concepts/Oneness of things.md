“Oneness of things” — Zhuangzi 2.

The Zhuangzi's second chapter contains the claim in this passage ([Zhuangzi 2, trans. Burton Watson, Terebess](https://terebess.hu/english/chuangtzu.html)):

> "There is nothing in the world bigger than the tip of an autumn hair, and Mount T'ai is tiny. No one has lived longer than a dead child, and P'eng-tsu died young. Heaven and earth were born at the same time I was, and the ten thousand things are one with me. We have already become one, so how can I say anything? But I have just said that we are one, so how can I not be saying something? The one and what I said about it make two, and two and the original one make three. If we go on this way, then even the cleverest mathematician can't tell where we'll end, much less an ordinary man. If by moving from nonbeing to being we get to three, how far will we get if we move from being to being? Better not to move, but to let things be!" (Zhuangzi, [Chapter 2, trans. Burton Watson, Terebess Asia Online](https://terebess.hu/english/chuangtzu.html))

Read the passage twice, because it performs what it describes. It first dissolves the scales we sort the world by, big and small, long-lived and short-lived, Mount T'ai and a hair's tip, and lands on the claim that heaven, earth and the speaker share one birth. Then it catches itself: to say "we are one" already makes a second thing, the saying, and the counting runs past any mathematician. The oneness the chapter points at cannot be stated without the statement becoming one more thing beside it. That is not a defect of the chapter; it is the method. The practice this concept names is not to hold a doctrine that all is one, but to notice the sorting and the saying, and to let things be. The Butterfly Dream story in this library carries the same structure: a self that cannot finally say which side of the dream it is on.
