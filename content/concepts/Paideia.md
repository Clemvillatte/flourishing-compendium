Paideia is the Greek tradition of formation — the rearing and education of the ideal member of the polis. The Wikipedia summary:

> "Paideia... referred to the rearing and education of the ideal member of the ancient Greek polis or state. ... Paideia was meant to instill aristocratic virtues in the young citizen men who were trained in this way. An ideal man within the polis would be well-rounded, refined in intellect, morals, and physicality, so training of the body, mind, and soul was important." ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paideia))

### What the formation included

The training was not one subject but a whole person: practical schooling in the liberal arts — rhetoric, grammar, mathematics, philosophy — alongside medicine; gymnastics and wrestling "valued for their effect on the body"; and moral education "imparted by the study of music, poetry, and philosophy". The article gives the tradition's summary of the aim: "The culture and the youth were formed to the ideal of kalos kagathos ('beautiful and good')."

The list reads as one curriculum rather than three. Gymnastics answers a question about the body, the liberal arts a question about the intellect, and music, poetry and philosophy a question about character, and the ideal the article describes is well-rounded because the thing being formed is a person rather than a field. On this account the word's usefulness for a modern reader lies in that completeness: it names an education whose subject is the whole person.

The word for that person, kalos kagathos, joins the beautiful and the good, and the article describes moral education as the part of the formation aimed at the second half of the pair. The aim, on this account, is a kind of person rather than a body of knowledge, and the curriculum is described in terms of its effect on that person rather than for its own sake.
