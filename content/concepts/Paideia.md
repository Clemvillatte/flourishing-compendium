Paideia is the Greek tradition of formation — the rearing and education of the ideal member of the polis. The Wikipedia summary:

> "Paideia... referred to the rearing and education of the ideal member of the ancient Greek polis or state. ... Paideia was meant to instill aristocratic virtues in the young citizen men who were trained in this way. An ideal man within the polis would be well-rounded, refined in intellect, morals, and physicality, so training of the body, mind, and soul was important." ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paideia))

### What the formation included

The training was not one subject but a whole person: practical schooling in the liberal arts — rhetoric, grammar, mathematics, philosophy — alongside medicine; gymnastics and wrestling "valued for their effect on the body"; and moral education "imparted by the study of music, poetry, and philosophy". The article records the culture's own summary of the aim: "The culture and the youth were formed to the ideal of kalos kagathos ('beautiful and good')."

The list is worth reading as one curriculum rather than three, since the parts answer different questions and the tradition treats the answers as belonging to a single formation. Gymnastics answers a question about the body, the liberal arts a question about the intellect, and music, poetry and philosophy a question about character, and the ideal the tradition aimed at is described as well-rounded precisely because it is a person rather than a field that the parts are meant to serve. A formation that trained only one of the three would not have counted as paideia, and the word's usefulness for a modern reader lies in that completeness: it names an education whose subject is the whole person.

The word for that person, kalos kagathos, is also worth holding as a pair rather than one term, since the tradition's own ideal joins the beautiful and the good, and the join is what the moral education was for. The formation's aim was therefore not literacy or even knowledge, but a kind of person, and the curriculum was selected for its effect on that person rather than for its own sake.
