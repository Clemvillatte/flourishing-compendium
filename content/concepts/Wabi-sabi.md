Wabi-sabi (侘び寂び) is the Japanese aesthetic of the imperfect, the impermanent and the incomplete. "In traditional Japanese aesthetics, wabi-sabi centers on the acceptance of transience and imperfection" ([Wikipedia, Wabi-sabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wabi-sabi)). It compounds two older words; the Stanford Encyclopedia translates them as "wabi (subdued, austere beauty), sabi (rustic patina)" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Japanese Aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-aesthetics/)). The two were first praised in poetry, then fused in the tea room: "it is in the art of tea, and the context of Zen, that the notion of wabi is most fully developed" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Japanese Aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-aesthetics/)).

### The two halves

Sabi began as desolateness and aged into beauty: "The term sabi occurs often in the Manyōshū, where it has a connotation of desolateness (sabireru means 'to become desolate'), and later it seems to acquire the meaning of something that has aged well, grown rusty (another word pronounced sabi means 'rust'), or has acquired a patina that makes it beautiful" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Japanese Aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-aesthetics/)). A commentator renders it as "aged" in the sense of "ripe with experience and insight" and "infused with the patina that lends old things their beauty," along with "tranquility, aloneness, deep solitude" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Japanese Aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-aesthetics/)). Wabi is the counterpart stance toward insufficiency: understated beauty praised in poetry, as when Kenkō asks whether we are "to look at cherry blossoms only in full bloom, at the moon only when it is cloudless" and answers that "branches about to blossom or gardens strewn with faded flowers are worthier of our admiration" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Japanese Aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-aesthetics/)).

### The tea room

Sen no Rikyū's tea ceremony made the aesthetic concrete. In the Nampōroku, the record of his sayings:

> "In the small [tea] room, it is desirable for every utensil to be less than adequate. There are those who dislike a piece when it is even slightly damaged; such an attitude shows a complete lack of comprehension" (Hirota 226, quoted in [Stanford Encyclopedia, Japanese Aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-aesthetics/))

The consequence runs against every instinct of display:

> "Implements with minor imperfections are often valued more highly, on the wabi aesthetic, than ones that are ostensibly perfect; and broken or cracked utensils, as long as they have been well repaired, more highly than the intact" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Japanese Aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-aesthetics/))

His crawl-in door (nijiriguchi), sized so that swords and status hats could not pass through, made the same point socially: "worldly rank is ignored" in the tea room ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Japanese Aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-aesthetics/)). His Raku tea bowls, made by a local potter, were "often asymmetrical and imperfect in form and finish" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Japanese Aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-aesthetics/)).

### An inner stance, not a decoration style

The 1828 Zencharoku is explicit that wabi is a way of holding one's circumstances:

> "Wabi means that even in straitened circumstances no thought of hardship arises. Even amid insufficiency, one is moved by no feeling of want. Even when faced with failure, one does not brood over injustice. If you find being in straitened circumstances to be confining, if you lament insufficiency as privation, if you complain that things have been ill-disposed—this is not wabi" (Hirota 275, quoted in [Stanford Encyclopedia, Japanese Aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-aesthetics/))

The repair art of kintsugi carries the same spirit into object form: "as a philosophy, it treats breakage and repair as part of the history of an object, rather than something to disguise" ([Wikipedia, Kintsugi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kintsugi)).

### Why it matters for flourishing

The roots are Buddhist: wabi-sabi "derives from the Buddhist teaching of the three marks of existence... impermanence (mujō), suffering (ku), and emptiness or absence of self-nature (kū)" ([Wikipedia, Wabi-sabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wabi-sabi)). The payoff is not resignation: "awareness of the fundamental condition of existence is no cause for nihilistic despair, but rather a call to vital activity in the present moment and to gratitude for another moment's being granted to us" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Japanese Aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-aesthetics/)). The compendium's principle [[D108 — Accept imperfection]] reads the same lesson off Daodejing 45: acknowledge a thing's faults without treating it as worthless. A chipped bowl that serves tea is not a failed bowl.
