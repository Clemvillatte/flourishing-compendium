Perspective-getting is the practice of obtaining another person's perspective directly: asking them what they think, listening to the answer, or experiencing the situation the way they do, rather than imagining it from outside. The term belongs to Tal Eyal, Mary Steffel and Nicholas Epley, whose review of 25 experiments concluded that understanding another mind is "enabled by getting perspective, not simply taking perspective" ([Eyal, Steffel and Epley 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620401/)).

### Why imagining falls short

The background finding is older. Epley, Keysar, Van Boven and Gilovich showed in 2004 that perspective-taking works by anchoring on one's own perspective and adjusting away from it, and that the adjustment is typically insufficient: egocentric biases grew under time pressure and shrank only when accuracy was paid for ([Epley et al. 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15382983/)).

Eyal, Steffel and Epley then tested the presumption itself across 25 experiments, from reading emotions in faces and postures to spotting fake smiles and predicting a spouse's preferences. Instructed perspective-taking produced no consistent accuracy gains; if anything, it decreased accuracy while occasionally increasing confidence. It did reduce egocentric bias, but the information put in place of the self's was no more accurate. A final experiment showed the difference: getting another's perspective directly, through conversation, increased accuracy where taking it did not.

### In practice

Getting is active. It asks a real question, waits through the answer, and treats a correction as data. The compendium's reflective-listening kernel K366 makes the tension explicit: it has the listener guess at what someone means underneath their words, but say the guess as a checkable statement, so that "if it misses, they correct you, and the correction is itself information."
