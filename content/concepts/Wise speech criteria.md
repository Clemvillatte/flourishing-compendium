Wise speech criteria are the test the Buddha gives in MN 58 for what is worth saying. Three factors decide: whether the statement is true, whether it is beneficial, and whether it is pleasing to others. The Buddha states only what is true and beneficial, and times the pleasing and displeasing versions. The possibility that a statement might be untrue yet beneficial is not even entertained ([dhammatalks.org introduction to MN 58](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN58.html)).

### The six cases

The sutta runs the criteria through both the agreeable and the disagreeable cases:

> "In the case of words that the Tathāgata knows to be factual, true, beneficial, but unendearing & disagreeable to others, he has a sense of the proper time for saying them. [...] In the case of words that the Tathāgata knows to be factual, true, beneficial, and endearing & agreeable to others, he has a sense of the proper time for saying them. Why is that? Because the Tathāgata has sympathy for living beings."

([MN 58, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, dhammatalks.org](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN58.html).)

In the other four cases — untrue, unbeneficial, or both — he does not say them, whatever their reception. The criterion that carries the rule is benefit: truth without benefit is not enough.

### The infant image

The sutta grounds the criteria in an image before it gives the list. A child puts a stick or pebble in its mouth; the father would take it out "even if it meant drawing blood. Why is that? Because I have sympathy for the young boy" ([MN 58, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN58.html)). Speech that hurts is sometimes the removal of something harmful; the timing question is how to do that without unnecessary damage.
