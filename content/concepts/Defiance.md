Defiance, in Camus's work, is the refusal to accept oppression or the world's injustice as the last word. The compendium takes the idea from The Rebel (1951), where his own term is revolt or rebellion. The Stanford Encyclopedia says that in this book "the act of rebellion assumes the status of a primary datum of human experience", something Camus starts from and does not try to justify ([SEP, "Albert Camus"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/camus/)).

### From private to shared

The Rebel begins "with the kind of revolt that rejects oppression and slavery, and protests against the world's injustice". It is first one person's rebellion, as in The Myth of Sisyphus, but "now Camus stresses that revolt creates values, dignity, and solidarity." His formula is "I revolt, therefore we are" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/camus/)). The entry asks how an "I" can lead to a "we", and answers that acting against oppression means appealing to social values and joining others in the struggle.

### Limits Camus sets

Defiance has a built-in restraint. Rebellion is, "in principle", "a protest against death, just as it is a source of the solidarity that binds the human community", so killing even an oppressor damages what the revolt defends. Camus also insists that the rebel "cannot, therefore, absolutely claim not to kill or lie, without renouncing his rebellion and accepting, once and for all, evil and murder." He leaves the tension open. He suggests two signs that a revolution remains faithful to rebellion, abolishing the death penalty and encouraging freedom of speech ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/camus/)).

The entry adds that Camus is hostile to hopes of a better future that justify sacrifice now: "Living for, and sacrificing humans to, a supposedly better future is, very simply, another religion."

### Scope

This page draws on one encyclopedia entry, not on The Rebel itself. The entry calls the book "a historically framed philosophical essay about underlying ideas and attitudes of civilization", and finds its last chapters hard to follow. What defiance means for personal suffering is argued more fully in The Myth of Sisyphus.
