"Schwartz" indexes Shalom H. Schwartz's theory of basic human values, developed in papers of 1990 (with W. Bilsky) and 1994 and summarized by the author in 2012. The theory's starting point is a definition of values as goals that transcend situations: "Values refer to desirable goals that motivate action," they "transcend specific actions and situations," and "the relative importance of multiple values guides action." Values are also affective: "Values are beliefs linked inextricably to affect. When values are activated, they become infused with feeling." (Shalom H. Schwartz, "An Overview of the Schwartz Theory of Basic Values," Online Readings in Psychology and Culture, 2012, [full text](https://scholarworks.gvsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1116&context=orpc))

### Ten values in a circle

The theory identifies ten basic values, each defined by a motivational goal. Schwartz's defining goals, verbatim:

| Value | Defining goal |
|---|---|
| Power | "social status and prestige, control or dominance over people and resources." |
| Achievement | "personal success through demonstrating competence according to social standards." |
| Hedonism | "pleasure or sensuous gratification for oneself." |
| Stimulation | "excitement, novelty, and challenge in life." |
| Self-Direction | "independent thought and action—choosing, creating, exploring." |
| Universalism | "understanding, appreciation, tolerance, and protection for the welfare of all people and for nature." |
| Benevolence | "preserving and enhancing the welfare of those with whom one is in frequent personal contact (the 'in-group')." |
| Tradition | "respect, commitment, and acceptance of the customs and ideas that one's culture or religion provides." |
| Conformity | "restraint of actions, inclinations, and impulses likely to upset or harm others and violate social expectations or norms." |
| Security | "safety, harmony, and stability of society, of relationships, and of self." |

The ten are not a flat list. They form a circle, and the circle is the theory's main claim: "At the heart of the theory is the idea that values form a circular structure that reflects the motivations each value expresses," and "this circular structure, that captures the conflicts and compatibility among the ten values is apparently culturally universal." The two axes of the circle: "one dimension contrasts 'openness to change' and 'conservation' values," between "values that emphasize independence of thought, action, and feelings and readiness for change (self-direction, stimulation)" and "values that emphasize order, self-restriction, preservation of the past, and resistance to change (security, conformity, tradition)." And "the second dimension contrasts 'self-enhancement' and 'self-transcendence' values," between "values that emphasize concern for the welfare and interests of others (universalism, benevolence)" and "values that emphasize pursuit of one's own interests and relative success and dominance over others (power, achievement)."

For my purposes the model offers a vocabulary for examining competing motivational goals: the values a person holds include ones the circle places in tension, so a felt conflict between independence and duty can be examined with the model's own dimensions rather than treated as a defect of the person. This application is the page's reading of the model, not a claim the model itself makes about individuals.
