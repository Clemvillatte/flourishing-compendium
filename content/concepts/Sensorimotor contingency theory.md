Sensorimotor contingency theory is J. Kevin O'Regan and Alva Noë's account of perception: perceiving is not processing images inside the brain but skillfully exploring the world, and what one knows in perceiving is how movement changes sensory input. Their 2001 paper states the central idea.

> "The central idea of our new approach is that vision is a mode of exploration of the world that is mediated by knowledge of what we call sensorimotor contingencies." (O'Regan & Noë, [A sensorimotor account of vision and visual consciousness](https://rctn.org/bruno/public/papers/oregan-noe01.pdf), Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24(5), 2001)

### Origin

The theory appeared in that 2001 Behavioral and Brain Sciences paper, with the authors at CNRS Paris and UC Santa Cruz, and Noë developed it further in a 2004 book.

### Core claims

The contingencies themselves are rules of interdependence between movement and stimulation.

> "We suggest that the basic thing people do when they see is that they exercise mastery of the sensorimotor contingencies governing visual exploration." (same paper)

The paper defines the contingencies as "the structure of the rules governing the sensory changes produced by various motor actions", and claims about consciousness that "the experience of seeing occurs when the organism masters what we call the governing laws of sensorimotor contingency". Seeing, on this account, is a skill: one has visual awareness of a thing when mastery of the relevant contingencies is integrated with thought, planning and action-guidance.
