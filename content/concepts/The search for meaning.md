The search for meaning is Viktor Frankl's name for the primary human drive. Against Freud's will to pleasure and Adler's will to power, logotherapy holds that "striving to find meaning in life is the primary, most powerful motivating and driving force in humans" ([Wikipedia, Logotherapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logotherapy)). The article notes that Frankl developed the theory before his internment in the Nazi camps and described, in the book, how it helped him survive them ([Wikipedia, Logotherapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logotherapy)).

### Three ways to find it

Frankl's own account of the search gives it three channels. "We can discover this meaning in life in three different ways: (1) by creating a work or doing a deed; (2) by experiencing something or encountering someone; and (3) by the attitude we take toward unavoidable suffering" ([Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning, as quoted in Wikipedia's Logotherapy article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logotherapy)). The first is creative work; the second is experience and love; the third is the one that made the book famous, because it claims a meaning available even when nothing else is.

### The last freedom

That third channel rests on the book's most quoted claim: "everything can be taken from a man but one thing: the last of the human freedoms – to choose one's attitude in any given set of circumstances" ([Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning, as quoted in Wikipedia's Logotherapy article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logotherapy)). Frankl held that no one escapes every biological, sociological, or psychological determinant, yet a person remains "capable of resisting and braving even the worst conditions" ([Frankl, The Will to Meaning, as quoted in Wikipedia's Logotherapy article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logotherapy)). The claim is not optimism; it is that the attitude remains the one thing not determined.
