Tight loose culture theory is the framework Michele Gelfand and colleagues introduced in their 33-nation Science study: cultures differ in the strength of their social norms and in their tolerance of deviance from them. The definitions in the abstract are the theory in one sentence: "With data from 33 nations, we illustrate the differences between cultures that are tight (have many strong norms and a low tolerance of deviant behavior) versus loose (have weak social norms and a high tolerance of deviant behavior)" ([Gelfand et al. 2011, Science, PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21617077/)).

### What drives the difference

The study's central claim is that tightness is a response to threat. Tightness-looseness is "part of a complex, loosely integrated multilevel system that comprises distal ecological and historical threats (e.g., high population density, resource scarcity, a history of territorial conflict, and disease and environmental threats), broad versus narrow socialization in societal institutions (e.g., autocracy, media regulations), the strength of everyday recurring situations, and micro-level psychological affordances (e.g., prevention self-guides, high regulatory strength, need for structure)" ([Gelfand et al. 2011, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21617077/)). The scale items are direct: "There are many social norms that people are supposed to abide by in this country" and "In this country, if someone acts in an inappropriate way, others will strongly disapprove" ([Science supplementary materials](https://www.science.org/action/downloadSupplement?doi=10.1126/science.1197754&file=gelfand.som.pdf)).

### Why the compendium keeps it

Two reasons. First, the theory explains a real trade-off in social life: tightness buys coordination and order at the price of freedom and openness to new behavior, and looseness buys the reverse. The compendium's principle Hold experience loosely (non-attachment) is an individual-level version of the same insight, and the theory shows why whole societies face the choice structurally. Second, the theory matters for cross-cultural practice: what counts as deviance depends on where you are, and a practice that reads as healthy autonomy in one setting reads as disorder in another.
