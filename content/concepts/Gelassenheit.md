Gelassenheit is a German word for a willed release of the will. It comes from the Middle High German gelâzenheit, which Meister Eckhart (c. 1260-1328) used in his sermons as a companion to detachment (abegescheidenheit). Martin Heidegger took the word up in the 1950s for his own account of thinking and of our relation to technology. In both uses it names an active letting-go, not a calm temperament and not passive acceptance.

### Eckhart's letting-go

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes Eckhart's gelâzenheit as one of his "negative virtues": attitudes that free the self from worldly attachments and keep "creaturely impulses" in check, so that the soul can become a receptacle for the divine. Attachment to anything distinct from God is what hinders the soul's freedom; the work is a self-dispossession that includes the "mine-ness" of one's actions. Eckhart is emphatic that this cannot be produced by technique or sought through special practices of devotion:

> "Whoever seeks God in a special way gets the way and misses God, who lies hidden in it" (Sermon 5b, quoted in [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Meister Eckhart](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/meister-eckhart/)).

The soul that has let go acts, in Eckhart's phrase, "without a why" (âne warumbe): God works through it, and its actions are performed for their own sake.

### Heidegger's releasement

Heidegger's "Conversation on a Country Path" (written 1944-45, published 1959) searches for a thinking that is not willing. The dialogue's teacher calls this thinking "higher acting" in the sense of waiting, and waiting that expects nothing:

> "We are to do nothing but wait" (Heidegger, Discourse on Thinking, p. 62, quoted in [Minerva: Heidegger on Gelassenheit](https://minerva.mic.ul.ie/vol10/Heidegger.html)).

The first step toward it is the paradoxical "I want non-willing": a willing renunciation of willing that prepares for releasement without producing it. In the "Memorial Address" of 1955 Heidegger brings the same disposition to technology. We can use technical devices and remain free to "let go of them at any time," denying them "the right to dominate us, and so to wrap, confuse, and lay waste our nature." He names this comportment releasement toward things, paired with openness to the mystery. Heidegger acknowledged Eckhart as the source of the word while explicitly distinguishing his use from Eckhart's theological one.

- [[Non-attachment]] — the Buddhist counterpart of releasing the grip on outcomes and possessions.
- [[Apatheia]] — the Stoic freedom from passion that Eckhart's negative virtues resemble.
