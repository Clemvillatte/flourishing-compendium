Gelassenheit is a German word for releasement or letting-go. It grows from the verb lassen, to let or to give something up. Meister Eckhart (c. 1260-1328) used the form gelâzenheit alongside detachment (abegescheidenheit), and Martin Heidegger later adapted the word for his own account of thinking and of the human relation to technology. The two uses differ on one point: Eckhart presents the letting-go as something a person actively cultivates, while for Heidegger releasement lies beyond willing altogether, and the will can at most renounce itself in preparation.

### Eckhart's letting-go

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes Eckhart's gelâzenheit as one of his "negative virtues": attitudes that free the self from worldly attachments and keep "creaturely impulses" in check, so that the soul can become a receptacle for the divine. True freedom, on this account, is a self-dispossession that includes letting go of the attachment to the "mine-ness" of one's actions. The Stanford Encyclopedia quotes Eckhart's warning against seeking God in a special way:

> "Whoever seeks God in a special way gets the way and misses God, who lies hidden in it" (Sermon 5b, quoted in [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy: Meister Eckhart](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/meister-eckhart/)).

The soul that has let go acts, in Eckhart's phrase, "without [a] why" (âne warumbe): God acts through it, and its actions are performed for their own sake.

### Heidegger's releasement

Heidegger's "Conversation on a Country Path" (written 1944-45, published 1959) searches for a thinking that is not willing. In the dialogue, the teacher says:

> "We are to do nothing but wait" (Heidegger, Discourse on Thinking, p. 62, quoted in [Minerva: Heidegger on Gelassenheit](https://minerva.mic.ul.ie/vol10/Heidegger.html)).

The waiting does not expect anything: on the article's reading of the dialogue, "'waiting' has no object". The first step toward it is the paradoxical "I want non-willing": a willing renunciation of willing that, at most, prepares for releasement.

In the "Memorial Address" of 1955 Heidegger brings the same disposition to technology: a person can use technical devices and remain free to "let go of them at any time," denying them "the right to dominate us, and so to wrap, confuse, and lay waste our nature." He names this comportment releasement toward things, paired with openness to the mystery. The Stanford Encyclopedia notes that Heidegger's adaptation of Eckhartian terms like Gelassenheit for his own philosophical purposes has further obscured Eckhart's own use of them.
