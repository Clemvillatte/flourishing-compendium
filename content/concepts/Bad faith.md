Bad faith (mauvaise foi) is Sartre's name for the self-deception by which a person refuses their own freedom — treating themselves as a fixed thing among things. The Stanford Encyclopedia gives the structural formula:

> "Any conduct can be seen from two perspectives – transcendence and facticity, being-for-itself and being-for-others. But it is the exclusive affirmation of one or the others of these (or a motivated and selective oscillation between them) which constitutes bad faith."
> — [Reynolds & Renaudie, "Jean-Paul Sartre", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sartre/)

The entry quotes Sartre's most famous case, the café waiter who plays at being a café waiter and "attempts to institutionalise themselves as this object":

> "There is no doubt I am in a sense a café waiter – otherwise could I not just as well call myself a diplomat or reporter? But if I am one, this cannot be in the mode of being-in-itself. I am a waiter in the mode of being what I am not." (Sartre 1943 [1956: 60])
> — [quoted in SEP, "Jean-Paul Sartre"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sartre/)

The escape does not work, on Sartre's account: the entry explains that his argument with Freud is precisely about how a single consciousness can be both the "liar" and the "lied to" — Sartre rejects any mental device that would split the subject so the deception could happen mechanically. The person in bad faith is not fooled by a hidden censor; the pretense is sustained by the agent, which is what makes it a refusal of freedom rather than a mere error.
