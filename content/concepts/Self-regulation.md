In Carver and Scheier's model, behavioral self-regulation means guiding behavior toward goals using feedback about current progress ([On the Self-Regulation of Behavior, ch. 5](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/on-the-selfregulation-of-behavior/14606DD9FB32DA230927C08B331BEF84/listing)). Their account covers action and emotion, rather than the physiological regulation of processes such as body temperature ([ch. 1](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/on-the-selfregulation-of-behavior/14606DD9FB32DA230927C08B331BEF84/listing)).

### Goals and feedback

> Life, in this framework, is a continual process of establishing goals and adjusting patterns of behavior to match those goals more closely, using informational feedback as a guide.
>
> Charles S. Carver and Michael F. Scheier, On the Self-Regulation of Behavior, [ch. 5, opening summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/on-the-selfregulation-of-behavior/14606DD9FB32DA230927C08B331BEF84/listing).

The model also includes scaling back a goal when progress is poor: trading it for a less demanding one is a form of limited disengagement ([ch. 11](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/on-the-selfregulation-of-behavior/14606DD9FB32DA230927C08B331BEF84/listing)). The authors present the book as a theoretical account in which speculation has a substantial role, including connections between research literatures that they acknowledge are sometimes tenuous ([Preface](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/on-the-selfregulation-of-behavior/14606DD9FB32DA230927C08B331BEF84/listing)).
