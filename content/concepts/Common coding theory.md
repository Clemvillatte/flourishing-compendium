## In plain words

Common coding theory says that seeing and doing share a mental code. Perceiving an event activates the action that goes with it, and performing an action activates the perception that goes with it. The theory, developed by Wolfgang Prinz, grounds imitation in this shared representation.

## Where it comes from

The theory "is a cognitive psychology theory describing how perceptual representations... and motor representations... are linked," claiming "that there is a shared representation (a common code) for both perception and action. More important, seeing an event activates the action associated with that event, and performing an action activates the associated perceptual event" ([Wikipedia, Common coding theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_coding_theory)). Its lineage runs through William James and Roger Sperry, who "argued that the perception-action cycle is the fundamental logic of the nervous system" ([Wikipedia, Common coding theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_coding_theory)).

## How it works

The classical "sandwich" model assumed three stages — perception, cognition, then action — with cognition translating between separate codes. The common coding account instead "claims that perception and action are directly linked by a common computational code" ([Wikipedia, Common coding theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_coding_theory)). Its ideomotor principle, "in line with the ideomotor theory of William James (1890) and Hermann Lotze (1852)... posits that actions are represented in terms of their perceptual consequences. Actions are represented like any other events" ([Wikipedia, Common coding theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_coding_theory)). Because the codes are shared, matching is similarity-based rather than rule-based: "similarity-based matching of commensurate codes requires no such preceding rule acquisition" ([Wikipedia, Common coding theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_coding_theory)).

## What it explains

The theory explains why watching can feel like doing: shared code means observed actions resonate with one's own. It is "closely related to research and theory in two intersecting fields of study: mirror neurons systems and embodied cognition," where "common coding seems to reflect the functional logic of mirror neurons and mechanisms in the brain" ([Wikipedia, Common coding theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_coding_theory)). It grounds imitation, action understanding, and the perception-action habits of skill learning.

## Everyday life

The practical read: action and perception train each other. To learn a movement, watch it done well; to read others' intentions, attend to what doing it yourself feels like. Environments full of demonstrated action are environments full of induced action.
