The power paradox is the psychologist Dacher Keltner's name for a finding from two decades of behavioural research: the qualities that get a person influence, such as empathy and listening, are the ones that tend to fade once they have it.

> "While people usually gain power through traits and actions that advance the interests of others, such as empathy, collaboration, openness, fairness, and sharing; when they start to feel powerful or enjoy a position of privilege, those qualities begin to fade."
> — [Keltner, Harvard Business Review, October 2016](https://hbr.org/2016/10/dont-let-power-corrupt-you)

The paradox is not that power corrupts, which Lord Acton had already said in the nineteenth century, but that the route to power and the effect of holding it run in opposite directions. People are given power by groups who trust them to advance shared interests, and the feeling of having it pulls attention back to one's own goals.

### What the evidence shows

Keltner's research programme, summarised in the 2016 book The Power Paradox: How We Gain and Lose Influence, covers how power changes behaviour. The HBR summary lists what the pattern leads to: more rude, selfish and unethical behaviour, which in turn undermines the reputation and influence the power rested on. His proposed counter is a set of habits rather than a personality change: notice the feelings that come with a rise in status, keep listening, and practise empathy, gratitude and generosity.
