Phenomenological psychology is "the use of the phenomenological method to gain insights regarding topics related to psychology" ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/phen-psy/)). The name joins two linked projects. Husserl built the philosophical side, delivering lectures on phenomenological psychology in the summer semester of 1925 and treating the analysis of consciousness as a descriptive science. The research side arrived later: Amedeo Giorgi and Colaizzi, working from the Duquesne tradition in the 1970s, turned phenomenology's epoché and descriptive discipline into qualitative methods a psychology researcher could actually run, on interviews and written accounts of lived experience.

### What the method does

The engine of the approach is the participant's concrete description. Giorgi's version has the researcher read the whole account, divide it into meaning units, and transform each unit into expressions that state its psychological meaning, ending in a general structure of the experience. The researcher works inside a modified phenomenological reduction, setting aside theories and expectations about the phenomenon. Giorgi defended this psychological adaptation of Husserl as "more appropriate for psychological analyses of human beings since the purpose of psychology as a human science is precisely the clarification of the meanings of phenomena experienced by human persons" (Giorgi, 2009, p. 98, quoted in [IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/phen-psy/)).

Colaizzi's 1978 variant runs the same spirit through seven steps: "Colaizzi's (1978) distinctive seven-step process provides a rigorous analysis, with each step staying close to the data" ([British Psychological Society](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/learning-lifeworld)), from familiarisation with the accounts, through significant statements, formulated meanings and theme clusters, to an exhaustive description condensed into a fundamental structure. Its last step returns that structure to the participants for verification, and it is the contested one. Giorgi objected in 2006 that "the researcher and participant inevitably have different perspectives" ([BPS](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/learning-lifeworld)): the researcher stands in the phenomenological attitude, the participant in the natural attitude.

### Descriptive or interpretive

The tradition's internal dividing line runs between description and interpretation. The descriptive camp, following Husserl and Giorgi, aims at the structure of an experience as it is given; the interpretive camp, drawing on Heidegger and hermeneutics, reads for meaning behind the account. The IEP marks the ambition of the descriptive side with a programmatic sentence: phenomenological psychology, as a science, "concerns itself with what is necessary and universal in human experience" ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/phen-psy/)). Critics from the interpretive side answer that pure description is not available, since the analyst's categories shape every step; the descriptive reply is that bracketing is a discipline, not a claim of innocence.
