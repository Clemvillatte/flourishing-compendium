Horton and Wohl's 1956 essay opens with the observation that the new mass media "give the illusion of face-to-face relationship with the performer", so that "the most remote and illustrious men are met as if they were in the circle of one's peers". They propose "to call this seeming face-to-face relationship between spectator and performer a para-social relationship", and the "simulacrum of conversational give and take" para-social interaction ([Horton and Wohl, Mass Communication and Para-Social Interaction, Psychiatry 19(3), 215-229, 1956, PDF copy](https://ia801503.us.archive.org/4/items/donald-horton-and-richard-wohl-1956/Donald%20Horton%20and%20Richard%20Wohl%201956.pdf)).

### One-sided by design

The authors locate the difference in experience in "the lack of effective reciprocity", which "the audience cannot normally conceal from itself". The interaction "is one-sided, nondialectical, controlled by the performer, and not susceptible of mutual development". They add that the spectator "is free to withdraw at any moment" and that the persona takes "the greatest pains" to create "an illusion of intimacy" (same essay).

### Complement or substitute

Horton and Wohl say that "for the great majority of the audience, the para-social is complementary to normal social life". The relationship becomes a worry only "when the para-social relationship becomes a substitute for autonomous social participation, when it proceeds in absolute defiance of objective reality", at which point it "can be regarded as pathological". They also write that personality programmes favour "compensatory attachments by the socially isolated, the socially inept, the aged and invalid, the timid and rejected". The seed's wording, "enduring bonds ... as if they were acquaintances", is close but omits this distinction, so state it when you use the concept.

### Later history

A Springer chapter notes the concept "initially does not provoke a scientific echo" and begins to establish itself in the 1970s; it is now applied to fictional characters, social networks, avatars and influencers ([Para-Social Interaction Theory chapter, Springer Nature Link](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-658-50422-9_2)). Britannica adds that, in Horton and Wohl's account, audience members must tune in regularly and of their own volition for the relationship to become parasocial ([Britannica, Parasocial interaction](https://www.britannica.com/science/parasocial-interaction)).
