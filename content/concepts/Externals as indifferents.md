Externals as indifferents is Epictetus' sorting of the world into what is yours and what is not. Body, property, reputation and office are external to the will; opinion, impulse, desire and aversion are your own acts. The Enchiridion opens with the division:

> "Of things some are in our power, and others are not. In our power are opinion (*ὑπόληψις*), movement towards a thing (*ὁρμή*), desire, aversion (*ἔκκλισις*, turning from a thing); and in a word, whatever are our own acts: not in our power are the body, property, reputation, offices (magisterial power), and in a word, whatever are not our own acts." ([Epictetus, Enchiridion 1, trans. George Long](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/epictetus/short-works/george-long/text/the-enchiridion))

### Indifferent does not mean worthless

The word "indifferent" (adiaphoron) does not counsel neglect. Epictetus' own gloss keeps both halves at once:

> "The materials of action are indifferent, but the use we make of them is not indifferent" (Discourses 2.5.1); "Externals must be used with care, because their usage is not an indifferent matter, yet at the same time with composure and tranquility, because the material being used is indifferent" (2.5.6). (quoted in [Graver, SEP, Epictetus §4.4](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epictetus/))

Margaret Graver's summary of the position: appearances and comfort, possessions, relationships, the success of projects, power and reputation are all contingent facts, features of experience rather than characteristics of the self, while convictions, attitudes, intentions and actions are truly ours ([Graver, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epictetus/)). Some externals are more natural to us than others, as a foot prefers to be clean, yet the foot, if it had a mind, would welcome mud for the sake of the whole (2.6.11). The practice is care without attachment: full attention to how externals are used, no stake in how they fall.
