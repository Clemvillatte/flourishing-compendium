Hubris and nemesis name a pair the Greeks watched closely: hubris, the overweening pride that puts others down, and nemesis, the reckoning that follows it. Aristotle's definition, in the Rhetoric, is still the sharpest, and he renders hubris as insolence:

> "Insolence is also a form of slighting, since it consists in doing and saying things that cause shame to the victim, not in order that anything may happen to yourself, or because anything has happened to yourself, but simply for the pleasure involved. (Retaliation is not 'insolence,' but vengeance.)" (Aristotle, [Rhetoric 2.2, 1378b](https://kairos.technorhetoric.net/stasis/2017/honeycutt/aristotle/rhet2-2.html), trans. Rhys Roberts)

The pleasure, he continues, comes from the thought of one's own superiority while ill-treating others. That is what makes hubris more than rudeness: it is an enjoyment of another's lowering.

Greek story and history then supply the second half of the pair. Britannica's survey of the concept points to figures like Xerxes punishing the sea for destroying his bridge, and to literary critics reading hubris as the tragic flaw of the Greek heroes ([Britannica, hubris](https://www.britannica.com/topic/hubris)). Wikipedia's account of the pattern gives the mythic case: Arachne, "a talented young weaver, was transformed into a spider when she said that her skills exceeded those of the goddess Athena" ([Wikipedia, Hubris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hubris)). The pride is real, the fall is real, and the two arrive in that order. In modern usage hubris has narrowed to overconfident pride combined with arrogance, the blindness that precedes the fall.

For the compendium the pair is a diagnostic: SE070 applies it to narcissistic patterns, where self-esteem that requires others keeps tripping the same wire.
