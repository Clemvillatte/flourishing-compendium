Compassion is being moved by suffering and motivated to act. Gilbert's concise definition, quoted in the review of compassion-focused therapy, gives the construct its full shape:

> "its essence is a basic kindness, with deep awareness of the suffering of oneself and of other living things, coupled with the wish and effort to relieve it" (Gilbert 2009, p. xiii, quoted in [Gilbert 2010](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/publications/ijct.2010.3.2.pdf)).

Psychology's working definitions split the construct into the feeling and the motivation, with the motivational side doing the conceptual work: pity observes, compassion moves. The Buddhist *karuna* is the same definition older: the trembling of the heart at suffering, paired with the wish and effort to relieve it.

### The caregiving ground

The mechanism is care-giving. Gilbert's account grounds compassion in the evolved caregiving mentality:

> "Gilbert (2007) describes compassion as a 'multifaceted process' that has evolved from 'the caregiver mentality' found in human parental care and childrearing." ([Gilbert 2010](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/publications/ijct.2010.3.2.pdf))

On the compendium's reading, this is why compassion training works through tone and imagery rather than argument. The compendium's forms are the relational practices: motivational displacement, the Confucian heart of humanity, and the psychological distinction that compassion includes self-compassion rather than ending at the self's edge.

### What it is not

The distinction to hold: compassion is not pity (which looks down), not sympathy (which feels *for* without acting), and not the exhaustion of empathy. Compassion fatigue is a failure of the *conditions* of practice, not of the disposition itself. The middle path applies: warm hands, dry feet, neither numbness nor fusion.

### In everyday life

The two-step on any witnessed suffering: feel it fully for one breath, then one small act, the name spoken, the load carried, the water brought. (Illustrative scenario, not a reported participant or study result.)

Its Buddhist original is [[Karuna]], its Confucian cousin [[Ren]], and its psychological near-neighbour [[Empathy]].
