Compassion is the feeling of being moved by suffering and motivated to act — at its heart, sensitivity to the suffering of others *together with* a commitment to do something about it ([Cambridge, "What Is Compassion?"](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/handbook-of-compassion-in-healthcare/what-is-compassion/272BA40B5682CA3B5B247114EF16B49B)). Psychology's working definition splits the construct into exactly those two components — the feeling and the motivation — with the motivational side doing the conceptual work: pity observes, compassion moves ([Gilbert's account](https://self-compassion.org/wp-content/uploads/publications/ijct.2010.3.2.pdf)). The Buddhist *karuna* is the same definition older: the trembling of the heart at suffering, paired with the wish and effort to relieve it.

The mechanism is care-giving: Paul Gilbert's account grounds compassion in the evolved caregiving mentality — warmth, soothing, approach — which is why compassion training works through tone and imagery, not argument. Its compendium forms are the relational practices: motivational displacement ([[R068 — Practise motivational displacement]]), the Confucian heart of humanity ([[Ren]]), and the psychological distinction that compassion includes self-compassion rather than ending at the self's edge ([[PP046 — Cultivate self-compassion]]; [[Self-compassion]]).

The distinction to hold: compassion is not pity (which looks down), not sympathy (which feels *for* without acting), and not the exhaustion of empathy — compassion fatigue is a failure of the *conditions* of practice, not of the disposition itself. The middle path applies: warm hands, dry feet — neither numbness nor fusion ([[B052 — Practice compassion (karuna)]]).

An example at its smallest: the two-step on any witnessed suffering — feel it fully for one breath, then one small act: the name spoken, the load carried, the water brought.

Its Buddhist original is [[Karuna]], its Confucian cousin [[Ren]], and its psychological near-neighbour [[Empathy]].
