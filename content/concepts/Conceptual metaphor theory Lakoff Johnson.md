**Conceptual metaphor theory Lakoff Johnson** — abstract thought is structured by embodied metaphors (time as space, importance as weight).

Conceptual metaphor theory is George Lakoff and Mark Johnson's proposal, set out in Metaphors We Live By (1980), that metaphor is not a decoration of language but the skeleton of thought. On this view, when people speak of love in terms of travel, that is not a stray figure; it reflects a standing disposition to think in those terms. The Stanford Encyclopedia states the thesis:

> "Lakoff and his collaborators maintain that metaphor is at bottom a conceptual matter, a matter of thinking of one thing as and in terms of another."

> "What Lakoff calls a conceptual metaphor or cross-domain map (e.g. love is a journey) is a standing pervasive culture-wide disposition to conceive one fixed sort of thing (e.g. love affairs), as and in terms of another fixed sort of thing (e.g. journeys)." ([Hills, "Metaphor", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, section 5.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/metaphor/))

The work gets done by correspondences between the two domains, and they are systematic rather than poetic:

> "The lovers correspond to travelers. The love relationship corresponds to the vehicle. The lovers' common goals correspond to their common destinations on the journey. Difficulties in the relationship correspond to impediments to travel." (Lakoff 1993, quoted in the same entry)

Once the map exists, ordinary speech runs on it without anyone noticing a metaphor at all:

> "Our relationship has hit a dead-end street... Look how far we've come. We can't turn back now. It's been a long, bumpy road. We're at a crossroads. We may have to go our separate ways. The relationship isn't going anywhere. We're spinning our wheels. Our relationship is off the track. This marriage is on the rocks." (Lakoff 1993, quoted in the same entry)

The reason the mappings are learnable and stable, on Lakoff's account, is that the source domain comes from the body. Journeys, containment, paths, up and down are things we know from moving around, and the theory's term for the relevant mental structures is sensorimotor image schemas: "simple diagrammatic mental images, images the unconscious employment of which routinely mediates the planning and execution of our voluntary bodily movements" (same entry, section 5.1). Abstract domains then borrow the structure of these bodily ones. The compendium's principle [[CO005 — Understand that thinking is embodied — the body shapes the mind]] rests partly on this theory, and its practical interest is the lever it offers: to change how someone thinks about something, one can attend to which metaphor they think with.
