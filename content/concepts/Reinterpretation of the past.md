“Reinterpretation of the past” — Nietzsche, Genealogy of Morals.

In the Second Essay of the Genealogy, Nietzsche argues that nothing keeps the meaning it was born with. Every practice gets commandeered by whatever force is currently superior to it.

> everything, anything, which exists and which prevails anywhere, will always be put to new purposes by a force superior to itself, will be commandeered afresh, will be turned and transformed to new uses; all "happening" in the organic world consists of overpowering and dominating, and again all overpowering and domination is a new interpretation and adjustment, which must necessarily obscure or absolutely extinguish the subsisting "meaning" and "end." (Nietzsche, Genealogy of Morals II.12, [Horace B. Samuel translation, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/52319/52319-h/52319-h.htm))

His case study is punishment: the act stayed recognizably the same across centuries while its meaning was rewritten again and again.

### The punishment case study

The evidence Nietzsche offers is the history of punishment itself, which he says manifests a whole synthesis of meanings:

> "With regard to the other element in punishment, its fluid element, its meaning, the idea of punishment in a very late stage of civilisation (for instance, contemporary Europe) is not content with manifesting merely one meaning, but manifests a whole synthesis 'of meanings.'" (Nietzsche, Genealogy of Morals II.13, [Horace B. Samuel translation, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/52319/52319-h/52319-h.htm))

He then lists the uses punishment has served, a long list that the page samples rather than quotes in full. The first four give its range, from neutralizing a threat, to compensating the injured party, to containing the disturbance, to inspiring fear of the punishers:

> "Punishment, as rendering the criminal harmless and incapable of further injury.—Punishment, as compensation for the injury sustained by the injured party, in any form whatsoever (including the form of sentimental compensation).—Punishment, as an isolation of that which disturbs the equilibrium, so as to prevent the further spreading of the disturbance.—Punishment as a means of inspiring fear of those who determine and execute the punishment." (Nietzsche, Genealogy of Morals II.13, [Horace B. Samuel translation, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/52319/52319-h/52319-h.htm))

The outcome of such a history is a concept too dense to open:

> "The past general history of punishment, the history of its employment for the most diverse ends, crystallises eventually into a kind of unity, which is difficult to analyse into its parts, and which, it is necessary to emphasise, absolutely defies definition." (Nietzsche, Genealogy of Morals II.13, [Horace B. Samuel translation, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/52319/52319-h/52319-h.htm))

The interest for a reader is double. Nietzsche's claim is about power: whoever is currently stronger rewrites what things mean. The compendium places this historical argument alongside its memory-updating principle, CO060, without equating them: the juxtaposition of a historical rewriting and a laboratory finding about reactivated memories is the compendium's own reading, offered as an essay angle rather than a claim that Nietzsche and the memory researchers describe the same mechanism. The two meet in the practice of narrative: the events of a life stay recognizably the same while what they mean is rewritten by whoever holds the pen.
