The introspection illusion is Emily Pronin's term for an asymmetric weighting of evidence. When people assess their own bias, they consider introspective information more than behavioral information; when they assess others, they do not. The 2004 review with Thomas Gilovich and Lee Ross places the asymmetry inside a larger pattern: people see others as more susceptible to cognitive and motivational biases than themselves, a blind spot the authors connect to the stance of naive realism.

> It is exacerbated, furthermore, by people's tendency to attach greater credence to their own introspections about potential influences on judgment and behavior than they attach to similar introspections by others. (Pronin, Gilovich & Ross 2004, [abstract, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15250784/))

Pronin and Matthew Kugler's 2007 experiments pinned the mechanism down. The bias blind spot persisted when observers had access to the judged person's introspections, so the difference was not access. Participants also said that they, but not their peers, should rely on introspections for self-assessments of bias. Only after being educated about the importance of nonconscious processes in guiding judgment and action, and the fallibility of introspection, did participants stop denying their relative susceptibility to bias.
