The uncanny is a specific kind of fright, set apart by its direction of travel: it does not come from the alien but from the familiar. Freud opens his 1919 study with the etymology. In German, *heimlich* means the homely, the familiar and congenial, but also "that which is concealed and kept out of sight," and so, he concludes, "heimlich is a word the meaning of which develops towards an ambivalence, until it finally coincides with its opposite, unheimlich" (Freud, "The 'Uncanny'," 1919, trans. Strachey; [Internet Archive full text](https://archive.org/download/pdfy-GZt1cfixjEeheTcZ/The%20Uncanny_djvu.txt)). The uncanny effect arises when something familiar has been estranged by repression and returns: "for this uncanny is in reality nothing new or foreign, but something familiar and old-established in the mind that has been estranged only by the process of repression." Freud takes his central example from E. T. A. Hoffmann's story "The Sand-Man," and from Ernst Jentsch he takes the cue that the strongest uncanny effect attaches to "doubts whether an apparently animate being is really alive; or conversely, whether a lifeless object might not be in fact animate" — waxwork figures, dolls, automatons. Heidegger gives the same word a second life in the analysis of anxiety. "In anxiety one feels 'uncanny.' ... But here 'uncanniness' also means 'not-being-at-home' [das Nichtzuhause-sein]" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, trans. Macquarrie and Robinson, p. 233; [beyng.com](https://www.beyng.com/pages/en/BeingandTimeMR/BeingandTimeMR.233.html)). As Dasein falls into the everyday, "anxiety brings it back from its absorption in the 'world.' Everyday familiarity collapses ... Being-in enters into the existential 'mode' of the 'not-at-home.'" The two accounts differ in register, one clinical and one existential, but they share the core: the uncanny is the familiar world with its cover removed, and the fright it produces is not a warning about the world but a disclosure about the one who is frightened. For practice, this means an uncanny experience, a place, a face, a returning memory that feels wrong, is best read as information about estrangement rather than about danger.
