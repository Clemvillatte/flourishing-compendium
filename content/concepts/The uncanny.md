The uncanny is a specific kind of fright, set apart by its direction of travel: it does not come from the alien but from the familiar. Freud opens his 1919 study with the etymology. In German, *heimlich* means the homely, the familiar and congenial, but also "that which is concealed and kept out of sight," and so, he concludes, "heimlich is a word the meaning of which develops towards an ambivalence, until it finally coincides with its opposite, unheimlich" (Freud, "The 'Uncanny,'" 1919, trans. Strachey; [Internet Archive full text](https://archive.org/download/pdfy-GZt1cfixjEeheTcZ/The%20Uncanny_djvu.txt)). The uncanny effect arises when something familiar has been estranged by repression and returns: "for this uncanny is in reality nothing new or foreign, but something familiar and old-established in the mind that has been estranged only by the process of repression." Freud takes his central example from E. T. A. Hoffmann's story "The Sand-Man," and from Ernst Jentsch he takes the cue that the uncanny attaches to "doubts whether an apparently animate being is really alive; or conversely, whether a lifeless object might not be in fact animate" — waxwork figures, dolls, automatons. Freud does not accept this as the full account: "I cannot think—and I hope that most readers of the story will agree with me—that the theme of the doll, Olympia, who is to all appearances a living being, is by any means the only element to be held responsible for the quite unparalleled atmosphere of uncanniness which the story evokes; or, indeed, that it is the most important among them." His own conclusion runs in two channels, and he is careful to keep them apart: "an uncanny experience occurs either when repressed infantile complexes have been revived by some impression, or when the primitive beliefs we have surmounted seem once more to be confirmed." The second channel explains the eeriness of places and coincidences: "we have surmounted such ways of thought; but we do not feel quite sure of our new set of beliefs, and the old ones still exist within us ready to seize upon any confirmation." Heidegger gives the same word a different use in the analysis of anxiety: "In anxiety one feels 'uncanny.' ... But here 'uncanniness' also means 'not-being-at-home' [das Nichtzuhause-sein]" (Heidegger, *Being and Time*, trans. Macquarrie and Robinson, p. 233; [beyng.com](https://www.beyng.com/pages/en/BeingandTimeMR/BeingandTimeMR.233.html)). Anxiety brings Dasein back from its absorption in the world: "Everyday familiarity collapses ... Being-in enters into the existential 'mode' of the 'not-at-home.'" The two accounts share the direction of travel, from the familiar to the estranged, while differing in what they find there.
