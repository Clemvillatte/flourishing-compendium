Biocentric egalitarianism is Paul Taylor's thesis that every living thing has a good of its own, and therefore equal inherent worth. The premise comes first:

> "Every organism, species population, and community of life has a good of its own which moral agents can intentionally further or damage by their actions. To say that an entity has a good of its own is simply to say that, without reference to any other entity, it can be benefited or harmed." (Taylor, "The Ethics of Respect for Nature", in [Respect for Nature](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil308/Taylor.pdf))

### What the equality carries

The good of an organism is defined from its own standpoint, not ours: "We can think of the good of an individual nonhuman organism as consisting in the full development of its biological powers. Its good is realized to the extent that it is strong and healthy." Since the good is determined from no privileged standpoint, no organism's good counts for more, and the human claim to superiority is, on Taylor's argument, a groundless prejudice analogous to aristocrats valuing their birth. The attitude that follows is respect: "Once we reject the claim that humans are superior either in merit or in worth to other living things, we are ready to adopt the attitude of respect." My principle R117 carries the kinship: the land ethic extends moral consideration to soils, waters, plants and animals; Taylor extends it to every living individual. A compendium exercise drawn from the equality is to ask of one convenience what living being's good it set back, and whether that good was counted at all. What the equality does not give: a rule that all conflicts resolve evenly; Taylor himself allows basic human interests to outweigh the non-basic, so the theory is a discipline of counting, not a demand of martyrdom.
