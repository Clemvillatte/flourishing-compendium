Mentalizing is, in Peter Fonagy's chapter, "the ability to understand actions by both other people and oneself in terms of thoughts, feelings, wishes and desires; it is a very human capability that underpins everyday interactions". A related chapter puts it in "non-technical language" as "attentiveness to thinking and feeling in oneself and others" ([Fonagy, What is mentalizing?, UCL repository](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1473464/1/Fonagy_Ch%2001%20What%20is%20mentalizing%20FINAL.pdf); [MBT chapter, UCL repository](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1489633/3/Fonagy_MBT%20chapter%20for%20Kaplan%20and%20Sadock%20complete%20071015.pdf)).

### Origins in attachment

The first chapter traces the model to a study in which infant attachment security was predicted by the parents' capacity to understand their own childhood relationships "in terms of states of mind" (Fonagy, Steele, Steele, Moran and Higgitt, 1991). It concludes that mentalizing, which "emerges in the context of early attachment relationships, may be a key determinant of self-organization and affect regulation". The central idea is that "one's understanding of others depends on whether one's own mental states were adequately understood by caring, attentive, non-threatening adults". The treatment chapter calls it "a central tenet" that "a sense of self and the capacity to mentalize both develop in the context of attachment relationships".

A recent clinical article adds that parents who mentalize "perceive their child as a psychological agent and respond sensitively to their mental states", and that repeated experiences of being understood build "epistemic trust" ([Making visible the clinical practice of mentalization-based therapy, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44192-026-00393-9)).

### More than empathy

Fonagy and Luyten caution that mentalizing "is often simplistically understood as synonymous with the capacity of empathy". It is "a spectrum of capacities" that "critically involve the ability to see one's own behavior as coherently organized by mental states" ([Attachment, Mentalization, and the Self, UCL repository](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1535395/1/Fonagy%20Luyten_with%20figures.pdf)). A clinical paper lists four axes with opposing poles: "implicit versus explicit, cognitive versus affective, internal versus external, and self versus others" ([Springer, BMC Psychiatry paper on MBT](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1186/s12888-024-05865-2.pdf?error=cookies_not_supported&code=d325282b-0b04-46a6-990c-f1907f231492)). A 2021 Frontiers study found deeper mentalizing associated with stronger functioning and well-being, and that "promoting mentalizing across development may improve mental health, even in non-clinical population" ([Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.566254/full)).

### Limits on the evidence

The theory was "originally formulated in the context of treating patients with borderline personality disorder". Fonagy and Luyten state that the treatment model "makes the strong and so far almost unwarranted assumption that the increasing capacity to mentalize drives an improvement in BPD symptoms". The MBT chapter agrees: "the evidence for mentalizing mediating therapeutic change in MBT is currently limited". The Springer study of mixed personality disorders cannot "make causal claims" because it is not experimental.
