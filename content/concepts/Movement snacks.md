Jenkins and colleagues tested brief stair-climbing "exercise snacks" in sedentary young adults ([Appl Physiol Nutr Metab 44(6), 2019, PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30649897/)):

> "We investigated the effect of stair climbing exercise "snacks" on peak oxygen uptake. Sedentary young adults were randomly assigned to perform 3 bouts/day of vigorously ascending a 3-flight stairwell (60 steps), separated by 1-4 h of recovery, 3 days/week for 6 weeks, or a nontraining control group (n = 12 each). Peak oxygen uptake was higher in the climbers after the intervention (P = 0.003), suggesting that stair climbing "snacks" are effective in improving cardiorespiratory fitness, although the absolute increase was modest." ([Jenkins et al., Appl Physiol Nutr Metab 44(6), 2019, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30649897/))

The arithmetic of the design is the point of the name: three bouts of sixty steps, separated by one to four hours of recovery, three days a week. Nothing in it requires a gym, a scheduled session, or a change of clothes; the snack is a single vigorous climb. The name is doing honest work, too: a snack is not a meal, and the study never claims more than a modest gain from a small dose. The trial was small, twelve climbers against twelve controls, so the concept earns its place as a low-barrier habit with measured benefits, not as a fitness program. For a reader deciding whether to take the stairs, that is the relevant scale of the question: not whether exercise works, but whether the smallest possible dose does. In this library it sits beside the micro-break and habit-formation pages: the smallest unit that still counts.
