**Meditation neurobiology theory Lazar** — sustained meditation practice measurably alters cortical thickness, network connectivity and stress physiology.

This page covers the neuroimaging research program Sarah Lazar leads at Massachusetts General Hospital and Harvard Medical School, which uses MRI to test whether meditation changes the brain. Two findings anchor it, both from Neuroreport.

### Structure: cortical thickness

The 2005 study scanned 20 participants with extensive Insight meditation experience and matched controls ([Lazar et al. 2005, PubMed PMID 16272874](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16272874/)):

> "Brain regions associated with attention, interoception and sensory processing were thicker in meditation participants than matched controls, including the prefrontal cortex and right anterior insula. Between-group differences in prefrontal cortical thickness were most pronounced in older participants, suggesting that meditation might offset age-related cortical thinning. Finally, the thickness of two regions correlated with meditation experience. These data provide the first structural evidence for experience-dependent cortical plasticity associated with meditation practice." ([abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16272874/))

The design compares practitioners with controls rather than following trainees over time, so the thickness differences are associations the authors read as consistent with practice-dependent plasticity.

### Function: activation during meditation

The earlier 2000 study used functional MRI to map what happens in the brain during a simple form of meditation ([Lazar et al. 2000, PubMed PMID 10841380](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10841380/)). Signal increases appeared in the dorsolateral prefrontal and parietal cortices, the hippocampus, the pregenual anterior cingulate cortex and the striatum, among other regions. The abstract's conclusion:

> "The results indicate that the practice of meditation activates neural structures involved in attention and control of the autonomic nervous system." ([abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10841380/))

That autonomic-system finding is the physiological half of the page's seed claim: meditation shows up both in brain activation and in the systems that regulate bodily arousal.
