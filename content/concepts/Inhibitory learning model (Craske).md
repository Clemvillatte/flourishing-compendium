The inhibitory learning model holds that exposure therapy works by building a new safety memory that competes with the old fear memory. It does not rely on habituation, the idea that fear simply wears down. The Jacoby and Abramowitz review describes the core: exposure creates "new non-threat (i.e., inhibitory) associations that compete with (rather than 'break') older threat associations". The aim of therapy is therefore "to promote the encoding and long-term recall of the newly learned non-threat connections so that they will inhibit fear-based learning" ([Jacoby and Abramowitz, 2016, Clinical Psychology Review, ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0272735816300599)).

### The mechanism

The authors of the Journal of Experimental Psychopathology review explain that "the original feared association (CS-US) remains but is inhibited by a newly formed association (CS-noUS) representing safety" ([Role of Inhibition in Exposure Therapy, Journal of Experimental Psychopathology](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.5127/jep.026511)). The evidence cited for this is "the retention of original excitatory associations (i.e., spontaneous recovery, context renewal, reinstatement and rapid reacquisition)" (same paper). The central move is "a paradigmatic shift from fear reduction throughout exposure as the primary index of successful outcomes to enhancement of inhibitory learning" (same paper). Exposure tasks "are not guided by premises of fear reduction, nor to 'stay in the situation until fear declines'".

A review of variability in exposure puts the lesson plainly: "fear is not necessarily reduced in exposure; rather, an increased tolerance of fear is learned" ([Enhancing Inhibitory Learning: The Utility of Variability in Exposure, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6884337/)).

### Practical strategies

The 2022 update by Craske and colleagues groups strategies into fundamental ones (expectancy violation, attention to the feared stimulus, removal of safety signals, mental rehearsal after exposure), advanced ones (deepened extinction, occasional reinforced extinction), and those that promote generalisation (retrieval cues, multiple contexts, stimulus variability, positive affect) ([Optimizing exposure therapy with an inhibitory retrieval approach and the OptEx Nexus, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35325683/)). Expectancy violation is "the discrepancy between a patient's anticipated consequence of an exposure task (e.g., being laughed at for mispronouncing a word) and the actual consequence (e.g., people don't seem to care)" ([Blakey and Abramowitz, 2019, PDF](https://jonabram.web.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/2968/2019/02/Blakey-and-Abramowitz-2019-safety-aids-and-retrieval-cues.pdf)). Safety aids can interfere, because they may prevent violation of negative expectancies and generalisation of the new learning.

### What remains unsettled

The evidence base is described as emerging. Jacoby and Abramowitz note that the theory is "consistent with empirical evidence that raises questions about the more traditional (i.e., habituation) explanation", while discussing "the limitations of existing studies". One review concluded that "although these findings need to be replicated in clinical populations, maximizing expectancy violation is among the most promising strategies to increase the efficacy of exposure" ([Exposure therapy augmentation review, PDF](https://www.actbeyondocd.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/1-s2.0-S0272735817301885-main.pdf)). For PTSD, a PMC paper states that "more empirical work is necessary" to assess the approach in routine care ([Maximizing expectancy violation and exposure outcomes, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11721951/)). A claustrophobia study found that cognitive interventions before exposure did not reduce gains, against the original caution ([Cognitive restructuring before versus after exposure, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9523819/)).

This page describes a research model for clinical treatment. It is not guidance for treating oneself.
