"Experimental reform" — Addams, Twenty Years at Hull-House.

Experimental reform is Jane Addams' practice, in Twenty Years at Hull-House (1910), of treating social reform as something tried rather than announced. The settlement's founding self-description puts the word at the center of its method: "an experimental effort to aid in the solution of the social and industrial problems which are engendered by the modern conditions of life in a great city" (Addams, [Twenty Years at Hull-House](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1325)).

The book reports the failures as plainly as the intentions:

> "There was room for discouragement in the many unsuccessful experiments in cooperation which were carried on in Chicago during the early nineties; a carpenter shop on Van Buren Street near Halsted, a labor exchange started by the unemployed, not so paradoxical an arrangement as it seems, and a very ambitious plan for a country colony which was finally carried out at Ruskin, Tennessee." (Addams, [Twenty Years at Hull-House](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1325))

That sentence is the method in one view: reform attempts named, dated and counted as experiments, their failure recorded rather than buried. What survives the failures survives on evidence.

In the compendium the idea supports [[P132 — Make reform experimental]] and [[SO040 — Treat institutions as knowledge infrastructures]]: an institution that runs trials and keeps their records is doing research, not only doing good.
