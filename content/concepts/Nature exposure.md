Nature exposure is contact with natural settings as a practice with measurable psychological effects. The concept's anchor study is Bratman and colleagues' 2015 experiment: after a single 90-minute walk through grassland with scattered oaks, participants reported less rumination, and arterial-spin-labeling-measured regional cerebral blood flow dropped in the subgenual prefrontal cortex, whereas a matched walk along a busy urban street showed no significant change on either measure ([Bratman et al. 2015, PNAS 112(28):8567-8572](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4507237/), Results). Rumination here is an outcome the study measured; the experiment did not establish it as a mediator of improved mental health. The attention-restoration theory the study's discussion draws on is neighboring theoretical context rather than a mechanism this experiment tested. The kernel's own schedule — green space, water, sky, weather on the skin, at least thirty minutes several times a week — is the compendium's prescription; the single-walk experiment says nothing about the duration or frequency of routine exposure.

### The study (Bratman et al. 2015)

The result, in the paper's own words:

> “We show in healthy participants that a brief nature experience, a 90-min walk in a natural setting, decreases both self-reported rumination and neural activity in the subgenual prefrontal cortex (sgPFC), whereas a 90-min walk in an urban setting has no such effects on self-reported rumination or neural activity.” (Bratman et al. 2015, [PNAS 112(28):8567-8572](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4507237/), abstract)

The paper's definition of rumination, in its own terms:

> “Rumination is a prolonged and often maladaptive attentional focus on the causes and consequences of emotions—most often, negative, self-relational emotions.” (Bratman et al. 2015, [PNAS 112(28):8567-8572](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4507237/), introduction)

The study is honest about its scope: thirty-eight healthy urban-dwelling participants, one walk, and the subgenual prefrontal cortex as the prespecified region of interest, with whole-brain analyses also reported. One qualification the Results section makes explicit: at the conventional .05 threshold the self-report data did not establish a difference in rumination change between the groups (the time-by-environment interaction term reached P=.07; the nature group decreased significantly within itself, the urban group showed no significant change), whereas the subgenual-prefrontal-cortex interaction was significant ([Bratman et al. 2015, PNAS 112(28):8567-8572](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4507237/), Results). The discussion connects the finding to the restorative-environments literature, whose measures include “somewhat effortless, ‘soft fascination’; the ‘sense of belonging’; and the ‘sense of being away’” ([same paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4507237/), discussion) — the Kaplan framework the compendium's attention-restoration material carries in full. The authors offer the rumination reduction as one possible mechanism by which urbanization, which reduces opportunities for nature experience, may be linked to mental illness ([same paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4507237/), discussion).
