Clarity of mind is this compendium's label for the unclouded mental state the contemplative traditions describe from three directions: the Stoic sobriety of assent — holding an impression and examining it before granting it, Epictetus's "Impression, wait for me a little... you are an impression" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Epictetus"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epictetus/)); the Daoist purity-and-stillness (qingjing) of the Daodejing, whose forty-fifth chapter makes stillness the world's standard — "purity and stillness give the correct law to all under heaven" ([Daodejing 45, Legge translation](https://www.yellowbridge.com/onlinelit/daodejing45.php)); and the Buddhist account of the hindrances as the five clouds that obscure an otherwise luminous mind ([[B034 — Know the five hindrances]]).

The mechanism: clarity is degraded by specific, nameable processes — intoxicants ([[B151 — Keep the mind clear — sobriety]]), the hindrances, and hasty assent — and restored by their specific counters: guarding the sense doors, examining impressions, sobriety. The Dhammapada's formulation is that the mind precedes the world: "a clear mind meets what is" — the lens you bring is the first fact about what you find ([[B136 — Mind constructs the world you meet]]).

The distinction to hold: clarity is not emptiness or the absence of affect. The clear mind is response-rich but unclouded — weather passes through without becoming the sky. Suppression produces a quiet mind, not a clear one.

An example at its smallest: the fifth precept as guard — the instrument not clouded before the day's work ([[B151 — Keep the mind clear — sobriety]]).

Its contemplative cognates are [[Sati]] (the noticing that clarity requires) and the epoché interval ([[Epoché]]) — the Pyrrhonian and Stoic suspensions are clarity's method, not its rival.
