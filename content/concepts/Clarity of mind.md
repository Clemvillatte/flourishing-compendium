Clarity of mind is this compendium's label for the unclouded mental state the contemplative traditions describe from three directions: the Stoic sobriety of assent (holding an impression and examining it before granting it), the Daoist purity-and-stillness (qingjing) of the Daodejing, and the Buddhist account of the hindrances as the five clouds that obscure an otherwise luminous mind.

The Stoic direction is Epictetus's pause before the impression:

> "Impression, wait for me a little. Let me see what you are, and what you represent" (Epictetus, *Discourses* 2.18.24, quoted in the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epictetus/)).

The Daoist direction is the Daodejing's forty-fifth chapter, which makes stillness the world's standard:

> "Purity and stillness give the correct law to all under heaven." (Daodejing 45, [trans. James Legge](https://www.yellowbridge.com/onlinelit/daodejing45.php))

### How clarity degrades and restores

On my reading, clarity is degraded by specific, nameable processes, intoxicants, the five hindrances, and hasty assent, and restored by their specific counters: guarding the sense doors, examining impressions, sobriety. The Dhammapada's opening formulation is that the mind precedes the world: the lens you bring is the first fact about what you find.

### The distinction to hold

Clarity is not emptiness or the absence of affect. The clear mind is response-rich but unclouded: weather passes through without becoming the sky. Suppression produces a quiet mind, not a clear one.

### In everyday life

The fifth precept as guard: the instrument not clouded before the day's work. (Illustrative scenario, not a reported participant or study result.)

Its contemplative cognates are [[Sati]] (the noticing that clarity requires) and the epoché interval ([[Epoché]]): the Pyrrhonian and Stoic suspensions are clarity's method, not its rival.
