"The undiminishing past" is the companion's heading for an argument Boethius stages in Book II of *The Consolation of Philosophy*, in Fortune's reply to a man who has been stripped of the highest offices and goods. Boethius protests that Fortune, having raised him, has cast him down; Fortune answers that her gifts were hers to give and hers to take, and then turns to what cannot be taken. "Hast thou forgotten," she asks, "the extent and bounds of thy felicity?" — and she recounts the honors, the alliance with the highest in the state, the virtues of his wife, the blessing of male issue, before arguing the point directly: "If the fruition of any earthly success has weight in the scale of happiness, can the memory of that splendour be swept away by any rising flood of troubles?" (Boethius, *The Consolation of Philosophy*, trans. H. R. James, Book II, prose 3; [Project Gutenberg, eBook 14328](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/14328)). The argument is arithmetic: what has been enjoyed was enjoyed, and no later loss reaches back to cancel it; the past good remains possessed in memory. Boethius gives the rejoinder that has made the book famous, and it is the honest objection to any easy gratitude: "For truly in adverse fortune the worst sting of misery is to have been happy" (Book II, prose 4). Fortune's reply reopens the account rather than denying the sting: "come reckon up with me how rich thou art in the number and weightiness of thy blessings," and she counts what remains, his father-in-law safe, his wife living, his sons in health and honour, before the argument moves to the deeper question of where happiness properly lies. The teaching is double: the same undiminishing past can be drawn on or suffered. Held as a store of finished goods it funds gratitude in need; held as a standard against the present it becomes, in Boethius's own words, the worst sting of misery. The memory is one thing; the use made of it is another.
