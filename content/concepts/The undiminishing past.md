"The undiminishing past" is the companion's heading for an argument Boethius stages in Book II of *The Consolation of Philosophy*, in Fortune's answer to a man who has lost everything. Boethius protests that Fortune, having raised him, has cast him down; Fortune replies that her gifts were hers to give and hers to take, and then turns to what cannot be taken. "If the fruition of any earthly success has weight in the scale of happiness, can the memory of that splendour be swept away by any rising flood of troubles? That day when thou didst see thy two sons ride forth from home joint consuls, followed by a train of senators, and welcomed by the good-will of the people" (Boethius, *The Consolation of Philosophy*, trans. H. R. James, Book II, prose 4; [Project Gutenberg, eBook 14328](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/14328)). The point is arithmetic: what has been enjoyed was enjoyed, and no later loss reaches back to cancel it. The past good is banked in memory, and the person who has it is, in that respect, still rich. Boethius gives the rejoinder that has made the book famous, and it is the honest objection to any easy gratitude: "in adverse fortune the worst sting of misery is to have been happy." Philosophy's answer is not to deny the sting but to reopen the account: "come reckon up with me how rich thou art in the number and weightiness of thy blessings," and she counts what remains, his father-in-law safe, his wife living, his sons in health and honour, before turning to the deeper argument that happiness depends on nothing Fortune can give. The teaching for practice is double. The same undiminishing past can be drawn on or suffered: held as a store of finished goods it funds gratitude in need, and held as a standard against the present it becomes the bitterest portion of the unhappy. The memory is one thing; the use made of it is another.
