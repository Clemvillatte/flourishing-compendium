Grounding techniques are brief exercises that shift attention to present sensory experience, such as naming things you see, hear and touch. They are widely used for dissociation, panic and flashbacks. Linehan's 1993 skills manual, the seed's source, was not opened for this page, so its specific wording is not quoted. The sources below concern the techniques in general, and the main point from them is that the evidence base is thin.

### What the techniques look like

The US National Center for PTSD describes a grounding exercise in which a person is asked to name something they can see, hear and touch right now, then two new things for each sense, and notes that the questions should cover several sensory modalities. It adds that "if your attempt at this technique yields no improvement or a worsening of symptoms", the technique can be dropped or tried again later. ([National Center for PTSD, Strategies: PTSD in Others](https://www.ptsd.va.gov/professional/treat/care/toolkits/police/managingStrategies.asp))

### What the evidence shows

A 2026 critical review in Trauma, Violence & Abuse (Hammond and Brown) reports that "grounding techniques have not been subjected to efficacy studies due to a lack of consensus regarding a measurable operational definition". It found 1,894 results and 19 sources suitable for review, of which only 4 defined grounding, 12 mentioned it and 3 described it. The authors propose an operational definition emphasising physiological equilibrium and tentative guidelines for use in treatment. ([Building an Operational Definition of Grounding, PubMed 40575850](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40575850/))

A trial-development paper on exposure for PTSD states that "studies that empirically test the efficacy of distraction skills or grounding techniques to block dissociation during trauma exposure do not exist". In its own small sample, antidissociative skills were followed by a decrease in dissociation in 82% (32 of 39) of cases. These data come from a development study without a control condition. ([PMC 4936796](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4936796/))

A review of psychological interventions for dissociative disorders describes grounding as helping a person detach from emotional pain and anchor in the present, and says that for therapies in this area "empirical evidence is lacking". ([PMC 7001344](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7001344/))

### What this page does not claim

The sources do not show that grounding reduces PTSD, anxiety or dissociation in controlled trials, and they do not test Linehan's version specifically. The compendium's grounding principles are best read as common clinical practice with a plausible mechanism and limited trial evidence. People with trauma should use these techniques with the guidance of a clinician when symptoms are severe.
