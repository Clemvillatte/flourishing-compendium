Social identity theory, formulated by Henri Tajfel and John Turner, explains intergroup behavior through the part of a person's self-image that comes from the groups they belong to. They define social identity as "those aspects of an individual's self-image that derive from the social categories to which he perceives himself as belonging" ([Tajfel & Turner, "The Social Identity Theory of Intergroup Behavior," p. 283, third-party hosted copy](https://christosaioannou.com/Tajfel%20and%20Turner%201986.pdf)).

> "1. Individuals strive to achieve or to maintain positive social identity. 2. Positive social identity is based to a large extent on favorable comparisons that can be made between the in-group and some relevant out-groups: the in-group must be perceived as positively differentiated or distinct from the relevant out-groups." (Tajfel & Turner, p. 284, [copy](https://christosaioannou.com/Tajfel%20and%20Turner%201986.pdf))

### The core claims

The authors add a third principle: "When social identity is unsatisfactory, individuals will strive either to leave their existing group and join some more positively distinct group and/or to make their existing group more positively distinct" (p. 284). They also place behavior on a continuum between two poles, interpersonal behavior fully determined by individual relationships and intergroup behavior "fully determined by their respective memberships in various social groups or categories" (p. 277), and say the pure forms are rare in real life.

### What the theory does not say

It is easy to read the theory as saying that group identity breeds hostility. Harwood's encyclopedia entry notes that "ingroup favoritism is not isomorphic with outgroup derogation" ([Harwood, Social Identity Theory, International Encyclopedia of Media Psychology, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdfdirect/10.1002/9781119011071.iemp0153?download=true)), so favoring one's own group and disliking another are separate matters. The theory is also primarily about relations between groups, whereas the compendium uses the phrase for health and prosocial benefits.

### In the compendium

The page lists two facets, "health function" and "and prosocial behaviour," the second tied to Cialdini and colleagues (1997). Neither Cialdini et al. nor Jetten, Haslam and Alexander's The Social Cure, also among the seed sources, was opened for this page, so the facets are not supported here. They are the compendium's extension. Principles such as SO051 (Use group identity for social cure) rest on that unopened literature, and R101 and R102 on relational autonomy and constitutive relationality come from different traditions.
