**Sapir-Whorf Hypothesis** — language shapes habitual thought; grammatical and lexical categories bias attention to experience.

The hypothesis pairs a claim from Edward Sapir with the sharper one from Benjamin Lee Whorf, whom Sapir taught. Sapir's 1929 statement is a claim about habit: "Human beings do not live in the objective world alone, nor alone in the world of social activity as ordinarily understood, but are very much at the mercy of the particular language which has become the medium of expression for their society." And: "The fact of the matter is that the 'real world' is to a large extent unconsciously built up on the language habits of the group." (Edward Sapir, "The Status of Linguistics as a Science," Language 5(4), 1929, [full text PDF](https://www.morrissey.ch/language-realities/02_Sapir_(1929)_Status_of_Linguistics_as_a_Science.pdf))

### The shaper of ideas

Whorf's 1940 essay "Science and Linguistics" takes the claim into the grammar itself: the grammar of each language, he writes, "is itself the shaper of ideas, the program and guide for the individual's mental activity, for his analysis of impressions, for his synthesis of his mental stock in trade." Whorf writes: "We dissect nature along lines laid down by our native languages." The categories, he argues, are not found staring every observer in the face; the world arrives as "a kaleidoscopic flux of impressions which has to be organized by our minds — and this means largely by the linguistic systems in our minds." The organization is social: "We cut nature up, organize it into concepts, and ascribe significances as we do, largely because we are parties to an agreement to organize it in this way — an agreement that holds throughout our speech community and is codified in the patterns of our language." (Benjamin Lee Whorf, "Science and Linguistics," MIT Technology Review, 1940, [full text PDF](https://web.mit.edu/allanmc/www/whorf.scienceandlinguistics.pdf))

### Strong and weak forms

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy's treatment puts the famous passages in context: "In fairness it must be stressed that these passages come from a single essay, 'Science and Linguistics,' of 1940, and in other places Whorf's tone is often more measured." The same supplement distinguishes the versions: "The linguistic relativity hypothesis comes in stronger and weaker forms, depending on the hypothesized forms and the hypothesized strength of the hypothesized influence," and names the strongest version: "The view that language completely determines how we think is often called linguistic determinism." Between trivially true and almost certainly false versions, the supplement holds, the key question "is whether there are interesting and defensible versions of linguistic relativism between those that are trivially true... and those that are dramatic but almost certainly false." ([SEP, supplement on the linguistic relativity hypothesis](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2012/entries/relativism/supplement2.html))
