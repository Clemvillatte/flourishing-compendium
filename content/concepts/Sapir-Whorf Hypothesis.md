**Sapir-Whorf Hypothesis** — language shapes habitual thought; grammatical and lexical categories bias attention to experience.

The hypothesis pairs two claims, one from Edward Sapir and the sharper one from his student Benjamin Lee Whorf. Sapir's 1929 statement is a claim about habits, not prison walls: "Human beings do not live in the objective world alone, nor alone in the world of social activity as ordinarily understood, but are very much at the mercy of the particular language which has become the medium of expression for their society." And: "The fact of the matter is that the 'real world' is to a large extent unconsciously built up on the language habits of the group." (Edward Sapir, "The Status of Linguistics as a Science," Language 5(4), 1929, [full text PDF](https://www.morrissey.ch/language-realities/02_Sapir_(1929)_Status_of_Linguistics_as_a_Science.pdf))

### The shaper of ideas

Whorf's 1940 essay "Science and Linguistics" takes the claim into the grammar itself: "It was found that the background linguistic system (in other words, the grammar) of each language is not merely a reproducing instrument for voicing ideas but rather is itself the shaper of ideas, the program and guide for the individual's mental activity, for his analysis of impressions, for his synthesis of his mental stock in trade." The essay's most quoted sentence gives the cutting image: "We dissect nature along lines laid down by our native languages." The categories, Whorf argues, are not found staring every observer in the face; the world arrives as "a kaleidoscopic flux of impressions which has to be organized by our minds — and this means largely by the linguistic systems in our minds." The organization is social: "We cut nature up, organize it into concepts, and ascribe significances as we do, largely because we are parties to an agreement to organize it in this way — an agreement that holds throughout our speech community and is codified in the patterns of our language." (Benjamin Lee Whorf, "Science and Linguistics," MIT Technology Review, 1940, [full text PDF](https://web.mit.edu/allanmc/www/whorf.scienceandlinguistics.pdf))

### Habit, not destiny

Read as a claim about bias rather than about limits, the hypothesis survives its strong form's collapse. The later literature usually reads these sentences as claims about habitual attention: the categories of one's language train a default carve-up of experience, which can be noticed and overridden. For a practice that works with attention and interpretation, the use is diagnostic — when a fixed way of describing suffering closes options, the description itself is part of what holds the suffering in place.
