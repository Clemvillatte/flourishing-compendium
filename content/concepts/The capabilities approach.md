The capabilities approach is a way of evaluating how well a life is going, and how fair a society is, by looking at what people are able to do and to be. The economist-philosopher Amartya Sen pioneered it from the late 1970s, and the philosopher Martha Nussbaum developed it into a specific account of the capabilities every person needs. Its core move is to shift the question away from what people have (income, resources) or report feeling (satisfaction), toward the real freedom they have to live ways of life they "have reason to value" (Sen's phrase, quoted in the Stanford Encyclopedia entry).

> "The capability approach is a theoretical framework that entails two normative claims: first, the claim that the freedom to achieve well-being is of primary moral importance and, second, that well-being should be understood in terms of people's capabilities and functionings." (Ingrid Robeyns and Morten Fibieger Byskov, ["The Capability Approach"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/), Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)

### Origin

The approach began in Sen's critique of the informational bases of economics and ethics: utilitarianism reduces well-being to satisfaction, and resource-based accounts reduce it to goods held, and both miss what a person can do with what they have. The encyclopedia entry traces earlier versions of the idea to Aristotle, Adam Smith and Marx, and credits Sen as the pioneer, with Nussbaum among the scholars who developed it.

### Core claims

The approach's central distinction is between what has been achieved and what could be chosen:

> "Capabilities are the doings and beings that people can achieve if they so choose" (Robeyns and Byskov, ["The Capability Approach, section 2.1"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/), Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy)

> "Functionings simply denotes those capabilities that have been achieved whether voluntarily or by chance." (same entry, section 2.2)

Nussbaum groups the capabilities she takes to be basic into ten "central human capabilities": life; bodily health; bodily integrity; senses, imagination and thought; emotions; practical reason; affiliation; other species; play; and control over one's environment. For her, the state should secure each citizen a threshold level of each capability, so that no life is "so impoverished that it is not worthy of the dignity of a human being" (Nussbaum, as quoted in the entry). Sen, by contrast, refuses to name the capabilities for everyone: he "consistently and explicitly refuses to defend 'one pre-determined canonical list of capabilities, chosen by theorists without any general social discussion or public reasoning'" (Sen 2005a: 158, as quoted in the entry).

### What the approach evaluates

The approach is a framework, not a finished theory. The entry stresses that it "should be defined as a normative framework, which can host a wide variety of more specific theories and applications", and that reading it as a complete theory of justice "is mistaken". What it fixes is the question asked in evaluation: not "how much does this person have?" but "what can this person do and be?". Two persons with similar goods and resources can reach very different ends depending on their circumstances.

### In everyday life

The resource a person holds is not yet the life they can lead. Someone with a wheelchair and someone who walks, holding the same income, do not have the same capability to enter a building whose only entrance is a flight of steps. (Illustrative scenario, not a reported study result.) The approach directs attention to that gap between having and being able, and to whether a society's arrangements close it or leave it open.
