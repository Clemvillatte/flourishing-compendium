“Skepticism answered by life” — Hume, Enquiry, Section XII, the backgammon passage.

The idea is that radical doubt about reason, the senses and the external world does not survive ordinary living. Hume makes the point in Section XII of An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding ("Of the Academical or Sceptical Philosophy"), where he calls the extreme position Pyrrhonism or "excessive" scepticism. The backgammon image that the compendium's seed line attaches to the Enquiry is in fact from his earlier Treatise of Human Nature (see below).

### The Enquiry's reply

Hume does not claim to refute the sceptic's arguments. He says the doubts can flourish in the schools, where refutation is "difficult, if not impossible", and lose their force once real objects and passions are present.

> "The great subverter of Pyrrhonism or the excessive principles of scepticism is action, and employment, and the occupations of common life." (Hume, Enquiry XII, Part II, para. 126, [Gutenberg #9662](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9662))

Paragraph 128 pushes it further. A Pyrrhonian "cannot expect, that his philosophy will have any constant influence on the mind", and "Nature is always too strong for principle." The doubter may cause "a momentary amazement and confusion", but the first trivial event in life will put his doubts to flight.

### The backgammon passage

The game appears in the last section of Book I of the Treatise (Part IV, Section VII, "Conclusion of this book"). Hume first describes how the "intense view" of reason's contradictions has left him asking "Where am I, or what?", then reports that reason cannot clear the clouds and nature does ("nature herself suffices to that purpose").

> "I dine, I play a game of backgammon, I converse, and am merry with my friends; and when after three or four hours' amusement, I would return to these speculations, they appear so cold, and strained, and ridiculous, that I cannot find in my heart to enter into them any farther." (Hume, Treatise 1.4.7, [Gutenberg #4705](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/4705))

### What it settles and what it leaves

The reply is about what a person can sustain, not about whether the doubts are true. Hume himself concedes in paragraph 126 that they are hard to answer on their own ground.

He also does not end in dogmatism. Paragraph 129 recommends a "mitigated" scepticism that keeps "a degree of doubt, and caution, and modesty, which, in all kinds of scrutiny and decision, ought for ever to accompany a just reasoner."
