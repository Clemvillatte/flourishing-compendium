Gabriel Marcel's contrast between two ways of facing a question. A problem lies before you, complete, ready to be worked on. A mystery is something you are inside of, and the person doing the asking is part of what is being asked about. A broken watch is a problem. Whether a friendship is dying is a mystery.

Marcel's clearest formulation:

> "A problem is something which I meet, which I find completely before me, but which I can therefore lay siege to and reduce. But a mystery is something in which I am myself involved, and it can therefore only be thought of as a sphere where the distinction between what is in me and what is before me loses its meaning and initial validity." — [Marcel, Being and Having (1949), p. 117, as quoted in Treanor & Sweetman, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/marcel/)

A problem's solver is interchangeable. The broken watch is the same watch whoever opens it, and the fix is "common property, that consequently can, at least in theory, be rediscovered by anybody at all" ([Marcel 1951a, p. 213, in SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/marcel/)). A mystery resists this. Marcel calls it a "problem that encroaches on its own data" ([Marcel 1995, p. 19, in SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/marcel/)): once you set the friendship on the examining table, you have already changed it, because the examiner is one of the parties. Evil is his worked example: "Evil which is only stated or observed is no longer evil which is suffered: in fact, it ceases to be evil. In reality, I can only grasp it as evil in the measure in which I *touches* me—that is to say, in the measure in which I am *involved*" ([Marcel 1995, p. 19, in SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/marcel/)). A mystery is not a problem you happen not to have solved yet: "The mysterious is not the unknowable, the unknowable is only the limiting case of the problematic" ([Marcel 1949, p. 118, in SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/marcel/)).

The distinction is not a counsel of vagueness. Mysteries can be entered more or less honestly, and Marcel's secondary reflection is the discipline of thinking about them without pretending the thinker stands outside. Treanor and Sweetman note that Marcel ties the distinction to "the notion of participation": the modern broken world, "riddled with problems and, on the other, determined to allow no room for mystery," treats as real only what a technique can fix, and so mistakes the terms of its own life ([Marcel 1995, p. 12, in SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/marcel/)).
