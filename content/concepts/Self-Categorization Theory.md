Self-categorization theory (SCT) "was developed by John Turner and colleagues, and along with social identity theory it is a constituent part of the social identity approach" ([Self-categorization theory, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-categorization_theory)). A review of the social identity field explains the shift from the earlier theory: "Whereas social identity theory posited an interpersonal-intergroup continuum to address the salience of social identity, self-categorization theory conceptualizes the self at different levels of abstraction (e.g., personal, group, human)" ([Spears, Group Identities: The Social Identity Perspective, PDF](https://graduatesocialpsych.weebly.com/uploads/1/9/0/7/19075053/spears_2011_social_identity_review.pdf)). The seed's "varying levels of abstraction" is accurate.

### What makes a category salient

The theory holds that salience depends on accessibility, "the perceiver's readiness to make use of" a category, and on comparative and normative fit ([Social Identity, Psychology of, ScienceDirect Topics](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/nursing-and-health-professions/depersonalization)). Comparative fit follows the meta-contrast principle: "meta-contrast is maximized to the extent that between-group differences are large and within-group differences are small on a given dimension. The higher the meta-contrast, the higher the likelihood that the categorization will become salient" (Spears). Normative fit is whether behaviour matches the category's norms. A Wiley review in a mixed-identity context says SCT postulates that categorizations of the self and others "are context-dependent and vary according to judgements of fit, frame of reference and relative accessibility" ([Being One or the Other, Both or Neither, European Journal of Social Psychology](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ejsp.3155)).

### Depersonalization and norms

When a category becomes salient, "people come to see themselves more as the interchangeable exemplars of a social category than as unique personalities defined by their differences from others" (Turner and colleagues 1987, quoted on Wikipedia). A PMC paper stresses that depersonalization "shouldn't be conflated with any psychopathological symptom", being neither a loss of individual identity nor dehumanizing, but "a change from the personal to the social level of identity" ([Being one of us: we-identities and self-categorization theory, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12886312/)). Under these conditions ingroup prototypes serve as norms and reference points for social influence (ScienceDirect Topics). A historical review found that the effects of prototypicality and norms on social influence emerged only under high salience or identification ([Social Identity Theory and Self-categorization Theory: A Historical Review, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1751-9004.2007.00066.x)).

### Limits

The sources I opened are reviews and descriptions of the theory; I did not open a critical paper. The PMC article says SCT is often described as primarily cognitive, though it can accommodate the emotional significance of membership.
