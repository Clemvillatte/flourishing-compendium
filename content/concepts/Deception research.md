Deception research studies how often people lie, why, and how well others detect it. Two findings from the journal abstracts give the outline.

### Lying is common and mostly minor

DePaulo and colleagues (1996) ran two diary studies. "77 college students reported telling 2 lies a day, and 70 community members told 1." People "told more self-centered lies than other-oriented lies", though in pairs made up only of women, other-oriented lies were as common as self-centered ones. Participants "did not regard their lies as serious and did not plan them much or worry about being caught." Still, "social interactions in which lies were told were less pleasant and less intimate than those in which no lies were told" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8656340/)).

The abstract therefore supports a cost to the quality of an interaction. It does not report that lying raised the liar's cognitive load or distress, which is how the finding is sometimes summarised.

### Detecting lies is hard

Bond and DePaulo (2006) synthesised "research results from 206 documents and 24,483 judges." Without special aids or training, "people achieve an average of 54% correct lie-truth judgments, correctly classifying 47% of lies as deceptive and 61% of truths as nondeceptive." Their meta-analyses found that "people are more accurate in judging audible than visible lies, that people appear deceptive when motivated to be believed, and that individuals regard their interaction partners as honest." They propose "that people judge others' deceptions more harshly than their own and that this double standard in evaluating deceit can explain much of the accumulated literature" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16859438/)).
