Conformity is the psychologist's name for the finding that a unanimous majority can bend a person's stated judgment even against the plain evidence of their senses. Solomon Asch demonstrated it in line-judgment experiments from 1951: seated among confederates who all gave the same wrong answer, most participants went along at least once, and about a third of all responses matched the erroneous majority.

The anchor result:

> "a sizable minority of responses conformed to the actors' (incorrect) answer (35.7%)" — [Wikipedia, "Asch conformity experiments"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asch_conformity_experiments)

The same page records that 74% of participants gave at least one incorrect answer across the 12 critical trials, and that 26% consistently defied the majority. In control conditions without group pressure, errors ran under 1%. The finding is usually split into two pressures: normative influence, "the willingness to conform publicly to attain social reward and avoid social punishment," and informational influence, going along because the group seems better informed.

Two boundaries matter for a writer. First, the effect is fragile: a single dissenting ally collapses it, and answering in private reduces it sharply. Second, it varies with culture and era — Bond and Smith's 1996 meta-analysis found conformity levels differ across societies, so Asch's numbers are a demonstration, not a constant.

Asch himself read the result as a civic warning, not a curiosity:

> "That we have found the tendency to conformity in our society so strong that reasonably intelligent and well-meaning young people are willing to call white black is a matter of concern. It raises questions about our ways of education and about the values that guide our conduct." — [Asch 1955, "Opinions and Social Pressure", Scientific American, p. 34](https://web.archive.org/web/20160602001325/http://pioneersofhumanbehaviour.com/solomon-asch/)
