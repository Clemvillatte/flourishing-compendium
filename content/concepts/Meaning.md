Meaning — the sense that life is about something: purpose, coherence, and mattering. Frankl made it the primary human drive; the traditions converge on meaning as made through work, love, and the stance taken toward suffering.

Viktor Frankl survived the camps and built a psychology around the claim that meaning is a primary human need, not a luxury that arrives after comfort. He set his term against the two psychologies he knew best:

> That is why I speak of a will to meaning in contrast to the pleasure principle (or, as we could also term it, the will to pleasure) on which Freudian psychoanalysis is cantered, as well as in contrast to the will to power on which Adlerian psychology, using the term "striving for superiority," is focused.

In Man's Search for Meaning he sets out the routes plainly:

> According to logotherapy, we can discover this meaning in life in three different ways: (1) by creating a work or doing a deed; (2) by experiencing something or encountering someone; and (3) by the attitude we take toward unavoidable suffering.

The first way is achievement. The second runs through experience: goodness, truth, beauty, nature, culture, or another person "in his very uniqueness;—by loving him". The third Frankl names without decoration:

> The third way of finding a meaning in life is by suffering.

He guards the third way with a condition that matters for any account of flourishing. Suffering is not a requirement for meaning; where suffering can be removed, the meaningful act is to remove it. What he insists on is that meaning survives even the fate that cannot be changed:

> In accepting this challenge to suffer bravely, life has a meaning up to the last moment, and it retains this meaning literally to the end. In other words, life's meaning is an unconditional one, for it even includes the potential meaning of unavoidable suffering.

### Why it matters for flourishing

Read one way, the three ways echo the seed's triad: work and deeds can carry purpose, the sense of being about something; love and encounter can carry mattering, the sense that someone and something count; the stance taken toward unavoidable suffering can hold a life story together when circumstances do not. That mapping is this page's reading, not a claim Frankl makes. What his text does support is the guard: none of this romanticises pain. Where a cause of suffering can be removed, removing it is the meaningful act. When suffering is unavoidable, Frankl identifies a further possibility of meaning in the attitude taken toward it.

On this reading meaning is assembled from deeds, encounters, and stances. Frankl's unconditional-meaning passage is the ground for saying it can hold under conditions that strip everything else away.
