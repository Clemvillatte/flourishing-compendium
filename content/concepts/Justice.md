Justice is the virtue of giving each their due, and the institutional principle that sorts what each is due. The compendium's page for it carries three anchors: Aristotle's treatment of justice as a virtue in Nicomachean Ethics V, Plato's staging of the hardest challenge to it in Republic I, and Rawls's reconstruction of it as fairness for institutions.

> "And for this same reason Justice alone of all the Virtues is thought to be a good to others, because it has immediate relation to some other person, inasmuch as the Just man does what is advantageous to another, either to his ruler or fellow-subject." (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics V.1, Chase translation, [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.html))

### Aristotle: the virtue with two branches

Aristotle's definition runs through capacity and wish: "all men mean by the term Justice a moral state such that in consequence of it men have the capacity of doing what is just, and actually do it, and wish it" (Nicomachean Ethics V.1, [Chase translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.html)). The particular justice then splits in two. Distributive justice governs "the distributions of honour, or wealth, or such other things as are to be shared among the members of the social community," where what is just is "a certain proportionable thing" — the shares must follow a rate, and the parties quarrel over which (V.3). Corrective justice governs transactions, voluntary and involuntary, and is arithmetical rather than proportional: the judge "is meant to be the personification of the Just," restoring equality between gain and loss (V.4).

### Thrasymachus's challenge

Plato gives the concept its hardest opening statement. In Republic I, Thrasymachus proclaims "that justice is nothing else than the interest of the stronger," arguing that each form of government makes laws "with a view to their several interests," so that "everywhere there is one principle of justice, which is the interest of the stronger" (Republic I, Jowett translation, [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1497/1497-h/1497-h.htm)). Socrates presses the opening: rulers err, so obedience can command what injures the stronger. Thrasymachus refines rather than concedes — the ruler, "in so far as he is a ruler, is unerring" — and the exchange continues without agreement (Republic I, [Jowett translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1497/1497-h/1497-h.htm)).

### Rawls: justice as fairness

The modern anchor reconstructs justice for institutions. Rawls's two principles, as the Stanford Encyclopedia gives them from Justice as Fairness: each person's "same indefeasible claim to a fully adequate scheme of equal basic liberties," and social and economic inequalities attached to offices "open to all under conditions of fair equality of opportunity" and "to the greatest benefit of the least-advantaged members of society" (the difference principle) (SEP, [John Rawls](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rawls/), citing JF 42-43). The principles are chosen from the original position, which translates the question of fair terms of cooperation into the question of what free and equal citizens "would agree to under fair conditions."
