The self as process is the psychological reading of an old philosophical thought: that a self is something a person is always doing, not a thing they have. The modern evidence base is Markus and Wurf's 1987 review of the dynamic self-concept. Its unifying premise is that the self-concept mediates and regulates behavior rather than merely reflecting it, and that it has therefore "been viewed as dynamic-as active, forceful, and capable of change" ([Markus and Wurf, The Dynamic Self-Concept, Annual Review of Psychology 1987](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/pdf/10.1146/annurev.ps.38.020187.001503)). The self-concept "interprets and organizes self-relevant actions and experiences; it has motivational consequences, providing the incentives, standards, plans, rules, and scripts for behavior; and it adjusts in response to challenges from the social environment" ([Markus and Wurf 1987](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/pdf/10.1146/annurev.ps.38.020187.001503)).

### The working self

The review's most quotable move is to deny that there is one stable inventory of self-knowledge being consulted. "The working self-concept, or the self-concept of the moment, is best viewed as a continually active, shifting array of accessible self-knowledge" ([Markus and Wurf 1987](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/pdf/10.1146/annurev.ps.38.020187.001503)). The stable part is the archive; the moment-by-moment part is which files are open. The same review solves the older puzzle of how one self can hold contradictory descriptions: "view the self-concept as a multifaceted phenomenon, as a set or collection of images, schemas, conceptions, prototypes, theories, goals, or tasks" ([Markus and Wurf 1987](https://www.annualreviews.org/doi/pdf/10.1146/annurev.ps.38.020187.001503)).

### The older company it keeps

The idea has older company, which the page's source list records. William James's Principles of Psychology (1890) and Whitehead's Process and Reality are the two Western anchors the compendium files it under; the Zhuangzi pages carry a third, the transformation of things (wuhua), where the self is what changes rather than what resists change. Those anchors are inherited citations from the seed, and how each argues the point belongs to their own pages; the psychological claim here stands on the 1987 review.
