Reflective practice is "the ability to reflect on one's thoughts, emotions, actions, decisions, assumptions, and professional experiences so as to take a critical stance or attitude towards improving one's own practice, understanding, judgment, and future actions, while challenging automatic reactions and assumptions and engaging in a process of continuous adaptation and learning" ([Wikipedia, Reflective practice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflective_practice)). Donald Schön's 1983 book The Reflective Practitioner is the founding citation, and its two concepts still organize the field: "reflection-on-action and reflection-in-action which explain how professionals meet the challenges of their work with a kind of improvisation that is improved through practice" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflective_practice)). Reflection-in-action happens mid-task; reflection-on-action happens afterward, when the practitioner replays what occurred and what could have gone differently.

### The models that followed

Schön was building on a longer line. Borton's 1970 cycle compressed reflection into three questions: What, So what, and Now what. Kolb and Fry's experiential learning cycle (1975) gave the same movement a four-stage form. Argyris and Schön's 1978 theory of action added the rungs that matter for practice: single-loop learning corrects the error and keeps the strategy, while double-loop learning "involves the modification of objectives, strategies or policies so that when a similar situation arises a new framing system is employed" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflective_practice)). Gibbs's 1988 structured debriefing turns the cycle into questions a professional can run after any episode: description, feelings, evaluation, analysis, conclusion, action plan.

### The honest caveat

The article carries the limit that matters for anyone tempted to treat journaling as a cure-all: "Although reflection alone is not fully reliable, because insight, introspection, and self-appraisal can be distorted by cognitive biases, reflective practice can still be a major source of professional development when combined with critical analysis, feedback, and evidence-based knowledge" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflective_practice)). Boud and colleagues state the positive case: "Reflection is an important human activity in which people recapture their experience, think about it, mull it over and evaluate it. It is this working with experience that is important in learning" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflective_practice)). The same page notes that scholars find precursors in Buddhist teachings and Marcus Aurelius' Meditations, which is the bridge the compendium's principles cross daily.
