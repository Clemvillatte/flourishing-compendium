Seeing-as, or aspect perception, is Wittgenstein's name for noticing a different aspect of something that has not itself changed. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy places the discussion in the final part of the Philosophical Investigations (section xi) and describes it as a "key Wittgensteinian term", where "two uses of the word 'see'" are separated ([SEP: Ludwig Wittgenstein](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/wittgenstein/)).

### Two kinds of seeing

The first use of "see" is plain recognition. The second is seeing a likeness between two things, and it is the one that "has given rise to the question of aspect perception". The entry quotes Wittgenstein: "I observe a face, and then suddenly notice its likeness to another. I see that it has not changed; and yet I see it differently. I call this experience 'noticing an aspect'" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/wittgenstein/)).

The entry adds that aspect seeing "involves noticing something about an object" that one had not noticed before, and that "it also arises as a result of a change of context of our perceptions" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/wittgenstein/)).

### In art and music

The entry on Wittgenstein's aesthetics gives a musical case: "to see one thing in light of another is not the same as seeing that one thing." Hearing a melody as a variation of another differs from hearing the melody alone, so that the hearing becomes an "imagination-assisted perception informed by cultural awareness" ([SEP: Wittgenstein's Aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/wittgenstein-aesthetics/)). That entry also treats seeing connections as often "a matter of aspect-seeing, or seeing-as".

### Scope

My label names the duck-rabbit drawing. The two Stanford entries consulted for this page describe the aspect-seeing passages without naming that figure, so the duck-rabbit detail rests on my earlier note and has not been checked against the Investigations text here. Nothing in these sources claims that learning to shift aspects reduces suffering. Any link to practice is an inference for the reader to weigh.
