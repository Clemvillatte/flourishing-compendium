The hermeneutics of faith is Paul Ricoeur's name for one of the two poles he saw in all interpretation: the reading that listens for meaning rather than hunting for what meaning hides. He set the two poles side by side in Freud and Philosophy:

> "A general theory of interpretation would have two account for…two interpretations of interpretation, the one as recollection of meaning, the other as the reduction of illusions and lies of consciousness…" (Ricoeur, Freud and Philosophy, 1970, in [Stanford Humanities Center](https://shc.stanford.edu/arcade/interventions/suspicion-solidarity))

### The two schools

The second pole is the school of suspicion: "Three masters, seemingly mutually exclusive, dominate the [second] school of suspicion: Marx, Nietzsche and Freud," masters who share the "decision to look on all of consciousness primarily as 'false' consciousness." The first pole, the hermeneutics of faith, is characterised, as a study of Ricoeur's symbols work puts it, "by an attitude of openness and willingness to listen to what the symbols say" ([Itao, 2010](https://www.kritike.org/journal/issue_8/itao_december2010.pdf)). The two are not rivals but the two halves of a mature reading: hermeneutics as a whole is animated, in Ricoeur's phrase, by a "double motivation: willingness to suspect, willingness to listen; vow of rigor, vow of obedience." My principle E090 carries this as a practice: practise the hermeneutics of faith, read for what a person or a text is saying before reading them for what they might be hiding. A compendium exercise drawn from the concept is to spend the first pass of any difficult reading listening for the meaning offered, and only then run the suspicious pass. What the concept does not give: a criterion for when each pole fits; Ricoeur's answer is that suspicion clears the horizon for a fuller hearing, not that either pole can stand alone.
