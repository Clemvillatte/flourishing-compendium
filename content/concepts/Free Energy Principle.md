The free-energy principle (FEP) is Karl Friston's proposal that perception, action and learning can be described as one kind of optimisation. His 2010 review in Nature Reviews Neuroscience opens: "A free-energy principle has been proposed recently that accounts for action, perception and learning." It adds that "the same quantity keeps emerging, namely value (expected reward, expected utility) or its complement, surprise (prediction error, expected cost)", and that this "is the quantity that is optimized under the free-energy principle" ([Friston, 2010, PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20068583/)).

### The wider claim

Philosophers Matteo Colombo and Cory Wright state the broader version: "all systems that minimize their free energy resist a tendency to physical disintegration". They note it has been applied well beyond perception, to "the evolution, development, morphology, anatomy and function of the brain" ([Colombo and Wright, Synthese](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-018-01932-w)).

### The dispute over its status

The principle has been called "a postulate, an unfalsifiable principle, a natural law, and an imperative", and Colombo and Wright write that "its epistemic status is unclear". They argue that it is inconsistent with organicism and with mechanistic approaches in the life sciences. They also write that if the principle were a tautology, free-energy theorists "may have a triviality problem".

Mel Andrews replies that critiques have focused on "a lack of empirical support and a failure to generate concrete, falsifiable predictions", and proposes that the FEP "be reserved to designate a model structure", with scientists and philosophers adding various construals to produce many models. On that view, demanding that the FEP itself be falsifiable "rest[s] on a category error" ([Andrews, Biology & Philosophy, 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10539-021-09807-0)).

### How to use it in the essays

Treat the principle as a contested unifying framework, not as an established mechanism. The testable claims sit in the specific models built on it.
