The observing self is Arthur Deikman's name for awareness itself: the "I" that witnesses thoughts, feelings and sensations without being identical to any of them. He set it out in The Observing Self: Mysticism and Psychotherapy (1982), arguing that Western psychology had no place for what cannot be studied as an object ([Deikman 1982](https://www.deikman.com/observing.html)). William James drew a related line in The Principles of Psychology (1890): the Me is all a person can call their own and observe, the I the passing thought that does the observing ([James 1890](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin10.htm)).

### A self that cannot be seen

Deikman describes awareness as "the ground of conscious life, the background or field in which all elements exist, different from thoughts, sensations, or images" ([Deikman 1982](https://www.deikman.com/observing.html)). Thoughts, memories and feelings are contents we witness; the observer is different in kind: "we cannot observe the observing self; we must experience it directly. It has no defining qualities, no boundaries, no dimensions." He links clinical trouble to this confusion: "at the heart of psychopathology lies a fundamental confusion between the self as object and the self of pure subjectivity."

### James and the passing thought

"The Empirical Self of each of us is all that he is tempted to call by the name of me," James wrote ([James 1890](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin10.htm)). In his psychological account the I is the present thought that appropriates earlier experience, not a substantial soul, and the IEP notes he did not treat it as a substance or an object of science ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/james-o/)). The accounts differ on what the observer is: featureless awareness for Deikman, the passing thought for James.

### What the research found

In their 2011 review, Ethan Kross and Ozlem Ayduk report experiments in which people who analyzed a memory of anger or sadness from "the perspective of a fly on the wall" felt less distress immediately and ruminated less on returning to the memory up to a week later than people who replayed it through their own eyes. Benefits were reported when people reflected on why they had felt as they did; evidence for vulnerable groups was preliminary ([Kross and Ayduk 2011](https://rascl.studentorg.berkeley.edu/assets/files/kross_ayduk_2011_cd.pdf)). Acceptance and commitment therapy makes a related move with self-as-context, the perspective from which experience is noticed, developed through defusion and mindfulness ([Webster 2011](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/advances-in-psychiatric-treatment/article/introduction-to-acceptance-and-commitment-therapy/D67B44FDED4147CA35C1AA8FA9D3DDA5)).
