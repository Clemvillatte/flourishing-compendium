The expanding circle is Peter Singer's name, from his 1981 book, for the history and the task of moral concern. The claim is descriptive and normative at once: over human history the set of beings whose welfare counts has grown, and reason presses it to keep growing. His own summary, from the book:

> "The circle of altruism has broadened from the family and tribe to the nation and race, and we are beginning to recognize that our obligations extend to all human beings. The process should not stop there." (Singer, [The Expanding Circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Expanding_Circle), 1981)

### Where the circle stops

Singer's stopping rule is the book's sharpest sentence: "The only justifiable stopping place for the expansion of altruism is the point at which all whose welfare can be affected by our actions are included within the circle of altruism." The argument for going past the species line, as he puts it there, is symmetry: "it is as arbitrary to restrict the principle of equal consideration of interests to our own species as it would be to restrict it to our own race." The engine of the expansion is the impartial element in ethical reasoning, the willingness to count another's interests as one among many rather than as nothing, and the book's sociobiological grounding, as the Wikipedia summary describes it, explains why the circle starts small: altruism evolved for kin and reciprocators, and reasoning is what carries it past its origins.

For practice, the circle gives a direction rather than a rule: the next person, group or species one currently discounts is the work.
