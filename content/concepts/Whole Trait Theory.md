Whole Trait Theory joins the two halves of a trait: what a person actually does from moment to moment, and the mechanisms that produce those moments. Its authors state the program:

> "Personality researchers should modify models of traits to include mechanisms of differential reaction to situations. Whole Trait Theory does so via five main points." ([Fleeson & Jayawickreme, 2015, abstract](https://wisdomcenter.uchicago.edu/publications/whole-trait-theory))

### Origin

The theory grew out of William Fleeson's experience-sampling research, published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology in 2001 — three studies covering two to three weeks of everyday life ([Fleeson, 2001, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11414368/)). It appeared amid psychology's long person-situation debate, which Fleeson worked to move beyond ([Fleeson, Moving Personality Beyond the Person-Situation Debate](http://wholebeinginstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/W-Fleeson_Moving-Personality-Beyond-Person.pdf)). Fleeson and Eranda Jayawickreme presented the theory in their 2015 article in the Journal of Research in Personality (56, 82–92).

### Core claims

In their abstract, the authors identify five points:

> "First, the descriptive side of traits can be conceptualized as density distributions of states. Second, it is important to provide an explanatory account of the Big 5 traits. Third, adding an explanatory account to the Big 5 creates two parts to traits, an explanatory part and a descriptive part, and these two parts can be recognized as separate entities that are joined into whole traits. Fourth, Whole Trait Theory proposes that the explanatory side of traits consists of social-cognitive mechanisms. Fifth, social-cognitive mechanisms that produce Big-5 states should be identified." ([Fleeson & Jayawickreme, 2015, abstract](https://wisdomcenter.uchicago.edu/publications/whole-trait-theory))

### The two sides

The descriptive side is a distribution, not a fixed level. As Fleeson summarises the finding in the figure caption of his later paper:

> "The typical person's behavior differs as much from occasion to occasion as does his or her emotion, and more than the amount people differ from each other on average." (Fleeson, [Moving Personality Beyond the Person-Situation Debate](http://wholebeinginstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/W-Fleeson_Moving-Personality-Beyond-Person.pdf), Figure 2 caption, quoting Fleeson, 2001, p. 1015)

The 2001 abstract reports substantial variation within individuals alongside stability at the level of individual differences: the typical individual "regularly and routinely manifested nearly all levels of all traits in his or her everyday behavior", while "individual differences in central tendencies of behavioral distributions were almost perfectly stable" ([2001 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11414368/)). The explanatory side is social-cognitive: "within-person variability in extraversion was shown to reflect individual differences in reactivity to extraversion-relevant situational cues" ([2001 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11414368/)).

### In everyday life

On this theory, being an extravert means frequent extraverted moments, not constant ones: the same colleague can be the loudest at Friday drinks and the quietest at Monday's stand-up, and both count as points in one person's distribution. (Illustrative scenario, not a reported participant or study result.)
