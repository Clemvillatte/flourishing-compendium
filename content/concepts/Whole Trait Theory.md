Whole Trait Theory joins the two halves of a trait: what a person actually does from moment to moment, and the mechanisms that produce those moments. Its authors open with the program: "Personality researchers should modify models of traits to include mechanisms of differential reaction to situations. Whole Trait Theory does so via five main points" ([Fleeson & Jayawickreme, 2015, abstract](https://wisdomcenter.uchicago.edu/publications/whole-trait-theory)).

### Origin

The theory grew out of William Fleeson's experience-sampling research, published in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology in 2001 — three studies covering two to three weeks of everyday life ([Fleeson, 2001, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11414368/)). It appeared amid psychology's long person-situation debate, which Fleeson worked to move beyond ([Fleeson, 2007](http://wholebeinginstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/W-Fleeson_Moving-Personality-Beyond-Person.pdf)). Fleeson and Eranda Jayawickreme named and framed the whole theory in 2015, in the Journal of Research in Personality (56, 82–92).

### Core claims

The five points, in the authors' words:

> "First, the descriptive side of traits can be conceptualized as density distributions of states. Second, it is important to provide an explanatory account of the Big 5 traits. Third, adding an explanatory account to the Big 5 creates two parts to traits, an explanatory part and a descriptive part, and these two parts can be recognized as separate entities that are joined into whole traits. Fourth, Whole Trait Theory proposes that the explanatory side of traits consists of social-cognitive mechanisms. Fifth, social-cognitive mechanisms that produce Big-5 states should be identified." ([Fleeson & Jayawickreme, 2015, abstract](https://wisdomcenter.uchicago.edu/publications/whole-trait-theory))

### The two sides

The descriptive side is a distribution, not a fixed level: "The typical person's behavior differs as much from occasion to occasion as does his or her emotion, and more than the amount people differ from each other on average" (Fleeson, 2001, p. 1015, as reprinted in [Fleeson, 2007](http://wholebeinginstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/W-Fleeson_Moving-Personality-Beyond-Person.pdf)). Yet the distribution holds its shape: people "regularly and routinely manifested nearly all levels of all traits" while their central tendencies "were almost perfectly stable" ([2001 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11414368/)). The explanatory side is social-cognitive — goals, interpretations and situations produce the states that accumulate into the trait: "within-person variability in extraversion was shown to reflect individual differences in reactivity to extraversion-relevant situational cues" ([2001 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11414368/)).

### In everyday life

On this theory, being an extravert means frequent extraverted moments, not constant ones: the same colleague can be the loudest at Friday drinks and the quietest at Monday's stand-up, and both count as points in one stable distribution. (The office image is illustrative; the 2001 studies measured everyday behaviour, not office life.)
