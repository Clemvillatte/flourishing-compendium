The illusion of transparency is the tendency to overestimate how visible one's inner states are to other people. The nervousness before speaking, the effort of hiding a reaction, the thought behind a face: each is experienced from the inside at full volume, and the person quietly assumes the room hears it too. The name and the classic studies come from Thomas Gilovich, Kenneth Savitsky and Victoria Medvec's 1998 paper, which showed that liars overestimate how detectable their lies are, that people believe their disgust shows more than it does, and that the same overestimate touches reluctance to intervene in emergencies. A standard summary:

> "The illusion of transparency is a tendency for people to overestimate the degree to which their personal mental state is known by others." ([Wikipedia, Illusion of transparency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_transparency))

### The tapping test

Elizabeth Newton's Stanford dissertation illustration makes the gap vivid. A tapper taps out a well-known song such as "Happy Birthday to You" and a listener guesses it; the tapper hears the tune and the lyrics in their head, while the listener only hears rhythmic knocking:

> "People usually estimate that the song will be guessed correctly in about 50 percent of the tests, but only 3 percent pick the correct song." ([Newton's test, as summarised in Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_transparency))

The 1998 authors attribute the bias to an anchoring problem: people judge how visible their feelings are by starting from their own experience of those feelings and adjust too little for the fact that observers do not share that access (Gilovich, Savitsky and Medvec, 1998, [APA PsycNet](https://psycnet.apa.org/doi/10.1037/0022-3514.75.2.332)).

### Speech anxiety, and the remedy inside the diagnosis

The cost shows up clearly in public speaking. Savitsky and Gilovich's 2003 follow-up in the Journal of Experimental Social Psychology put it this way:

> "Individuals often believe their internal states are more apparent to others than is actually the case, a phenomenon known as the illusion of transparency. In the domain of public speaking, for example, individuals who are nervous about delivering a public speech believe their nervousness is more apparent to their audience than it actually is, a finding we document in Study 1." ([Savitsky & Gilovich 2003, abstract, via Docslib](https://docslib.org/doc/4481417/the-illusion-of-transparency-and-the-alleviation-of-speech-anxietyq))

Their second study divided speakers into control, reassured and informed groups; only the group taught about the illusion improved on every measure, in their own ratings and the observers' ([Wikipedia, Illusion of transparency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_transparency)). Knowing about the bias is itself the remedy, which is what makes this concept practical rather than merely diagnostic.
