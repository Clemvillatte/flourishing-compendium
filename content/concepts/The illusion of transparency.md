The illusion of transparency is the tendency to overestimate how visible your inner states are to other people. The nervousness you feel before speaking, the effort of hiding a reaction to bad news, the thought behind a face — you experience all of it from the inside, at full volume, and you quietly assume the room hears it too.

The name and the classic demonstration come from Elizabeth Newton's Stanford dissertation work, usually dated to 1990. Her test is famous because it is almost unbearably simple: one person taps out a well-known song, another guesses which song. The tapper hears the tune and the lyrics in their head; the listener hears knocking.

> "People usually estimate that the song will be guessed correctly in about 50 percent of the tests, but only 3 percent pick the correct song." — [Newton's tapping test, as summarised in Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illusion_of_transparency)

Gilovich, Savitsky and Medvec gave the effect its name and its home in a 1998 paper, running it through three settings: liars overestimated how detectable their lies were, tasters overestimated how visible their disgust at foul drinks was, and people in a staged emergency overestimated how alarmed they looked. They also connected it to the bystander effect: everyone plays it cool and monitors the others, and a group of nonchalant faces can talk itself out of a real emergency.

The practical payoff is Savitsky and Gilovich's 2003 speech-anxiety study. Speakers split into control, reassured and informed groups; only the group told about the illusion of transparency improved on their own ratings and on the observers'. Knowing that your anxiety is not broadcasting is itself a treatment.
