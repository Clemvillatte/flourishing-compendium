State-dependent memory is the finding that material is recalled better when the person's internal state at retrieval matches their state at learning. The compendium's earlier note pairs Godden and Baddeley (1975) with Eich (1995). The two studies address different things, so they are described separately below.

### Drug state: Goodwin and colleagues

The classic human demonstration used alcohol. In the 1969 study, male volunteers performed four memory tasks sober or intoxicated and were tested again 24 hours later in the same or the opposite condition. "In tasks measuring recall and interference, learning transfer was better when the subject was intoxicated during both sessions than when he was intoxicated only during the learning session. In a task measuring recognition, transfer was not significantly affected by changing state." The authors conclude that "not all forms of memory are equally sensitive to the phenomenon" ([Goodwin et al. 1969, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5774177/)).

### Mood: Eich

Eich's 1995 overview states that "events encoded in a certain state of affect or mood are most retrievable in that state" only sometimes, and that "neither the circumstances under which mood dependent memory (MDM) occurs nor the mechanisms that enable its emergence are as yet well understood" ([Eich 1995, Psychological Science](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.1995.tb00309.x/)). Eich and Metcalfe's 1989 paper reports that internal events are less likely than external events to be recalled after a shift in mood state ([Eich & Metcalfe 1989](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/metcalfe/PDFs/Eich%20Metcalfe%201989.pdf)).

### Physical context: Godden and Baddeley

Godden and Baddeley's divers learned word lists on land or underwater: "Lists learnt underwater were best recalled underwater, and vice versa" ([Godden & Baddeley 1975](https://open.unisq.edu.au/pluginfile.php/92783/mod_folder/content/0/Memory%20Reading%20Files/Godden%20%20Baddeley,%201975.pdf)). That study concerns the surrounding environment and not the person's internal state. A 2021 replication with sixteen divers reports that "contrary to the original study, we did not find that recall in the same context where the words had been learned was better than recall in the other context" ([Murre 2021, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34760281/)).

### Scope

State dependence appears task-specific. Goodwin's recognition task showed no state effect, and the mood work describes unresolved conditions. Neither mood nor drug studies here test whether matching states can be used to support wellbeing, so the compendium's practical reading is an inference.
