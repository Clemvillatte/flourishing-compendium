Hindsight bias is the finding that people see outcomes as more predictable after they know how things turned out. Barry Fischhoff's original experiments in 1975 showed the effect with the Nixon visit to China, and later work treats it as a standard distortion of memory and judgment rather than an occasional slip.

> "Hindsight bias occurs when people feel that they \"knew it all along,\" that is, when they believe that an event is more predictable after it becomes known than it was before it became known." — [Roese & Vohs, Perspectives on Psychological Science 7(5) (2012), 411–426, full text](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2026-01/vohs-et-al-2012-hindsight-bias.pdf)

### Three distortions in one

> "Hindsight bias embodies any combination of three aspects: memory distortion, beliefs about events' objective likelihoods, or subjective beliefs about one's own prediction abilities." — [Roese & Vohs 2012](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2026-01/vohs-et-al-2012-hindsight-bias.pdf)

The review separates memory distortion (I said it would happen), inevitability (it was bound to happen), and foreseeability (I saw it coming), and the practical damage is the same across the three: the past looks more orderly and more foreseeable than it was, which is why the facet below names learning from feedback as the first casualty. Feedback that arrives after an outcome the learner already feels they predicted carries no surprise, and without surprise there is little to update.

## Facets

- impairs learning from feedback
