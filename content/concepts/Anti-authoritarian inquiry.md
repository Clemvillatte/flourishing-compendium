Anti-authoritarian inquiry is Dewey's account of what replaces external authority in a democratic society: the disposition to inquire, cultivated by education. His own statement: "Since a democratic society repudiates the principle of external authority, it must find a substitute in voluntary disposition and interest; these can be created only by education" ([Dewey, Democracy and Education, ch. 7, University of Oslo PDF](https://www.uio.no/studier/emner/uv/uv/UV9406/dewey-john-(1980).-democracy-and-education.pdf)). In a 1936 restatement: "When external authority is rejected, it does not follow that all authority should be rejected, but rather that there is need to search for a more effective source of authority" ([quoted in the Purdue paper on authority in Korean education](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1507&context=eandc)).

### The method that replaces it

Dewey's answer is organized intelligence, the scientific method extended to moral and social questions. His own words: "We need an authority that, unlike the older forms in which it operated, is capable of directing and utilizing change" and the question "whether and how scientific method, which is the method of intelligence in experimental action, can provide the authority that earlier centuries sought in fixed dogmas" ([Dewey Dictionary of Education, archive.org](https://archive.org/stream/johndeweydiction012543mbp/johndeweydiction012543mbp_djvu.txt)). The scientific attitude is defined by inquiry, examination, and conclusions "only on the basis of evidence after taking pains to gather all available evidence" ([same](https://archive.org/stream/johndeweydiction012543mbp/johndeweydiction012543mbp_djvu.txt)).

### The connection to Peirce

The compendium's principle on fixing belief by experiment gives the epistemology behind this. Peirce's "Fixation of Belief" (1877) surveys four methods of settling belief: tenacity, authority, a priori reasonableness, and science ([Peirce, The Fixation of Belief, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Fixation_of_Belief)). Dewey takes the same argument into education: a school that supplies ready-made material trains "docile or passive taking in of the material presented," where "acquiring takes the place of inquiring" ([Dewey 1903, "Democracy in Education," MW3: 236, as quoted in the UCLA eScholarship dissertation](https://escholarship.org/content/qt26k2t4w2/qt26k2t4w2.pdf)). The anti-authoritarian claim is not that students should believe whatever they like; it is that the only durable substitute for imposed belief is tested belief.
