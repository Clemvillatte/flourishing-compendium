Attentional bias theory, in Colin MacLeod and Andrew Mathews' account, holds that anxiety is maintained partly by where attention goes: anxious individuals allocate attention preferentially toward threat cues, and the allocation keeps the anxiety supplied with its evidence. The founding experimental demonstration is the 1986 dot-probe study by MacLeod, Mathews and Tata, which introduced the paradigm the field still uses.

> Clinically anxious (but not clinically depressed) subjects consistently shifted attention toward threat words, resulting in reduced detection latencies for probes appearing in the vicinity of such stimuli. Normal control subjects, on the other hand, tended to shift attention away from such material. — MacLeod, Mathews and Tata, ["Attentional bias in emotional disorders" (1986), Journal of Abnormal Psychology 95(1), 15-20](https://scispace.com/papers/attentional-bias-in-emotional-disorders-c9tlej18p2)

### How the bias was isolated

The dot-probe design sidestepped the response-bias problem: subjects made a neutral response, a button press, to a neutral stimulus, a visual dot probe, whose position was manipulated relative to displayed words that were either threat related or neutral in content. Probe detection latencies then indicated the impact of the threat words on the distribution of visual attention. The abstract's interpretation carries the theory's maintenance claim: the results "were interpreted as supporting the existence of anxiety-related encoding bias, and it is suggested that this cognitive mechanism may contribute to the maintenance of such mood disorders". The anxious-but-not-depressed contrast in the same data is what tied the bias to anxiety specifically. Whether the bias causes vulnerability, not merely accompanies it, was settled later by the research program covered on the Attentional bias research page.
