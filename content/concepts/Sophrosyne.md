"Sophrosyne" — Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics III.10.

Sophrosyne is the Greek virtue of measured selfhood: sound-mindedness, moderation, the holding of oneself in proportion. The compendium's Aristotelian anchor is the Nicomachean Ethics, where the concept appears as the virtue concerning pleasures. In Chase's translation the word is rendered "perfected Self-Mastery", and the mean doctrine gives it its shape: ruined by excess and by defect alike, preserved by the mean.

> "He that tastes of every pleasure and abstains from none comes to lose all self-control; while he who avoids all, as do the dull and clownish, comes as it were to lose his faculties of perception: that is to say, the habits of perfected Self-Mastery and Courage are spoiled by the excess and defect, but by the mean state are preserved." (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book II, D.P. Chase translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt))

### What the virtue is

Aristotle places the discussion of pleasures and self-mastery at Book III.10 in the standard chapter numbering; the passage above is the mean doctrine from Book II, quoted from Chase's rendering, and it carries the idea whole: sophrosyne is not the refusal of pleasure but the refusal of both extremes, the drowning and the deadening. For the compendium, the concept names a whole register of Greek wisdom from Delphi's "nothing in excess" to Plato's city-soul analogies: the person in proportion, whose desires have a measure.
