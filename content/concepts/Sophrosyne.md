Sophrosyne is the Greek virtue of measured selfhood, usually rendered as temperance, self-mastery or sound-mindedness. My anchor is Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics, where it is the virtue concerned with pleasures. D.P. Chase's translation, quoted below, renders the word as "perfected Self-Mastery". Other translators choose other words, and this page quotes Chase throughout.

### The virtue as a mean

In Book II, Aristotle argues that virtues are spoiled by excess and defect and preserved by the mean:

> "He that tastes of every pleasure and abstains from none comes to lose all self-control; while he who avoids all, as do the dull and clownish, comes as it were to lose his faculties of perception: that is to say, the habits of perfected Self-Mastery and Courage are spoiled by the excess and defect, but by the mean state are preserved." (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II, Chase translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt))

His summary table places it with pleasures and pains: "The Mean state here is perfected Self-Mastery, the defect total absence of Self-control." On the other extreme he says, "there are really no people who are chargeable with it, so, of course, there is really no name for such characters", and proposes to call them "insensible" ([Chase translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt)).

### Appetite obedient to reason

The discussion of the virtue near the end of Book III says desires "ought to be moderate and few, and in no respect to be opposed to Reason". It states that "the man of perfected self-mastery desires what he ought in right manner and at right times, which is exactly what Reason directs" ([Chase translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt)). The model is a child who should live "with constant regard to the orders of its educator", just as the appetitive principle should follow Reason's orders.

### Scope

This page reads Aristotle in one English translation. It does not cover Plato's use of sophrosyne or other Greek sources, and it makes no claim about the history of the word beyond Aristotle's text.
