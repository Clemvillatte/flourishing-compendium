Dynamic skill theory, developed by Kurt Fischer and colleagues, describes development as the building of skills that always depend on a context. Fischer and Yan call the theory a way "to unpack but not dissect human development", and trace it to Fischer's 1980 Psychological Review paper, "A Theory of Cognitive Development: The Control and Construction of Hierarchies of Skills" ([Fischer and Yan, "The Development of Dynamic Skill Theory"](https://paperzz.com/doc/7731855/the-development-of-dynamic-skill-theory-kurt-fischer)).

### What a skill is

Skill is the starting concept. "Different from competence, ability, or capacity, skill is a concept that is context-based and task-specific." It is the one concept Fischer found that "directly included both person and environment in the same entity" ([Fischer and Yan](https://paperzz.com/doc/7731855/the-development-of-dynamic-skill-theory-kurt-fischer)).

### Levels and spurts

Fischer separates levels from stages: "Levels are not stages, because there is so much variability in each child's activities that children do not show ladder-like changes from one stage to the next at specific ages." What they show instead are "qualitative changes in skill organization, especially in their most complex activities". Growth spurts were chosen as an empirical test: "discontinuities in growth curves, such as spurts and drops, provided an excellent empirical criterion for emergence of a new level" ([Fischer and Yan](https://paperzz.com/doc/7731855/the-development-of-dynamic-skill-theory-kurt-fischer)).

### Support changes the ceiling

The part most useful to a person trying to grow is the role of support. Fischer and colleagues describe two upper limits. With high support, which means priming the key parts of a task, performance reaches an optimal level. With no priming it stays at a functional level. The optimal level "showed sharp discontinuities in growth at specific ages, such as jumps in skill level", while the functional level "showed no systematic discontinuities and commonly slow, smooth growth" ([Fischer and Yan](https://paperzz.com/doc/7731855/the-development-of-dynamic-skill-theory-kurt-fischer)).

In the authors' summary, activity varies "from moment to moment up and down a developmental complexity scale" as a function of "degree of contextual support, emotional match, and specific task demands of the moment". As they put it, "when contextual support changes, a person's level changes. The upper limit on performance (the competence) varies directly with support" ([Fischer and Yan](https://paperzz.com/doc/7731855/the-development-of-dynamic-skill-theory-kurt-fischer)). A later chapter by Fischer and Bidell frames the theory as an account of "the dynamic organization of self-constructed, socially embedded skills and activities (actions, thoughts, and emotions)" ([Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9780470147658.chpsy0107)).

### Scope

The seed line "skills develop in predictable growth spurts" needs the qualification above: spurts are the signature of optimal-level skill under strong support, and ordinary everyday skill grows more smoothly and slowly. Most of the evidence shown here concerns children's social and cognitive skills, and the page does not claim it carries over unchanged to adult wisdom practice.
