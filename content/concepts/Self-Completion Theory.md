**Self-Completion Theory** — Wicklund, Gollwitzer — when identity claims are incomplete, people display symbols and seek recognition to complete them.

## What the theory says

Robert Wicklund and Peter Gollwitzer developed self-completion theory in the early 1980s to explain how people build an identity. The theory treats facets of the self as goals. Someone who wants to see themselves as a musician, a scientist, or a good parent reaches for things that seem to prove it: the right clothes, the title, the stated intention, the audience that noticed.

> According to this theory, people conceptualize facets of their own identity as personal goals. People then use a wide array of activities that reflect on their personal goals to build their identity, a process called symbolic self-completion. For instance, people can exhibit behavior that reflects their identity goals in order to achieve a state of self-completion. Alternatively, they can display material symbols congruent with such goals or simply ascribe themselves goal-congruent characteristics. In either case, self-completion theory predicts that successfully enacting symbolic activities leads to fewer subsequent congruent activities, because people derive a sense of progress or goal achievement from successful symbolic activities, which in turn leads to reduced tension and striving for similar goal-congruent activities (Wicklund and Gollwitzer, 1981).

The strange part is what counts as progress. A symbol works even when it is a poor substitute for the thing itself. Announcing a goal can quiet the drive to pursue it, and once the identity feels complete, striving relaxes. The authors of a 2020 paper testing the theory found the same pattern with moral behavior: a prosocial act led to less subsequent helpfulness when the act had been socially recognized than when it went unnoticed.

Recognition by others is the hinge of the whole process. A symbolic act settles the identity only when someone sees it. When no one recognizes what you did, you lack a sense of progress because it, in Gollwitzer's words, "did not become a social fact". People keep striving for an identity goal as long as their previous activity remains unrecognized. The drive is not strategic self-presentation. It runs deeper than a wish to impress, since any audience at all matters more than who the audience is.

## In the compendium

The principle on identity disturbances describes fragmentation as uncertainty in one's values, beliefs and preferences. Self-completion theory names the opposite pull: the urge to close gaps in self-definition quickly, with whatever symbols are at hand. The two together map a real tension. A shaky identity invites symbol-gathering, and symbol-gathering can hold the identity together without ever making it solid.

The compendium's principle on challenge and growth takes a different road. It holds that development happens in the gap between what you can do alone and what you can do with help, and it counsels deliberately seeking the task just beyond independent reach. Self-completion theory supplies the cautionary complement. Tokens of an identity are easy to acquire, and acquiring them can still the very striving that would earn the identity itself.

The principle on amor fati points at another escape from the completion treadmill. The wish that nothing be different, in front or behind, loosens the grip on self-images that need constant symbolic maintenance.
