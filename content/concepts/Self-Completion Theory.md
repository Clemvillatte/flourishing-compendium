**Self-Completion Theory** — Wicklund, Gollwitzer — when identity claims are incomplete, people display symbols and seek recognition to complete them.

## What the theory says

The theory was developed by Robert Wicklund and Peter Gollwitzer in the early 1980s. Its exposition here follows Susewind and Walkowitz's 2020 review of it in Frontiers in Psychology. On their account, people conceptualize facets of their own identity as personal goals, and reach for activities that reflect on those goals. Someone who wants to see themselves as a musician, a scientist or a good parent can exhibit behavior that reflects the identity, display material symbols congruent with it, or simply ascribe the characteristics to themselves.

> According to this theory, people conceptualize facets of their own identity as personal goals. People then use a wide array of activities that reflect on their personal goals to build their identity, a process called symbolic self-completion. For instance, people can exhibit behavior that reflects their identity goals in order to achieve a state of self-completion. Alternatively, they can display material symbols congruent with such goals or simply ascribe themselves goal-congruent characteristics. In either case, self-completion theory predicts that successfully enacting symbolic activities leads to fewer subsequent congruent activities, because people derive a sense of progress or goal achievement from successful symbolic activities, which in turn leads to reduced tension and striving for similar goal-congruent activities (Wicklund and Gollwitzer, 1981).
> — Susewind & Walkowitz (2020), Frontiers in Psychology, 11:560188, summarizing Wicklund and Gollwitzer's theory

Two predictions from that account matter here. The first is that enacting symbolic activities can reduce subsequent striving, because people derive a sense of progress from them. The second concerns recognition. A central postulate, quoted by Susewind and Walkowitz, states that people can only derive a sense of goal progress from their symbolic activities when these activities are recognized by others. Essentially, when people are not recognized by others, they lack a sense of progress because what they did "did not become a social fact" (Gollwitzer, 1986, p. 144), quoted in Susewind and Walkowitz (2020). On the same account, people keep striving for an identity goal as long as their previous activity remains unrecognized, and the drive is not strategic self-presentation, since any audience at all matters more than who the audience is.

Susewind and Walkowitz tested the recognition postulate with moral behavior. In their first study, a prosocial act led to less subsequent helpfulness when the act had been socially recognized than when it went without recognition.

## In the compendium

The compendium's principle on challenge and growth counsels deliberately seeking the task just beyond independent reach, in the zone where what you can do alone meets what you can do with help. That is a philosophical counsel rather than a finding, and it can be set beside the theory's prediction. The theory expects that a person who has enacted a symbol of an identity will strive less for it, because the symbol itself supplies the sense of progress. The compendium's counsel pulls against that drift, toward the doing rather than the token.

The self-actualization principle carries Maslow's formulation: a musician must make music, an artist must paint, and what a man can be, he must be. Read beside self-completion theory, the difference is instructive. Maslow centers the enacting of the capacity itself. The theory predicts that symbols of an identity can quiet the striving, because the symbol itself supplies the sense of progress. Both are stated claims rather than rivals in a test, and the compendium keeps them side by side.
