**Self-Completion Theory** — Wicklund, Gollwitzer — when identity claims are incomplete, people display symbols and seek recognition to complete them.

## What the theory says

The theory was developed by Robert Wicklund and Peter Gollwitzer in the early 1980s. Its exposition here follows Susewind and Walkowitz's 2020 review of it in Frontiers in Psychology. On their account, people conceptualize facets of their own identity as personal goals, and reach for activities that reflect on those goals. Someone who wants to see themselves as a musician, a scientist or a good parent can exhibit behavior that reflects the identity, display material symbols congruent with it, or simply ascribe the characteristics to themselves.

> According to this theory, people conceptualize facets of their own identity as personal goals. People then use a wide array of activities that reflect on their personal goals to build their identity, a process called symbolic self-completion. For instance, people can exhibit behavior that reflects their identity goals in order to achieve a state of self-completion. Alternatively, they can display material symbols congruent with such goals or simply ascribe themselves goal-congruent characteristics. In either case, self-completion theory predicts that successfully enacting symbolic activities leads to fewer subsequent congruent activities, because people derive a sense of progress or goal achievement from successful symbolic activities, which in turn leads to reduced tension and striving for similar goal-congruent activities (Wicklund and Gollwitzer, 1981).
> — Susewind & Walkowitz (2020), Frontiers in Psychology, Introduction, "The Importance of Social Recognition" section ([article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7498691/)), summarizing Wicklund and Gollwitzer's theory

Two predictions from that account matter here. The first is that enacting symbolic activities can reduce subsequent striving, because people derive a sense of progress from them. The second concerns recognition. In Susewind and Walkowitz's account of the theory, "One central postulate states that people can only derive a sense of goal progress from their symbolic activities when these activities are recognized by others." Without recognition, the progress does not register, because what was done "did not become a social fact" (Gollwitzer, 1986, p. 144), quoted in Susewind and Walkowitz (2020). On the same account, the striving continues as long as the previous activity remains unrecognized, and the process is described there as having little to do with impression management: the mere presence of any audience matters more than who the audience is ([Susewind & Walkowitz, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7498691/)).

Susewind and Walkowitz tested the recognition postulate with moral behavior. In their first study, a prosocial act led to less subsequent helpfulness when the act had been socially recognized than when it went without recognition ([Susewind & Walkowitz, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7498691/)).

## In the compendium

The principle on challenge and growth recommends deliberately seeking the task just beyond independent reach, in the zone where what you can do alone meets what you can do with help. That is the compendium's recommendation, recorded here as such.

The self-actualization principle carries Maslow's formulation: a musician must make music, an artist must paint, and what a man can be, he must be. Self-completion theory makes a different kind of statement, a prediction about what symbols of an identity do to subsequent striving, tested by Susewind and Walkowitz in the moral domain.
