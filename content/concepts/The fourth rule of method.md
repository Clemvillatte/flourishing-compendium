“The fourth rule of method” — Descartes, Discourse on Method.

In Part II of the Discourse on the Method, René Descartes reduces the many precepts of logic to four rules. The fourth is the one the seed names. This page quotes John Veitch's English translation from the Project Gutenberg text, which was opened.

### The rule

Descartes introduces the set this way:

> "instead of the great number of precepts of which logic is composed, I believed that the four following would prove perfectly sufficient for me, provided I took the firm and unwavering resolution never in a single instance to fail in observing them."

He then lists them. The fourth, which Veitch renders as "the last", reads:

> "And the last, in every case to make enumerations so complete, and reviews so general, that I might be assured that nothing was omitted." ([Descartes, Discourse on Method, Part II, trans. Veitch](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/59))

The other three give it context. The first is "never to accept anything for true which I did not clearly know to be such"; the second divides each difficulty "into as many parts as possible", and the third proceeds from "objects the simplest and easiest to know" by "little and little" to the complex. The fourth is the check that closes the sequence: a complete enumeration of the parts and a general review of the whole, so that nothing is left out.

### What it is for

Descartes ties the rules to his admiration for the long chains of simple reasonings used by geometers. He says they led him to think that all things humans can know "are mutually connected in the same way". The rule is a discipline for avoiding omissions, and it does not say how to decide what counts as complete. Descartes assumes the earlier analysis has produced parts that can be listed.

### Limits of this page

Only Part II was used. This page does not assess whether the rule works in practice. The compendium's links to debiasing and to practice are the compendium's own extensions, and Descartes does not discuss cognitive bias.
