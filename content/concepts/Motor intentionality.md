### Definition

Motor intentionality is the directedness of the lived body toward the world before any thought steps in — the body's grip on a possibility of action rather than a representation of a fact. Merleau-Ponty coined it for the intentionality that unites us with the world through action: a hand reaching for a handle, a stride adjusting to uneven ground, a player losing herself in the game. The movement knows where it is going without needing to think about it. As the Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on Merleau-Ponty puts it, the body relates to space intentionally, but as "an 'I can' rather than an 'I think'":

> The body's relationship with space is therefore intentional, although as an "I can" rather than an "I think"; bodily space is a multi-layered manner of relating to things, so that the body is not "in" space but lives or inhabits it. ([SEP, Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/))

### Origin

The idea is the backbone of Merleau-Ponty's Phenomenology of Perception (1945). He worked it out through pathological cases such as the phantom limb, which he read as the body refusing to let go of its possibilities rather than a mere memory defect. The entry summarises the result: the body's typical mode of existence is "being-toward-the-world", a pre-objective orientation toward a vital situation that is explicable neither by third-person causal interactions nor by explicit judgments or representations ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)). Motor intentionality thus gives perception a practical floor: things show up as reachable, graspable, walkable, before they show up as objects of knowledge.

### Related concepts

- [[Body schema]]: the pre-conscious system of movement and spatial equivalences that carries this intentionality

- [[Situated cognition]]: cognition shaped by the place and task the body is engaged with
- [[Ready-to-hand]]: Heidegger's parallel description of equipment used without thematic awareness
- [[Habit]]: the sedimented "habit body" through which motor intentionality is stored

### A real-life example

A rock climber on an unfamiliar route does not first represent each hold and then decide. Her hand is already moving toward the next edge, her eyes a beat ahead of her fingers, weight shifting before she could state what she is doing. When the flow breaks — a hold crumbles, panic arrives — she discovers how much silent knowledge her movement had been carrying. That unthematic, forward-leaning grip on possibilities is motor intentionality.
