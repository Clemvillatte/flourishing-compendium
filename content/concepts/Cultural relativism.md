Cultural relativism is the observation that customs, values and moral judgments vary across cultures, together with the stance of treating each culture's practices on their own terms rather than as deviations from one's own. Its classical statement is Herodotus's story of Darius in the Histories (3.38), which ends with the Pindaric saying that "use and wont is lord of all". Herodotus draws the descriptive conclusion: "For if it were proposed to all nations to choose which seemed best of all customs, each, after examination made, would place its own first; so well is each persuaded that its own are by far the best."

> "When Darius was king, he summoned the Greeks who were with him and asked them what price would persuade them to eat their fathers' dead bodies. They answered that there was no price for which they would do it. Then he summoned those Indians who are called Callatiae, who eat their parents, and asked them (the Greeks being present and understanding by interpretation what was said) what would make them willing to burn their fathers at death. The Indians cried aloud, that he should not speak of so horrid an act. So firmly rooted are these beliefs; and it is, I think, rightly said in Pindar's poem that use and wont is lord of all." (Herodotus, [Histories 3.38, Godley translation, full PDF](https://dn790000.ca.archive.org/0/items/herodotuswitheng02herouoft/herodotuswitheng02herouoft.pdf))

### Method, not verdict
The story is descriptive: it records what each group finds unthinkable, not what is true. The compendium's use of the concept turns on that gap. Cultural relativism as a research stance treats each culture on its own terms; as a moral verdict it would treat all practices as equally good, which is a different claim and one the compendium rejects (SO016).
