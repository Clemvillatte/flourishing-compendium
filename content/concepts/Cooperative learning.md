Cooperative learning is the classroom application of social interdependence theory, the research program David Johnson and Roger Johnson built on foundations from Kurt Lewin and Morton Deutsch. Their definition:

> "Cooperative learning is the instructional use of small groups so that students work together to maximize their own and each other's learning." — Johnson, Johnson and Holubec, quoted in Johnson and Johnson 2014 ([link](https://revistas.um.es/analesps/article/view/analesps.30.3.201241))

The premise of the underlying theory is that "the type of social interdependence structured in a situation determines how individuals interact with each other that, in turn, largely determines outcomes" ([Johnson and Johnson 2014](https://revistas.um.es/analesps/article/view/analesps.30.3.201241)). The research base behind it is large: "Over 685 studies have been conducted over the past 195 years to give an answer to the question of how successful competitive, individualistic, and cooperative efforts are in promoting productivity and achievement" ([Johnson and Johnson 2014](https://revistas.um.es/analesps/article/view/analesps.30.3.201241)).

The approach is structural rather than just grouping:

> "Simply placing individuals in groups and telling them to work together does not in and of itself result in cooperative efforts." — Johnson and Johnson 2014 ([link](https://revistas.um.es/analesps/article/view/analesps.30.3.201241))

The conditions for cooperation, in the Johnsons' phrasing, are that it "will only develop under a certain set of conditions: positive interdependence, individual accountability, promotive interaction, social skills, and group processing" ([Johnson and Johnson 2014](https://revistas.um.es/analesps/article/view/analesps.30.3.201241)). In order to build and maintain cooperative effects, the Johnsons add, "these five essential elements must be carefully structured into the situation" ([Johnson and Johnson 2014](https://revistas.um.es/analesps/article/view/analesps.30.3.201241)).
