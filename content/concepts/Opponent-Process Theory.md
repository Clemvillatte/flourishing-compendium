Solomon and Corbit's 1974 abstract describes the theory as one that "assumes that many hedonic, affective, or emotional states are automatically opposed by central nervous system mechanisms which reduce the intensity of hedonic feelings, both pleasant and aversive. The opponent processes for most hedonic states are strengthened by use and are weakened by disuse" ([Solomon and Corbit, An opponent-process theory of motivation, Psychological Review 81(2), 119-145, abstract on SciSpace](https://scispace.com/papers/an-opponent-process-theory-of-motivation-i-temporal-dynamics-516bk2bcfj)).

### The a-process and b-process

In Solomon's 1980 account, the primary process (a) "in turn arouses a b process that functions to oppose and suppress the affective or hedonic state initially generated by the onset of the a process". The b process is "of sluggish latency, inertial, or slow to build to its asymptote, and slow to decay". When the stimulus ends, affect falls past baseline and "a new, contrasting affective state (State B) emerges", which then slowly returns to baseline ([Solomon, The Opponent-Process Theory of Acquired Motivation, 1980, PDF; file title truncated](https://www.appstate.edu/~steelekm/classes/psy5300/Documents/Solomon1980_Opponent.pdf)).

### What repetition does

Solomon's third postulate: "The primary hedonic process aroused by onset of a reinforcer remains unchanged when the reinforcer is repeatedly presented. In contrast, the opponent hedonic process is strengthened by use and weakened by disuse." The fourth adds a condition: strengthening occurs only if the opponent process is aroused again "at inter-reinforcer time intervals less than the time interval required to allow the opponent process to decay to some near-neutral baseline value" (same PDF). The seed's "dominate" is looser than this. The source says the opponent process grows and the primary one stays the same, so the net feeling shifts as b grows.

### Use in addiction research

A PMC review on addiction applies the account to drugs: the a-process is a positive hedonic response that "shows tolerance", while the b-process "appears after the a-process has terminated, consists of negative hedonic responses... and gets larger with repeated exposure" ([Neurobiological Substrates for the Dark Side of Compulsivity in Addiction, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2637927/)).

### Tests in humans

A Motivation and Emotion study tested three hypotheses in humans: an opposite emotional response after removal of charged stimuli, that this response reacts to the initial affect, and that co-occurring opponent processes cancel. It reports that when they cooccur "they appear to interact and cancel" ([Opponent processes in human emotions?, Motivation and Emotion](http://link.springer.com/10.1007/BF00992358)). I did not open the full results for the first two hypotheses.
