Suler's 2004 article opens: "While online, some people self-disclose or act out more frequently or intensely than they would in person." He names six factors "that interact with each other in creating this online disinhibition effect: dissociative anonymity, invisibility, asynchronicity, solipsistic introjection, dissociative imagination, and minimization of authority", and adds that "personality variables also will influence the extent of this disinhibition" ([Suler, The Online Disinhibition Effect, CyberPsychology and Behavior 2004, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15257832/)). The seed lists three of the six factors, so it is partial.

### Two directions

Suler writes that disinhibition "can work in two seemingly opposing directions". Benign disinhibition covers sharing "very personal things", revealing "secret emotions, fears, wishes", and "unusual acts of kindness and generosity". Toxic disinhibition covers "rude language, harsh criticisms, anger, hatred, even threats", and visits to "the dark underworld of the Internet" ([Suler, full article PDF](https://johnsuler.com/article_pdfs/online_dis_effect.pdf)). The factors usually "intersect and interact with each other, supplement each other, resulting in a more complex, amplified effect".

### The three factors in the seed

On anonymity: "When people have the opportunity to separate their actions online from their in-person lifestyle and identity, they feel less vulnerable about self-disclosing and acting out." On asynchronicity: people "don't interact with each other in real time", and "not having to cope with someone's immediate reaction disinhibits people" (Suler PDF). Invisibility is the case where, in text-driven environments, people cannot see each other.

### Not a revealed true self

Suler's abstract rejects one common reading: "Rather than thinking of disinhibition as the revealing of an underlying 'true self,' we can conceptualize it as a shift to a constellation within self-structure, involving clusters of affect and cognition that differ from the in-person constellation" (PubMed).

### Later research

A study of young gamers notes that "all six dimensions ... appear to contribute to negative behavior online", that benign disinhibition "remains less studied", and that anonymity appears to lead to more positive self-disclosure ([Meriläinen and Ruotsalainen, Social Media + Society, 2024](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/20563051241274669)). A thesis summarising Lapidot-Lefler and Barak reports that unidentifiability was the strongest source of both benign and toxic disinhibition, while anonymity should not be called the most substantial contributor ([DiVA thesis, PDF](https://www.diva-portal.org/smash/get/diva2:944023/FULLTEXT01.pdf)).
