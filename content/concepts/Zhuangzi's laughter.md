“Zhuangzi's laughter” — Daoism, Zhuangzi.

Zhuangzi's laughter is the Daoist art of taking the world lightly: laughing at grandiosity from below, at oneself, and at the brevity that makes laughter precious. The book's first image is small creatures laughing at a great bird's flight (a cicada and a dove, in chapter 1, find the idea of rising ninety thousand li absurd), and the laughter is affectionate but also a lesson: the cicada cannot imagine the sky the bird knows. In the Autumn Floods, the River Spirit laughs for joy at his fullness, then falls silent at the sight of the ocean. Laughter in the Zhuangzi marks the edges of what a perspective can hold.

> "Man's greatest age is one hundred years. A medium old age is eighty years. The lowest estimate is sixty years. Take away from this the hours of sickness, disease, death, mourning, sorrow, and trouble, and there will not remain more than four or five days a month upon which a man may open his mouth to laugh." (Zhuangzi, ch. 29, Robber Chih, public-domain translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/59709/pg59709.txt))

### What the laughter is for

The counting is the point: laughter is rare because life is short and mostly occupied, and a practice that adds laughing days is a practice of time well spent. The chapter carrying this speech is marked spurious in this edition, and its speaker is a bandit scolding Confucius; the compendium borrows the arithmetic and leaves the abuse behind. The compendium's principle SE033 carries the application: hold your imperfections with humour, because lightness is a virtue and the alternative costs days.
