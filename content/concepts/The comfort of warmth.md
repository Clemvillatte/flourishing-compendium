In the Essays Montaigne several times notes what warmth does for him, and this page carries the sense its citing principle uses: his own remarks about warmth. In the essay on the resemblance of children to their fathers he explains why medical orders cost him so little when they suit his nature.

> "I give them leave to command me to keep myself warm, because I naturally love to do it, and to appoint leeks or lettuce for my broth" (Montaigne, Essays II.37, "Of the Resemblance of Children to their Fathers", Cotton translation, [Project Gutenberg #3600](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3600/3600-0.txt))

### What he says about warmth

In "Of Experience" he recalls the German stoves of Augsburg and defends their heat against French hearths: the stove's heat is "always equal, constant, and universal, without flame, without smoke". He adds that "All heat that comes from the fire weakens and dulls me", and reports Evenus's saying that fire was the best condiment of life before choosing any other way of making himself warm. These are reports of his own constitution; the Essays offers them as self-observation.
