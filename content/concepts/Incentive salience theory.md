Incentive salience theory, developed by Kent Berridge and Terry Robinson, separates two parts of reward. A review by Berridge and Robinson puts it: "the brain circuitry that mediates the psychological process of 'wanting' a particular reward is dissociable from circuitry that mediates the degree to which it is 'liked'". Incentive salience, or "wanting", "is generated by large and robust neural systems that include mesolimbic dopamine", while "liking", "the actual pleasurable impact of reward consumption, is mediated by smaller and fragile neural systems, and is not dependent on dopamine" ([Berridge and Robinson, 2016, American Psychologist, PDF](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/berridge-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/743/2019/10/2016-Berridge-Robinson-Liking-wanting-IS-theory-of-addiction-Am-Psychol.pdf)).

### Wanting is cue-triggered

A chapter from the Robinson lab describes incentive salience as "a specific form of Pavlovian-related motivation for rewards". Cues linked with a reward become "especially attractive and wanted", in the 2001 account by Robinson and Berridge, which describes stimuli that "grab the attention" ([Robinson and Berridge, 2001, University of Michigan PDF](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/berridge-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/743/2019/10/RobinsonBerridgeAddictionshort2001.pdf)). Wanting "typically coheres with 'liking'" for the same reward, but the two "can be dissociated" ([Incentive Salience in Addiction and Over-Consumption, Wesleyan PDF](https://robinsonlab.research.wesleyan.edu/files/2014/01/Robinson-2014.pdf)).

### The addiction claim

Berridge and Robinson's incentive-sensitization theory holds that the essence of addiction is "excessive amplification specifically of psychological 'wanting', especially triggered by cues, without necessarily an amplification of 'liking'", caused by long-lasting "neural sensitization" in susceptible people. In their words, "liking" "need not increase with sensitization, and may even decrease", and sensitized wanting "can persist for years, even if the person cognitively does not want to take drugs, does not expect the drugs to be very pleasant" (2016 PDF above).

A review of pleasure research describes the outcome: "the addict may come to 'want' what is neither 'liked' nor expected to be liked", possible because wanting mechanisms are "largely subcortical and separable from cortically mediated declarative expectation and conscious planning" ([The Neuroscience of Happiness and Pleasure, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3008658/)).

### A qualification on dopamine

The seed note calls wanting "dopamine-driven". The chapter summary adds nuance: "'wanting' can be generated by dopamine enhancements as well as by opioid enhancements in several broadly defined regions throughout mesocorticolimbic circuitry" ([Cambridge Handbook chapter summary](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-substance-and-behavioral-addictions/sensitization-of-incentive-salience-and-the-transition-to-addiction/E904DBA2BA0DE15831A17D6A11F928F7)).

### Status

An Annual Review of Psychology article evaluates the theory "30 years on", comparing it with opponent-process, habit and prefrontal-dysfunction theories of addiction ([Annual Reviews](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-psych-011624-024031?crawler=true&mimetype=application/pdf)). I opened only its opening summary, so I do not report its verdict here.
