Habit maintenance is the question of what keeps a habit going once it has formed. The short answer from the habit-formation literature is that a well-formed habit mostly runs on its cues and not on motivation, and the evidence on how long that takes to build, and what happens after, is thinner than the popular numbers suggest.

### What the Lally study found

Lally and colleagues asked 96 volunteers to choose an eating, drinking or activity behaviour and do it daily in the same context, such as "after breakfast", for 12 weeks. Participants rated how automatic it felt each day. Time to reach 95% of each person's asymptote of automaticity "ranged from 18 to 254 days; indicating considerable variation in how long it takes people to reach their limit of automaticity and highlighting that it can take a very long time" ([Lally et al., 2010, European Journal of Social Psychology 40(6): 998-1009](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674)). One result matters for maintenance: "Missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit formation process" (same source).

A limit worth stating: the study ran 84 days and 82 participants gave enough data, the model fitted 62 of them, and only 39 of those fits were good. It followed habit formation, not what happened months after the habit was established, so it cannot by itself say how long a habit lasts.

### Why habits persist

Gardner, Lally and Wardle give the mechanism. In their words, "Mere repetition of a simple action in a consistent context leads, through associative learning, to the action being activated upon subsequent exposure to those contextual cues." Once the action is handed to external cues, "dependence on conscious attention or motivational processes is reduced", and "habits are likely to persist even after conscious motivation or interest dissipates" ([Gardner, Lally & Wardle, 2012, British Journal of General Practice](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3505409/)). They also note the common "66 days" figure is an average: "Automaticity plateaued on average around 66 days after the first daily performance", with wide variation. They warn that variation in behaviours and settings is "incompatible with development of automaticity", so maintenance advice that stresses variety to keep things interesting can work against the habit.

### What this suggests for practice

These points follow from the sources above, and the wording is the compendium's, not the authors':

- Keep the cue and the context steady, because the cue is doing the work.
- Do not treat one missed day as a collapse; Gardner and colleagues report that automaticity gains soon resumed after one missed performance.
- Expect a long build for complex behaviours, and do not hold to 21 or 66 days as a promise.
- The studies here do not test long-term follow-up, so extra monitoring and adaptation, as the maintenance principles suggest, rest on those principles' own sources.
