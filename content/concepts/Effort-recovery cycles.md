The effort-recovery idea is that work draws on a person, and that time away from work lets that drain ease. Sonnentag and Fritz (2007) built four short self-report scales for how people "unwind and recuperate from work during leisure time". In a sample of 930 people, they showed that "four recovery experiences can be differentiated: psychological detachment from work, relaxation, mastery, and control."

In a further subsample of 271, the recovery experiences had "moderate relations ... with measures of job stressors and psychological well-being", while "relations with coping and personality variables were generally low" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17638488/)).

### What the evidence covers

The study is about measurement: it shows that these four kinds of recovery can be told apart and that they relate moderately to stressors and well-being. The abstract does not state that well-being depends on alternating strain with recovery, or that continuous output harms it. That wider claim is a reasonable reading of the effort-recovery literature, and a reader who needs it should look to studies designed to test it.

The scales were developed for use "in longitudinal and diary research", so the authors themselves present them as tools for later studies.
