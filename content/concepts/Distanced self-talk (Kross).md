Distanced self-talk means referring to yourself by name or with non-first-person pronouns while you reflect on something stressful. The two studies named in the seed support the core idea, with different kinds of evidence. The claim about self-control is the weaker half: Moser and colleagues phrase it as a possibility, and the evidence they report is neural.

### Kross et al. (2014): seven studies on social stress

Seven studies (total N = 585) tested the language people use for the self during introspection.

> "They demonstrated that using non-first-person pronouns and one's own name (rather than first-person pronouns) during introspection enhances self-distancing." (Kross et al. 2014, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 106:304-324, [PubMed 24467424](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24467424/))

In the impression-making and public-speaking studies, the non-first-person group performed better according to objective raters, showed less distress, and engaged in less maladaptive postevent processing. They also appraised future stressors "in more challenging and less threatening terms". A meta-analysis in the paper found none of the effects were moderated by trait social anxiety.

### Moser et al. (2017): what happens in the brain

Moser and colleagues asked whether third-person self-talk is "a relatively effortless form of self control".

> "Study 1 demonstrated that third-person self-talk reduced an ERP marker of self-referential emotional reactivity (i.e., late positive potential) within the first second of viewing aversive images without enhancing an ERP marker of cognitive control (i.e., stimulus preceding negativity)." (Moser et al. 2017, Scientific Reports, [PubMed 28674404](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28674404/))

A second study using fMRI found reduced medial prefrontal activation, a marker of self-referential processing, when people reflected on negative memories, again without raised activity in control-related regions. The authors conclude that third-person self-talk "may constitute a relatively effortless form of self-control". The word "may" and the use of brain markers, not behavioural outcomes of self-control, are the limits to keep in mind.

### Related finding on wise reasoning

Grossmann and Kross (2014) report that across three experiments (N = 693), people reasoned more wisely about another person's problems than about their own, and that self-distancing eliminated this asymmetry. ([Psychological Science, PubMed 24916084](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24916084/)) This is why the compendium links the concept to wise reasoning, but it is a separate study from the two the seed names.
