Latent learning is the finding, from Edward C. Tolman's laboratory, that animals learn the layout of an environment without any reward for doing so, and reveal that knowledge only once it pays. Tolman's 1948 Psychological Review paper "Cognitive Maps in Rats and Men" made the case: "We believe that in the course of learning something like a field map of the environment gets established in the rat's brain" ([Tolman 1948, p. 192](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Tolman/Maps/maps.htm)).

### Learning without reward

The term credits Tolman's predecessor: "This learning, which did not manifest itself until after the food had been introduced, Blodgett called 'latent learning'" ([Tolman 1948, p. 194](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Tolman/Maps/maps.htm)). Rats that ran a maze unrewarded for days suddenly matched rewarded rats once food appeared, showing that "during the non-rewarded trials these animals had been learning much more than they had exhibited" ([p. 194](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Tolman/Maps/maps.htm)). The results argued against the strict behaviorist view that learning requires reinforcement. Tolman's broader conclusion was that responses are guided by the map, not by stimulus-response bonds: "it is this tentative map, indicating routes and paths and environmental relationships, which finally determines what responses, if any, the animal will finally release" ([p. 192](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Tolman/Maps/maps.htm)).
