Latent learning is the finding from Edward C. Tolman's maze experiments that rats can learn a maze's layout without any reward for doing so, and reveal that knowledge only once food is introduced. Tolman's 1948 Psychological Review paper "Cognitive Maps in Rats and Men" made the case:

> "We believe that in the course of learning something like a field map of the environment gets established in the rat's brain." ([Tolman 1948, p. 192](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Tolman/Maps/maps.htm))

### Learning without reward

Tolman credits Blodgett with the term: "This learning, which did not manifest itself until after the food had been introduced, Blodgett called 'latent learning'" ([p. 194](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Tolman/Maps/maps.htm)). Rats that ran a maze unrewarded for days suddenly matched rewarded rats once food appeared, showing that "during the non-rewarded trials these animals had been learning much more than they had exhibited" ([p. 194](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Tolman/Maps/maps.htm)). The results argued against the strict behaviorist view that learning requires reinforcement. Responses, on Tolman's account, are guided by the map: "it is this tentative map, indicating routes and paths and environmental relationships, which finally determines what responses, if any, the animal will finally release" ([p. 192](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Tolman/Maps/maps.htm)).
