Fredrickson and Roberts (1997) offer the theory "as a framework for understanding the experiential consequences of being female in a culture that sexually objectifies the female body". It "posits that girls and women are typically acculturated to internalize an observer's perspective as a primary view of their physical selves. This perspective on self can lead to habitual body monitoring, which, in turn, can increase women's opportunities for shame and anxiety, reduce opportunities for peak motivational states, and diminish awareness of internal bodily states" ([Fredrickson and Roberts, Objectification Theory, Psychology of Women Quarterly 21(2), 173-206, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1471-6402.1997.tb00108.x)).

### Mental health risks

The abstract says such accumulated experiences "may help account for an array of mental health risks that disproportionately affect women: unipolar depression, sexual dysfunction, and eating disorders" and that the theory "illuminates why changes in these mental health risks appear to occur in step with life-course changes in the female body".

### Reading the seed note

The seed's "flow disruption" is a gloss: the abstract speaks of fewer opportunities for "peak motivational states", and I did not open the full text to check whether it uses the word flow. The abstract also uses "may" and "can", so the seed's "leads to" is stronger than the authors' hedged phrasing.

### Later research

A decade review of the field says the 1997 paper proposed an "integrative framework for understanding how women's socialization and experiences of sexual objectification are translated into mental health problems", and reviews research on self-objectification, sexual objectification experiences as a proposed precursor, and disconnection from bodily functions ([Objectification theory and psychology of women: A decade of advances, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1471-6402.2008.00452.x)). A study in gay, lesbian and heterosexual samples found "mixed evidence for proposed pathways" ([Tests of Objectification Theory in Gay, Lesbian, and Heterosexual Community Samples, Sex Roles](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11199-011-9958-8)). Another study extending the theory found that coping through internalization and self-blame, and self-objectification, each mediated the link between sexual objectification experiences and distress, while internalization of cultural beauty standards did not ([Springer, Experiencing and Coping with Sexually Objectifying Treatment](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11199-014-0392-6)).
