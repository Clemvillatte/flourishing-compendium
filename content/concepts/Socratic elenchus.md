In Plato's Meno, Socrates tests Meno's answers about virtue through further questions. The exchange leaves Meno unable to say what virtue is.

> "For my soul and my tongue are really torpid, and I do not know how to answer you; and though I have been delivered of an infinite variety of speeches about virtue before now, and to many persons-and very good ones they were, as I thought-at this moment I cannot even say what virtue is." (Meno, [80b, trans. Jowett](http://classics.mit.edu/Plato/meno.html))

### How the refutation runs

Meno offers a definition: "Virtue is the desire of things honourable and the power of attaining them" ([77b](http://classics.mit.edu/Plato/meno.html)). Socrates first tests the desire half: do any desire evil? Those who desire evil believe it good, and "if there is no one who desires to be miserable, there is no one, Meno, who desires evil" ([78b](http://classics.mit.edu/Plato/meno.html)). Since the desire of good is common to all, it cannot distinguish the virtuous, so the definition shrinks to the power of attaining.

Socrates then tests that half with the case of getting gold unjustly, and proposes the requirement that "justice or temperance or holiness, or some other part of virtue... must accompany the acquisition", with which Meno agrees ([78d](http://classics.mit.edu/Plato/meno.html)). The definition fails on the dialogue's own stated ground: Socrates had asked for virtue "whole and unbroken", and Meno's answer defines it "with a part of virtue", "as though you had told me and I must already know the whole of virtue" ([79a](http://classics.mit.edu/Plato/meno.html)). Meno, numb like the man stung by the torpedo fish, compares Socrates to it: "you seem to me both in your appearance and in your power over others to be very like the flat torpedo fish, who torpifies those who come near him and touch him" ([80a](http://classics.mit.edu/Plato/meno.html)).
