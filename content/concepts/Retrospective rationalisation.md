Retrospective rationalisation is what Nietzsche catches historians and moralists doing: reading a thing's current use back into its origin, then treating that invented origin as the thing's essence. The Genealogy of Morals states the principle in the second essay's treatment of punishment:

> The origin of the existence of a thing and its final utility, its practical application and incorporation in a system of ends, are toto coelo opposed to each other—everything, anything, which exists and which prevails anywhere, will always be put to new purposes by a force superior to itself, will be commandeered afresh, will be turned and transformed to new uses.

(Nietzsche, On the Genealogy of Morals, Essay II, §12, Samuel translation, [Project Gutenberg 52319](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/52319/52319-h/52319-h.htm))

The same passage gives the psychological engine: understanding the final utility of a thing does not for a minute imply any comprehension of its origin, yet "it has been the immemorial belief that understanding the final cause or the utility of a thing, a form, an institution, means also understanding the reason for its origin." The eye was made to see, the hand was made to grasp, so punishment must have been invented for punishing. Nietzsche's answer is that all ends and utilities are only signs that a will to power has imposed a meaning on a weaker force, so the history of a thing is a continuous sign-chain of new interpretations whose causes need have no mutual connection.

The companion passage is the lightning-doer argument, Essay II, §13: "there is no 'being' behind doing, working, becoming; 'the doer' is a mere appanage to the action. The action is everything." The strong man, Nietzsche says there, will not separate the lightning from its light, and the retroactive invention of a doer behind the deed is the same rationalising move at the level of persons.

The compendium pairs this with the psychology of [[CO024 — Recognise hindsight bias — I knew it all along.]]: Fischhoff's judges, knowing the outcome, overestimated what they would have known without it, and were largely unaware of the effect the outcome knowledge had. Nietzsche's historian and Fischhoff's judge make the same error in opposite registers, one about institutions, the other about events.
