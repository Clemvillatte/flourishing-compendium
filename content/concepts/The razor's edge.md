The razor's edge is the Katha Upanishad's image for the path to the Self: so narrow and sharp that walking it demands complete attention. The verse sits in the dialogue between the boy Nachiketas and Yama, death, and its warning is spoken to someone who has already chosen the harder boon:

> "'Rise, awake! having obtained your boons, understand them! The sharp edge of a razor is difficult to pass over; thus the wise say the path (to the Self) is hard.'" (Katha Upanishad 1.3.14, trans. Max Müller, [Sacred Books of the East 15](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/sbe15/sbe15012.htm))

### What the verse carries

The verse pairs a summons with a warning. The summons comes first, "Rise, awake!", and the warning follows immediately: the difficulty is stated plainly, not as a deterrent but as honest information from a teacher who knows the terrain. The next verse names what the path leads to: "He who has perceived that which is without sound, without touch, without form, without decay, without taste, eternal, without smell, without beginning, without end, beyond the Great, and unchangeable, is freed from the jaws of death." The razor image works on two levels at once: the path is narrow, allowing no divided attention, and it is sharp, cutting away what is carried onto it. My principle SP088 carries the same expectation forward: the excellent is difficult and rare, and a practice that pretends otherwise misprices it. What the verse does not claim: that difficulty is valuable in itself; the image states a fact about the path, not a virtue of suffering.
