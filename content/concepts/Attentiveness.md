Attentiveness is the deliberate practice of receiving what is there rather than projecting onto it. Its two classic sources agree on the receiving. Weil calls attention a suspension of the will to interpret, and Noddings names the carer's stance with a word she borrows for it.

> "Attention consists of suspending our thought, leaving it detached, empty, and ready to be penetrated by the object." (Simone Weil, "Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies", in Waiting for God, trans. Emma Craufurd, [Matheson Trust text](https://www.themathesontrust.org/papers/christianity/Weil-Reflections.pdf))

> "The one-caring (or carer) is first of all attentive. This attention, which I called 'engrossment' in Caring (Noddings, 1984), is receptive; it receives what the cared-for is feeling and trying to express. It is not merely diagnostic, measuring the cared-for against some pre-established ideal." (Nel Noddings, [Caring in Education](https://www.uvm.edu/~rgriffin/NoddingsCaring.pdf))

### Why it is a practice, not a mood

Weil's essay makes attention trainable: she assigns school exercises, "each one", on the idea that it will "help to form in them the habit of that attention which is the substance of prayer". Her hardest claim gives the page its ethical weight: "The capacity to give one's attention to a sufferer is a very rare and difficult thing; it is almost a miracle; it is a miracle" (same text). I treat attentiveness as the substrate of both wisdom and care, since seeing what is actually there precedes every response that fits.
