Theoria is contemplation — for Aristotle the highest human activity, the exercise of intellect on what is noble and divine, and in that exercise the closest a human life comes to the life of God. The Greek word covers seeing, spectating and knowing; Aristotle's use of it for the best activity makes knowing the highest form of seeing.

His clinching argument in the Nicomachean Ethics strips the concept down:

> "Now if you take away from a living being action, and still more production, what is left but contemplation? Therefore the activity of God, which surpasses all others in blessedness, must be contemplative; and of human activities, therefore, that which is most akin to this must be most of the nature of happiness."
> — [Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.8, trans. W. D. Ross, aristotle-ethics.com](https://www.aristotle-ethics.com/book/book-10/chapter/chapter-8/paragraph/paragraph-2-the-contemplative-nature-of-supreme-happiness)

The reasoning is by subtraction. A god does not make contracts, run risks or resist temptation; those belong to creatures with needs. Take away action and production from a living being and what remains is contemplation — so the divine life is a contemplative one, and the human activity most akin to it is the most happy.

### What it is not

Theoria is not inquiry. It is the having of understanding, not the search for it: the activity of one who already knows, enjoying what is known. It is also not a replacement for practical life. Aristotle is explicit that moral virtue and the practical wisdom that governs action remain necessary for a complete human life; contemplation is the completion of practical life, not an exemption from it. The contemplative life is highest because intellect is the best thing in us, but the political and moral life is still a life of virtue — a second-best happiness rather than none.
