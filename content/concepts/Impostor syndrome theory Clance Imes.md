Pauline Rose Clance and Suzanne Imes introduced the term in 1978 in "The Imposter Phenomenon in High Achieving Women: Dynamics and Therapeutic Intervention" (Psychotherapy: Theory, Research and Practice 15(3)). They used it "to designate an internal experience of intellectual phoniness", found "particularly prevalent and intense among a select sample of high achieving women". Despite outstanding accomplishments, such women "persist in believing that they are really not bright and have fooled anyone who thinks otherwise" ([Clance and Imes, 1978, author-hosted PDF](https://paulineroseclance.com/pdf/ip_high_achieving_women.pdf)). Their own title says "phenomenon", not "syndrome", and they state that women who show it "do not fall into any one diagnostic category".

### What the authors observed

Their sample was clinical and classroom contact over five years with "over 150 highly successful women", "primarily white middle- to upper-class women between the ages of 20 and 45". About a third were therapy clients with other presenting problems; the rest were in groups or classes taught by the authors. They wrote that in their clinical experience the phenomenon "occurs with much less frequency in men", and added: "This clinical observation needs to be researched."

### Origins and the maintaining cycle

They describe two early family patterns: one child designated the "sensitive" sibling while another is the "intelligent" one, and one child told she is superior "in every way", who later doubts her parents' praise. In the cycle that keeps the belief going, "worry about intelligence -- hard work and cover-up strategies -- good grades or performance -- approval and temporary good feelings, is reinforcing", yet "the success is an empty one... because the underlying sense of phoniness remains untouched". Two other maintaining behaviours are "intellectual inauthenticity" (giving superiors what they want to hear) and using charm to win approval, after which any praise is discounted.

### What later reviews found

A systematic review of 62 studies and 14,161 participants reports prevalence "from 9 to 82% largely depending on the screening tool and cutoff used", with impostor syndrome "common among both men and women". It is "often comorbid with depression and anxiety", and the authors conclude "no published studies evaluated treatments for this condition" ([Bravata et al., 2020, Journal of General Internal Medicine, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31848865/)). Sixteen of 33 gender comparisons found women reporting significantly higher rates ([same review, Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11606-019-05364-1)).

### For the essays

The original claim was a clinical observation in a specific sample, and later work has widened it beyond women and beyond diagnosis. The seed phrase "luck or deception" is a simplification: the 1978 paper also lists hard work, charm and other explanations that the person does not count as intelligence.
