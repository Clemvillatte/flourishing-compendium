Sustainability of well-being gains is the question of whether boosts in happiness from intentional effort can outlast the initial lift, and what makes them last. Sonja Lyubomirsky, Kennon Sheldon and David Schkade framed the question in 2005 with a model that splits the influences on a person's chronic happiness into three factors: "a genetically determined set point for happiness, happiness-relevant circumstantial factors, and happiness-relevant activities and practices" ([Lyubomirsky, Sheldon and Schkade 2005](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Pursuing_Happiness-_The_Architecture_of_Sustainable_Change.pdf), Review of General Psychology 9(2)).

> "changing one's intentional activities may provide a happiness-boosting potential that is at least as large as, and probably much larger than, changing one's circumstances." (Lyubomirsky, Sheldon and Schkade, [Pursuing happiness: the architecture of sustainable change](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Pursuing_Happiness-_The_Architecture_of_Sustainable_Change.pdf), Review of General Psychology 9(2):111-131, 2005)

### Why activity is the candidate

The paper's summary of the evidence at the time gave "genetics account for approximately 50% of the population variation" and "circumstances account for approximately 10%", leaving "as much as 40% of the variance for intentional activity". The authors describe activities as "by definition, episodic and transient", which suggests "individuals may adapt less readily to new activities than to new circumstances". The episodic nature of activity also means timing and variety matter: the paper's examples include counting one's blessings after a difficult period rather than by rote every day.

### The honest limit

The 50/10/40 split is a cross-sectional estimate the authors drew from past twin and survey research, not a demonstration that 40% of any individual's happiness is controllable, and the interventions they discussed were, in their own words, "2 preliminary happiness-increasing interventions".

### How gains erode

The same research group later specified the mechanism of erosion in the hedonic adaptation prevention model ([Sheldon, Boehm and Lyubomirsky 2013](https://sonjalyubomirsky.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/Sheldon-Boehm-Lyubomirsky-2012.pdf), Oxford Handbook of Happiness). The model "specifies two major routes extending away from the positive events and toward final well-being". The emotions route is bottom-up: initial boosts are maintained "to the extent that there have been more discrete positive emotions experienced between Time 2 and Time 3". The aspirations route is top-down: "when things are going well, one starts to take them for granted and starts assuming that they will always be there", and "the more one's aspirations and expectancies increase, the less the resulting Time 3 well-being". The chapter's moderator discussion gives variety the central role: "variety appears as a moderator in three different places within the model (moderating the positive events to aspiration level link, the number of positive events to positive emotions link, and the number of positive emotions to sustained well-being link), endowing it with a special role for reducing hedonic adaptation and increasing the durability of happiness changes."
