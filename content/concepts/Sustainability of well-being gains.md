Sustainability of well-being gains is the question of whether boosts in happiness from intentional effort can outlast the initial lift, and what makes them last. Sonja Lyubomirsky, Kennon Sheldon and David Schkade framed the question in 2005 with a model that splits the influences on a person's chronic happiness into three factors: "a genetically determined set point for happiness, happiness-relevant circumstantial factors, and happiness-relevant activities and practices".

> "changing one's intentional activities may provide a happiness-boosting potential that is at least as large as, and probably much larger than, changing one's circumstances." (Lyubomirsky, Sheldon and Schkade, [Pursuing happiness: the architecture of sustainable change](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Pursuing_Happiness-_The_Architecture_of_Sustainable_Change.pdf), Review of General Psychology 9(2):111-131, 2005)

### Why activity is the candidate

The paper's summary of the evidence at the time gave "genetics account for approximately 50% of the population variation" and "circumstances account for approximately 10%", leaving "as much as 40% of the variance for intentional activity". Circumstances are one-off changes that people adapt to; activities are different in kind. In the authors' words, "activities are, by definition, episodic and transient", which suggests "individuals may adapt less readily to new activities than to new circumstances". The episodic nature of activity also means timing and variety matter: the paper's examples include counting one's blessings after a difficult period rather than by rote every day.

### The honest limit

The 50/10/40 split is a cross-sectional estimate the authors drew from past twin and survey research, not a demonstration that 40% of any individual's happiness is controllable, and the interventions they discussed were, in their own words, "2 preliminary happiness-increasing interventions".
