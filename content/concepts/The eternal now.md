The eternal now is the *nunc stans*, the standing present, which Boethius defines in Consolation of Philosophy Book V:

> "Eternity, then, is the complete, simultaneous and perfect possession of everlasting life; this will be clear from a comparison with creatures that exist in time... for it is one thing to progress like the world in Plato's theory through everlasting life, and another thing to have embraced the whole of everlasting life in one simultaneous present."

(Boethius, *Consolation*, V.VI.; [SEP, Eternity in Christian Thought](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/eternity/).)

### The definition at work

The Latin is *interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio* — "the complete possession all at once of illimitable life" ([Muslim Philosophy, Eternity](https://www.muslimphilosophy.com/ip/rep/K017.htm)). The contrast with time is built into the comparison: whatever lives in time "grasps not yet tomorrow, while it has already lost yesterday; nay, even in the life of to-day ye live no longer than one brief transitory moment" ([Project Gutenberg, Consolation V.VI](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/14328/14328-h/14328-h.htm)). The world, even if it never began or ended, would be everlasting rather than eternal, because its life is stretched out in succession instead of possessed whole.

### The practical reading

Spinoza later borrows the structure for the *sub specie aeternitatis* view, and the contemplative traditions use the same wedge from the other side: the present moment is the only part of time a creature actually inhabits. Boethius's own conclusion is theological — the distinction lets divine knowledge see all of time at once without causing it — but the definition has outlived the problem it was built for. As SEP summarizes the doctrine: "God does not exist in time at all. He is apart from his creation, transcendent over it" ([SEP, Eternity](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2014/entries/eternity/)).
