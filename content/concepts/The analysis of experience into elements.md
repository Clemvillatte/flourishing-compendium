The analysis of experience into elements is the practice of taking lived experience apart into components so that each can be examined. Husserl's Ideas I gives phenomenology's version: consciousness is analyzed into intentional acts, and the analysis asks what an act is directed at and how. The entry presents this as a discipline with results rather than a private mood: its first key observation is that every act of consciousness is a consciousness of something.

> "Husserl defined phenomenology as 'the science of the essence of consciousness', centered on the defining trait of intentionality, approached explicitly 'in the first person'. (See Husserl, Ideas I, §§33ff.)" (Smith, "Phenomenology", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, section "The History and Varieties of Phenomenology")

### What the analysis yields

Smith's entry describes the first key result: "each act of consciousness is a consciousness of something, that is, intentional, or directed toward something". The observation fixes the unit of analysis: not experience in bulk, but individual acts, each with a direction that can be examined. The Buddhist parallel works the same direction on a different map: [[B120 — Experience is five streams (the aggregates)]] analyzes experience into streams rather than acts. The compendium's principles use the practice on ordinary states, decomposing a mood or a thought pattern into parts that can be named and examined separately.
