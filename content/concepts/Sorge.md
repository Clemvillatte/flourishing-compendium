### Definition

Sorge is Heidegger's word for care, and it names nothing sentimental: it is the structural fact that a human existence is always already concerned with its own being. The Stanford Encyclopedia's reading of Being and Time puts it plainly. In anxiety, when everyday preoccupation falls away, what remains is the root:

> We learn, in particular, that even when all else fails we still care about our being, and all the other things we concern ourselves with are rooted ultimately in this fundamental care. ([Wrathall, SEP entry on Heidegger](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/))

### Origin

In Being and Time (1927), care is the formal unity of the human mode of being: ahead of itself in projects, already in a world, and amid the things it encounters. The entry renders the temporal structure: Dasein is "simultaneously ahead-of-ourselves, already-being-in (a world) and being-amidst (entities encountered in the world)" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/)). A later commentator's line captures the whole: "To be a self is to be defined by care-laden openness to the world" (McMullin 2013, quoted in [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/)).

Care is therefore not one activity among others. It is what moods, projects, tools, and other people are all made of, for a being whose existence is an issue for it.

### A real-life example

A man loses his job and discovers, in the shock, that he was never merely "doing tasks". The morning runs, the colleagues, the worry about the report: all of it was his care, structured into a world. The empty Monday is unbearable precisely because a caring being has lost its object, not because a task-doer has lost his tasks.
