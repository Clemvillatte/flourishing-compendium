"Judging by where one rests" — Confucius, Analects 2.10.

Analects 2.10 concerns assessing a person's character. In Legge's translation, Confucius says:

> See what a man does. Mark his motives. Examine in what things he rests. How can a man conceal his character? How can a man conceal his character? (Analects 2.10, Legge translation, [Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/analects/wei-zheng/zh))

The Chinese is 子曰：「視其所以，觀其所由，察其所安。人焉廋哉？人焉廋哉？」 — shì qí suǒ yǐ, guān qí suǒ yóu, chá qí suǒ ān. The three verbs escalate in scrutiny: shì (look at) what he does, guān (observe) what he follows, chá (examine) where he rests ([Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/analects/wei-zheng/zh)). The doubled closing question, "How can a man conceal his character?", marks the passage as a claim about the impossibility of hiding who you are from these three angles at once.

A practical reading is to consider the three observations together when assessing character: conduct, motives and the things in which the person rests. The third observation is the deepest of the three. Anyone can produce good conduct on a good day, and motives can be narrated in flattering terms; but what a person settles into when nothing is pressing (the conversations, the pastimes, the company, the ease or unease they actually rest in) is the residue that stays after the performance is over. This is why Confucius names it last and asks the concealing question twice: the resting place is the hardest thing to fake.

The passage sits in the Wei Zheng (為政) chapter, between Confucius' praise of his student Hui (2.9) and the teaching about cherishing old knowledge while acquiring new (2.11) ([Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/analects/wei-zheng/zh)); it belongs to the chapter's running concern with how a person of character is recognised and governed.
