"REACH model of forgiveness" — Worthington et al., 2014.

The model leads participants through five steps, built as an acronym: Recall the Hurt, Empathize with the Offender, give an Altruistic Gift of Forgiveness, Commit to the Forgiveness Experience, and Hold on to Forgiveness When Doubt Occurs.

> "According to this model, participants are led through five steps to REACH Forgiveness: R=Recall the Hurt, E=Empathize with the Offender, A=Give an Altruistic Gift of Forgiveness, C=Commit to the Forgiveness Experience, and H=Hold on to Forgiveness When Doubt Occurs." (Harper et al., 2014, Journal of Clinical Psychology 70(12), 1158–1169, [VCU Scholars Compass](https://scholarscompass.vcu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1053&context=psyc_pubs))

The model is primarily aimed at emotional forgiveness, which the paper distinguishes from decisional forgiveness:

> "The REACH Forgiveness intervention is primarily intended to facilitate emotional forgiveness (i.e., replacement of unforgiving emotions with positive other-oriented emotions) that may or may not be accompanied by decisional forgiveness (i.e., a behavioral intention to treat the offender as a worthwhile person of value...)."
> — [Harper et al., 2014, VCU Scholars Compass](https://scholarscompass.vcu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1053&context=psyc_pubs)

The workbook's own aim follows the same two-sided account: the first sections "inform the reader that the focus of the workbook is to facilitate emotional forgiveness by assisting victims in replacing negative emotions (i.e., anger, fear, bitterness) toward an offender with positive emotions (i.e., empathy, compassion...)"; the later sections then "guide the participant through making a decision to forgive the offender, and working systematically through the five steps to REACH emotional forgiveness" ([Harper et al., 2014](https://scholarscompass.vcu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1053&context=psyc_pubs)).

The trial evidence attaches to the workbook delivery: in a randomized wait-list control study, participants who completed the six-hour workbook "reported greater changes in the expected directions on each forgiveness outcome that was assessed relative to participants in a wait-list control condition", and "those in the immediate treatment condition maintained their gains two weeks after having completed the workbook" ([Harper et al., 2014](https://scholarscompass.vcu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1053&context=psyc_pubs)). The paper's benchmarking analysis compared the workbook's standardized change score (z = 1.05) against seven published group-therapy trials of the same intervention (z = .53), and found the workbook "at least as efficacious, if not more so, than delivery of the intervention via psycho-educational group therapy" ([same source](https://scholarscompass.vcu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1053&context=psyc_pubs)).

The effect sizes from the trial are moderate: unforgiveness (TRIM) d = -.56, decisional forgiveness (DFS) d = .45, emotional forgiveness (EFS) d = .50, and forgiveness (RFS) d = .69 ([Harper et al., 2014](https://scholarscompass.vcu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1053&context=psyc_pubs)). The authors' own caveat is honest: the study is one trial with 41 undergraduates, and "future research is required to further examine the clinical utility of the forgiveness workbook" ([same source](https://scholarscompass.vcu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1053&context=psyc_pubs)).
