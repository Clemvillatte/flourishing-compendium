The relational-interdependent self-construal is Susan Cross and colleagues' name for a way of thinking about yourself: in terms of your relationships with close others. Their 2000 paper, in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, defines it in the abstract as "the tendency to think of oneself in terms of relationships with close others" (Cross, Bacon & Morris, JPSP 78(4), 2000, [PubMed 10794381](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10794381/)).

The construct matters because it is measurable and has consequences the studies traced. People scoring high on the Relational-Interdependent Self-Construal (RISC) Scale described their important relationships as closer and more committed than low scorers did, were more likely to take others' needs and wishes into account when deciding, and, in a dyadic study with strangers, were seen by those partners as open and responsive, which tracked positive evaluations of the relationship.

### How it differs from related ideas

Cross's construct is a narrower thing than the Markus and Kitayama interdependent self-construal, which is a culture-level contrast; the RISC scale measures an individual difference in how much one's close relationships are part of the self-definition. My principle [[B138 — Empty the relations too (who bows to whom)]] cites the construct in a Buddhist register: if the self is co-constituted with others, then even the relations that make up the self can be held as costumes rather than cast.
