In the sources I opened, the idea goes by the name mitochondrial psychobiology, not "mitochondrial theory of psychology". It is defined as "the study of the interactions between psychological states and the biological processes that take place within mitochondria", and it also examines how mitochondrial biology influences "neural, endocrine, and immune systems known to transduce psychological experiences into health outcomes" ([Mitochondrial Psychobiology Lab, research themes](https://www.picardlab.org/research.html); [Picard, Trumpff and Burelle, 2019, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32637466/)). Treat the seed title as a wiki label for this field.

### The framework

Picard and McEwen's framework paper says an "emerging concept proposes that mitochondria sense, integrate, and transduce psychosocial and behavioral factors into cellular and molecular modifications", and that mitochondrial signalling "might in turn contribute to the biological embedding of psychological states". Chronic stress induces "structural and functional recalibrations of mitochondria, which constitutes mitochondrial allostatic load", and mitochondrial signalling "modulates the rate of cellular aging" ([Psychological Stress and Mitochondria: A Conceptual Framework, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5901651/)). Its authors add that "more research is needed to test various elements of this model, particularly in humans".

A related review lists four links between mitochondria and stress: energy supply for stress responses, production and metabolism of glucocorticoids, responsiveness to neuroendocrine mediators, and the finding that manipulating mitochondrial function alters responses to psychological stress ([An energetic view of stress, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5964020/)).

### What the evidence shows

The companion systematic review included 23 studies, all in male laboratory animals; 19 showed adverse effects of psychological stress on mitochondria and four found increases in function or size. In humans, "only six observational studies were available, none with experimental designs", and most measured markers "that do not directly reflect mitochondrial function" ([Psychological Stress and Mitochondria: A Systematic Review, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29389736/?dopt=Abstract)). A later laboratory study found acute stress raised anxiety, cortisol and norepinephrine, while cell-free mitochondrial DNA and IL-6 rose in both the stress and control conditions ([crossover study, Picard lab PDF](https://www.picardlab.org/uploads/7/7/8/4/77845210/1-s2.0-s0306453025003671-main.pdf)).

A PNAS study of postmortem brain tissue found that "higher well-being is linked to greater abundance of the mitochondrial OxPhos machinery, whereas higher negative mood is linked to lower OxPhos protein content" ([PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.2317673121)). The same abstract says the causal direction may run either way.

### How to use it

The claim in the seed that mitochondria "shape brain function, mood and ageing" is supported as a hypothesis and as associations, not as an established mechanism in humans. In essays, say that mitochondria are one proposed pathway by which stress becomes biology.
