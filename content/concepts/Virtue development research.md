Virtue development research studies how stable virtuous character traits are acquired. Sanderse examines a neo-Aristotelian account of the core mechanism, habituation, in a Journal of Moral Education article:

> "Full virtue and practical wisdom comprise the end of neo-Aristotelian moral development, but wisdom cannot be cultivated straight away through arguments and teaching. Wisdom is integrated with, and builds upon, habituation: the acquisition of virtuous character traits through the repeated practice of corresponding virtuous actions."
> — [Sanderse, "Does Aristotle believe that habituation is only for children?", Journal of Moral Education, Abstract (Taylor & Francis full text)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03057240.2018.1497952)

The same account adds what the practice delivers: "Habit formation equips people with a taste for, and commitment to, the good life; furthermore it provides one with discriminatory and reflective capacities to know how to act in particular circumstances" ([Sanderse, JME, Abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03057240.2018.1497952)). The article's question is whether habituation is only for children; its conclusion: "wisdom-guided habituation is also possible for adults who continue and confirm their already established virtuous habits" ([Sanderse, JME, Abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03057240.2018.1497952)). His conclusion concerns adults continuing and confirming already-established virtuous habits; he discusses implications for professional moral education.
