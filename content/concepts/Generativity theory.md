“Generativity theory” — McAdams and de St. Aubin, 1992.

Generativity is Erikson's name for the adult concern for guiding and providing for the next generation, against its failure state, stagnation. McAdams and de St. Aubin turned it from a stage of life into a working research program: a configuration of features that can be measured in real people rather than only assigned to an age.

> "McAdams and de St. Aubin's approach described generativity as an arrangement of seven empirically grounded facets including desire, cultural demand, concern, belief, commitment, action, and narration." — [Rubinstein et al., The Gerontologist 55(4) (2015), full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4542587/)

### Origin

Erik Erikson placed generativity — care for guiding and providing for the next generation, against its failure state, stagnation — in his map of the life stages. McAdams and de St. Aubin turned the stage into a research program in their 1992 article, building the seven-facet model above and the Loyola Generativity Scale to measure it.

### Core claims

The same review gives the causal spine of the model: "cultural demand in combination with inner desire (for agentic immortality and communal nurturance) fosters a conscious concern for future generations. Concern for future generations is strengthened by a belief in the goodness of humankind." Belief is said to lead to generative commitment. "Generative commitment subsequently can initiate generative action." "Ultimately, the multifaceted connection of these components is assembled through narration of the life story."

### How the theory explains generativity

The sequence runs from outside and inside at once: cultural demand meets inner desire, concern forms, belief strengthens it, and commitment and action follow. The last step is why the theory belongs beside narrative identity: one becomes generative through doing things for others and through telling a life story in which those things cohere. On the theory's own terms, midlife meaning is not a mood but a structure: a concern fed by demand, desire, and faith in people, expressed in acts, and given form in story.

### In everyday life

The model began as a measurement program: its everyday face is the Loyola Generativity Scale, a self-report measure of generative concern that the 1992 studies validated against reports of actual generative acts, such as teaching a skill, and against themes of generativity in autobiographical accounts ([McAdams & de St. Aubin 1992, abstract via Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-theory-of-generativity-and-its-assessment-through-McAdams-Aubin/7f22484c3ede554d3ae7f2b1e8af1232aaec2d47)).
