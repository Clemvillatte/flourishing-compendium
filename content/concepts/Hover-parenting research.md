Studies on overprotective, over-involved parenting showing it undermines children's autonomy, confidence, and risk tolerance.

The clearest single study is Schiffrin and colleagues' survey of 297 American college students, which asked what over-controlling, so-called helicopter parenting does to the children once they have left home:

> "Students who reported having over-controlling parents reported significantly higher levels of depression and less satisfaction with life. Furthermore, the negative effects of helicopter parenting on college students' well-being were largely explained by the perceived violation of students' basic psychological needs for autonomy and competence." (Schiffrin et al., [Helping or Hovering? The Effects of Helicopter Parenting on College Students' Well-Being](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10826-013-9716-3.pdf), Journal of Child and Family Studies 23, 2014, abstract)

The finding has a shape worth noticing: the parents' intentions are usually loving, and the damage runs through the child's sense of being trusted to handle things. The mechanism is not the hovering itself but what the hovering tells the child about their own competence. The authors frame the results through self-determination theory, which holds that autonomy and competence are basic needs, so their violation costs well-being.

The compendium's principle D063, help without meddling, is the practical rule this research backs: support the person's own agency rather than replace it.
