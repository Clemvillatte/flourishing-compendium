Sisyphus is the figure from Greek myth whom Camus takes as the "absurd hero" in the final chapter of The Myth of Sisyphus (published 1942; [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/camus/)): condemned by the gods to roll a rock up a mountain forever, the stone falling back each time. Camus sets him against the workman of today, who "works every day in his life at the same tasks, and this fate is no less absurd. But it is tragic only at the rare moments when it becomes conscious." ([final chapter](https://archive.org/stream/CamusMythOfSisyphusAndOtherReadings/Camus%20Myth%20of%20Sisyphus%20and%20other%20readings_djvu.txt))

> "Sisyphus, proletarian of the gods, powerless and rebellious, knows the whole extent of his wretched condition: it is what he thinks of during his descent. The lucidity that was to constitute his torture at the same time crowns his victory. There is no fate that cannot be surmounted by scorn." (Camus, [The Myth of Sisyphus, final chapter, trans. Justin O'Brien 1955](https://archive.org/stream/CamusMythOfSisyphusAndOtherReadings/Camus%20Myth%20of%20Sisyphus%20and%20other%20readings_djvu.txt))

### The conscious descent

Camus's attention is fixed on the walk back down: "That hour like a breathing-space which returns as surely as his suffering, that is the hour of consciousness" ([final chapter](https://archive.org/stream/CamusMythOfSisyphusAndOtherReadings/Camus%20Myth%20of%20Sisyphus%20and%20other%20readings_djvu.txt)). Consciousness is both the torture and the victory, because the fate is seen whole and owned: "His fate belongs to him. His rock is his thing." ([final chapter](https://archive.org/stream/CamusMythOfSisyphusAndOtherReadings/Camus%20Myth%20of%20Sisyphus%20and%20other%20readings_djvu.txt)) In the descent, "he is superior to his fate. He is stronger than his rock." ([final chapter](https://archive.org/stream/CamusMythOfSisyphusAndOtherReadings/Camus%20Myth%20of%20Sisyphus%20and%20other%20readings_djvu.txt)) The essay closes on its instruction:

> "The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy." (Camus, [The Myth of Sisyphus, final chapter, trans. Justin O'Brien 1955](https://archive.org/stream/CamusMythOfSisyphusAndOtherReadings/Camus%20Myth%20of%20Sisyphus%20and%20other%20readings_djvu.txt))
