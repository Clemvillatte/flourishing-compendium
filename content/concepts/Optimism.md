Optimism, in Martin Seligman's sense, is not cheerfulness but a style of explaining misfortune. The source is Seligman's Learned Optimism (1991), whose opening contrast runs:

> "The defining characteristic of pessimists is that they tend to believe bad events will last a long time, will undermine everything they do, and are their own fault. The optimists, who are confronted with the same hard knocks of this world, think about misfortune in the opposite way. They tend to believe defeat is just a temporary setback, that its causes are confined to this one case." (Seligman, [Learned Optimism](https://eduteka.icesi.edu.co/pdfdir/SELIGMAN_Optimism.pdf))

### What the difference does

The two styles face the same events; what differs is the recovery. Seligman's summary: "Optimists recover from their momentary helplessness immediately. Very soon after failing, they pick themselves up, shrug, and start trying again." The book's later claim — "Life inflicts the same setbacks and tragedies on the optimist as on the pessimist, but the optimist weathers them better" — makes the point plain: optimism changes the weathering, not the weather.

The contrast in the definition is built on three claims a pessimist's explanation makes about a bad event: that it will last a long time, that it will undermine everything the person does, and that it is the person's own fault. The optimist's version answers each the other way (defeat is a temporary setback, its causes confined to this one case), and the two passages together make the style a matter of what a person says about an event's causes rather than about the event.

What the style is described as affecting is the recovery, not the event. The passage says life inflicts the same setbacks and tragedies on the optimist as on the pessimist; the difference is that optimists "recover from their momentary helplessness immediately", and "very soon after failing... pick themselves up, shrug, and start trying again". The style is doing its work in the interval between a setback and the next attempt, which is a place a person can watch in their own case.

The contrast with cheerfulness holds because the definition locates optimism in explanations rather than in mood. A cheerful person can hold the pessimist's explanations of bad events, and a low-spirited person can hold the optimist's. What the two passages describe is the shape of what a person says to themselves after a failure, not the face they make while saying it.
