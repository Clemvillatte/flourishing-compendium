### What it is

The end-of-history illusion is the finding, reported in Science by Quoidbach, Gilbert and Wilson, that people of every adult age acknowledge how much they have changed while expecting relatively little further change. Across six studies of more than 19,000 people aged 18 to 68, they measured personalities, values and preferences, comparing the predictions of people aged a with the reports of people aged a+10: "Young people, middle-aged people, and older people all believed they had changed a lot in the past but would change relatively little in the future. People, it seems, regard the present as a watershed moment at which they have finally become the person they will be for the rest of their lives" (Quoidbach, Gilbert & Wilson, 2013, p. 96, [Harvard-hosted article](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Quoidbach%20et%20al%202013.pdf)).

### Where it comes from

The authors' definition is compact: "We call this tendency to underestimate the magnitude of future change the 'end of history illusion'" (Quoidbach, Gilbert & Wilson, 2013, p. 96, [Harvard-hosted article](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Quoidbach%20et%20al%202013.pdf)).

### Core claims

The magnitude varies with age, but the finding is broad: "Although the magnitude of this end of history illusion in some of our studies was greater for younger people than for older people, it was nonetheless evident at every stage of adult life that we could analyze" (Quoidbach, Gilbert & Wilson, 2013, p. 98, [Harvard-hosted article](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Quoidbach%20et%20al%202013.pdf)). The mechanism is an imagination error: "If people find it difficult to imagine the ways in which their traits, values, or preferences will change in the future, they may assume that such changes are unlikely. In short, people may confuse the difficulty of imagining personal change with the unlikelihood of change itself" (Quoidbach, Gilbert & Wilson, 2013, p. 98, [Harvard-hosted article](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Quoidbach%20et%20al%202013.pdf)).

It also carries costs: the illusion "had practical consequences, leading people to overpay for future opportunities to indulge their current preferences" (Quoidbach, Gilbert & Wilson, 2013, p. 96, [Harvard-hosted article](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Quoidbach%20et%20al%202013.pdf)). The examples are concrete: "Young adults pay to remove the tattoos that teenagers paid to get, middle-aged adults rush to divorce the people whom young adults rushed to marry" (Quoidbach, Gilbert & Wilson, 2013, p. 96, [Harvard-hosted article](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Quoidbach%20et%20al%202013.pdf)).

### How it explains the temporal-self principle

SE007 holds that past, present and future selves all belong to the person. The illusion is the standard error this principle corrects: the present self is treated as final, so the future self's interests are discounted or ignored. The remedy is calibration rather than introspection: a forecast of the next decade can be checked against what people a decade older report having experienced over the past one.

### An everyday example

Imagine a person in her thirties committing to a mortgage, a career and a tattoo on the assumption that the tastes doing the choosing are permanent. Her fifties, in the study's logic, may be inhabited by someone with different values, and the bill for today's certainties arrives on that person's desk.
