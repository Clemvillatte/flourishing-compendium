### What it is

The end-of-history illusion is the finding, reported by Quoidbach, Gilbert and Wilson in Science, that people of every adult age acknowledge how much they have changed while expecting almost no further change. Across six studies of more than 19,000 people aged 18 to 68, they measured personalities, values and preferences, and compared the predictions of people aged a years with the retrospective reports of people aged a+10: "Young people, middle-aged people, and older people all believed they had changed a lot in the past but would change relatively little in the future. People, it seems, regard the present as a watershed moment at which they have finally become the person they will be for the rest of their lives" (Quoidbach, Gilbert & Wilson, 2013, p. 96, [author manuscript, Harvard](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Quoidbach%20et%20al%202013.pdf)).

### Where it comes from

The name borrows Francis Fukuyama's phrase about liberal democracy and applies it to the self. The authors' definition is compact: "We call this tendency to underestimate the magnitude of future change the 'end of history illusion'" (Quoidbach, Gilbert & Wilson, 2013, p. 96, [author manuscript, Harvard](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Quoidbach%20et%20al%202013.pdf)).

### Core claims

The illusion appears at every adult age, though it is larger for younger people. The proposed mechanism is an imagination error: "If people find it difficult to imagine the ways in which their traits, values, or preferences will change in the future, they may assume that such changes are unlikely. In short, people may confuse the difficulty of imagining personal change with the unlikelihood of change itself" (Quoidbach, Gilbert & Wilson, 2013, p. 98, [author manuscript, Harvard](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Quoidbach%20et%20al%202013.pdf)).

It also carries costs: the illusion "had practical consequences, leading people to overpay for future opportunities to indulge their current preferences" (Quoidbach, Gilbert & Wilson, 2013, p. 96, [author manuscript, Harvard](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/Quoidbach%20et%20al%202013.pdf)). The tattooed teenager and the young adult who pays to remove the tattoos are the same person, surprised twice.

### How it explains the temporal-self principle

SE007 holds that the self has temporal depth, that past, present and future selves all belong to the person. The illusion is the standard error this principle corrects: the present self is treated as final, so the future self's interests are discounted or ignored. The remedy is arithmetic rather than introspection: whatever change the last decade brought, plan on a comparable amount in the next one.

### An everyday example

A person in her thirties commits to a mortgage, a career and a tattoo on the assumption that the tastes doing the choosing are permanent. Her fifties, in the study's logic, will be inhabited by someone with different values, and the bill for today's certainties arrives on that person's desk.
