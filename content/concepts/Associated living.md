Associated living is John Dewey's term for the mode of social life that democracy consists of: shared interests, freely interacting groups, communication as the means by which experience becomes common. The concept comes from Democracy and Education (1916), with a later development in The Public and Its Problems (1927).

> "A democracy is more than a form of government; it is primarily a mode of associated living, of conjoint communicated experience. The extension in space of the number of individuals who participate in an interest so that each has to refer his own action to that of others, and to consider the action of others to give point and direction to his own, is equivalent to the breaking down of those barriers of class, race, and national territory which kept men from perceiving the full import of their activity." (Dewey, Democracy and Education, ch. 7, [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/852/852-0.txt))

### What the passage packs in

Dewey's two criteria for the democratic society come just before the quotation: "more numerous and more varied points of shared common interest, but greater reliance upon the recognition of mutual interests as a factor in social control," and "freer interaction between social groups... but change in social habit — its continuous readjustment through meeting the new situations produced by varied intercourse" (ch. 7, [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/852/852-0.txt)). Associated living is the sociological substance; the governmental form is a container for it. The passage's second sentence carries the mechanism: as more individuals participate in an interest, each has to refer his own action to that of others, which Dewey equates with the breaking down of class, race and national barriers that kept people from perceiving the full import of their activity.
