“The irritation of doubt” — Peirce, Collected Papers.

### What it is

The phrase is Peirce's, from his 1877 essay "The Fixation of Belief", and it names the engine of inquiry. Doubt is not a gap in knowledge but a discomfort: "Doubt is an uneasy and dissatisfied state from which we struggle to free ourselves and pass into the state of belief; while the latter is a calm and satisfactory state which we do not wish to avoid, or to change to a belief in anything else" (Peirce, "The Fixation of Belief", 1877, [peirce.org](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p107.html)). The struggle to end it is what inquiry is: "The irritation of doubt causes a struggle to attain a state of belief. I shall term this struggle inquiry, though it must be admitted that this is sometimes not a very apt designation" (Peirce, "The Fixation of Belief", 1877, [peirce.org](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p107.html)).

### What it carries

The restriction that matters is that doubt must be real to do its work. Peirce mocks the fashionable instruction to question everything: "The mere putting of a proposition into the interrogative form does not stimulate the mind to any struggle after belief. There must be a real and living doubt, and without this all discussion is idle" (Peirce, "The Fixation of Belief", 1877, [peirce.org](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p107.html)). A paper doubt produces paper inquiry.

The P024 principle takes this as its test: let doubt be genuine, felt in a specific place, about a specific claim, before treating the question as worth pursuing. The everyday misuse runs the other way, the whirl of free-floating doubt that feels rigorous because it doubts everything and settles nothing. Peirce's picture makes the difference physical: real doubt irritates, and its cessation is a changed belief one can act on, not a shrug.
