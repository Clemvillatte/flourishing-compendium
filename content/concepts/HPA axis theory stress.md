HPA axis theory explains the body's hormonal stress response as a cascade from hypothalamus to pituitary to adrenal cortex, tuned for adaptation. James Herman and colleagues open their review of the system with its function:

> "The hypothalamo-pituitary-adrenocortical (HPA axis) is required for stress adaptation. Activation of the HPA axis causes secretion of glucocorticoids, which act on multiple organ systems to redirect energy resources to meet real or anticipated demand." ([Herman et al., 2016, PMC4867107](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4867107/))

### How the cascade runs

The response is neural at the top and hormonal below it: "The HPA stress response is driven primarily by neural mechanisms, invoking corticotrophin releasing hormone (CRH) release from hypothalamic paraventricular nucleus (PVN) neurons." Then, "stimulated corticotrophs then release adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) into the systemic circulation, whereby it promotes synthesis and secretion of glucocorticoids (cortisol in some species (e.g., man), corticosterone in others (e.g., rats, mice)) at the adrenal cortex" ([Herman et al., 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4867107/)). The system shuts itself down the same way it fires: "control of glucocorticoid release is mediated by glucocorticoid feedback at varying levels of the HPA axis, serving to limit prolonged exposure to catabolic actions of glucocorticoids."

### The chronic cost

The theory's practical weight is in what prolonged activation does:

> "Proper control of the stress response is of critical importance, as inappropriate or prolonged HPA axis activation is energetically costly and is linked with numerous physiological and psychological disease states." ([Herman et al., 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4867107/))

Chronic stress takes several forms, the review notes: "chronic basal hypersecretion, sensitized stress responses, even adrenal exhaustion," with the manifestation dependent on the stressor's chronicity, intensity, frequency and modality. Chronic exposure changes baseline function and stress responsiveness in ways the authors describe as long-lasting.
