The information-processing approach to development asks how children represent, transform and use information. A Wiley handbook chapter states its questions: "what kinds of information children represent in their thinking, how they represent and process this information, how these representations guide their behavior, and what mechanisms lead to changes in these processes across development" ([Information Processing Approaches to Development, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/pdf/10.1002/9780470147658.chpsy0210)). Robert Siegler's emphasis is on the last question: "To develop is to change. Without a good understanding of the mechanisms that produce change, no comprehensive understanding of development is possible." ([Siegler, Mechanisms of Cognitive Development, Annual Review, 1989](https://siegler.tc.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/Mech-Cog-Dev-Annual-Review-1989.pdf))

### Shared themes

An encyclopedia chapter by S. Strauss lists unifying themes: "thinking is information processing", with attention to "the ways children represent knowledge, how they transform information, and the processing limitations that constrain the inductive inferences they can make about their world". Learning overcomes those limits "via mechanisms such as strategy construction, automatization, encoding, generalization, and analogy construction" ([Strauss, 2000, chapter PDF](https://www.tau.ac.il/~sidst/Papers/Sid/encyclopedia%20chapter%20learning%20and%20development.PDF)). The Strauss text names several research programmes (production systems, rule assessment, skill acquisition), so no single model stands for the whole approach.

### Encoding

For Siegler, "encoding is the process by which stimuli are represented in a particular situation." Siegler adds that "in almost any context, some information is encoded and used to operate on the environment; other information is encoded but not used; yet other information is not encoded."

### Strategy choice

Children use more than one strategy at a time. "The majority of first graders have been found to use at least three strategies in solving a set of simple addition problems": counting from one, counting from the larger addend, and retrieving an answer from memory. This variety "has great advantages; it allows them to fit their strategies to constantly changing knowledge and situational demands". Children "choose among strategies in ways that yield desirable combinations of speed and accuracy": preschoolers relied mainly on retrieval for easy problems and on backup strategies for harder ones. Strategies also change: "Children continually learn new strategies and change their frequency of use of existing strategies."

### Limits stated by the author

Siegler wrote that "the basic issue raised by the review is whether these mechanisms are best thought of as five distinct entities or as five facets of a single underlying mechanism", and that he did not resolve it. He also left out "processing efficiency", "conceptual constraints" and "social scaffolding" for lack of space. He noted that connectionist models "assume veridical encoding of stimuli at the input level from the beginning of learning".

### Relation to Piaget

A textbook slide set (McGraw-Hill) contrasts the approach with Piaget by saying information-processing developmentalists see "a gradually increasing capacity for processing information", not abrupt knowledge change in distinct stages. This is a textbook simplification; Siegler's article quoted above treats change as continual strategy learning.
