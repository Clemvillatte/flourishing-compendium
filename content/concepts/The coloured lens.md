The heading names an image rather than a technical term: the mind's current mood or state works like tinted glass, coloring what reaches awareness. In the philosophy of emotion this is the standard shorthand for the perceptualist view of moods, which holds that moods function like colored lenses and alter the way the evaluative properties of encountered objects are perceived ([Gallegos, 2017](https://philarchive.org/archive/GALMAN-2)). Emerson gave the image its most quoted form:

> "Life is a train of moods like a string of beads, and as we pass through them they prove to be many-colored lenses which paint the world their own hue, and each shows only what lies in its focus." (Emerson, "Experience", Essays: Second Series, 1844)

The same article states the reading's detail and its best objection. On the perceptualist view, a person in a mood becomes disposed to see encountered objects as carrying evaluative properties that fit the mood, threatening in an anxious mood, pleasant in a cheerful one, and to miss what does not fit. Gallegos argues the metaphor misleads in important ways, because entering a mood also changes how accessible things are, not merely how they look.

### In the compendium

The seed line ties the image to the Buddhist family of [[Right view]], and the linked principles carry the compendium's psychological reading of the lens: anxiety and depression treated as distortions of what gets noticed and remembered.
