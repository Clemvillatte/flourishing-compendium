(Kasser and Ryan, 1996; Kasser, 2002), wealth, fame, and image goals correlate with lower well-being even when achieved.

The correlational part of the seed holds. The "even when achieved" part is supported only by a later study, not by the 1996 paper, and Kasser (2002), a book, was not opened for this page. The effects across studies are small to moderate.

### Kasser and Ryan (1996)

> "In a sample of adult subjects (Study 1), the relative importance and efficacy of extrinsic aspirations for financial success, an appealing appearance, and social recognition were associated with lower vitality and self-actualization and more physical symptoms." (Kasser & Ryan 1996, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 22(3):280-287, [abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167296223006))

Intrinsic aspirations (self-acceptance, affiliation, community feeling, physical health) were associated with higher well-being and less distress. Study 2 replicated the pattern in a college sample and extended it to narcissism and daily affect. The measure that the abstract calls "efficacy" is the rated likelihood of attaining a goal, not actual attainment, so this paper does not show what happens once the goals are achieved.

### Does attainment help? Niemiec, Ryan and Deci (2009)

A one-year study of a post-college sample addressed attainment directly.

> "Yet, whereas attainment of intrinsic aspirations related positively to psychological health, attainment of extrinsic aspirations did not; indeed, attainment of extrinsic aspirations related positively to indicators of ill-being." (Niemiec, Ryan & Deci 2009, Journal of Research in Personality 43(3):291-306, [PubMed 20161160](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20161160/))

Placing importance on either type of aspiration related positively to attaining those goals. The change in attainment of intrinsic aspirations and the change in psychological health were linked through satisfaction of the needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness. This is the evidence behind "even when achieved", with one qualification: the study found no benefit from extrinsic attainment and some links to ill-being, which is weaker than showing that achievement lowers well-being.

### Meta-analytic scale

Dittmar and colleagues (2014) pooled 753 effect sizes from 259 independent samples on materialism and well-being. Materialism was associated with significantly lower well-being on the most widely used measures (materialist values and beliefs, r = -.19; relative importance of materialist goals, r = -.16), and the link was weaker for life satisfaction and negative affect (r = -.13 to -.15) than for risky health and consumer behaviours and negative self-appraisals. They suggest the negative link may be explained by poor psychological need satisfaction, and call for more experimental, longitudinal and developmental research. ([Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 107(5), PubMed 25347131](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25347131/))

### Limits

These are mainly correlational and self-report studies. The meta-analysis found that effect sizes depended on demographic factors, on value context (study and work environments, cultures) and on cultural economic indicators, so the size of the effect varies by context.
