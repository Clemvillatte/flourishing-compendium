Second nature is the idea that a settled habit comes to work like a natural disposition, so that a person acts well without having to force it. The compendium's seed points to Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II. The phrase "second nature" itself does not appear in the Ross translation of the passages opened here. Two passages carry the idea.

### Book II: virtue by habit

In Book II chapter 1 Aristotle argues that moral virtue is neither given by nature nor against it:

> "Neither by nature, then, nor contrary to nature do the virtues arise in us; rather we are adapted by nature to receive them, and are made perfect by habit." (Aristotle, NE II.1, Ross translation, [Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.ii.html))

He adds that "the virtues we get by first exercising them, as also happens in the case of the arts," so that "we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts." He concludes that "states of character arise out of like activities," and that "it makes no small difference, then, whether we form habits of one kind or of another from our very youth; it makes a very great difference, or rather all the difference" ([Aristotle, NE II.1](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.ii.html)). His argument for why virtue is not natural is that "nothing that exists by nature can form a habit contrary to its nature": a stone thrown upward ten thousand times does not learn to rise.

### Book VII: habit is like nature

The nearest text to the phrase is in Book VII chapter 10, on incontinence: "it is easier to change a habit than to change one's nature; even habit is hard to change just because it is like nature, as Evenus says: I say that habit's but a long practice, friend, And this becomes men's nature in the end" ([Aristotle, NE VII.10](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.7.vii.html)). Here the comparison is about how hard habits are to change, not about effortless virtue.

### In the compendium
