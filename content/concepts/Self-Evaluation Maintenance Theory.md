**Self-Evaluation Maintenance Theory** — Tesser — others' performance in self-relevant domains threatens the self; we bask, distance or redefine closeness and relevance.

The model, introduced by Abraham Tesser, is stated in one sentence with its parts hanging off it: "According to the self-evaluation maintenance model (SEM), social circumstances exert their influence through two complementary processes, a reflection process and a comparison process. Each of these processes depends on the performance and the psychological closeness of another in a particular situation. The relative importance of these processes is determined by the relevance of the performance dimension to one's own self-definition." (Tesser & Campbell, 1982, [ERIC full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED226263.pdf))

### Two processes, one switch

The two processes pull in opposite directions, and relevance is the switch. On a dimension the person does not define themselves by, a close other's excellence is free credit: "In order to take pride in the accomplishment of others two things are necessary: The other's performance has to be relatively good and we must, in some sense, be associated with or close to that other." On a dimension that is self-defining, the same closeness turns the same excellence into a threat: "It is just as plausible to assume, however, that being in close association with someone who performs well can make one's own performance look bad by comparison." The rule as the authors state it: "If the other is performing on a dimension which is highly relevant to the individual, he will tend to suffer by comparison to a close other's good performance. If the other is performing on a dimension which is not relevant to the individual, he will take pride in the reflection of a close other's good performance."

### What people do about it

The model's unsettling prediction is about relationships: the threat is managed by adjusting closeness itself. As Tesser writes in the 1988 statement of the model, "when relevance is high, the better the other's performance the less closeness or the more distance one will put between the self and other" (Tesser, 1988, p. 190, quoted in [Schmitt, Silvia & Branscombe, 2000](https://libres.uncg.edu/ir/uncg/f/P_Silvia_Intersection_2000.pdf)).
