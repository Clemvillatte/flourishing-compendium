**Theory of Work Adjustment** — Dawis, Lofquist — satisfaction and satisfactoriness are the fit between individual needs and work reinforcers.

The Minnesota theory, first published by Dawis, England and Lofquist and revised through the 1960s and 1984, is built on one concept, correspondence. The 1968 revision monograph is summarized in its ERIC record: "The Theory of Work Adjustment is based on the concept of correspondence between individual and environment. Achieving and maintaining correspondence with the environment are basic motives of human behavior. ... Correspondence is then the individual fulfilling the requirements of the work environment and the work environment fulfilling the requirements of the individual. The continuous and dynamic process by which the individual seeks to achieve and maintain correspondence with his work environment is called work adjustment." (Dawis et al., A Theory of Work Adjustment (A Revision), Minnesota Studies in Vocational Rehabilitation XXIII, 1968, as summarized in the [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED031740))

The theory's two great variables are the two directions of that fit. A paper from the same project restates the propositions:

> "Satisfaction is a function of the correspondence between the reinforcer system of the work environment and the individual's needs, provided that the individual's abilities correspond with the ability requirements of the work environment." And: "Satisfactoriness and satisfaction, then, are basic indicators of the degree of success an individual has achieved in maintaining correspondence between himself and his work environment." (Weiss, "Occupational Reinforcers, Vocational Needs, and Job Satisfaction", Work Adjustment Project, 1970, [open ERIC full text](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED015511.pdf))

Satisfaction is the individual's assessment of the environment; satisfactoriness is the environment's assessment of the individual. Both are functions of correspondence, and both jointly predict tenure, the theory's principal outcome. Adjustment is therefore bidirectional maintenance: the worker must keep meeting the job's requirements while the job keeps meeting the worker's.

The compendium's application: the theory is a model of fit as an ongoing achievement rather than a one-time choice, and it gives precision to the everyday word "fit" by splitting it into two judgments made by two different parties.
