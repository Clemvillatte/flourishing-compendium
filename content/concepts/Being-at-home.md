Being-at-home is the felt condition of dwelling: not owning a building but being held by a place that shelters one's inner life. Bachelard's formulation is the classic one:

> "If I were asked to name the chief benefit of the house, I should say: the house shelters day-dreaming, the house protects the dreamer, the house allows one to dream in peace." (Bachelard, The Poetics of Space, ch. 1, [Wikiquote](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Gaston_Bachelard))

### What the condition carries

For Bachelard the house integrates a person's thoughts, memories and dreams; it is the cradle before one is "cast into the world," the place from which ventures set out and to which they return. Heidegger makes the complementary point from the side of building: "We attain to dwelling, so it seems, only by means of building. The latter, building, has the former, dwelling, as its goal" ([Heidegger, Building Dwelling Thinking](https://www.beyng.com/pages/en/BasicWritings/BasicWritings.347.html)). His sharper observation is that being at home is not the same as being located: "The truck driver is at home on the highway, but he does not have his shelter there; the working woman is at home in the spinning mill, but does not have her dwelling place there." One can be at home in motion and homeless in a house, which is why the phenomenon is psychological and not architectural. The compendium principle E030 carries the practical reading: practise dwelling (wohnen), treating settledness as a skill of attention rather than a possession of property. A compendium exercise drawn from the condition is to notice where one's daydreaming is permitted, since that, by Bachelard's test, is where one actually lives. What the condition does not give: an argument that staying put is superior to movement; it describes a function of shelter, not a rule of residence.
