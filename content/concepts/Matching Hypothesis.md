The Matching Hypothesis is the social-psychological claim, associated with Elaine Walster (later Hatfield) and colleagues, that people pair with others of roughly their own social desirability, balancing the wish for an attractive partner against the realistic chance of acceptance. A recent network-analysis paper states the claim in its plainest form:

> "The matching hypothesis in social psychology claims that people are more likely to form a committed relationship with someone equally attractive." ([Jia, Spivey, Szymanski and Korniss, PLoS ONE](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4470921/))

### Where the evidence sits

The same paper extends the analysis from mate-choice models to networks, treating the hypothesis as a live pattern to test rather than a settled fact ([same paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4470921/)). The idea's pull is easy to state: everyone would prefer the most attractive partner, and the balance point of what one can realistically attract is what the hypothesis names. The mechanism the seed line describes (desirability weighed against probability of acceptance) is the hypothesis's own level-of-aspiration framing, not a verified psychological law.
