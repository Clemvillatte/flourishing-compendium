Collective intelligence names a group's general ability to perform a wide variety of tasks. The term is used by analogy with individual intelligence: where general intelligence is a statistical factor that emerges from one person's performance across cognitive tasks, Anita Woolley and her colleagues asked whether a similar factor exists for groups, and defined one.

> "By analogy with individual intelligence, we define a group's collective intelligence (c) as the general ability of the group to perform a wide variety of tasks." (Woolley, Chabris, Pentland, Hashmi and Malone, [Evidence for a Collective Intelligence Factor in the Performance of Human Groups](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ab/Salon/research/Woolley_et_al_Science_2010-2.pdf), Science 330(6004):686-688, 2010, p. 686)

### What predicted it

Their finding: "In two studies with 699 people, working in groups of two to five, we find converging evidence of a general collective intelligence factor that explains a group's performance on a wide variety of tasks. This 'c factor' is not strongly correlated with the average or maximum individual intelligence of group members but is correlated with the average social sensitivity of group members, the equality in distribution of conversational turn-taking, and the proportion of females in the group" ([Woolley et al. 2010, abstract](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ab/Salon/research/Woolley_et_al_Science_2010-2.pdf)). The social sensitivity measure was the "Reading the Mind in the Eyes" test, with a significant correlation between c and the group average (r = 0.26, P = 0.002). In the groups whose conversations were recorded with sociometric badges, c was negatively correlated with the variance in speaking turns (r = -0.41, P = 0.01). In the authors' words, "groups where a few people dominated the conversation were less collectively intelligent than those with a more equal distribution of conversational turn-taking" ([Woolley et al. 2010, p. 687](https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~ab/Salon/research/Woolley_et_al_Science_2010-2.pdf)).
