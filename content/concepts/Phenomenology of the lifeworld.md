Husserl's lifeworld is the familiar world of lived experience, shaped by bodily activity, culture and shared history, which scientific investigation presupposes ([Dan Zahavi, Edmund Husserl, section 6](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). Investigating it helps clarify the background and horizons within which people build an objective account of the world ([Dermot Moran, introduction, p. 7](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/95361/excerpt/9780521895361_excerpt.pdf)).

> "The lifeworld is, not surprisingly, the world we live and feel at home in." (Dan Zahavi, [section 6](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)).

This is not a proposal to reject science: Zahavi describes everyday experience and scientific description as two ways in which the same world appears, with scientific practice itself rooted in shared experience ([section 6](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). Moran also stresses that Husserl's investigation is a route into transcendental phenomenology, rather than just a catalogue of ordinary habits ([introduction, pp. 7–8](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/95361/excerpt/9780521895361_excerpt.pdf)).

The presupposition claim is the part that does the work, since it says that the lifeworld is not a rival account of the world but the thing any account is built on top of. Scientific description is itself a practice carried out by people in a shared world, which means the lifeworld is not something a person could step outside of to check. Zahavi's word for the relation is that the two are ways in which the same world appears, and the phrasing keeps the world single rather than doubled.

The practical reading is about horizons rather than about habits. A person's lifeworld has a shape that is not visible from inside it, since the shape is what makes some things show up as familiar and others as strange, and noticing the shape is what Husserl calls investigating. The concept does not tell a person to doubt the familiar world; it tells a person that the familiar world has a structure, and that the structure is what any less familiar account of the world is downstream of.
