"The upright soul" is the companion's heading for a teaching Zarathustra gives twice in the opening chapters: uprightness is not first a property of character but of the speaking body. In "Backworldsmen," having dismissed the preachers of other worlds who hearken to "the preachers of death," he turns the instruction: "Hearken rather, my brethren, to the voice of the healthy body; it is a more upright and pure voice. More uprightly and purely speaketh the healthy body, perfect and square-built; and it speaketh of the meaning of the earth" (Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, Part I, "Backworldsmen," trans. Common; [Project Gutenberg, eBook 1998](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1998)). The next chapter names what that voice says. To the despisers of the body Zarathustra answers: "'Body am I entirely, and nothing more; and soul is only the name of something in the body.' The body is a big sagacity, a plurality with one sense, a war and a peace, a flock and a shepherd." What a person calls spirit is "a little instrument and plaything of thy big sagacity," and behind thoughts and feelings stands "a mighty lord," the Self, which "ruleth, and is also the ego's ruler." The soul that stands upright is therefore not the soul that masters the body by discipline; it is the soul that speaks with the body instead of over it, in the register the healthy body already uses. The ego's own speech learns this: "Always more uprightly learneth it to speak, the ego; and the more it learneth, the more doth it find titles and honours for the body and the earth." In the same movement the teaching connects to posture: the "perfect and square-built" body speaks of the meaning of the earth, and the person who straightens addresses the body's own lord, whose hearing and ruling do not stop at the neck.
