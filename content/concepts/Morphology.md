Morphology is the term Goethe coined for the study of the form, formation and transformation of living things (["Johann Wolfgang Goethe", Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, section 4](https://iep.utm.edu/goethe/)). The entry's account of his science puts it this way:

> "Whereas his earlier romanticism considered nature the raw material on which human emotions could be imparted, Goethe's studies in botany, mineralogy, and anatomy revealed to him certain common patterns in the development and modifications of natural forms. The name he gave to this new manner of inquiry was 'morphology'." (["Johann Wolfgang Goethe", Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, section 4](https://iep.utm.edu/goethe/))

The concept itself moved as its subject did: "No static concept, morphology underwent its own metamorphosis throughout Goethe's career." It was first named in his notes of 1796, and he only fully laid it out as an account of the form and transformation of organisms in the 1817 Zur Morphologie ([same entry](https://iep.utm.edu/goethe/)).

### The metamorphosis of plants

The 1790 essay "The Metamorphosis of Plants" carries the method's botanical case. Considering the leaf as an Urphänomen, Goethe traced its metamorphosis "from a seed into the stem, then leaves, then flowers, and finally its stamen or pistil", a continuous development he described as an "intensification" or Steigerung of the original form ([same entry](https://iep.utm.edu/goethe/)). In his representation, the intensification of a plant results from the interaction between the nutritive forces of the plant and the organic form of the primal leaf ([same entry](https://iep.utm.edu/goethe/)).

The contrast with the botany of his time is part of the point. The entry notes that Goethe's morphology stood "in opposition to the static taxonomy of Linnaeus", studying these perceptible limitations as "instances of natural generation for the sake of intuiting the inner working of nature itself, whole and entire" ([same entry](https://iep.utm.edu/goethe/)).
