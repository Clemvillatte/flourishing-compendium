### Definition

Respect is a posture toward a thing that holds back before it. The Stanford Encyclopedia's account frames it as a relation in which a subject responds to an object "from a certain perspective in some appropriate way" ([Dillon, SEP entry on Respect](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/respect/)). Kant gave the moral core its classic form:

> "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or the person of any other, never simply as a means but always at the same time as an end." (Kant, Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, 1785, 4:429, as quoted in [Dillon, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/respect/))

### Origin

The term's modern map was drawn by Darwall (1977), who split it into two kinds. Recognition respect is a constraint: giving appropriate weight in deliberation to some fact about the thing, whether a law, a cliff edge, or a person. Appraisal respect is a grade: thinking highly of someone's character or conduct. Kant's practical teaching belongs to the first kind, and he defined its discipline precisely:

> "to be understood as the maxim of limiting our self-esteem by the dignity of humanity in another person, and so as respect in the practical sense." (Kant, Metaphysics of Morals, 6:449, as quoted in [Dillon, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/respect/))

Respect, in this light, is less a feeling than a curb: the refusal to inflate oneself by using, mocking, or dismissing another.

### A real-life example

A manager fires someone. She can do it by text, or she can do it face to face, with reasons given, the door left open for questions. The outcome is identical; the respect is not. The second way treats the person as someone the decision concerns, rather than as an obstacle the day's schedule must clear.
