Goal-directed attentional control is attention steered by what you are trying to do, as opposed to attention pulled around by whatever is loud, new or threatening. The name comes from cognitive neuroscience. Corbetta and Shulman reviewed evidence for two partly separate brain systems. One, including parts of the intraparietal and superior frontal cortex, "is involved in preparing and applying goal-directed (top-down) selection for stimuli and responses." The other works as a "circuit breaker" for the first, "directing attention to salient events" ([Corbetta & Shulman, 2002, Nature Reviews Neuroscience 3: 201-215](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11994752/)). Both systems interact during normal vision. So the goal-directed system is one half of a working pair, and its job is not to block every interruption.

### Can a plan help it?

The seed claims that setting intentions improves attentional selection and reduces distraction. Gollwitzer's 1999 review of implementation intentions, the "if situation X, then I will do Y" plans, supports a careful version of that claim. In his distraction studies, participants did arithmetic while award-winning commercials played at random intervals:

> Simple goal intentions ("I will not let myself get distracted!") were less effective in protecting participants from these distractions (measured as level of performance on the task at hand) than goal intentions that are furnished with implementation intentions.

(Gollwitzer, 1999, American Psychologist 54(7): 493-503, [full text copy](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/server/api/core/bitstreams/14cc2a36-5f01-4dc1-b9ca-f2d0ca0c8930/content).)

The wording of the plan mattered. Plans phrased as distraction-inhibiting ("Whenever the distraction arises, I will ignore it!") warded off distractions in general. Plans phrased as task-facilitating ("I will increase my efforts at the task at hand") did so only when motivation was low or medium, and could backfire when motivation was high. Gollwitzer's practical advice was that people who get distracted should form implementation intentions that "focus on the ignoring of distractions, rather than on stepping up efforts" (same source).

### How far the evidence goes

Gollwitzer summarises the mechanism as follows: "Implementation intentions delegate the control of goal-directed behaviors to specified anticipated environmental stimuli." That is a form of goal-directed control that runs through a cue, with less effort in the moment. The 1999 paper is a review of mostly laboratory tasks. A later meta-analysis of 94 studies found a medium-to-large effect of implementation intentions on goal achievement (d = .65), and described them as effective in "protecting ongoing efforts from distractions" ([Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006, abstract](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/chapter/bookseries/pii/S0065260106380021)). Those results are about goal pursuit generally. Shelton and Alain (2014), cited in the seed, was not located or opened, so nothing here rests on it.
