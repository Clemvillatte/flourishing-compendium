The veil of ignorance is John Rawls's device in A Theory of Justice (1971) for making a fair choice of first principles. The parties to the choice are placed in a hypothetical "original position" and stripped of the knowledge that would let them write rules favoring their own case: they do not know their class, status, talents, conception of the good, or their society's circumstances. The device is Rawls's answer to the question of what makes an agreement fair rather than merely voluntary.

### The passage

The canonical statement is in Section 3:

> "Among the essential features of this situation is that no one knows his place in society, his class position or social status, nor does any one know his fortune in the distribution of natural assets and abilities, his intelligence, strength and the like. I shall even assume that the parties do not know their conceptions of the good or their special psychological propensities. The principles of justice are chosen behind a veil of ignorance."

([Rawls, A Theory of Justice, TJ p. 12/11, quoted in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Original Position"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/original-position/).)

The purpose follows immediately: "This ensures that no one is advantaged or disadvantaged in the choice of principles by the outcome of natural chance or the contingency of social circumstances. Since all are similarly situated and no one is able to design principles to favor his particular condition, the principles of justice are the result of a fair agreement or bargain" ([Rawls, TJ p. 11/12, quoted at NYU Stern](https://pages.stern.nyu.edu/~gsimon/Rawls01.pdf)).

### What the veil is for

The veil is not a claim that people are ignorant. It is a specification of what counts as a morally relevant reason in choosing principles: facts about one's own position do not count. Rawls's summary of the thought: "The purpose of the veil of ignorance is to deny to the contracting parties the knowledge of their own characteristics and circumstances that would enable them to propose or insist on principles designed to favor themselves" ([University of Chicago Law Review, "The Theory of Justice"](https://chicagounbound.uchicago.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=3767&context=uclrev)). The parties keep general knowledge — economics, psychology, the circumstances of justice — and lose only the particular facts about themselves ([SEP, "Original Position"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/original-position/)).

The concept matters to the compendium because it is the cleanest tool for testing whether a rule you endorse would survive being on the receiving end of it, and [[Justice]] lists this page for comparison with other fairness devices.
