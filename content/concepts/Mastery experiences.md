Bandura's term for the strongest of the four sources of self-efficacy: direct experiences of succeeding at a task. The other three (vicarious experience, social persuasion, somatic and emotional states) also feed efficacy beliefs ([Bandura, "Self-Efficacy," 1994](https://coalea.univ-lorraine.fr/sites/coalea.univ-lorraine.fr/files/bandura1994ehb.pdf)).

> The most effective way of creating a strong sense of efficacy is through mastery experiences. Successes build a robust belief in one's personal efficacy. Failures undermine it, especially if failures occur before a sense of efficacy is firmly established. If people experience only easy successes they come to expect quick results and are easily discouraged by failure. A resilient sense of efficacy requires experience in overcoming obstacles through perseverant effort.

(Albert Bandura, "Self-Efficacy," Encyclopedia of Human Behavior, 1994, [PDF](https://coalea.univ-lorraine.fr/sites/coalea.univ-lorraine.fr/files/bandura1994ehb.pdf))

Bandura adds that some setbacks serve a useful purpose in teaching that success usually requires sustained effort, and that people who become convinced they have what it takes persevere in the face of adversity and rebound from setbacks ([Bandura, "Self-Efficacy," 1994](https://coalea.univ-lorraine.fr/sites/coalea.univ-lorraine.fr/files/bandura1994ehb.pdf)).

The design implication Bandura draws: easy successes make brittle belief. A resilient sense of efficacy is built on tasks that include obstacles, because the person learns that effort carries them through. The four sources do not arrive equally either — seeing a similar person succeed can raise a belief, but a success of one's own is the one a person cannot argue with. My SE060 (build self-efficacy through doing) and BE060 (build competence through practice) inherit this structure.
