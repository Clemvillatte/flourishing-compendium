Trust — the willingness to be vulnerable to another's good will. It is the medium of every relational tradition and the precondition of the community of inquiry.

The research definition the field converged on is Mayer, Davis and Schoorman's, in their integrative model of organizational trust:

> "The definition of trust proposed in this research is the willingness of a party to be vulnerable to the actions of another party based on the expectation that the other will perform a particular action important to the trustor, irrespective of the ability to monitor or control that other party." (Mayer, Davis & Schoorman, "An Integrative Model of Organizational Trust", Academy of Management Review 20(3), 1995, p. 712, [article PDF](https://people.wku.edu/richard.miller/Mayer%20Trust%20article.pdf))

Two features of that definition carry the concept's weight. Trust is a willingness, not the deed itself — one can be willing to be vulnerable and still be let down, which is what makes the willingness real rather than a wager on certainty. And the definition makes monitoring and control beside the point: where you can compel or verify, you do not need trust. What the trustor leans on is a judgment of the trustee, and the model names its recurring components:

> "three characteristics of a trustee appear often in the literature: ability, benevolence, and integrity." (same article)

The model separates trust from trustworthiness: ability, benevolence and integrity are properties of the one trusted, while trust is the stance of the one trusting, shaped also by a general propensity to trust that varies between people.

### In the compendium

The compendium's principles use trust where relationships are load-bearing: friendship needs it because friends hold what is fragile in each other, and every tradition's community of inquiry presupposes it, since teaching and testimony hand the learner what they cannot yet check.
