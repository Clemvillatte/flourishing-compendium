Egocentric projection in social inference is the habit of reading another person's mind from one's own. A 2020 study in the Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition opens its abstract with the pattern:

> "Perceivers of other minds often overestimate the similarity between their own and other people's perspectives." (Damen and colleagues, [Tilburg University Research Portal](https://research.tilburguniversity.edu/en/publications/can-the-curse-of-knowing-be-lifted-the-influence-of-explicit-pers))

### The anchoring account

The same abstract says this "egocentric projection" is argued to come from the tendency "to use their own perspective as a referential anchor from which they insufficiently adjust away". The account it cites is Epley, Keysar, Van Boven and Gilovich (2004), who proposed "that people adopt others' perspectives by serially adjusting from their own" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15382983/)).

### What the experiments found

In Epley and colleagues' abstract, estimates of others' perceptions "were consistent with one's own but differed in a manner consistent with serial adjustment". Participants were slower to say another person's perception would differ from their own than to say it would be similar. Egocentric biases "increased under time pressure" and "decreased with accuracy incentives". Adjustments tend to be insufficient "in part, because people stop adjusting once a plausible estimate is reached".

### A limit on the easy fix

Damen and colleagues tested whether a deliberate focus on the other person's point of view would reduce the bias. It did not: enhancing attention to the other person's perspective, before and during perspective-taking, "did not diminish egocentric projection". They discuss the role of feedback in making accurate adjustments.
