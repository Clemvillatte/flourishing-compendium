Temporality (Zeitlichkeit) is Martin Heidegger's term in Being and Time for the temporal structure that makes a human life the kind of being it is: we exist ahead of ourselves, already in a world, and amidst the things we deal with, all at once. The Stanford Encyclopedia's reading of the book's central claim:

> "Heidegger argues that all human actions and experiences are temporally structured in that they involve a 'retention' of the past and an 'awaiting' of the future that together inform how the present shows up as soliciting us to respond (Heidegger refers to this as 'a making-present that awaits and retains'; SZ 355)." ([SEP, Martin Heidegger, §2.5](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/))

### What the term carries

Temporality is not clock time. It is the structure of caring about anything at all: time is, in a line SEP quotes from Heidegger's lectures, "the self's way of letting itself be concerned about anything" ([SEP, §2.5](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/)). The entry distinguishes Heidegger's lowercase temporality, "Dasein's temporal structure, in virtue of which we are simultaneously ahead-of-ourselves, already-being-in (a world) and being-amidst (entities encountered in the world)" (SZ 327), from the historical temporality of a people, and it is the lowercase structure that the compendium's principles put to work.

### In the compendium

- [[E008 — Practise the structure of temporality]] pairs this with Husserl's retention-protention structure of experience: the principle's practical claim is that a moment of experience already holds the just-elapsed and the about-to-come, and Heidegger's temporality is the same insight at the scale of a whole life.
