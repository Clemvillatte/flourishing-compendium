Extrinsic motivation means doing something for what it gets you, not for the activity itself. Ryan and Deci put it this way:

> Extrinsic motivation is a construct that pertains whenever an activity is done in order to attain some separable outcome.

(Ryan & Deci, 2000, Contemporary Educational Psychology 25: 54-67, [full text](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_IntExtDefs.pdf).) The contrast is with intrinsic motivation, doing an activity for the enjoyment of the activity itself. A plain reading would say extrinsic is always the worse kind. Self-determination theory says otherwise.

### Not one thing, but a range

Ryan and Deci write that "SDT proposes that extrinsic motivation can vary greatly in the degree to which it is autonomous" ([Ryan & Deci, 2000](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_IntExtDefs.pdf)). They describe four types along that range:

- External regulation: actions "performed to satisfy an external demand or obtain an externally imposed reward contingency". People typically experience them as controlled or alienated.
- Introjected regulation: internal but still controlling, done to avoid guilt or anxiety or to gain pride. In their phrase, "introjection represents regulation by contingent self-esteem".
- Identified regulation: the person has identified with the value of the activity. Their example is a student who values learning as a life goal.
- Integrated regulation: the most autonomous form, where the reasons are assimilated into one's other values and needs. It resembles intrinsic motivation, yet it is still extrinsic because the behaviour serves an outcome separate from itself.

The authors add that "the more one internalizes the reasons for an action and assimilates them to the self, the more one's extrinsically motivated actions become self-determined."

### Extrinsic goals are a separate question

The seed cites Kasser and Ryan (1993), and that study is about something related but distinct: extrinsic aspirations, specifically wealth. Across three studies, they found that when money-related values and expectancies are more central to a person than other self-relevant values, they are negatively associated with adjustment and well-being ([Kasser & Ryan, 1993, PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8366427/)). In the third study, a non-college sample, a high centrality of financial aspirations went with lower interview-rated global adjustment and social productivity, and more behavioural disorders. The abstract concerns goal content (what a person wants), not the reason behind an act (why they do it), so this compendium should keep the two ideas apart. A follow-up on attaining such goals reports that attainment of extrinsic aspirations (money, fame, image) was unrelated to need satisfaction and related slightly negatively to psychological health ([Niemiec, Ryan & Deci, 2009, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2736104/)).
