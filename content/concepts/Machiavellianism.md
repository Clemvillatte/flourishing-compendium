Machiavellianism is a personality trait named after Niccolò Machiavelli and measured since Richard Christie and Florence Geis's 1970 Studies in Machiavellianism. In the research literature's summary: "People exhibiting high levels of Machiavellianism (Christie and Geis, 1970) are characterized by interpersonal manipulation, such as the use of flattery and deceit, as well as by aloof, cynical, and traditionally amoral viewpoints adopted in order to promote their own goals/interests" ([Al Aïn et al., Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00454/full)).

### What the scale measures and what correlates follow

The standard instrument, the Mach-IV, "is a self-report Likert type scale which evaluates three dimensions: (1) the use of deceit in interpersonal relationships (nine items), (2) a cynical view of human nature (nine items), and (3) the lack of morality (two items)" ([same article](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00454/full)). The correlates the seed line points to are documented: in a Korean adult sample (N = 1,177), "Machiavellianism and psychopathy were negatively and narcissism was positively associated with well-being" ([Joshanloo, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8144451/)). In relationships, "Women with high levels of Machiavellianism reported lower relationship satisfaction, perceived their partners to be less dependable, reported less faith in their partners, and were less willing to persist with the relationship" ([Brewer and Abell, Europe's Journal of Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5590532/)).
