“Harmony” — Confucius, The Analects; Plato, Republic.

Three classical traditions converge on this word, and each makes a different instrument of it. Heraclitus draws on the lyre: harmony is not the absence of tension but the product of opposing pulls. Confucius draws on social relations: harmony is a way of holding one's own among others without demanding they become oneself. Plato draws on music theory: harmony is the correct ordering of parts within a whole, first of the soul, then of the city.

### Heraclitus: attunement out of opposition

The oldest of the three uses is also the most paradoxical. Fragment B8: "What opposes converges; from things that differ comes the most beautiful attunement; and all things come to be through strife." And B51: "They do not comprehend how, at variance with itself, it agrees with itself: a back-turning attunement, as of the bow and the lyre" ([Heraclitus, fragments, via Open Scriptorium](https://openscriptorium.com/books/heraclitus.txt); B51 after Hippolytus, Refutation IX 9). The bow works because the string pulls against the wood; the lyre sings because opposing tensions hold. Harmony here is structural, not sentimental.

### Confucius: affability without flattery

Analects XIII.23 gives the term its social sense:

> "The superior man is affable, but not adulatory; the mean man is adulatory, but not affable." (Confucius, Analects XIII.23, Legge translation, [Standard Ebooks](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/confucius/analects/james-legge/text/chapter-13))

Later readers compressed the contrast into the formula the compendium's principle takes up — harmony without uniformity (he er bu tong). The gentleman holds his own position while remaining easy toward others; the small man follows the crowd while having no ease in himself.

### Plato: the soul as attunement

In the Republic, justice turns out to be an internal arrangement. Socrates concludes that the just man has "harmonized these three principles, the notes or intervals of three terms quite literally the lowest, the highest, and the mean" ([Plato, Republic IV.443d, Shorey translation, Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0168%3Abook%3D4%3Asection%3D443d)). Reason, spirit and appetite are tuned to each other, and the tuned soul can then hold a tuned city together.

### Why the three travel together

All three uses share a core: harmony requires parts that differ. There is no attunement without differing tones, no affability without distinct positions, no ordered soul without separate faculties. The concept earns its place in the flourishing literature for the same reason — a life in harmony is not a life without tension, but one in which the tensions sound together.
