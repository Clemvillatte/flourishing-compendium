The chiasm is Merleau-Ponty's name for the crossing between sensing and being sensed. In the Stanford Encyclopedia's account of The Visible and the Invisible, it appears in the final chapter, "The Intertwining—The Chiasm". The entry explains that "chiasm has two senses": a physiological one, a crossed arrangement such as the optic nerves, and a literary one, figures of speech that repeat structures in reverse order (AB:BA). For Merleau-Ponty it combines "the unity-in-difference" of the first with "the reversal and circularity" of the second ([SEP, Merleau-Ponty](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)).

### Self-touch as the model

The paradigmatic example is "the body's doubling into sensible and sentient aspects during self-touch". The entry lists three consequences. The body is an "exemplar sensible" showing kinship between subject and object. The relationship is reversible, like "two segments of one sole circular course". And the sentient and sensible never strictly coincide, because a gap, an "écart", separates them ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)).

### Flesh and the handshake

Chiasm is "a bi-directional becoming or exchange between the body and things" that justifies speaking of a "flesh" of things, closest to an "element" in the classical sense. The entry extends this to other people: just as my two hands communicate, "I can touch the sensibility of another", and Merleau-Ponty writes, "The handshake too is reversible" (The Visible and the Invisible, 187/142) ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)). The entry also stresses that there is "not one chiasm but rather various chiasmic structures at different levels", across touch and vision, body and world, self and other, perception and language ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/merleau-ponty/)).

### Scope

The sources are one encyclopedia entry and the quotations it reproduces from The Visible and the Invisible, which was not read directly. The essay "Eye and Mind", cited for the alias "Intertwining", was not consulted. The sources describe a philosophical ontology and offer no empirical test of its practical effects.
