Grit is a personality trait introduced to psychology by Angela Duckworth and colleagues in 2007. The article defines it by what it sustains: effort and interest kept up over years, through failure, adversity, and plateaus in progress.

> "We define grit as perseverance and passion for long-term goals." (Duckworth, Peterson, Matthews and Kelly 2007, p. 1087, as quoted verbatim in the opened [Hou et al. 2021 meta-analysis](https://www.pure.ed.ac.uk/ws/files/245949034/HouEtalPSPB2021DoGrittierPeople.pdf); [primary PDF](https://www.timeandlearning.org/files/-duckwort/images/grit%20jpsp.pdf), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 92(6))

### What the studies found

The published abstract states the findings directly:

> "Defined as perseverance and passion for long-term goals, grit accounted for an average of 4% of the variance in success outcomes, including educational attainment among 2 samples of adults (N = 1,545 and N = 690), grade point average among Ivy League undergraduates (N = 138), retention in 2 classes of United States Military Academy, West Point, cadets (N = 1,218 and N = 1,308), and ranking in the National Spelling Bee (N = 175)." (Abstract, [Duckworth et al. 2007](https://www.timeandlearning.org/files/-duckwort/images/grit%20jpsp.pdf); the PDF's text layer renders the equals signs of the Ns as a font artifact, normalised here)

Four percent of variance is a small share, and the article presents grit as one predictor among several rather than a master trait.

The trait has two proposed facets: consistency of interest and perseverance of effort. The first holds a goal steady over years; the second keeps working at it through setbacks.
