“Haslam et al” — 2009.

The 2009 reference is Haslam, Jetten, Postmes and Haslam's editorial "Social Identity, Health and Well-Being: An Emerging Agenda for Applied Psychology" (Applied Psychology 58(1):1-23), which introduced a special issue on how group membership affects health. Its core claim, often called the social cure, is that groups matter for health through the way they become part of a person's sense of who they are.

### The core claim

The authors say groups are "not simply external features of the world that provide a setting for our behaviour. Instead they shape our psychology through their capacity to be internalised and contribute to our sense of self." They use Tajfel's definition of social identity as "knowledge that [we] belong to certain social groups together with some emotional and value significance to [us] of this group membership".

> "Groups that provide us with a sense of place, purpose, and belonging tend to be good for us psychologically. They give us a sense of grounding and imbue our lives with meaning." (Haslam et al. 2009, [editorial PDF](https://www.dhi.ac.uk/san/waysofbeing/data/communities-murphy-haslam-2009.pdf))

They add that these effects "can buffer well-being when it is threatened". The editorial organises the special issue around five themes: symptom appraisal and response, health-related norms and behaviour, social support, coping, and clinical outcomes.

### The built-in caution

The same passage warns that "at other times group membership can compromise health because the content of social identity is inconsistent with health-enhancing activity". Losing a valued group can hurt as much as joining one helps; the authors note that changes that compromise valued social identities, such as moving home or losing one's job, "can be at least as devastating as the upside of group life is positive".

### Follow-up evidence

Cruwys and colleagues' 2013 population study of depression and group membership is a later test of the idea. In data tracked over two years (N = 5055) and four years (N = 4087), the number of groups a person belonged to was a strong predictor of later depression, with fewer groups predicting more depression. Depressed respondents with no groups who joined one reduced their relapse risk by 24%, and by 63% if they joined three. ([PubMed 24331897](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24331897/)) The analyses control for several background variables, but the abstract frames the result as evidence that suggests group membership is protective and curative, not as proof of causation.
