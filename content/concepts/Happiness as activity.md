For Aristotle, happiness is something we do and not something we have. He builds this into his definition in Nicomachean Ethics I. After arguing that the function of a human being is an activity of the soul that follows reason, he concludes:

> human good turns out to be activity of soul in accordance with virtue, and if there are more than one virtue, in accordance with the best and most complete. But we must add 'in a complete life.' For one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day; and so too one day, or a short time, does not make a man blessed and happy.

([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics I, trans. W. D. Ross, Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html), ch. 7.)

### Why activity and not a state

In chapter 8 Aristotle says it makes "no small difference whether we place the chief good in possession or in use, in state of mind or in activity." His reasoning: "the state of mind may exist without producing any good result, as in a man who is asleep or in some other way quite inactive, but the activity cannot; for one who has the activity will of necessity be acting, and acting well." He then compares it to the Olympic Games: "it is not the most beautiful and the strongest that are crowned but those who compete... so those who act win, and rightly win, the noble and good things in life" ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics I, trans. W. D. Ross, Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html), ch. 8).

So the claim is not that happiness is a pleasant feeling. He adds that the lives of those who act well are "also in itself pleasant", with pleasure arriving alongside the activity.

### Two cautions for essays

- He says happiness needs "a complete life", and that "many changes occur in life, and all manner of chances", with Priam as an example of someone who ends wretchedly and cannot be called happy ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics I, trans. W. D. Ross, Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html), ch. 9). The activity view is not a promise that effort guarantees happiness.
- Translators render the key terms differently (eudaimonia as happiness, blessedness or flourishing; arete as virtue or excellence). Quotes here are from one translation, Ross's.
