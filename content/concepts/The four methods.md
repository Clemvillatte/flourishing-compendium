“The four methods” — Peirce, "The Fixation of Belief".

In "The Fixation of Belief" (Popular Science Monthly 12, November 1877), Charles Sanders Peirce compares four ways of settling what to believe: tenacity, authority, the a priori method and the method of science. The page rests on the full text, which was opened, and the quotes below were checked against it. The seed names the essay but not the four methods, so the list is spelled out here.

### Why a method is needed

Peirce starts from doubt, not from truth.

> "The irritation of doubt is the only immediate motive for the struggle to attain belief." ([Peirce 1877, section IV](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p107.html))

He then claims that "the sole object of inquiry is the settlement of opinion", because a firm belief satisfies us "whether the belief be true or false". The four methods are four answers to how that settling is to be done.

### The first three

On tenacity, Peirce says the believer simply clings to a chosen belief, and that:

> "this method of fixing belief, which may be called the method of tenacity, will be unable to hold its ground in practice. The social impulse is against it."

On authority, where an institution keeps doctrines "before the attention of the people", he names it "the method of authority" and grants it "immeasurable mental and moral superiority to the method of tenacity", pointing to the structures and faiths it has produced.

On the a priori method:

> "They have been chiefly adopted because their fundamental propositions seemed 'agreeable to reason.' This is an apt expression; it does not mean that which agrees with experience, but that which we find ourselves inclined to believe."

He concludes of this method that it "does not differ in a very essential way from that of authority", since development removes the effect of some casual circumstances but magnifies that of others.

### The method of science

> "it is necessary that a method should be found by which our beliefs may be determined by nothing human, but by some external permanency – by something upon which our thinking has no effect." ([Peirce 1877](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p107.html))

Peirce states its hypothesis as: "There are Real things, whose characters are entirely independent of our opinions about them". On that hypothesis anyone with sufficient experience who reasons enough will be led to the same conclusion. He also says a private inspiration is only "a form of the method of tenacity". The compendium principle that compares these methods follows Peirce's ranking. Note that his case for science rests on this hypothesis about Reals, and he takes up the objection that the method cannot be used to prove its own hypothesis.
