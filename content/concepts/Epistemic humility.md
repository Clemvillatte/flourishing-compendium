Epistemic humility, in the psychology literature usually called intellectual humility, is the disposition to treat your own beliefs as possibly wrong. Mark Leary's research group, which built the standard scale, opens its four studies with the short form: "Four studies examined intellectual humility—the degree to which people recognize that their beliefs might be wrong" ([Leary et al., 2017](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/bitstreams/c55d87f1-2f28-408d-9f2c-efbc4da85990/download)). The fuller definition adds what separates the trait from mere lack of confidence:

> "As we use the term here, intellectual humility is defined as recognizing that a particular personal belief may be fallible, accompanied by an appropriate attentiveness to limitations in the evidentiary basis of that belief and to one's own limitations in obtaining and evaluating relevant information." ([Leary et al., 2017, p. 1](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/bitstreams/c55d87f1-2f28-408d-9f2c-efbc4da85990/download))

### What it does

The trait is not timidity. The studies found that people scoring high in intellectual humility judged others less on the basis of their religious opinions, were less inclined to see politicians who changed their minds as "flip-flopping," and "were more attuned to the strength of persuasive arguments than those who were low" ([Leary et al., 2017](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/bitstreams/c55d87f1-2f28-408d-9f2c-efbc4da85990/download)). Holding a belief firmly and tracking its weakest support turn out to travel together.
