## In plain words

Attribution theory is the psychology of how people explain behaviour: the umbrella term for models of "how individuals perceive the causes of everyday experience, as being either external or internal" ([Wikipedia, Attribution theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attribution_theory)). The explanations people settle on matter, because they carry the emotions and expectations that follow.

## Where it comes from

The founding text is Fritz Heider's The Psychology of Interpersonal Relations (1958). Heider explored what he called "common sense" or "naïve psychology," holding that "people observe, analyze, and explain behaviors with explanations" ([Wikipedia, Attribution theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attribution_theory)). He found it useful to group explanations into two categories: internal (personal) attributions, where the cause is assigned to "the individual's characteristics such as ability, personality, mood, efforts, attitudes, or disposition," and external (situational) attributions, where the cause is assigned to the situation, "such as the task, other people, or luck" ([Wikipedia, Attribution theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attribution_theory)).

## How it works

Harold Kelley's covariation model sets out the evidence people weigh: consensus information ("how other people in the same situation and with the same stimulus behave"), distinctiveness information ("how the individual responds to different stimuli"), and consistency information ("how frequent the individual's behavior can be observed with similar stimulus but varied situations") ([Wikipedia, Attribution theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attribution_theory)). Weiner's achievement model adds three dimensions: "stability (stable and unstable), locus of causality (internal and external), controllability (controllable or uncontrollable)"; stability shapes expectancy about the future, control relates to persistence, and causality influences emotional responses to outcomes ([Wikipedia, Attribution theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attribution_theory)). In this model the locus dimension (internal versus external) is separate from the controllability dimension (whether the cause can be influenced).

## What it explains

The theory explains why the same failure can feel different depending on its explanation: in the model, stability shapes the expectation that tomorrow will resemble today, control bears on persistence, and the attributed cause colours the emotional response to the outcome.

## Everyday life

When a friend cancels on you, the internal attribution ("she doesn't care") and the external one ("her week collapsed") produce different feelings in you. Kelley's three questions are a portable check: would anyone have done the same (consensus), is this unlike her with others (distinctiveness), does this happen every time (consistency)?
