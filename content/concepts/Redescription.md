Redescription is Richard Rorty's name for the move by which a person or a culture changes its words before its beliefs. In Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity (1989) he builds the idea out of the claim that vocabularies are contingent tools rather than mirrors of reality, so progress comes from new ways of speaking rather than from better arguments in the old way. His ironist is defined by relation to her own "final vocabulary," the words in which she justifies her actions and tells the story of her life: "if doubt is cast on the worth of these words, their user has no noncircular argumentative recourse" (p. 73).

> "her method is redescription rather than inference" (Rorty, Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity, p. 78, quoted in Müller, [Rortyan Irony as Civic Virtue in Our Myside Society](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0487/ch7.xhtml))

### What redescription does

The ironist, Rorty says, is defined by relation to the final vocabulary: such a person does not hope to have their doubts about it settled by something larger than themselves (p. 97), and ironism itself "results from awareness of the power of redescription" (p. 89). The work is done on the self as much as on the world: "Private autonomy can be gained by redescribing one's past in a way which had not occurred to the past" (p. 101). On the public side, Rorty's famous passage on solidarity says the process of seeing other human beings as "one of us" rather than as "them" is a matter of detailed description of what unfamiliar people are like and of redescription of what we ourselves are like, a task for the novel and the ethnography rather than for theory.
