A stress mindset is a person's standing belief about the nature of stress itself: either that stress enhances performance, health and growth, or that it debilitates them. Alia Crum, Peter Salovey and Shawn Achor introduced the construct in 2013 with an eight-item Stress Mindset Measure and three studies testing whether the belief is distinct from other stress variables and whether it changes outcomes ([Crum, Salovey and Achor 2013](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/crum_salovey_anchor_2013_-_rethinking_stress.pdf), JPSP 104(4)).

> "In the context of stress, one's stress mindset can be conceptualized as the extent to which one holds the belief that stress has enhancing consequences for various stress-related outcomes such as performance and productivity, health and wellbeing, and learning and growth (referred to as a 'stress-is-enhancing mindset') or holds the belief that stress has debilitating consequences for those outcomes (referred to as a 'stress-is-debilitating mindset')." (Crum, Salovey and Achor, [Rethinking stress: the role of mindsets in determining the stress response](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/crum_salovey_anchor_2013_-_rethinking_stress.pdf), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 104(4):716-733, 2013, article opening)

### What the studies found

Study 1 presented reliability and validity data for the Stress Mindset Measure, and the authors' structural equation modeling treated stress mindset as distinct from the amount of stress, stress appraisal and coping. Study 2 showed the mindset can be shifted: employees who watched short film clips presenting factual information biased toward stress-is-enhancing developed more of that mindset, and "participants in the enhancing condition reported improved psychological symptoms and better work performance, whereas their counterparts in the control or debilitating conditions did not". Study 3 put the mindset under an acute laboratory stressor and reported that "a stress-is-enhancing mindset is associated with moderate cortisol reactivity and high desire for feedback under stress". That cortisol measure is a physiological association observed in one study; the symptom and performance results were self-report, and the study period was short.

### What it is not

The paper draws two boundaries. Stress mindset is not appraisal: "Whereas appraisal of stress refers to the evaluation of a particular stressor as more or less stressful, stress mindset refers to the evaluation of the nature of stress itself as enhancing or debilitating." And it is not a coping strategy: stress mindset "may inform the coping strategy that one utilizes, serving as the mental and motivational context in which coping actions are chosen and employed", but it is not itself one.

## Related concepts

- [[Stress appraisal]] — the paper's own boundary: appraising a specific stressor, rather than holding a standing belief about stress itself
- [[Coping flexibility]] — coping is the response to a live stressor; stress mindset is the context in which coping actions are chosen
- [[Cognitive reappraisal]] — a comparison: reinterpreting a stressor, as distinct from believing stress is enhancing
