### Definition

The planning fallacy is the confident belief that your project will run as planned, held even while you know that most similar projects ran late. [[T022 — Kahneman, Daniel|Kahneman]] and Tversky named it in 1979, and Buehler, Griffin and Ross gave it its working definition in the 1994 paper that made it a research program:

> The tendency to hold a confident belief that one's own project will proceed as planned, even while knowing that the vast majority of similar projects have run late, has been termed the planning fallacy (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979). ([Buehler, Griffin & Ross 1994](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/buehler-et-al-1994.pdf))

### Origin

Kahneman and Tversky's insight was that forecasters plan from the inside, from the singular best case, while ignoring the distribution of similar outcomes. Buehler and colleagues tested this against real predictions, with students estimating thesis and shopping times. Their abstract reports the mechanism:

> Think-aloud procedures revealed that Ss focused primarily on future scenarios when predicting their completion times. ([Buehler et al. 1994](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/buehler-et-al-1994.pdf))

The cure their Study 4 found is telling: the bias vanished for people told to connect their predictions to their own past experiences. Memory, not willpower, is the corrective.

### A real-life example

The essay will take "a couple of hours." It takes the day. Every essay of the writer's life has taken the day, and this knowledge sits in the drawer undisturbed while the plan, shining and singular, gets all the attention. The repair is not to try harder, but to open the drawer and read.
