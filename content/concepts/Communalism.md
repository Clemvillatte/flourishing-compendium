“Communalism” — Gyekye, Tradition and Modernity.

Communalism is the name Kwame Gyekye gives to the communal character of traditional African social life, and to the philosophy built on it. The core of the idea is a claim about what a person is. Where liberal thought starts from the individual and treats groups as optional associations, communalism starts from embeddedness:

> "Communitarianism immediately sees the human person as an inherently (intrinsically) communal being, embedded in a context of social relationships and interdependence, never as an isolated, atomic individual. Consequently it sees the community not as a mere association of individual persons whose interests and ends are contingently congruent, but as a group of persons linked by interpersonal bonds, biological and/or non-biological, who consider themselves primarily as members of the group and who have common interests, goals, and values." ([Gyekye, "Person and Community in African Thought"](https://res.cloudinary.com/segl/image/upload/v1583436111/Gyekye-Communitarianism_ycw6vs.pdf))

The claim has edges, and Gyekye is known for trimming them. Against the radical reading associated with Mbiti and Menkiti, in which the community constitutes the person so thoroughly that individuality is a late arrival, he argues for a moderated version:

> "It seems to me that restricted communitarianism offers a more appropriate and adequate account of the self than the unrestricted or radical account in that the former addresses the dual features of the self: as a communal being and as an autonomous, self-determining, self-assertive being with a capacity for evaluation and choice." (same essay)

One feature of the life remains non-negotiable even in the moderate version:

> "That the human person does not voluntarily choose to enter into human community, that is, that community life is not optional for any individual person." (same essay)

For the compendium, communalism is the philosophical backbone of [[R001 — I am because we are]]: it is the worked-out African tradition behind the claim that a human life flourishes or withers in company, and its moderate version is a standing reminder that belonging and selfhood are not rivals but two halves of one account.
