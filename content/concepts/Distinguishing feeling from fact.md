“Distinguishing feeling from fact” — Stoics, Epictetus.

The Stoic distinction runs between things and the judgements about them. Epictetus opens the Enchiridion by marking what is ours and what is not: "Some things are under our control, while others are not under our control. Under our control are conception, choice, desire, aversion, and, in a word, everything that is our own doing; not under our control are our body, our property, reputation, office, and, in a word, everything that is not our own doing" (Epictetus, [Enchiridion 1, Long translation](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1EDkL63FuSpabjbB7amxNodYocj_q_vaH/view)). The practical rule follows in the fifth chapter:

> It is not the things themselves that disturb men, but their judgements about these things. For example, death is nothing dreadful, or else Socrates too would have thought so, but the judgement that death is dreadful, this is the dreadful thing. (Epictetus, [Enchiridion 5, Long translation](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1EDkL63FuSpabjbB7amxNodYocj_q_vaH/view))

The practice he prescribes is naming a thing's nature before the feeling attaches: "With everything which entertains you, is useful, or of which you are fond, remember to say to yourself, beginning with the very least things, “What is its nature?” If you are fond of a jug, say, “I am fond of a jug”; for when it is broken you will not be disturbed" (Epictetus, [Enchiridion 3, Long translation](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1EDkL63FuSpabjbB7amxNodYocj_q_vaH/view)).

The compendium pairs the Stoic discipline with modern work on the observing self, on noticing affect without becoming it, and on the affect heuristic, where a feeling stands in for a judgment. The felt certainty is the fact-shaped thing to check.
