Sleep architecture is the organised pattern of sleep stages through the night. In the description Matthew Walker gives in his 2009 review, "the sleep of mammalian species has been broadly classified into two distinct types: non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep and rapid eye movement (REM) sleep, with NREM sleep being further divided in primates and cats into four substages (1-4) corresponding, in that order, to increasing depth of sleep" ([Walker, 2009, Annals of the New York Academy of Sciences 1156, 168-197](https://walkerlab.berkeley.edu/reprints/Walker_NYAS_2009.pdf)).

> "In humans, NREM and REM sleep alternate or 'cycle' across the night in an ultradian pattern every 90 min. Although this NREM-REM cycle length remains largely stable across the night, the ratio of NREM to REM within each 90-min cycle changes, so that early in the night stages 3 and 4 of NREM dominate, while stage 2 NREM and REM sleep prevail in the latter half of the night." (Walker, 2009, [PDF](https://walkerlab.berkeley.edu/reprints/Walker_NYAS_2009.pdf))

### Slow-wave sleep

The same paper says the deepest NREM stages, 3 and 4, "are often grouped together under the term 'slow wave sleep' (SWS), reflecting the occurrence of low frequency waves (1-4 Hz and <1 Hz)." The paper attributes the four-substage scheme to Rechtschaffen and Kales (1968).

### The functions claim

The compendium's seed says each stage serves distinct functions. The best-documented proposal here concerns REM. Walker and van der Helm's 2009 review proposes "a rapid eye movement (REM) sleep hypothesis of emotional-memory processing," in which overnight modulation of affective neural systems and the reprocessing of recent emotional experiences "appear to redress the appropriate next-day reactivity of limbic and associated autonomic networks" ([Walker & van der Helm, 2009, Psychological Bulletin 135(5), 731-748, abstract via ERIC](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ859933)). It is a hypothesis, and the abstract itself calls it "proposed." A later review states that "several recent studies are challenging this 'depotentiated' theory," and that "whether REM-sleep plays a role in the maintenance of the emotional tone or the depotentiation of the emotional tones thus requires extensive further research" ([Emotion, emotion regulation and sleep: An intimate relationship, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7181893/)). An essay should present REM's emotional role as a leading hypothesis under test, not a settled fact.
