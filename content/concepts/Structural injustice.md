Structural injustice concerns harms produced through social processes, including many people and institutions acting within accepted rules. Iris Marion Young developed the concept in Responsibility for Justice (2011); the book's examples of implicated participation include buying goods produced in sweatshops and participating in booming housing markets that leave many homeless.

> "...exists when social processes put large groups of persons under systematic threat of domination or deprivation of the means to develop and exercise their capacities, at the same time that these processes enable others to dominate or to have a wide range of opportunities for developing and exercising capacities available to them. Structural injustice is a kind of moral wrong distinct from the wrongful action of an individual agent or the repressive policies of a state. Structural injustice occurs as a consequence of many individuals and institutions acting to pursue their particular goals and interests, for the most part within the limits of accepted rules and norms." (Young, Responsibility for Justice, 2011, p. 52, as quoted in Virginia Mantouvalou, ["Structures of Injustice, the Law, and Exploitative Work"](https://academic.oup.com/book/58181/chapter/480373130), Oxford University Press)

### Responsibility without blame

Young's answer to this diagnosis is a redesign of responsibility, which the book calls the "social connection" model: political responsibility for changing unjust structures need not depend on blame for causing a particular harm. Her worked example is Sandy, a single mother of two who faced the prospect of homelessness through no fault of her own, in an affluent society where no one should be in a position of housing insecurity. The people she dealt with acted according to the law and treated her with decency, and the contributing causes are diffuse:

> "[s]ome laws, such as municipal zoning laws, and some policies, such as private investment policies, contribute to the structural processes that cause Sandy's plight, but none can be singled out as the major cause" (Young, Responsibility for Justice, p. 47, quoted in Mantouvalou, same chapter)

The Mantouvalou chapter states what the model does to attention: "it shifts attention away from individual responsibility in two ways: on the one hand, it shows that people in Sandy's position are not responsible for their predicament. On the other hand, it illustrates how those with whom Sandy interacted along the way complied with societal rules and practices so they are also not necessarily responsible for having caused direct harm to her."

> "The responsibility in which Young was interested was individual, forward-looking, and political (rather than legal)." (Mantouvalou, ["Structures of Injustice, the Law, and Exploitative Work"](https://academic.oup.com/book/58181/chapter/480373130), describing Young)

> "everyone has 'political responsibility' to address unjust structures, and particularly those who are not directly responsible for causing a particular harm" (Mantouvalou, ["Structures of Injustice, the Law, and Exploitative Work"](https://academic.oup.com/book/58181/chapter/480373130), describing Young)

> "However, they still have forward-looking responsibility to address it, because they benefit from the injustice." (Mantouvalou, ["Structures of Injustice, the Law, and Exploitative Work"](https://academic.oup.com/book/58181/chapter/480373130), describing Young)
