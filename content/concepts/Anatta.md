Anatta (Pali; Sanskrit anātman) is the Buddhist teaching that there is no permanent, unchanging, independent self. The word is often translated as "non-self" or "not-self" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatta)). It is one of the Three Marks of Existence in Buddhist doctrine, along with dukkha ("suffering, dissatisfaction") and anicca ("impermanence"). The teaching denies that there is any unchanging essence — an ātman in Sanskrit, an attan in Pali — that persists beneath the changing stream of experience. The foundational texts include the Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta (the "Discourse on the Mark of Not-Self"), where the Buddha examines the five aggregates and finds none of them to be the self. The Milindapañha (Questions of King Milinda) presents the famous chariot analogy: just as "chariot" is only a conventional label for the assembled parts, "Nāgasena" is a convention for the ever-changing aggregates (Mil 3.1.1) ([e-Buddhism](https://e-buddhism.com/milindapanha/)). Sister Vajirā's verse (SN 5.10) is cited in support.

The argument proceeds through the five aggregates (khandhas): form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness. Each aggregate, examined individually or in combination, is found not to be the self. No stable essence is located either in the body or in the mind. The sense of self persists because language and psychological habit project continuity onto a causal process that is in fact a bundle of interdependent, momentary events. Continuity is causal, not substantial — like a flame that appears continuous while consuming new fuel.

## What it is NOT




Anatta is not nihilism, and it is not a claim that persons do not exist conventionally. Buddhism distinguishes conventional truth from ultimate truth: in everyday life "I" and "you" remain useful designations. Nor is anatta equivalent to low self-esteem or self-abandonment. The point is analytic, not self-referential: it is a statement about what does not exist, not about how to feel. In some later Buddhist traditions — notably the Mahāyāna Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra and the Tathāgatagarbha tradition — the concept is qualified by a doctrine of Buddha-nature, which itself is described as "not-self". Scholars such as Gethin, Gombrich and Wynne argue that the early teaching was "not-self" (a method of analysis) that later hardened into "no-self" (a metaphysical claim).

## Practical implications




For flourishing, anatta reframes identity from a fixed possession to a set of habits, roles, and narratives that can be examined, revised, or released. It is a functional parallel to modern research on identity flexibility and self-concept change: the person you are is a pattern you are maintaining, not a substance you are discovering. Cognitive-behavioural and acceptance-based therapies use a similar move — "I am having the thought that I am a failure" — to loosen identification with painful self-judgments. Anatta generalises this: no mental event, however central, has to be "me".
