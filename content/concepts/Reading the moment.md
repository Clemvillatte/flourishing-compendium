“Reading the moment” — the Zhouyi, the Book of Changes' timing tradition.

The label is the compendium's summary, and the seed names a tradition without a passage. The Zhouyi (Changes of Zhou) is the core text of the Yijing, and its commentaries (the Ten Wings) develop the idea of acting in tune with the configuration of the moment. The account below rests on two Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries read as search excerpts; the primary text was not opened.

### The moment as a field of action

The Stanford Encyclopedia describes each hexagram as "a record of fortune-telling", consisting of a hexagram name that describes a specific situation and six lines "detailing the alternative moves in a given situation". The entry adds that for the Changes' authors divination is "intrinsically philosophical because its goal is to provide an opportunity for self-reflection", in which inquirers "review their past, examine their present, and make plans for the next step". ([Chinese Philosophy of Change (Yijing), Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-change/))

### Timeliness

In the commentary called the Tuan, the six lines of the first hexagram, Qian, are read as six dragons. The Stanford entry reports that the authors stress "mounting of the six dragons in a timely fashion" (shi cheng liu long), and that they ask readers to pick the dragon that suits their situation: a hidden dragon when starting a new venture, a flying dragon when things flourish, and to "avoid becoming an arrogant dragon when everything looks perfect but a decline is imminent". The entry sums it up: the Tuan authors "urge readers to view Qian metaphorically as a spatial-temporal grid to reflect on their surroundings, to look for alternatives, and to anticipate dangers and pitfalls." ([Stanford Encyclopedia, section 1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-change/))

The Chinese concept behind this is shi, glossed in another Stanford entry as "the seasons, the temporal configuration of a given moment, and the ability to act according to the demands of that moment". ([Metaphysics in Chinese Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2018/entries/chinese-metaphysics/))

### A caution on options

The commentator Wang Bi read the six lines as "room to maneuver", and the Stanford entry says that "with proper action, one can turn what appears to be a failure into a blessing", while lacking it "one can make what appears to be flourishing into a disaster". The text treats the moment as something to respond to with judgement, not a fixed verdict.

### What this page does not claim

The sources are secondary and the Zhouyi text and the Ten Wings were not read directly. They describe a reflective use of the text and do not show that casting hexagrams predicts events. The "third kind of knowledge" and other links in the principles are the compendium's own bridges.
