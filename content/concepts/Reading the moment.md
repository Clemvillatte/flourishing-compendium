"Reading the moment" names the timing tradition of the Zhouyi, or Book of Changes: the practice of consulting its hexagrams to read the present situation before acting. Traditional accounts describe the first layer of the core text as graphic symbols, the 8 trigrams and 64 hexagrams, allegedly composed by King Wen and the Duke of Zhou in the 11th century BCE, and the first two layers of the classic are oracles from the Western Zhou period ([Hon, SEP, §§1, 2.3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-change/)). The philosophy that grew around the practice is, in Chung-ying Cheng's description as quoted in the entry, "a sustained discussion about time, location, and judgment that are derived from the divinatory practices" ([§1.4](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-change/)).

> "It is from divination that we develop an acute sensitivity to the combined effects of temporal and spatial factors that determine our judgment." (Hon, [Chinese Philosophy of Change (Yijing), §1.4](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-change/))

### What the reading gives

The entry describes the practice from the inside. A divination result reached the inquirer as a code, and the inquirer, aided by experts, had to interpret it; that interpretation, called prognostication, was the most important step, where critical decisions were made ([Hon, §1.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-change/)). An extended passage of the Xici discusses how to perform divination with 50 yarrow stalks ([§1.4](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-change/)). The consultation belongs to a person at a crossroads, with no precedent and no rule book to follow, and the trigrams and hexagrams are intended to capture that moment ([§1.4](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/chinese-change/)).
