Trivers (1972) defines parental investment as "any investment by the parent in an individual offspring that increases the offspring's chance of surviving (and hence reproductive success) at the cost of the parent's ability to invest in other offspring". His reasoning: "the sex whose typical parental investment is greater than that of the opposite sex will become a limiting resource for that sex. Individuals of the sex investing less will compete among themselves to breed with members of the sex investing more" ([Trivers, Parental investment and sexual selection, 1972, PDF](https://courses.washington.edu/ccab/Trivers%20-%20SS%20&%20PI%20-%201972.pdf)). He states the general rule: "What governs the operation of sexual selection is the relative parental investment of the sexes in their offspring."

### What it predicts

Where male investment is comparable to female investment, Trivers expects male and female reproductive success to vary in similar ways and "female choice to be no more discriminating than male choice". Where male investment "strongly exceeds that of the female", he expects females to compete and males to be selective (same paper). The seed's "the sex investing more becomes the choosier one" is correct and the theory makes no claim that it is always females.

### Not the same as parental care

Trivers recalled that his definition differed from parental care and from the cost of parental care, and was an analogy between investing money and investing in offspring to get more offspring in the future ([Writing Trivers's 1972 Theory of Parental Investment and Sexual Selection, Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/45515/chapter/393445523)). Kokko and Jennions add that his argument "hinged on anisogamy" and that sex-role reversal, where females compete more strongly, is associated with greater male than female investment ([Kokko and Jennions, Parental investment, sexual selection and sex ratios, 2008, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1420-9101.2008.01540.x)).

### Debate

The same Kokko and Jennions review says it is "incontrovertible" that Trivers identified a causal link between greater pre-mating female investment and stronger sexual selection on males, yet its model examines how other factors can alter parental investment: offspring need, multiple mating, sexual selection and sex-specific mortality. The review treats investment, sexual selection and sex ratios as interacting, so the seed's one-way phrase "parental effort drives mating dynamics" is a simplification. Trivers himself wrote that sexual selection is "both controlled by the parental investment pattern and a force that tends to mold that pattern" (1972 paper). In humans, Buss applies the theory to say that the higher-investing sex should be "most discriminating", but that is the human-application literature and not Trivers's own empirical tests ([Buss, Human mating strategies, 2002, PDF](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/buss/files/2015/10/Buss-2002-human-mating-strategies.pdf)).
