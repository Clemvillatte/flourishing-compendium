Anger's attributional analysis is the research line that treats anger as what follows a judgment about who is to blame. Novaco's stress-inoculation work is the clinical anchor, and the key research document is his 1979 chapter, "The cognitive regulation of anger and stress," which gives the standard cognitive-behavioral account: "Novaco's (1978) theory indicates that it is individual interpretations of aversive situations through personal scripts and schemas that mediate the transformation of information into behavioural actions" ([Wood and Newton, Kent repository PDF](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/26033/1/PDF_KAR_Wood_&_Newton_paper.pdf)). Novaco's own definition of the state itself: "Anger can be defined as a response to an aversive state consisting of both cognitive and physiological components (Novaco, 1997)" ([same](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/26033/1/PDF_KAR_Wood_&_Newton_paper.pdf)).

### The four cognitive dimensions

The Glasgow PAC research gives the four attributions that the anger literature converges on: "attribution of hostile intent, unfairness, victimhood, and helplessness" ([Willner et al., Glasgow repository PDF](http://eprints.gla.ac.uk/116402/1/116402.pdf)). The measurement follows the same lines: Novaco's Provocation Inventory uses scenarios like "you are in a queue to get something and someone pushes in front of you," rated for anger intensity ([same](http://eprints.gla.ac.uk/116402/1/116402.pdf)).

The clinical treatment literature states the appraisal mechanism plainly: "Anger also increases when the person [...] codes events in highly polarized, negative ways [...], attributes malevolent intent to the perceived source of anger (i.e., hostile attributional bias)" ([DiGiuseppe and Tafrate, anger treatment chapter, PDF](https://biblioteca.cognitivo.com/pdf/bib_9_20210318043216.pdf)). The anger treatment chapter gives the judgment that mobilizes the state: "the person makes the judgment that something did or could happen that should not happen" ([same](https://biblioteca.cognitivo.com/pdf/bib_9_20210318043216.pdf)). Attribution of intent, controllability, and justification are the three appraisal lines that run through the anger research; a negative event read as intended, controllable, and unjust is what the analysis predicts will produce the most anger.

### Why I hold it

My Buddhist principle on the three sources of harm asks a person to distinguish harm from accident, harm from ill intent, and harm that is only the self's own aversion. The attributional literature is the research version of that instruction, and it adds the operational detail: the appraisal is the intervention target, because the appraisal is where the bias lives.
