Mastery orientation is a way of pursuing achievement in which the aim is to grow in competence. An Annual Review chapter defines a mastery goal orientation as "a focus on developing one's abilities, mastering a new skill, trying to accomplish something challenging, and trying to understand learning materials". Success is judged "in terms of self-improvement, and students derive satisfaction from the inherent qualities of the task, such as its interest and challenge" ([Annual Review of Psychology chapter on classroom goal structures, 2006, PDF](http://sohs.pbs.uam.es/webjesus/motiv_ev_autorr/lects%20extranjeras/anual%20review%202006.pdf)). The contrast is a performance orientation, "a focus on demonstrating high ability relative to others", where accomplishment comes "from doing better than others".

### Names for the same idea

Researchers used several labels for roughly the same pair. The chapter lists "task focused versus ability focused" among them and says most researchers "view these goal sets as having sufficient overlap to be treated as conceptually similar constructs". A study of student goals gives the mapping: learning goals (Dweck, 1986), task-involvement (Nicholls, 1984) and mastery goals (Ames, 1992) all concern people who "believe ability is malleable" ([Do Students Distinguish Between Different Types of Performance Goals?, Journal of Experimental Education](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00220973.2012.745464)).

### What it predicts

According to an ERIC summary of Ames's 1992 review, "holding mastery goals is associated with the belief that effort brings success", and with "increased time spent on learning, greater persistence (especial[ly] after failure) and a greater likelihood of using effective strategies for learning, problem solving, and self-regulation" ([ERIC document ED377212](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED377212.pdf)). The Annual Review chapter adds that environments stressing "mastery, understanding, and improving skills and knowledge" go with "the most positive motivation and learning patterns", while competitive settings "can increase the academic performance of some students" but diminish motivation for many.

The link to mindset is partial. A study of early childhood reports a meta-analytic finding that growth mindset beliefs relate positively to mastery goals and negatively to performance goals, "with weaker associations when studying academic domains, and individual studies sometimes show no association or even negative associations" ([Understanding Motivation in Early Childhood, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12837912/)).

### The framework later grew

The simple pair was extended with an approach and avoidance split. The Achievement Goals chapter describes mastery-approach (master a task; improve over time) and mastery-avoidance goals (not fall short of mastering a task; not decline over time), alongside performance-approach and performance-avoidance goals ([Achievement Goals, University of Lausanne repository](https://api.unil.ch/iris/server/api/core/bitstreams/b38d7c34-9025-447c-898e-752050600320/content)). Mastery-approach goals "tend to be most strongly associated with interest-driven processes and outcomes", predicting "deep learning strategies", enjoyment and intrinsic motivation "while often being unrelated to achievement". The same chapter notes that because the goals are conceptually orthogonal, "endorsing the two goals jointly could be adaptive on the whole".

### Limits

Research that only contrasts mastery with performance goals "does not capture the complex motivational processes" in writing, according to the ERIC document. This page describes a research construct measured in student samples. Applying it to adult life and wisdom is an extension.
