Michael Anderson's target article in Behavioral and Brain Sciences (2010) opens by calling reuse "a central organizational principle" of the brain: "it is quite common for neural circuits established for one purpose to be exapted (exploited, recycled, redeployed) during evolution or normal development, and be put to different uses, often without losing their original functions". The article contrasts this with the view of "a functional architecture for the brain whereby individual regions are dedicated to large-scale cognitive domains like vision, audition, language, and the like" ([Anderson, Neural reuse: A fundamental organizational principle of the brain, 2010, PDF](https://web.sas.upenn.edu/rozin/files/2022/06/273ModularPreadaptBBS2010.pdf)).

### How it differs from plasticity

The abstract draws three lines between reuse and ordinary plasticity: circuits "can continue to acquire new uses after an initial or original function is established"; new uses "need not involve unusual circumstances such as injury or loss of established function"; and a new use need not involve "(much) local change to circuit structure" (same article).

### Massive redeployment and its predictions

Anderson's own hypothesis rests on the "simple observation that evolutionary considerations might often favor reusing existing components for new tasks over developing new circuits de novo". Three predictions follow: a typical brain region supports many functions in diverse task categories, evolutionarily older areas are deployed in more functions, and more recent functions use more, and more scattered, areas (same article; also stated in [Anderson, Evolution and neuroscience paper, PDF copy](https://web-archive.southampton.ac.uk/cogprints.org/5017/1/anderson-evol-neuro.pdf)). A summary by other authors of Anderson's neuroimaging meta-analysis reports that of 86 regions activated by at least one task, 66% were activated by tasks from at least three categories ([Neuroemergentism, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6326375/)).

### Criticism

Flusberg's commentary agrees the article marshals strong evidence against strictly modular views, but argues that neural reuse "still suffer[s] from some of the same difficulties these modular views have", because it keeps a fixed "working" for each circuit and still maps high-level functions one-to-one onto a distributed set of circuits ([Flusberg, Neural reuse commentary, PDF](http://www.stephenflusberg.com/uploads/2/6/9/4/26942597/2010_toskosdils_flusberg_-_redeployment.pdf)). A commentary from Claremont notes that Anderson predicts a function's phylogenetic age should correlate with how localized it is in the brain ([Claremont Colleges, PDF](https://scholarship.claremont.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1084&context=pitzer_fac_pub)).

### Reading the seed note

"Recycled across functions rather than dedicated to single tasks" matches the abstract. The theory says regions are not dedicated to single high-level tasks, and it keeps each circuit's low-level contribution constant; it does not say any region can do anything.
