The expectation fulfilment theory of dreaming was proposed by the psychologist Joe Griffin in 1993. It holds that the main function of REM dreaming is to act out, in metaphor, emotional arousals that were not discharged during the previous day ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expectation_fulfilment_theory_of_dreaming)). The compendium's earlier note also names Revonsuo, whose work is a different theory, so this page covers Griffin's account only.

### What the theory says

The Human Givens Institute, which promotes the theory, summarises it in three points: "Dreams are metaphorical translations of waking expectations"; expectations "which cause emotional arousal that is not discharged during the day become dreams during sleep"; and dreaming "deactivates that emotional arousal by completing the expectation pattern metaphorically, freeing the brain to respond afresh to the emotional challenges of each new day" ([Human Givens Institute](https://www.hgi.org.uk/articles/why-we-dream/)). Griffin writes that his dream records showed that "dreams dealt not with emotional concerns per se but with those emotional concerns that had not been dealt with satisfactorily" ([Human Givens Institute](https://www.hgi.org.uk/articles/why-we-dream-to-forget-i-dont-dream/)).

### The case against

Wikipedia states that the theory, while widely validated anecdotally, "is not able to be put to rigorous scientific testing, as interpretations of dream events are necessarily subjective" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expectation_fulfilment_theory_of_dreaming)). A textbook table lists it as "controversial and hard to test" ([Lumen Learning](https://content.one.lumenlearning.com/introductiontopsychology/chapter/reading-dreams/)). A review of Griffin and Tyrrell's book Dreaming Reality in Positive Health calls the theory "too simple". The reviewer argues that it assumes emotional arousal drives dreams, that cognitive factors seem more significant, and that self-reported dream examples are "massively open to expectation effects" ([Positive Health review](https://www.positivehealth.com/review/dreaming-reality-how-dreaming-keeps-us-sane-or-can-drive-us-mad)).

### Scope

The Wikipedia article states that the theory fulfils the criteria for a satisfactory dream theory set out by the sleep researcher G. William Domhoff, but it also notes that Domhoff "did not consider" the theory in his review, so that match is the proponents' claim and not Domhoff's finding. No peer-reviewed test of the theory was found for this page, and the sources above are a proponent's organisation, an encyclopedia article, a textbook table and one review. Treat the theory as a hypothesis, not an established account of why people dream.
