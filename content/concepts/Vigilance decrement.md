The vigilance decrement is the decline in the ability to detect critical signals as time on a monitoring task increases. It is one of the most consistently replicated findings in attention research. The founding studies:

> "The first major laboratory studies of vigilance by Mackworth in 1948 and later revealed a decline in the probability of detecting brief targets as the time on task increases."
> — [Klein & Feltmate, "The vigilance decrement: its first 75 years," Frontiers in Cognition (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13281068/)

Mackworth's Clock Test simulated a radar watch: a pointer stepping around a blank clock face, with rare double jumps as the signal. Operators watching for two hours missed noticeably more signals as the watch went on, with the steepest fall in the first half hour. The finding mattered because the task was easy and the operators were trained and motivated — the decrement was not fatigue or indifference but something about monitoring itself.

The practical discoveries came in the same program, and they are the reason the concept matters for design rather than diagnosis:

> "Importantly, in this monograph, Mackworth discovered that a salient interruption (telephone call or a half-hour break from the task) or the provision of performance feedback could reduce or eliminate the decrement while urging the subject to be especially attentive had no effect."
> — [Klein & Feltmate, Frontiers in Cognition (2025)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13281068/)

Exhortation does nothing; structure does everything. Breaks, interruptions and feedback restore detection. A person cannot, by trying, keep their attention from decaying, but they can arrange the conditions under which it renews.
