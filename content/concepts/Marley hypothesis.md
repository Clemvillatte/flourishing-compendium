The Marley hypothesis proposes that differences in knowledge of Black history help explain racial-group differences in recognizing racism ([Martin & Johnson, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10972773/)).

> "The Marley hypothesis suggests that racial differences in perceptions of racism stem from privileged group members' (e.g., White Americans) lack of knowledge about Black history (Nelson et al., 2013). These authors found that White (compared to Black) Americans lacked knowledge about Critical Black History (i.e., Black history of injustice), which explained their inability to recognize racism, relative to Black Americans." (Martin & Johnson, [You Can't Dismantle What You Don't Recognize](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10972773/), Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2023, "Learning Critical Black History", summarizing Nelson, Adams & Salter 2013)

Two experiments with 1,853 White Americans tested what follows from the gap. Learning Critical Black History raised perspective-taking, which in turn increased racism recognition and acknowledgment, support for anti-racist healthcare policies, and recognition of systemic contributors to Black-White health disparities; a celebratory lesson in Black achievement and a control lesson did neither. As the authors summarize: "learning about Critical Black History in healthcare increased White Americans' perspective-taking and led to more recognition of and support for addressing racism broadly, and racism in healthcare specifically." The hypothesis is not a blame device; it is a knowledge-gap account of why racism goes unrecognized, and an argument that the gap can be taught closed. That is why the concept sits beside the WEIRD critique and the language-as-practice principle in this library: what a group knows shapes what its members can see.
