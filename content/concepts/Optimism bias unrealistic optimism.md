Optimism bias, also called unrealistic optimism, is the finding that people rate their own chances of good events as above average and their chances of bad events as below average. The founding study is Neil Weinstein's "Unrealistic optimism about future life events" (JPSP, 1980). Its abstract:

> Two studies investigated the tendency of people to be unrealistically optimistic about future life events. In Study 1, 258 college students estimated how much their own chances of experiencing 42 events differed from the chances of their classmates. Overall, they rated their own chances to be above average for positive events and below average for negative events, ps<.001.

(Weinstein, 1980, [abstract](https://scispace.com/topics/optimism-1uqbd6e9/1980))

The study's second move is the diagnosis: people "focus on factors that improve their own chances of achieving desirable outcomes and fail to realize that others may have just as many factors in their favor." When a second group of students read the first group's lists of factors, their own optimism dropped, though it did not disappear.

My [[CO039 — Recognise optimism bias — you underestimate your personal risk]] and [[PP016 — Distinguish realistic from unrealistic optimism]] turn this into practice: the bias is not a moral failing but a structural feature of self-judgment, and the countermeasure is deliberately asking what makes me different from the average case.
