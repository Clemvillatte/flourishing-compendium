the method of defining a problem as the barrier between a current state and a goal state, then removing the barrier rather than forcing the outcome (D'Zurilla and Nezu, Problem-Solving Therapy, Springer Publishing).

Problem-solving therapy (PST), developed by D'Zurilla and Goldfried in the 1970s and refined by D'Zurilla and Nezu, treats distress as partly a problem in living: it trains a general coping process rather than disputing thoughts. A scoping review defines the underlying process:

> "Problem solving in real-life contexts (also called *social* problem solving) describes 'the self-directed process by which individuals attempt to identify [ …] adaptive coping solutions for problems, both acute and chronic, that they encounter in everyday living' (p.8) [16]." (Krause et al., [Problem-solving training as an active ingredient of treatment for youth depression, BMC Psychiatry 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8383463/))

### The skills trained

The same review summarizes what PST builds: "PST aims to promote adaptive PS by helping clients foster an optimistic and self-confident attitude towards problems (i.e., a positive problem orientation), and by helping them develop and internalize four core PS skills: (a) defining the problem; (b) brainstorming possible solutions; (c) appraising solutions and selecting the most promising one; (d) implementing the preferred solution and reflecting on the outcome". The seed line above states the compendium's own reading: the problem is the barrier between the current state and the goal state, and the work is removing the barrier rather than forcing the outcome.

### Facets

- **orientation** — the schema side: beliefs and attitudes about problems and one's ability to cope, trained alongside the skills
