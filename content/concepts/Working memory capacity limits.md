Working memory capacity is the number of separate items a person can hold and use at the same time. The classic figure was Miller's seven plus or minus two, but the modern estimate is smaller. In a review of capacity-limit experiments, Nelson Cowan concludes that when rehearsal and grouping strategies are blocked, young adults typically retain three to five separate items, with the average close to four.

> "A single, central capacity limit averaging about four chunks is implicated along with other, noncapacity-limited sources." (Cowan, [The magical number 4 in short-term memory](https://memory.psych.missouri.edu/assets/doc/articles/2001/cowan-bbs-2001.pdf), Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24(1), 2001)

### Why the number fell from seven to four

Miller's 1956 figure was, in Cowan's reading of the history, "meant more as a rough estimate and a rhetorical device than as a real capacity limit". The seven-item performances come from strategies: grouping items into larger familiar units (chunks) and rehearsing them lets a person recall far more than the underlying store holds. When experiments arrange conditions so that items cannot be recoded into bigger chunks, the residual limit shows itself, and it sits near four chunks in normal adults.

The practical meaning for a writer: the bottleneck is real but elastic at the surface. Anything that reduces grouping or rehearsal (unfamiliar material, distraction, time pressure) pushes performance toward the bare limit; expertise does the opposite.
