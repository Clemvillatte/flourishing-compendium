“The insatiability of desire” — Buddhism, Dhammapada; Schopenhauer, World as Will and Representation.

The insatiability of desire is the claim that wanting does not end when it is fed. Two traditions carry the idea in this compendium: the Dhammapada, a Buddhist verse collection, describes craving (tanha, "thirst") as a plant that regrows from its root unless the root is dug out, and Arthur Schopenhauer describes the will as a demand that no attained object can finally quiet.

> "All willing arises from want, therefore from deficiency, and therefore from suffering. The satisfaction of a wish ends it; yet for one wish that is satisfied there remain at least ten which are denied." (Schopenhauer, [The World as Will and Idea, vol. 1, Haldane & Kemp translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/38427), close of § 38)

### The Buddhist image

The Dhammapada's chapter on thirst (chapter XXIV in Max Müller's translation) gives the root image:

> "As a tree, even though it has been cut down, is firm so long as its root is safe, and grows again, thus, unless the feeders of thirst are destroyed, the pain (of life) will return again and again." (Dhammapada 338, [Müller translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2017))

Schopenhauer states the same returning-demand structure in psychological terms: in the passage opened here, every satisfied wish "makes room for a new one", and no attained object of desire gives more than "a fleeting gratification".
