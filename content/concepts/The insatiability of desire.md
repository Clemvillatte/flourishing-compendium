“The insatiability of desire” — Buddhism, Dhammapada; Schopenhauer, World as Will and Representation.

The insatiability of desire is the claim that wanting does not end when it is fed. The Dhammapada, a Buddhist verse collection, pictures craving as a tree cut down but not uprooted; Schopenhauer describes the will as a demand that each satisfied wish only replaces.

> "All willing arises from want, therefore from deficiency, and therefore from suffering. The satisfaction of a wish ends it; yet for one wish that is satisfied there remain at least ten which are denied." (Schopenhauer, [The World as Will and Idea, vol. 1, Haldane & Kemp translation, § 38](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/38427))

### The Buddhist image

The Dhammapada's chapter XXIV, "Thirst" in Max Müller's translation, gives the root image:

> "As a tree, even though it has been cut down, is firm so long as its root is safe, and grows again, thus, unless the feeders of thirst are destroyed, the pain (of life) will return again and again." ([Dhammapada 338, Müller translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/2017))

Read as a pair, the passages make a related point by different routes. Schopenhauer's is a psychology of wishing: every satisfied wish "makes room for a new one", and what an attained object gives is "a fleeting gratification". The Dhammapada's is a diagnosis of thirst (Pali tanha) whose root must be dug out or the pain returns.
