### Definition

The actualizing tendency is Rogers's name for the single motivational force he saw at work in every living thing: the push to develop, preserve, and improve oneself. His 1959 formal statement reads:

> Actualizing tendency. This is the inherent tendency of the organism to develop all its capacities in ways which serve to maintain or enhance the organism. ([Rogers 1959](https://dn710806.ca.archive.org/0/items/30-07-24-rapport-sur-la-cybercriminalit-2024-numerique/rogers-1959.pdf))

The earlier, famous phrasing from Client-Centered Therapy is blunter: "The organism has one basic tendency and striving — to actualize, maintain, and enhance the experiencing organism" (Rogers 1951, p. 487).

### Origin

Rogers built the concept out of Kurt Goldstein's organismic work and his own therapy observations: clients who stopped performing their conditions of worth and started trusting their experience grew, reliably, in directions he had not scripted. The 1959 chapter adds the direction of the tendency, "development toward autonomy and away from heteronomy, or control by external forces" ([Rogers 1959](https://dn710806.ca.archive.org/0/items/30-07-24-rapport-sur-la-cybercriminalit-2024-numerique/rogers-1959.pdf)). Later theory split the notion in two: the overall organismic tendency, and the narrower self-actualizing tendency that follows the self-structure, which can work at cross purposes with it when the self is built on borrowed conditions.

### A real-life example

A plant leans toward the window; nobody taught it the curriculum. A person in a job that fits their actual interests finds energy they cannot explain, while a job of equal difficulty done against the grain drains them by Friday. Rogers's wager is that the difference is not moral fibre but whether the actualizing tendency has a climate to work in.
