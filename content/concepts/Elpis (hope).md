Elpis is the Greek word for hope. Its best-known early appearance is in Hesiod's story of Pandora in the Works and Days. In the Evelyn-White translation, before Pandora opened the jar, "the tribes of men lived on earth remote and free from ills and hard toil." Then "the woman took off the great lid of the jar with her hands and scattered all these and her thought caused sorrow and mischief to men." One thing stays inside: "Only Hope remained there in an unbreakable home within under the rim of the great jar, and did not fly out at the door" ([Hesiod, Works and Days, trans. Evelyn-White, lines 90-100](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HesiodWorksDays.html)).

### Is Hope a blessing or another evil

The lines do not say. Hope is inside a jar of troubles, and the passage never calls it a good. Elsewhere in the same poem Hesiod is plainly wary of it when it replaces work: "The idle man who waits on empty hope, lacking a livelihood, lays to heart mischief-making; it is not an wholesome hope that accompanies a need man who lolls at ease while he has no sure livelihood" ([Works and Days, the passage between lines 493 and 502](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HesiodWorksDays.html)). The qualification matters here. Hesiod sets a hope that waits against the industrious man who prospers his house and the command to "build barns" before winter.

### Plato's Gorgias

Plato's Gorgias is sometimes brought into the discussion. In Jowett's translation, the myth that closes the dialogue has Zeus say: "I will deprive men of the foreknowledge of death, which they possess at present: this power which they have Prometheus has already received my orders to take from them" ([Plato, Gorgias, trans. Jowett, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1672)). That is a passage about not knowing the hour of one's death. The word hope does not appear in this passage, so nothing here shows that the Gorgias teaches anything about elpis. The connection is at most that both myths concern what human beings are spared from seeing ahead.
