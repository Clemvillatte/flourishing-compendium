The place theory of hearing holds that pitch is coded by location: each frequency of sound sets the basilar membrane vibrating most strongly at one particular place, and the brain reads the place, not the wave. The account, from the review of place and temporal theories of pitch:

> The responses of the basilar membrane are sharply tuned and highly specific: at low to medium sound levels, a certain frequency will cause only a local region of the basilar membrane to vibrate. Because of its structural properties, the apical end of the basilar membrane responds best to low frequencies, whereas the basal end responds best to high frequencies. Thus, every place along the basilar membrane has its own "best frequency" or "characteristic frequency" (CF) — the frequency to which that place responds most strongly.

(Carlyon, "Revisiting place and temporal theories of pitch," [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4215732/))

The frequency-to-place mapping is called tonotopic organization, and it "is maintained throughout the auditory pathways up to primary auditory cortex." The idea originates with Helmholtz, who argued in On the Sensations of Tone that the basilar membrane worked like tuned piano strings. Georg von Békésy's experiments on human cadavers won the 1961 Nobel Prize and confirmed the geometry: the wave's amplitude "is dependent upon the frequency of the stimulus," peaking near the base for high frequencies and near the apex for low ones.
