“Habermas” — 1981.

The date points to Jürgen Habermas's The Theory of Communicative Action (1981), the start of his mature work and a framework that combines a theory of meaning and action, a typology of social action, a social ontology and a critical social theory. This page summarises that work and the discourse rules the compendium draws on, using the Stanford Encyclopedia's entry; the 1981 book itself was not opened.

### Communicative action

At the centre is a contrast between communicative action, aimed at agreement, and instrumental or strategic action, aimed at success. Habermas's basic idea in formal pragmatics is that "We understand a speech-act when we know what makes it acceptable". Every speech-act, on his account, makes claims to truth, rightness and sincerity, and the hearer can accept or reject it on any of the three grounds. The entry reports that he treats communicative action as the "basic form of action", with strategic action "parasitic" on it. ([Stanford Encyclopedia, section 2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/habermas/))

### Lifeworld and system

Habermas pairs communicative action with the lifeworld, the background stock of cultural knowledge that is "always already" familiar to agents. He pairs strategic action with systems, mainly the economy and bureaucracy, which run through the steering media of money and power. Societies, he says, should be seen "simultaneously as systems and lifeworlds". His worry is that people in spheres steered by money and power drift toward instrumental and strategic orientations. ([Stanford Encyclopedia, section 2.3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/habermas/))

### Rules of discourse

The entry lists rules that govern argument as a rule-governed practice, designed to leave only the "unforced force of the better argument":

> "(1) Every subject with the competence to speak and act is allowed to take part in a discourse. (2) a. Everyone is allowed to question any assertion whatever. b. Everyone is allowed to introduce any assertion whatever into the discourse. c. Everyone is allowed to express his attitudes, desires, and needs. (3) No speaker may be prevented, by internal or external coercion, from exercising his rights as laid down in (1) and (2)" (Stanford Encyclopedia, section 3.1)

The entry notes that the list is a representative sample borrowed from Robert Alexy, not a complete list from Habermas.

### Where critics push back

Commentators disagree about whether the theory's central claims are established. The entry says that "most commentators agree that Habermas fails to establish the irreducibility thesis by argument", the thesis that communicative rationality cannot be reduced to instrumental rationality. Other critics deny that strategic action is necessarily selfish, and that economic or bureaucratic spheres force a single type of action. Some think the basic distinction is still useful even if his own argument is not clinching. ([Stanford Encyclopedia, section 2.2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/habermas/))
