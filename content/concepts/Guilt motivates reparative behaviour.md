Guilt is the pain of a specific act, and its adaptive job is to move the person who did the harm toward undoing it. The claim's anchor is June Tangney's research programme, summarised in Tangney, Stuewig and Mashek's 2007 review "Moral emotions and moral behavior": "guilt, on balance, is the more moral or adaptive emotion. Guilt appears to motivate reparative action, foster other-oriented empathy, and promote constructive strategies for coping with anger" ([Tangney et al., Annu Rev Psychol 58 (2007), p. 351, quoted in PhilArchive](https://philarchive.org/archive/COVIME); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16953797/)).

### Why guilt repairs and shame hides

The distinction is about focus. Guilt is pain about a behavior, "I did that bad thing"; shame is pain about the whole self, "I am bad," and the two produce opposite action tendencies: guilt motivates confession, apology and efforts to undo the harm, while shame motivates denial, hiding and escape ([PMC, Shame, Guilt and Remorse](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3328863/)). Guilt keeps the deed somewhat apart from the self, so "people stricken with guilt are drawn to consider their behavior and its consequences, rather than feeling compelled to defend the self" ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3328863/)).

The consensus has limits worth naming. Later work argues that either emotion can be adaptive or maladaptive "depending on contextual factors and the regulation strategies used," and proposes redrawn criteria: guilt implies perceived "power and willingness to be harmful," shame implies perceived "lack of power" to meet one's ideal self ([PMC, Reconsidering the Differences](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6143989/)).
