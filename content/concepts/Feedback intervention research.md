Feedback intervention research is the body of controlled studies on what happens when someone is given information about their performance. Its landmark is Avraham Kluger and Angelo DeNisi's 1996 meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin, which pulled together a century of feedback experiments and found that the average effect conceals a split verdict.

> "A meta-analysis (607 effect sizes; 23,663 observations) suggests that FIs improved performance on average (d =.41) but that over 1/3 of the FIs decreased performance." — [Kluger & DeNisi, Psychological Bulletin 119(2) (1996), 254–284, abstract](https://cris.huji.ac.il/en/publications/the-effects-of-feedback-interventions-on-performance-a-historical/)

### What the numbers rest on

The review screened roughly 3,000 studies reaching back to 1905 and kept only 131 that met its design criteria; those reported 607 effect sizes across 12,652 participants. In 50 of the 131 studies, about 38 percent, feedback actually lowered performance. For the flow principle that cites this research, the honest reading is that specific, task-directed feedback earns the positive average, while feedback that pulls attention toward the self often pays the cost.
