Narrative empathy is the sharing of feeling and perspective that comes from engaging with stories. The psychological account the compendium draws on is Raymond Mar and Keith Oatley's 2008 theory of fiction, in which literary narratives work as simulations of social life: readers run the social situations in their minds and feel the emotions the characters feel. Mar and Oatley's argument, in their abstract, is that stories "offer models or simulations of the social world via abstraction, simplification, and compression."

> "The simulation of social experience that literary narratives afford provides an opportunity for empathic growth." (Mar and Oatley, [The Function of Fiction is the Abstraction and Simulation of Social Experience](https://www.yorku.ca/mar/mar%20&%20oatley%202008_PPS_function%20of%20fiction.pdf), Perspectives on Psychological Science 3(3), 2008, p. 181)

### Why stories reach the feelings

The theory separates two things stories do. They model the social world in simplified form, which makes the social information easier to take in, and they induce an immersive experience in which the reader mentally enacts the events. Empathy can enter in that second part, as the reader rehearses the characters' beliefs and emotions at a safe distance. The training reaches beyond the characters too: the same passage says fiction "trains us to extend our understanding toward other people" and, ultimately, toward understanding ourselves. Fictional literature "brings close attention to distant worlds that would otherwise remain unknown" (p. 181).
