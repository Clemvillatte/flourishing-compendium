"Wu (non-being)" — Laozi, Daodejing 11. In the Daoist vocabulary, wu is what is absent, empty, or not-yet, and the chapter the original entry names makes its case with three objects:

> "The thirty spokes unite in the one nave; but it is on the empty space (for the axle), that the use of the wheel depends. Clay is fashioned into vessels; but it is on their empty hollowness, that their use depends. The door and windows are cut out (from the walls) to form an apartment; but it is on the empty space (within), that its use depends. Therefore, what has a (positive) existence serves for profitable adaptation, and what has not that for (actual) usefulness." (Laozi, Daodejing, ch. XI, trans. James Legge, [Project Gutenberg #216](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

The wheel is wood plus a hole; the vessel is clay plus a hollow; the room is walls plus an opening. In each case the useful part is the part that is not there. The chapter does not say that being is worthless ("what has a (positive) existence serves for profitable adaptation"), only that the usefulness runs through what being frames and leaves out.

Non-being is also one half of a pair, and chapter 2 gives the pairing's rhythm:

> "So it is that existence and non-existence give birth the one to (the idea of) the other; that difficulty and ease produce the one (the idea of) the other..." (Laozi, Daodejing, ch. II, trans. James Legge, same source)

The Buddhist parallels cite this page, and the comparison holds only so far: shunyata in the Madhyamaka tradition is an argument about the emptiness of essence in all things, while wu is a way of talking about the absent and the uncarved as what works. The compendium's principles use wu for the practical point: rest, silence, the unplanned hour, the empty room are the load-bearing part of a life rather than its leftover.
