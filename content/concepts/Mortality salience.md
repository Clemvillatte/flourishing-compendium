Mortality salience names the experimental reminder of death used to test terror management theory (TMT). In the paradigm, participants are asked to think about their own death (or given a subtler death reminder) while controls answer neutral questions; the theory predicts the reminder will change how people defend their worldviews and self-esteem. Greenberg, Solomon and Pyszczynski introduced TMT in 1986, and a later meta-analytic team states the hypothesis this way:

> "TMT postulates that investment in cultural worldviews and self-esteem serves to buffer the potential for death anxiety; the MS hypothesis states that, as a consequence, accessibility of death-related thought (MS) should instigate increased worldview and self-esteem defense and striving." (Burke, Martens and Faucher, [Two decades of terror management theory: a meta-analysis of mortality salience research, PSPR 14(2), 2010, PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20097885/), abstract)

### What the accumulated experiments show

The meta-analysis covered 164 articles with 277 experiments. Across them, mortality salience yielded moderate effects (r = .35) on a range of worldview- and self-esteem-related outcomes, with larger effects for experiments using American participants, college students, a longer delay between the reminder and the outcome measure, and people-related attitudes as the outcome (Burke, Martens and Faucher, 2010, abstract). The same record cautions that gender and self-esteem may moderate the effects differently than previously thought, and that alternative explanations of TMT were part of the comparison.
