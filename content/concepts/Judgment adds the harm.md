Judgment adds the harm is the Stoic claim that events do not disturb people; the opinions about the events do. The doctrine is Enchiridion 5: "Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things: for example, death is nothing terrible, for if it were, it would have seemed so to Socrates; for the opinion about death, that it is terrible, is the terrible thing" ([Epictetus, Enchiridion 5, Perseus translation](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0557.tlg002.perseus-eng3:5/?right=perseus-eng4)). The chapter adds the grading of progress: the uninstructed blame others, the beginner blames himself, the finished learner blames neither.

### The mechanism

The Stoic psychology behind the line treats emotions as judgments. An impression arrives, death, loss, insult, and the judgment that it is dreadful supplies the dread. The practice is to examine the impression before assenting to it, which is why Epictetus elsewhere says: "distinguish within your own mind... 'It's not the accident that distresses this person... it is the judgment which he makes about it'" ([Enchiridion texts collection](https://www.ldysinger.com/@texts/0110_epictetus/02_epictetus_texts.htm)). Albert Ellis, who built rational-emotive behaviour therapy on exactly this doctrine, opened his commentary on the chapter by citing the line ([Donald Robertson, commentary on Handbook 5](https://donaldrobertson.substack.com/p/it-is-not-things-that-disturb-us)).

### The Buddhist parallel

The Pali canon carries the same distinction in the Sallatha Sutta's two arrows: "when touched with a feeling of pain, the uninstructed run-of-the-mill person sorrows, grieves, & laments, beats his breast, becomes distraught. So he feels two pains, physical & mental. Just as if they were to shoot a man with an arrow and, right afterward, were to shoot him with another one," while the well-instructed disciple "feels one pain: physical, but not mental" ([SN 36.6, Thanissaro translation](https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn36/sn36.006.than.html)). The compendium's B069 and B182 carry this arrow directly, as a comparison tradition rather than a shared doctrine.

### In the compendium

The principles apply the doctrine across cases. A116 examines fear as the judgment-dependent passion; SP037 warns against the mob's judgments; SP020 converts pity, itself a judgment, into compassion and equity; D088's empty boat works the same separation when the "offense" has no author; ST085's quarrel at Kosambī shows a community adding arrows; and K360, K259, K258 carry the judgment discipline into professional practice, where particulars and stakeholders come before rules.
