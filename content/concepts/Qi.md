Qi (氣) is the Chinese term for vital energy or life force — the flowing, aliveness-bearing energy that classical Chinese thought treats as permeating all living beings and the cosmos. The most enduring interpretation of yinyang in Chinese thought is related to the concept of qi, with yin and yang seen as qi (in both yin and yang forms) operating in the universe ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)). Here, yin and yang are the qi of the universe; these qi flow within the natural as well as the human worlds, and they are the basic fabric of existence ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)). Thus qi, a force arising from the interplay between yin and yang, becomes a context in which yinyang is seated and functions ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)). In Chinese medicine, Daoist ideas about the flow of qi and the balance of yin and yang have contributed to theories of health and healing ([Fabrizio Musacchio](https://www.fabriziomusacchio.com/weekend_stories/told/2025/2025-01-02-daoism/)). Daoist internal alchemy (neidan) involved meditation, breathing exercises, and dietary practices aimed at refining the body's vital energies ([Fabrizio Musacchio](https://www.fabriziomusacchio.com/weekend_stories/told/2025/2025-01-02-daoism/)).

## What it is NOT


Qi is not a substance in the Western physical sense. The classical texts describe it as a dynamic and natural form of flowing energy, a complementary in the primordial potency of the universe, that takes on various forms and is convertible from one form to another with order and pattern ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)) — a description of how living systems behave, not a chemical hypothesis. It is not a fixed endowment but a flow that can be cultivated: when qi moved, the clear and light rose to be heaven and the muddy and heavy fell to become earth, and when these two qi interacted and attained the stage of harmony (he), human life began ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)).

The classical applications of qi are found in both practice and clinical doctrine. The Huangdi neijing (The Yellow Emperor's Classic of Internal Medicine) provides a detailed account of physiological functions and pathological changes in the body and guidance for diagnosis and treatment in terms of yinyang ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)). In that framework, the activity or function of each organ is its yang aspect while its substance is its yin aspect; the zang organs (kidneys, liver, heart, spleen, lungs) are classified as yin and control the storage of vital substance and qi, while the fu organs (gallbladder, stomach, small and large intestines, urinary bladder, triple burner) are yang and control the transport and digestion of food ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)). Yin and yang do not exist in isolation but are in a dynamic state in which they interact and fashion the complicated and intricate system of the human body ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)).

Note on evidence: the metaphysical claims of qi theory are not the same as the empirical claims of the practices built around them. The former are classical doctrine; the latter can be evaluated on their own terms without taking a position on the former.

## Practical implications


For flourishing, the concept's value is as a reminder that vitality is something cultivated through practice rather than a fixed endowment — that rest, breath, movement, and nourishment are not optional extras but the medium of a functioning life. Concretely: treat breathing as something that can be practised; treat energy as something that can be depleted by over-exertion and restored by rest, rather than as an infinite reserve; and treat the body as a system whose balance must be continually maintained rather than a machine that either works or does not.
