The book of nature is a long-lived metaphor in Christian and early modern thought: the visible world as a text written by God, open to anyone who learns its characters. Its most famous formulation is Galileo's, in The Assayer of 1623, dropped into a quarrel about comets.

> "Philosophy is written in this grand book, the universe, which stands continually open to our gaze. But it cannot be understood unless one first learns to comprehend the language and recognize the letters in which it is composed. It is written in the language of mathematics, and its characters are triangles, circles and other geometric figures without which it is humanly impossible to understand a single word of it. Without these, one wanders about in a dark labyrinth." (Galileo, The Assayer 1623, quoted in [Biagioli, Stress in the book of nature: the supplemental logic of Galileo's realism](https://www.abdn.ac.uk/media/site/modernthought/content-images/booknature.pdf), MLN 118, 2003)

### What the metaphor claimed

The topos predates Galileo. As Mario Biagioli summarizes the history, since the late medieval period nature had been represented as a book that, like the Scripture, had signs, meanings, and secrets for the reader to interpret. The older version was democratic about its readership: the Interdisciplinary Encyclopedia's account records that the Fathers of the Church used to say the Book of Nature was readable by everyone, even the illiterate, the obstacles being the absence of contemplative spirit and humility and, for medieval theologians, human sin ([The Metaphor of the Book of Nature](https://inters.org/Galilei-book-of-nature)). Galileo's version changed both the language and the readership: the book is written in mathematics, so "it can be read only by those who know this language", and the true obstacle is "just the ignorance of geometry and mathematics". His two-books argument was strategic: in Biagioli's words, "By positing the existence of two equally divine and true books, Galileo was trying to turn a hierarchical relationship between theology and astronomy into a parallel one".

### The honest limit

Historians read the metaphor as an argument made under pressure, not a neutral description of how nature is known. In Biagioli's words, "The topos of the book of nature did not emerge as an abstract methodological reflection, but as a context-specific response to critics who had invoked the absolute authority of another book: the Scripture". He calls its logic supplemental: nature had to be a sacred book to carry authority in the theologians' regime of truth, but to be the domain of things-in-themselves it "could be neither a book (an instance of human writing) nor a Book (a divinely-inspired text)". The break with the older topos was real: earlier the book required interpretation, while Galileo's nature became, in Biagioli's summary of Bono's point, a text that could be "read but not interpreted".
