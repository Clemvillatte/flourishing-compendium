The aesthetic is Søren Kierkegaard's name for the first sphere of existence: the life lived for the moment, the interesting, the enjoyable, before any commitment shapes it. The Stanford Encyclopedia introduces the schema:

> "A common way to interpret Kierkegaard's thought is in terms of three 'existence-spheres': the aesthetic, the ethical and the religious." ([Lippitt & Evans, SEP, Kierkegaard](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/))

### What the aesthetic life is

The first volume of Either/Or presents it through the papers of a nameless young man, "A", who "in a series of sometimes witty, sometimes rambling, sometimes astute and sometimes despairing writings, presents a view of what it means to live 'aesthetically'". As is clearest in his essay "Crop Rotation", he "sets himself the task of living to avoid boredom at all costs" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)). The aesthetic life is "the ceaseless pursuit of 'the interesting'", but it carries an irony: the aesthete brings "an attitude of disinterested, disengaged contemplation to life as a whole, viewing it as a spectacle to be savored rather than as involving 'tasks' or projects to be fulfilled. His is the view of a spectator rather than a player" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)).

The life has a built-in flaw the book is built to expose. "A" himself realizes "that his lifestyle has led to a kind of despair", and the ethicist Judge William urges him to "choose despair": to "recognize that despair arises from, and is essential to, the aesthetic life, and that an authentic self cannot be achieved thorough the aesthetic life alone" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)). One way to gloss the claim that "A" lacks a self is that "the aesthetic life is one of self-alienation" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)). The aesthetic is not simply left behind, though: "Kierkegaard consistently maintains that all human life contains an aesthetic dimension, so the aesthetic is never really left behind" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)).
