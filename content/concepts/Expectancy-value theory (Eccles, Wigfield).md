Expectancy-value theory explains why people choose and persist at tasks by two beliefs: how well they expect to do, and how much the task is worth to them. Eccles and Wigfield (2020) summarise the original position this way: "expectancies for success (ESs) and subjective task values (STVs) are the most proximal psychological determinants of task and activity choice, performance, and engagement in the chosen activities" ([Eccles and Wigfield, 2020](https://msofc.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/2020-Situated-Expectancy-Value-Theory.pdf)).

### What counts as value

The theory splits subjective task value into four parts. Eccles proposed that "the overall value of a given task would be composed at least of four main person-task characteristics/constructs: - intrinsic value, attainment value, utility value, and cost" ([Eccles and Wigfield, 2020](https://msofc.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/2020-Situated-Expectancy-Value-Theory.pdf)). In the authors' own words:

- Intrinsic value is "the anticipated enjoyment one expects to gain from doing the task".
- Utility value is "how well a particular task fits into an individual's present or future plans".
- Attainment value is "the relative personal/identity-based importance attached by individuals to engage in various tasks or activities".
- Cost sits inside the value box because the authors treat value as "a net value derived from both the relative benefits and costs" of the available options. They list effort cost, opportunity cost and emotional cost.

Attainment value is the part closest to the question of who you are. A task matters more when it lets you express what you see as central to yourself.

### Not a simple multiplication

The popular shorthand "motivation = expectancy times value" goes further than the authors do. They report that researchers who tested whether expectancies and values "interact to influence their achievement" found that these interactions "add small but reliable increments in predictive validity" ([Eccles and Wigfield, 2020](https://msofc.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/2020-Situated-Expectancy-Value-Theory.pdf)). Their 2000 review defines the constructs and reviews how they relate to performance and choice among children and adolescents ([Wigfield and Eccles, 2000, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10620382/)). The 2002 Annual Review chapter places the model among theories that "integrate expectancies and values" ([Eccles and Wigfield, 2002, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11752481/)).

### Scope

The research behind the model comes mainly from education and child development. The 2020 article extends the theory to a developmental and sociocultural account, and presents it as a renamed and widened version, situated expectancy-value theory. Applying it to adult life, for example to career or purpose questions, is an extension of the evidence.
