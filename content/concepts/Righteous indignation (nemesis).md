“Righteous indignation (nemesis)” — Aristotle, NE IV.9.

Righteous indignation is Aristotle's name for the pain felt at good fortune that someone has not deserved. He treats it as the right-hand middle between two worse states, envy and spite, and as a sign of good character. The seed cites NE IV.9, but that chapter is Aristotle's discussion of shame; the passage on indignation sits in Book II, chapter 7 (the survey of the virtues) and in the Rhetoric, Book II, chapter 9.

### The mean between envy and spite

In W. D. Ross's translation, Book II, chapter 7 gives the shortest statement:

> "Righteous indignation is a mean between envy and spite, and these states are concerned with the pain and pleasure that are felt at the fortunes of our neighbours; the man who is characterized by righteous indignation is pained at undeserved good fortune, the envious man, going beyond him, is pained at all good fortune, and the spiteful man falls so far short of being pained that he even rejoices." (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.7, trans. Ross, [MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.ii.html))

He adds that there will be "an opportunity of describing" these states elsewhere. The Rhetoric treats indignation at greater length.

### Why it counts as good character

In Roberts's translation of the Rhetoric, indignation is the feeling "most directly opposed to pity", and both are signs of good character.

> "it is our duty both to feel sympathy and pity for unmerited distress, and to feel indignation at unmerited prosperity; for whatever is undeserved is unjust, and that is why we ascribe indignation even to the gods." (Aristotle, Rhetoric II.9, trans. Roberts, [MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/rhetoric.2.ii.html))

Envy, he adds, is not the same thing, even though they seem close. Envy is "excited not by the prosperity of the undeserving but by that of people who are like us or equal with us".

### Aquinas on anger

The seed's second source, Summa Theologiae II-II, q.158, is about anger, not indignation by name. In article 1 Aquinas argues that anger is evil only when it departs from right reason: "if one is angry in accordance with right reason, one's anger is deserving of praise." ([New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3158.htm)) That supports a measured anger in general. It does not speak to Aristotle's specific case of pain at undeserved good fortune.
