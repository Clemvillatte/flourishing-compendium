"Hope tempered by prudence" is this wiki's label, not a Stoic slogan. It names a pattern that can be read in Seneca and Epictetus: hope is treated as a thing to weigh, because it travels with fear and can mislead. Quotations below are from English translations I opened; the Greek and Latin were not consulted.

### Seneca: hope and fear keep step

In Letter 5, Seneca passes on a saying he found in the writings of Hecato: "Cease to hope... and you will cease to fear." He explains why the two go together: "Just as the same chain fastens the prisoner and the soldier who guards him, so hope and fear, dissimilar as they are, keep step together; fear follows hope." The shared root is "a mind that is in suspense, a mind that is fretted by looking forward to the future", and "the chief cause of both these ills is that we do not adapt ourselves to the present, but send our thoughts a long way ahead" ([Seneca, Letter 5, Roman Letters](https://romanletters.org/letters/seneca/5/)). He closes the letter with: "The present alone can make no man wretched."

### Seneca: weigh hope as you weigh fear

Letter 13 is more practical. Seneca advises: "not to be unhappy before the crisis comes; since it may be that the dangers before which you paled as if they were threatening you, will never come upon you". If fear persists: "in this matter, let prudence help you, and contemn with a resolute spirit even when it is in plain sight. If you cannot do this, counter one weakness with another, and temper your fear with hope." Then the balancing clause: "There is nothing so certain among these objects of fear that it is not more certain still that things we dread sink into nothing and that things we hope for mock us. Accordingly, weigh carefully your hopes as well as your fears" ([Letters From a Stoic, PDF edition](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/610bf0315fc547374dfb1e7a/t/613f6bc335b82f51c74105b9/1631546380377/Letters+From+a+Stoic+-+Lucius+Seneca.pdf); the passage is in [Letter 13 on Roman Letters](https://romanletters.org/letters/seneca/13/)).

Note how the two letters differ. Letter 5 repeats a saying that hope should be dropped to drop fear. Letter 13 uses hope on purpose, as a counterweight, and then tests it.

### Epictetus: what desire promises

Enchiridion 2 states what desire and aversion each assume: "desire contains in it the profession (hope) of obtaining that which you desire; and the profession (hope) in aversion... is that you will not fall into that which you attempt to avoid" (Long's translation, [Standard Ebooks](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/epictetus/short-works/george-long/text/the-enchiridion)). Epictetus then draws a limit: a person who desires something outside their control "must be unfortunate". In the other translation I opened, where acting is unavoidable, one should "do even this with discretion and gentleness and moderation" ([Enchiridion, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45109/45109-h/45109-h.htm)).

### Scope

I did not locate a Stoic passage that says "hope tempered by realism" in those words, which the earlier seed attributed to Epictetus. Treat the phrase as the wiki's paraphrase.
