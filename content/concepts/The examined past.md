In [On Anger 3.36](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Of_Anger/Book_III), Seneca describes a daily review of conduct. The heading is the compendium's own label for that review; Seneca gives it no name beyond the custom he reports.

> "The spirit ought to be brought up for examination daily." (Seneca, De Ira 3.36, Aubrey Stewart translation, 1900, [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Of_Anger/Book_III))

The custom was Sextius's first, and the questions Seneca records are pointed at habits rather than events:

> "What bad habit of yours have you cured to-day? what vice have you checked? in what respect are you better?" (Seneca, De Ira 3.36, describing Sextius's custom, Aubrey Stewart translation, 1900, [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Of_Anger/Book_III))

He reports following the custom himself: when the lamp is taken out of his sight and his wife, who knows his habit, has stopped talking, he goes over the whole day, repeating what he said and did, and hides nothing from himself. Seneca pardons each fault on the condition of not repeating it, and the corrections he records concern manner as much as content: arguing too contentiously, reprimanding someone with more freedom than he ought. The review closes with a reward he names directly: "how sweet is the sleep which follows this self-examination" (De Ira 3.36). The questions make the practice more than a diary. A record of events can be turned into an indictment or an alibi; the nightly questions ask what changed in the person doing the reviewing, and the pardon comes with a condition attached. That is the use the compendium's principles make of the concept: memory work that checks the past's tendency to flatter itself, whether as rosy retrospection or as comparison with other people's edited versions.
