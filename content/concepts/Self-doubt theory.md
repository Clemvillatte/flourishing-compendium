Self-doubt theory is the research program on self-doubt: lasting doubt about whether one's own competence is equal to one's undertakings. A 2012 review by Matthew Braslow, Jean Guerrettaz, Robert Arkin and Kathryn Oleson states the sense of the term the researchers use.

> "Given that, self-doubt as we use the concept should be described as doubt about one's feelings of self-competence." (Braslow, Guerrettaz, Arkin & Oleson, [Self-Doubt, Social and Personality Psychology Compass 6(6), 2012](http://www.homepages.se.edu/cvonbergen/files/2013/01/Self-Doubt.pdf))

The review separates self-doubt from neighboring constructs. Self-efficacy is an expectation about performance in one domain; self-doubt reaches further, into how one views oneself.

> "Self-efficacy is generally regarded as an expectation about how one will perform in a given domain; by contrast, self-doubt reaches beyond a specific sense of performance efficacy, reflecting an individual's investment - even rumination - over how to view themselves." (same review)

### The strategies doubt motivates

The review identifies a set of strategies people use to manage doubt about competence.

> "In sum, impostor feelings, self-handicapping, other-enhancement, overachievement, and perhaps still other maneuvers may comprise a constellation of diverse yet common strategies that reflect comparable, but distinct, ways to manage one's self-doubts about competence." (same review)

Self-handicapping, arranging an obstacle before a performance, is in the authors' words "perhaps the most widely cited strategy for managing one's self-doubt about competence". Subjective overachievement takes the opposite route, and the review describes it as involving "the exertion of extra effort in order to avoid failure". Other-enhancement attributes a loss to some advantage enjoyed by an opponent, and people with impostor feelings "often cite luck, timing, or good fortune" as the key to their successes. The strategies differ, and the best-known one has a documented price: the most persuasive handicaps interfere with performance itself, so protecting the perception of competence "carries with it the cost of not enjoying success".
