**Self-doubt theory** is the compendium's heading for the research program on chronic doubt about one's own competence: not a moment of hesitation before a task, but a lasting uncertainty about whether one's ability is equal to one's undertakings, joined to preoccupation with prospective failure and negative evaluation. The construct was developed by Robert Arkin, Kathryn Oleson and colleagues, and the review that consolidated it states its scope plainly.

> "Given that, self-doubt as we use the concept should be described as doubt about one's feelings of self-competence." (Braslow, Guerrettaz, Arkin & Oleson, [Self-Doubt](http://www.homepages.se.edu/cvonbergen/files/2013/01/Self-Doubt.pdf), Social and Personality Psychology Compass 6(6), 2012)

### The strategies doubt motivates

The review's central observation is that chronic doubt about competence does not stay private; it motivates recognizable protective maneuvers.

> "In sum, impostor feelings, self-handicapping, other-enhancement, overachievement, and perhaps still other maneuvers may comprise a constellation of diverse yet common strategies that reflect comparable, but distinct, ways to manage one's self-doubts about competence." (same source)

Self-handicapping, in which a person arranges an obstacle before a performance, is in the authors' words "perhaps the most widely cited strategy for managing one's self-doubt about competence". Subjective overachievement takes the opposite route, exerting extra effort to avoid failure, and other-enhancement attributes a loss to some advantage enjoyed by an opponent. Impostor feelings read one's own successes as luck or timing rather than ability. Each strategy protects the belief that one is competent, and each carries a cost: the most persuasive handicaps, the review notes, interfere with performance itself, so protecting the perception of competence "carries with it the cost of not enjoying success".
