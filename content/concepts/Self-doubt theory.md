Self-doubt theory is this compendium's heading for the psychological study of self-doubt. Braslow, Guerrettaz, Arkin and Oleson, in a 2012 review, use self-doubt to mean doubt about one's own competence.

> "Given that, self-doubt as we use the concept should be described as doubt about one's feelings of self-competence." (Braslow, Guerrettaz, Arkin & Oleson, [Self-Doubt, Social and Personality Psychology Compass 6(6), 2012, p. 471](http://www.homepages.se.edu/cvonbergen/files/2013/01/Self-Doubt.pdf))

The review separates self-doubt from neighboring constructs. Self-efficacy is an expectation about performance in one domain; self-doubt reaches further, into how one views oneself.

> "Self-efficacy is generally regarded as an expectation about how one will perform in a given domain; by contrast, self-doubt reaches beyond a specific sense of performance efficacy, reflecting an individual's investment – even rumination – over how to view themselves." ([same review, p. 472](http://www.homepages.se.edu/cvonbergen/files/2013/01/Self-Doubt.pdf))

### The strategies doubt motivates

The review identifies a set of strategies people use to manage doubt about competence.

> "In sum, impostor feelings, self-handicapping, other-enhancement, overachievement, and perhaps still other maneuvers may comprise a constellation of diverse yet common strategies that reflect comparable, but distinct, ways to manage one's self-doubts about competence." ([same review, p. 478](http://www.homepages.se.edu/cvonbergen/files/2013/01/Self-Doubt.pdf))

Self-handicapping, arranging an obstacle before a performance, is in the authors' words "perhaps the most widely cited strategy for managing one's self-doubt about competence" (p. 475). Subjective overachievement takes the opposite route, and the review describes it as involving "the exertion of extra effort in order to avoid failure". Other-enhancement attributes a loss to some advantage enjoyed by an opponent, and people with impostor feelings "often cite luck, timing, or good fortune" as the key to their successes. For self-handicapping, the review notes that handicaps can interfere with performance itself, so protecting the perception of competence "carries with it the cost of not enjoying success".
