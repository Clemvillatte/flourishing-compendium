Prosocial behavior is the family of actions the article lists as "such as helping, sharing, donating, co-operating, and volunteering" ([Wikipedia, Prosocial behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosocial_behavior)). The article adds a nuance worth keeping: "The person may or may not intend to benefit others; the behavior's prosocial benefits are often only calculable after the fact" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosocial_behavior)). Motives range from empathy and concern for others' welfare to egoistic or practical concerns like social status, and the same outward act can ride on any of them. The compendium's seed cites the finding that made the topic a fixture of well-being research: Dunn, Aknin and Norton's 2008 Science paper on prosocial spending, which reported that "spending more of one's income on others predicted greater happiness both cross-sectionally (in a nationally representative survey study) and longitudinally (in a field study of windfall spending)" ([Dunn, Aknin and Norton, 2008](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/DunnAkninNorton2008.pdf)).

### The flagship experiment

The experiment people remember is the envelope study: 46 participants received $5 or $20 in the morning, were randomly assigned to spend it on themselves or someone else by 5 that day, and reported their happiness in the evening. "Participants who were randomly assigned to spend money on others experienced greater happiness than those assigned to spend money on themselves" ([Dunn et al. 2008](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/DunnAkninNorton2008.pdf)). The paper's sting sits in a separate sample: 109 students asked to pick the condition that would make them happiest "were doubly wrong about the impact of money on happiness; we found that a significant majority thought that personal spending (n = 69) would make them happier than prosocial spending (n = 40) (P < 0.01) and that $20 (n = 94) would make them happier than $5 (n = 15) (P < 0.0005)" ([Dunn et al. 2008](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/DunnAkninNorton2008.pdf)). The data reversed the first prediction outright, and on amounts the paper reports that "[n]either the main effect of windfall size (F1,41 = 0.09, NS) nor the Windfall Size × Spending Direction interaction (F1,41 = 0.12, NS) approached significance" ([Dunn et al. 2008](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/DunnAkninNorton2008.pdf)). The authors' closing line: "policy interventions that promote prosocial spending might offer a potential path toward greater happiness" ([Dunn et al. 2008](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/DunnAkninNorton2008.pdf)).

### Replication status

The effect is real but smaller than the original splash suggested. A 2020 registered replication report by the original authors and colleagues re-ran the central paradigms at high power: Experiment 1 (712 students) supported the effect, Experiment 2 (1,950 adults recalling a spending memory) found no difference, and Experiment 3 (N = 5,199) found the prosocial-spending happiness difference again, but small ([Aknin, Dunn, Proulx, Lok and Norton, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32250135/)). Their summary: "Taken together, these studies support the hypothesis that spending money on others does promote happiness, but demonstrate that the magnitude of the effect depends on several methodological features" ([Aknin et al. 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32250135/)). Treat the spending variant as a genuine but modest lever, contingent on how the experiment is run.
