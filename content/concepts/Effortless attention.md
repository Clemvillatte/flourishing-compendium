Effortless attention is attention that sustains itself inside an absorbing activity, without the felt effort that ordinary concentration costs.

Bruya's introduction to Effortless Attention starts from the normal case: "the higher the demands of a course of action, the greater the effort required to sustain a level of efficacy". Against it stands the phenomenon: "There are times, however, when attention and action seem to flow effortlessly, allowing a person to meet an increase in demand with a sustained level of efficacy but without an increase in felt effort—even, at the best of times, with a decrease" (Bruya, [Introduction](http://web.archive.org/web/20210519072742/https://philarchive.org/archive/BRUITA-5), 2010). The volume's working definition:

> Effortless description of attention or action that (1) is not experienced as effortful or (2) involves exertion and, due to the autotelicity of experience, subjective effort is lower than in normal conditions, with effectiveness maintained at a normal or elevated level. (Bruya, [Introduction](http://web.archive.org/web/20210519072742/https://philarchive.org/archive/BRUITA-5), 2010)

The account ties the construct to Csikszentmihalyi, who "identified the phenomenon of effortlessness as autotelic experience—when a person's full engagement in an activity provides ongoing psychological value", and reports that "effortless attention is most likely to be achieved under domain-specific conditions: clear, sequential, short-term goals; immediate feedback; and a balance between opportunities for action and the individual's ability to act" (Bruya, [Introduction](http://web.archive.org/web/20210519072742/https://philarchive.org/archive/BRUITA-5), 2010). Effortlessness itself has two characteristics: "(1) full attention and (2) a dropping away of a salient sense of self" (Bruya, [Introduction](http://web.archive.org/web/20210519072742/https://philarchive.org/archive/BRUITA-5), 2010).

A later paper by Bruya and Tang describes fluid, or postvoluntary, attention as "goal-directed and selective, like voluntary attention, but also effortless and drawn to its source, like involuntary attention"; their account provides one approach to effortless attention ([Bruya and Tang, 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.704443/full)). The term postvoluntary originates in the Russian-language work of Nikolaj Dobrynin ([Bruya and Tang, 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.704443/full)). Their discussion of James quotes him: "There is no such thing as voluntary attention sustained for more than a few seconds at a time. What is called sustained voluntary attention is a repetition of successive efforts which bring back the topic to the mind" ([Bruya and Tang, 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.704443/full)).
