“The hermeneutic circle” — Gadamer, Truth and Method; Heidegger, Being and Time.

The hermeneutic circle describes how understanding moves between parts and wholes, and between what we already assume and what we meet. This page follows the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on hermeneutics (George), which was opened. Neither Truth and Method nor Being and Time was read directly, and the entry's own page references are the only pointers to them.

### Parts and whole

In its older formulation, associated with Schleiermacher and Dilthey, the circle concerns reading a text:

> "it is necessary to understand a text as a whole in order properly to understand any of its parts. On the other hand, however, it is necessary to understand the text in each of its parts in order to understand it as a whole." ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Hermeneutics, section 1.3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hermeneutics/))

### Heidegger: the circle of presuppositions

The entry says that in interpretive experience "a new understanding is achieved through renewed interpretive attention to further possible meanings of those presuppositions which, sometimes tacitly, inform the understanding that we already have". It adds that, since Heidegger, philosophers have stressed presuppositions of existential and historical context. In the entry's words, our attempts at understanding "remain conditioned by pre-structures that determine in advance which possibilities of a situation we find relevant" (it cites Being and Time, sections 28 to 34).

### Gadamer: tradition and horizon

Gadamer takes up Heidegger's pre-structures and calls them prejudices, because they are shaped by historically inherited meanings. The entry explains that, for Gadamer, "our attempts to understand are always guided more by tradition, and thus prejudice, than we are able to make explicit to ourselves" (section 4.3). On the "fusion of horizons", it reads him as saying that understanding should lead us to see "that our own horizon is not as insular or narrow as we first thought". It warns that the phrase can mislead if taken to mean that two separate horizons are merged.

### Limits

This page describes the idea as the encyclopedia presents it. It does not examine criticisms of Gadamer or whether the circle can be broken, and it does not test the compendium's links to stories and to the curse of knowledge, which are its own applications.
