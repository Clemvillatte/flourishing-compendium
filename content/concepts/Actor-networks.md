Actor-networks is Latour's name for what his sociology of associations follows. The book's central redefinition:

> "When social scientists add the adjective 'social' to some phenomenon, they designate a stabilized state of affairs, a bundle of ties that, later, may be mobilized to account for some other phenomenon. There is nothing wrong with this use of the word as long as it designates what is already assembled together [...] Problems arise, however, when 'social' begins to mean a type of material, as if the adjective was roughly comparable to other terms like 'wooden', 'steely', 'biological', 'economical', 'mental', 'organizational', or 'linguistic'."

([Latour, Reassembling the Social, Introduction, Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/52349/chapter-abstract/421264933?redirectedFrom=fulltext).)

### What the actor is

Latour's definition of the actor in the hyphenated expression:

> "An 'actor' in the hyphenated expression actor-network is not the source of an action but the moving target of a vast array of entities"

([Latour, Reassembling the Social, excerpt PDF](https://www.creativegames.org.uk/modules/Media_and_Cultural_Theory/Latour_Reassembling_the_Social.pdf).) The actor is not a billiard ball pushed around by social forces; it is "a star-shaped network, deeply entwined with other actor-networks" ([Nehrlich review of the book](https://www.nehrlich.com/blog/2006/07/04/reassembling-the-social-by-bruno-latour/)). The things that make the actor act are not made of "social stuff" — computer chips, standards, money, schooling, rewards — and that is why they can be associated in new ways ([excerpt PDF](https://www.creativegames.org.uk/modules/Media_and_Cultural_Theory/Latour_Reassembling_the_Social.pdf)).

### The two meanings of social

Latour's own compressed statement of the problem: the adjective "social" designates "two entirely different phenomena: it's at once a substance, a kind of stuff, and also a movement between non-social elements. In both cases, the social vanishes" ([Oxford Academic, Part II introduction](https://academic.oup.com/book/52349/chapter-abstract/421264975?redirectedFrom=fulltext)). The sociology of associations follows the second meaning and traces what the first meaning hides. The book's final framing is that "sociology is best defined as the discipline where participants explicitly engage in the reassembling of the collective" ([Oxford Academic, Conclusion](https://academic.oup.com/book/52349/chapter/421265000)).

The compendium holds the concept for the reason its principle states: things are participants. A practice, a habit, or a tool is not a neutral instrument being used by a social actor; it is part of what Latour would call the association that produces the action.
