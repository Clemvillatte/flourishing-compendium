The master's hand is the Dreyfus brothers' name for what expert skill becomes when the rules have been absorbed and left behind. In Mind Over Machine (1986) they model skill acquisition in five stages — novice, advanced beginner, competence, proficiency, expertise — with intuition, not calculation, as the endpoint:

> "A beginner calculates using rules and facts just like a heuristically programmed computer, but... with talent and a great deal of involved experience, the beginner develops into an expert who intuitively sees what to do without recourse to rules." ([Dreyfus & Dreyfus, "From Socrates to Expert Systems"](http://blogi.lu.lv/broks/files/2012/11/H.Dreyfus-From-Socrares-to.pdf))

### What the hand carries

The claim is not mystical; it is a description of trained perception. "The tradition has given an accurate description of the beginner and of the expert facing an unfamiliar situation, but normally an expert does not calculate. He does not solve problems. He does not even think. He just does what normally works and, of course, it normally works." The master's hand is therefore quiet in the act — the surgeon, the teacher, the listener whose response arrives before deliberation does, and whose deliberation would often slow the response down. The compendium principle CO041 carries the practical reading: trust intuition in the right domains, because in trained domains it is the residue of thousands of cases, not a guess. A compendium exercise drawn from the model is to notice, in a skill one has practised for years, how few rules one is actually consulting. What the hand does not give: expertise in one domain transferring to another; the intuition is the shape of the particular cases it was fed.
