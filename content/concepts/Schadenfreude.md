Schadenfreude is pleasure felt at someone else's misfortune. The term is German, borrowed whole into English. The dictionary gloss quoted in the review literature is "satisfaction or pleasure felt at someone else's misfortune" (Random House Webster's Unabridged Dictionary, 1998, quoted in Smith et al. 2009, [PDF](http://www.gruberpeplab.com/teaching/psych3131_summer2015/documents/6.1_Smith2009_ExploringWhenAndWhyOfSchadenfreude.pdf)).

### When it arises

The summary review by Smith, Powell, Combs and Schurtz lists the circumstances in which the emotion commonly appears: "Although schadenfreude is a socially undesirable emotion, research reveals at least three conditions in which it commonly arises. One condition is when observers gain from the misfortune. ... A second condition is when another's misfortune is deserved. ... A third condition is when a misfortune befalls an envied person." Of these, the deservingness route is the one the review maps most fully, through the research program of Norman Feather.

The envy route matters for practice, because it suggests a question to examine when the flash arrives: whether envy toward that person was already there, one of the routes the review names.

### What it costs

The pleasure is real and the price is social. A study of how schadenfreude expressers are perceived found: "Study 1 (N = 90) showed that actors expressing schadenfreude elicit a more negative impression and more avoidance intentions than those who display a general negative behavior." And the effect was not limited to competitive settings: "Study 2 (N = 90) showed that such an effect did not depend upon participants' competition with the target of the observed schadenfreude." (Pagliaro, Brambilla, Di Prinzio & Teresi, Psicologia sociale, 2019, [PDF](https://www.mbrambilla.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Pagliaro-et-al-2019-Schadenfreude.pdf)) The measured result: observers judge the expresser more negatively and keep more distance.
