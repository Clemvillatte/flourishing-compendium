Intellectual humility is the recognition that one's beliefs might be wrong. Leary and colleagues' 2017 paper calls it, in the shorthand of its abstract, "the degree to which people recognize that their beliefs might be wrong," and gives the fuller definition in the body:

> "As we use the term here, intellectual humility is defined as recognizing that a particular personal belief may be fallible, accompanied by an appropriate attentiveness to limitations in the evidentiary basis of that belief and to one's own limitations in obtaining and evaluating relevant information." (Leary et al., 2017, [Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/c55d87f1-2f28-408d-9f2c-efbc4da85990/content))

In Study 1, intellectual humility was associated with variables related to openness, curiosity, tolerance of ambiguity and low dogmatism. Study 4 found that people high in intellectual humility were more attuned to the strength of persuasive arguments than those who were low. Studies 2 and 3 add the interpersonal side: participants high in intellectual humility were less certain that their religious views were correct, preferred balanced perspectives over one-sided arguments, and were less inclined to think that politicians who changed their attitudes were "flip-flopping".

The definition covers both questions of fact and matters of opinion: a memory of a past event, an understanding of a scientific fact, a political attitude, a religious belief. And it names both a recognition and a follow-on attention: "an appropriate attentiveness to limitations in the evidentiary basis" of the belief, and to one's own limits in obtaining and evaluating the relevant information.
