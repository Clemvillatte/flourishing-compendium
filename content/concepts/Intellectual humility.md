Intellectual humility is the recognition that one's beliefs might be wrong. The construct's standard paper, in the shorthand of its abstract, calls it "the degree to which people recognize that their beliefs might be wrong," and gives the fuller definition in the body:

> "As we use the term here, intellectual humility is defined as recognizing that a particular personal belief may be fallible, accompanied by an appropriate attentiveness to limitations in the evidentiary basis of that belief and to one's own limitations in obtaining and evaluating relevant information." (Leary et al., 2017, [Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/c55d87f1-2f28-408d-9f2c-efbc4da85990/content))

The four studies behind the scale found the company the trait keeps: variables related to openness, curiosity, tolerance of ambiguity, and low dogmatism. One finding matters directly for learning: "people high in intellectual humility were more attuned to the strength of persuasive arguments than those who were low." The trait is not doubt about everything; it is calibrated attention to how good one's reasons are.

The definition also does two jobs at once. It names the recognition, and it names the attention that must follow: "an appropriate attentiveness to limitations in the evidentiary basis" of the belief and to one's own limits in getting and judging the relevant information. The humble person still holds beliefs; the holding just carries a margin, the way a careful builder allows for the load.
