The critique of mastery is the argument that treating the world as something to control and use up is a mistaken and dangerous way of relating to it. I link two sources: Heidegger's essay "The Question Concerning Technology" and Carolyn Merchant's The Death of Nature. This page draws on the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy account of the first.

### Heidegger: the world as stock

In the Stanford entry's reading, Heidegger names the basic character of entities in the technological age Bestand, "often translated as 'resource' but perhaps best translated as 'stock'." The entry explains: "Stock pieces are defined by their replaceability and mutual substitutability." Such stock is gathered by what Heidegger calls das Gestell, usually translated as "enframing", an idea that "entities are set within an exploitative framework." The entry reads the "positing" involved as "compelling or forcing the natural world to 'yield' to the demands of the technological order" ([Wrathall, SEP: Martin Heidegger](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/)).

### What goes wrong

The entry states that "technology discovers what entities are by compelling them to take a form that allows everything to be gathered and ordered and set on call." It adds that Heidegger regards this mode of revealing as "the supreme danger" to humanity, and that he saw the mood of the technological age as "a mood of profound boredom" ([SEP: Martin Heidegger](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/)). The danger is that human beings and nature alike come to count only as available inventory.

### Merchant

My principle on ecological humility quotes Merchant's 2006 retrospective on The Death of Nature, in which she argues that seventeenth-century science could be implicated in the domination of nature. Neither the article nor the book was consulted for this page, so that claim stays with the principle's own sourcing.

### Scope

The Stanford entry notes that scholars vary in how they understand the technological mode of revealing and the danger it poses. This page reports one summary of Heidegger's argument and does not cover his proposed alternative or the full essay.
