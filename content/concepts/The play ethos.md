The play ethos is Stuart Brown's research programme on play, set out in Play: How It Shapes the Brain, Opens the Imagination, and Invigorates the Soul (2009): play treated not as recreation from life but as a biological state central to development, mood and adaptability. His definition of play is state-based rather than activity-based:

> "Play is state of mind that one has when absorbed in an activity that provides enjoyment and a suspension of sense of time. And play is self-motivated so you want to do it again and again." (Stuart Brown, [National Institute for Play](https://nifplay.org/science/foundations))

### What the programme found

The state, not the behaviour, is what counts: "Two people might be throwing a ball... or typing words on a computer, and one might be playing while the other is not. To tell which one is playing … you have to infer from their expressions and the details of their actions" (Brown, in [NIFPlay](https://nifplay.org/science/foundations)). Brown's summary line for the stakes is the one most quoted: "The opposite of play is not work, but depression" ([Stanford Bing School talk](https://bingschool.stanford.edu/news/leading-researcher-play-speaks-bing)). His work grew out of clinical histories in which a near-total absence of play kept recurring in lives that had gone badly wrong, and he built the argument that play is a need across the whole lifespan rather than a childhood stage to outgrow. The compendium principle PP056 carries the practice: engage in play, and let the state, not the schedule, define what counts. A compendium exercise drawn from the definition is to notice which activities produce the suspension of time and the wish to repeat, and to protect one of them. What the programme does not give: randomised trials of play as treatment; the evidence is clinical histories, animal research and argument, and the depression claim is an aphorism of the stakes, not a measured effect size.
