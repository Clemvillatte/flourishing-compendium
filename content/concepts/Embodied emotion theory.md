**Embodied emotion theory** — bodily states and simulated bodily activity ground emotional experience, rather than following abstract appraisals alone.

Embodied emotion theory is the family of accounts on which emotional experience is built out of the body's own states and activity, so that feeling an emotion is inseparable from what the body is doing. The founding version is William James's 1884 proposal (independently echoed by Carl Lange in 1885) that emotions are feelings constituted by perceptions of physiological change, and that common sense has the causal direction backwards:

> "we feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble, and not that we cry, strike, or tremble, because we are sorry, angry, or fearful." (James 1884: 190, quoted in [Scarantino & De Sousa, "Emotion", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotion/))

### From the body to simulation

Later versions widen the claim from actual bodily change to simulated bodily activity. On these accounts the same sensory, motor and introspective states that run during an emotional episode are re-enacted to represent it, a theme the Stanford Encyclopedia's review notes now "looms large in affective science" (citing Niedenthal 2007). The account also carries a standing objection: Walter Cannon argued that the visceral reactions characteristic of fear and anger are indistinguishable, so they cannot be what tells emotions apart, and the SEP review notes later research has not fully settled whether distinct emotions have reliably different bodily profiles.
