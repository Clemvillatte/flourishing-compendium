Embodied theories of emotion propose that emotional processing draws on re-enactments of affective, sensory and motor states.

One early bodily account is William James's 1884 proposal that emotions are feelings constituted by perceptions of physiological change; Carl Lange offered a similar account in 1885. James took common sense to have the direction of causation backwards:

> "we feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble, and not that we cry, strike, or tremble, because we are sorry, angry, or fearful." (James 1884: 190, quoted in [Scarantino & De Sousa, "Emotion", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotion/))

### From the body to simulation

A Scientific Reports paper states the approach this way:

> "Embodied theories of emotion propose that emotional processing is grounded in situated conceptualizations and the re-enactment of the same affective, sensory and motor states that are activated when the emotion is experienced." ([Increased heart rate after exercise facilitates the processing of fearful but not disgusted faces, Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-017-18761-5))

On this perspective, the same brain and bodily resources support feeling an emotion, displaying it, and recognizing it when displayed by others.

### A standing objection

Cannon objected to the James-Lange account that the visceral reactions characteristic of fear and anger are indistinguishable, so those reactions cannot distinguish the emotions. The SEP review notes later research has not fully settled whether distinct emotions have reliably different bodily profiles.
