Embodied emotion theory — bodily states and simulated bodily activity ground emotional experience, rather than following abstract appraisals alone.

Embodied emotion theory is the family of accounts on which bodily states and simulations of them are what an emotional feeling is made of. One early bodily account is William James's 1884 proposal that emotions are feelings constituted by perceptions of physiological change; Carl Lange offered a similar account in 1885. James took common sense to have the direction of causation backwards:

> "we feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble, and not that we cry, strike, or tremble, because we are sorry, angry, or fearful." (James 1884: 190, quoted in [Scarantino & De Sousa, "Emotion", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotion/))

### From the body to simulation

Contemporary versions widen the claim from perceived bodily change to re-enacted states. A Scientific Reports paper states the approach this way:

> "Embodied theories of emotion propose that emotional processing is grounded in situated conceptualizations and the re-enactment of the same affective, sensory and motor states that are activated when the emotion is experienced." (L. Barca, [Increased heart rate after exercise facilitates the processing of fearful but not disgusted faces, Scientific Reports](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-017-18761-5))

One central claim the paper records for the approach is that changes in body state can produce emotional states.

### A standing objection

Walter Cannon argued that the visceral reactions characteristic of fear and anger are indistinguishable, so they cannot be what tells emotions apart. The SEP review notes later research has not fully settled whether distinct emotions have reliably different bodily profiles.
