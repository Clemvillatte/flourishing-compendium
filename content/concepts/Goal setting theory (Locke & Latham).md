specific, difficult goals produce better performance than vague or easy goals (Locke & Latham, 2002, 2006; Latham & Locke, 2007).

Edwin Locke and Gary Latham summarize 35 years of empirical research on goal-setting theory in their 2002 review, describing the core findings, the mechanisms by which goals operate, moderators of goal effects, and the theory's limitations ([Locke and Latham, 2002, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12237980/)). Research on goal setting has typically been conducted in organizational and laboratory settings ([Strecher et al., 1995, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7622387/)). A summary article with Latham as co-author states the theory's central prediction:

> "Goal setting theory predicts that, under certain conditions, setting specific difficult goals leads to higher performance when compared with no goals or vague, nonquantitative goals, such as 'do your best.'" (Strecher, Seijts, Kok, Latham, Glasgow, DeVellis, Meertens and Bulger, [Goal setting as a strategy for health behavior change, Health Education Quarterly 22 (1995), pp. 190-200, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7622387/))

### The conditions that matter

The same abstract adds the theory's other edge: it asserts a positive linear relationship between the degree of goal difficulty and the level of performance, in contrast to the graduated, easy goals often set in behavior change programs ([Strecher et al., 1995, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7622387/)). Later work in physical activity promotion adds a boundary: the theory has evolved to differentiate performance goals from learning goals, and for people new to physical activity, learning goals may be preferable, since in some cases performance goals can be detrimental to achieving desired outcomes ([Swann et al., 2021, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31900043/)).
