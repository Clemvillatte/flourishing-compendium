specific, difficult goals produce better performance than vague or easy goals (Locke & Latham, 2002, 2006; Latham & Locke, 2007).

Edwin Locke and Gary Latham built the theory across four decades, mostly in organizational and laboratory settings ([Locke and Latham, 2002, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12237980/)). A summary article with Latham as co-author states the theory's central prediction:

> "Goal setting theory predicts that, under certain conditions, setting specific difficult goals leads to higher performance when compared with no goals or vague, nonquantitative goals, such as 'do your best.'" (Strecher, Seijts, Kok, Latham, Glasgow, DeVellis, Meertens and Bulger, [Goal setting as a strategy for health behavior change, Health Education Quarterly 22 (1995), pp. 190-200, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7622387/))

### The conditions that matter

The same abstract adds the theory's other edge: it asserts a positive linear relationship between the degree of goal difficulty and the level of performance, in contrast to the graduated, easy goals often set in behavior change programs ([Strecher et al., 1995, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7622387/)). The theory's reviewers describe the core findings, the mechanisms by which goals operate, the moderators of goal effects, and the theory's limitations, without treating the prediction as unconditional ([Locke and Latham, 2002, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12237980/)). Later work in physical activity adds a boundary: for people new to a domain, learning goals can serve better than performance goals, and rigid specific goals can backfire ([Swann et al., 2020, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31900043/)).
