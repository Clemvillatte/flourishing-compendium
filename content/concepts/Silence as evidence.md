Silence as evidence names Peirce's warning that agreement produced by force is not agreement at all. In "The Fixation of Belief," his sketch of the method of authority turns on it:

> Let all men who reject the established belief be terrified into silence.

(Charles Sanders Peirce, "The Fixation of Belief," 1877, [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Fixation_of_Belief))

The method of authority silences disagreement rather than answering it, and Peirce's point is that the silence carries no information about whether the belief is true. A community's beliefs held by such means are settled, not tested; the failure of the method is that "no institution can be adopted that will prevent all private reflection," and thought survives where it is not allowed to speak. Silence, in other words, can be evidence of fear rather than evidence of conviction.

Peirce's contrast is the method of science, which lets belief be determined by "nothing human, but by some external permanency," and so makes disagreement itself informative rather than threatening. My [[CO023 — Understand survivorship bias — you see winners, not the full distribution]] is the statistical version of the same trap: a distribution looks clean because its failures are missing, and silence about failures is not evidence that failures did not happen.
