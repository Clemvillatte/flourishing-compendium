Process focus research asks whether attending to the means of a goal, the steps and actions, works better than attending to its desired result. A 2025 Springer paper on academic engagement states the distinction in the terms of Freund and colleagues: outcome focus "centers on the desired result and answers 'why' questions (e.g., attaining high grades)," while process focus "emphasizes the means of reaching that result and answers 'how' questions" ([Springer, Outcome focus up against process focus in academic engagement](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10212-025-00995-x)). The seed cites Vallerand (2010) and Zimmerman (2000), which were not opened for this revision.

### The founding experiment

Pham and Taylor (1999) had college freshmen spend 5 to 7 days before a midterm mentally simulating the process of doing well (good study habits), the outcome (getting a good grade), or both, with a self-monitoring control. Their abstract reports that "process simulation enhanced studying and improved grades; the latter effect was mediated by enhanced planning and reduced anxiety" ([Pham and Taylor, 1999, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 25(2):250-260](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167299025002010)). Taylor's later review reports that students who rehearsed the outcome studied no more and "performed significantly more poorly than students who practiced the process simulation." The one benefit of outcome simulation was that those students said they were highly motivated to study ([Taylor lab review, Envisioning the future and self-regulation](https://taylorlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2014/11/2011_Envisioning-the-Future-and-Self-Regulation.pdf)).

### A caution from the meta-analysis

A 2021 systematic review and multilevel meta-analysis by Cole, Smith, Ragan, Suurmond and Armitage covers 123 effect sizes (N = 5,685). It found a positive overall effect of mental simulation on behavior, Hedges' g = 0.49, 95% CI [0.37, 0.62] ([Cole et al., 2021, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8500882/)). On process simulation specifically, they write that focusing on instrumental plans rather than outcomes gave "a small positive effect that did not reach statistical significance," so the result "does not therefore clearly validate" the Taylor and Pham account. They also note that Pham and Taylor (1999) itself "failed to find a statistically significant effect of process simulation on exam performance (behavior) over the control group" (p < .09) ([Cole et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8500882/)). So the founding study is suggestive and the synthesis equivocal.

### Where process focus is studied now

The 2025 Springer paper summarises academic work by Krause and Freund (2016) as showing process focus to be "superior for positive goal-related outcomes (e.g., task persistence and performance, higher levels of self-efficacy)" and negatively linked to procrastination. It also says that combining process and outcome representations "may yield beneficial effects" ([Springer, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10212-025-00995-x)). I did not open Krause and Freund.

### In the compendium

P177 (Set process goals and let outcomes follow), PP021 and P104 fit the process-focus finding in education settings. K031 (Abandon any expectation of results) and Y098 (Act, release the fruits) go further: the research here compares ways of picturing goals, and none of it tests giving up expectations of results, nor is outcome picturing shown to be useless in general. The sources above report that outcome picturing raised motivation and that a combination can help.
