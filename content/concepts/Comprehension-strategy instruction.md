the learning-science finding that deep processing improves when learners are taught to interrogate the material directly (Pressley et al., 1992).

Comprehension-strategy instruction is the teaching counterpart of a simple observation: good readers do not just read, they work on the text while they read, and the working can be taught. The idea grew out of Pressley's transactional strategies instruction and the National Reading Panel's review, and its research base is unusually settled. A review for adult education states it plainly:

> "To improve ABE learners' comprehension of texts used during instruction (those ABE learners above Grade Equivalent 3), teach them strategies that can be used during the reading process and that enable them to become actively engaged in understanding a text. Eight effective strategies have been identified: comprehension monitoring, cooperative learning, graphic organizers, story structure, question answering, question generation, summarization, and multiple strategies (combining the above when appropriate)" (Kruidenier, 2002, quoted in [McShane, "Comprehension-Strategy Instruction", LINCS/Institute of Education Sciences](https://lincs.ed.gov/publications/html/mcshane/chapter7.html)).

The strategies share a family resemblance: each one makes the reader ask something of the text instead of receiving it. The best-studied packaging is reciprocal teaching:

> "In reciprocal teaching, the teacher first models the comprehension process, showing how she/he interacts with text, using two or more of the following strategies in combination: question generation, summarization of main ideas, clarification of word meanings or confusing text, and prediction of what will come next in the text." (same chapter)

> "Gradually, as they become more skilled in the use of the strategies, the teacher releases control of the process, and the readers use the strategies independently in their reading." (same chapter)

Summarization shows the teaching logic at its clearest. A summary is "a brief statement that contains the essential ideas of a longer passage or selection" (Harris and Hodges 1995, quoted in the same chapter), and the aim of teaching it is "to teach the reader to identify the main or central ideas of a paragraph or a series of paragraphs" (National Reading Panel report, quoted in the same chapter). The strategies also work best together: "Many of the strategies above are best used within a multiple-strategies approach" (same chapter).

The relevance to wisdom development is direct. Comprehension strategies are a trained habit of interrogating what one reads — asking what the main idea is, what remains unclear, what the next part will say — and that habit underlies [[Y086 — Examine what you hear]], my counsel to test rather than absorb what reaches you.
