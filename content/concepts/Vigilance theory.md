**Vigilance theory** — Oaten, Cheng — self-control is like a muscle: vigilance over one domain temporarily strengthens or depletes others.

The research the original entry points to is Oaten and Cheng's work on self-control as a strength that practice can build. Their study-program paper states its design and finding in the abstract:

> "In this study, we tested whether a study intervention program, a form of repeated practice of self-control, could improve regulatory strength and dampen the debilitating effects of exam stress." And: "Students receiving the study program, however, showed significant improvement in self-regulatory capacity as shown by an enhanced performance on a visual tracking task following a thought-suppression task. During examinations, these participants also reported significant decreases in smoking, alcohol, and caffeine consumption and an increase in healthy eating, emotional control, maintenance of household chores, attendance to commitments, monitoring of spending, and an improvement in study habits. Hence, the study program not only overcame deficits caused by exam stress but actually led to improvements in self-control even during exam time." (Oaten & Cheng, "Improved Self-Control: The Benefits of a Regular Program of Academic Study", Basic and Applied Social Psychology 28(1), 2006, pp. 1-16, [open article](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1207/s15324834basp2801_1))

Two things in this abstract carry the concept's weight. First, the measurement: regulatory capacity was tested as performance on a visual tracking task after a thought-suppression task, a sustained-attention load. Second, the transfer: the practice was a structured study program, yet the reported gains spread to smoking, drinking, spending, chores and emotional control, domains the program never mentioned. The original entry's one-line summary compresses this into the muscle image — short-run fatigue from use, long-run strengthening from practice.

A naming note for the owner: the opened sources present this as research on self-control strength, not as a theory published under the name "vigilance theory." The connection between the heading and the research is the original entry's framing, and the tracking task is a sustained-attention task only in description. The compendium's application of the original entry's framing is the paragraph above; the underlying findings are as quoted.
