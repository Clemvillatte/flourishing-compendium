**Vigilance theory** — Oaten, Cheng — self-control is like a muscle: vigilance over one domain temporarily strengthens or depletes others.

The research behind the heading is Oaten and Cheng's program of studies on self-control as a trainable strength. Their design measured regulatory capacity with a visual tracking task performed after a thought-suppression task, and their intervention studies gave participants a structured self-control practice for weeks. The abstract of the study-program paper states the finding:

> "Students receiving the study program, however, showed significant improvement in self-regulatory capacity as shown by an enhanced performance on a visual tracking task following a thought-suppression task. During examinations, these participants also reported significant decreases in smoking, alcohol, and caffeine consumption and an increase in healthy eating, emotional control, maintenance of household chores, attendance to commitments, monitoring of spending, and an improvement in study habits. Hence, the study program not only overcame deficits caused by exam stress but actually led to improvements in self-control even during exam time." (Oaten & Cheng, "Improved Self-Control: The Benefits of a Regular Program of Academic Study", Basic and Applied Social Psychology 28(1), 2006, pp. 1-16, [open article](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1207/s15324834basp2801_1))

The same authors ran the design with a physical-exercise program and with financial monitoring, and the pattern repeated: improvements showed up in domains the participants never practiced. The tracking task itself is a vigilance task, which is the heading's link to attention: sustained watching over moving targets is one of the first things to sag when self-control strength is spent, and one of the first to recover when it is trained. The seed's one-line summary compresses this into the muscle image — short-run fatigue from use, long-run strengthening from practice.

The compendium's principles use the account on both sides: attention is a limited resource that depletes, and self-control is something a regular practice can strengthen rather than a fixed trait.
