judging a conjunction (Linda is a feminist and a bank teller) as more probable than a component (Linda is a bank teller) (Tversky & Kahneman, 1983).

The conjunction fallacy is the mistake Amos Tversky and [[T022 — Kahneman, Daniel|Daniel Kahneman]] named in 1983: rating a combination of events as more probable than one of its parts. The combination cannot be, and the paper's opening states why:

> "Perhaps the simplest and the most basic qualitative law of probability is the conjunction rule: The probability of a conjunction, P(A&B), cannot exceed the probabilities of its constituents, P(A) and P(B), because the extension (or the possibility set) of the conjunction is included in the extension of its constituents. Judgments under uncertainty, however, are often mediated by intuitive heuristics that are not bound by the conjunction rule. A conjunction can be more representative than one of its constituents... The representativeness and availability heuristics therefore can make a conjunction appear more probable than one of its constituents." ([Tversky and Kahneman 1983, Psychological Review 90(4):293-315](https://joelvelasco.net/teaching/5311/tverskykahnamen83-Extensional.pdf))

> "The violation of the conjunction rule in a direct comparison of B to A&B is called the conjunction fallacy." (same paper)

The demonstration is the Linda problem, the most famous sketch in the judgment literature:

> "Linda is 31 years old, single, outspoken and very bright. She majored in philosophy. As a student, she was deeply concerned with issues of discrimination and social justice, and also participated in anti-nuclear demonstrations." (same paper)

Given that sketch, most respondents say it is more probable that Linda is a bank teller and active in the feminist movement than that she is a bank teller. The feminist detail makes the story cohere, and coherence is mistaken for probability: every feminist bank teller is, by definition, also a bank teller, so the plainer option cannot be less likely. In the direct comparison the paper reports: "Overall, 85% of respondents indicated that T&F was more probable than T, in a flagrant violation of the conjunction rule", and across the direct tests "the overall incidence of violations of the conjunction rule in direct tests is 88%, which virtually coincides with the incidence of the corresponding pattern in judgments of representativeness" (same paper).

The fallacy's lesson is not that people are fools but that the mind substitutes an easier question for a hard one. Likelihood is difficult; resemblance is easy, and Linda resembles a feminist bank teller more than she resembles a bank teller. The paper closes on the pattern that matters for practice:

> "People generally accept the conjunction rule in its abstract form (B is more probable than A&B) but defy it in concrete examples, such as the Linda and Bill problems, where the rule conflicts with an intuitive impression." (same paper)

Knowing the rule is not enough; the moment of choice is where it must be recalled. My principle [[CO035 — Recognise the representativeness heuristic — similarity is mistaken for probability]] turns this into a working habit.
