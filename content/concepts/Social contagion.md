Social contagion is the spread of affect and behavior from person to person, through observation and induction rather than direct instruction. The definitional core: "the propagation of behaviors, emotions, attitudes, and other feelings via the brain's associative mechanisms in social networks" ([Wikipedia, Social contagion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_contagion)). The Christakis and Fowler line of research gave the idea its modern evidence base, showing that traits like obesity spread through social ties to three degrees of separation ([Christakis and Fowler, NEJM 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17652652/)).

### The obesity study

The 2007 NEJM study tracked a densely interconnected social network of 12,067 people over 32 years. Its key result: "A person's chances of becoming obese increased by 57% (95% confidence interval [CI], 6 to 123) if he or she had a friend who became obese in a given interval. Among pairs of adult siblings, if one sibling became obese, the chance that the other would become obese increased by 40% (95% CI, 21 to 60). If one spouse became obese, the likelihood that the other spouse would become obese increased by 37% (95% CI, 7 to 73)" ([Christakis and Fowler 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17652652/)). The authors' conclusion: "obesity appears to spread through social ties" ([NEJM 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17652652/)). Geographic neighbors showed no such effects, and the clusters "did not appear to be solely attributable to the selective formation of social ties among obese persons" ([NEJM 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17652652/)).

### Mechanisms and dispute

The mechanisms proposed are mostly pre-conscious. Stephan and Walter's synthesis names automatic mimicry, direct affect induction, and ventriloquism, the last meaning the apparent transfer of a feeling that is really the observer's construction ([Stephan and Walter, Perceptual and Motor Skills 2020](https://doi.org/10.1177/0031512520918505), as summarized in [Wikipedia, Social contagion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_contagion)). The Wikipedia article also records the field's standing caution plainly: later research has been "mixed with some scholars disputing aspects of the research, particularly in relation to obesity and suicide" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_contagion)). The compendium's use of the concept survives the dispute either way: even the weaker reading, that moods and habits rub off on close company more than on distant neighbors, is enough for the practice principles.
