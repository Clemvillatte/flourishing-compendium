For [[T002 — Aristotle|Aristotle]], friendship requires mutually recognised goodwill, not simply a favourable feeling toward someone who does not know it ([Nicomachean Ethics VIII.2](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.8.viii.html)). He distinguishes friendships grounded in usefulness, pleasure and goodness; in the complete form, friends love one another for their character rather than for an incidental advantage ([VIII.3](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.8.viii.html)).

> "Thus the friendship of bad men turns out an evil thing (for because of their instability they unite in bad pursuits, and besides they become evil by becoming like each other), while the friendship of good men is good, being augmented by their companionship; and they are thought to become better too by their activities and by improving each other; for from each other they take the mould of the characteristics they approve-whence the saying 'noble deeds from noble men'." (NE IX.12, [W. D. Ross translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.9.ix.html)).

## Facets

- **as mutual flourishing**: Aristotle connects good friends' companionship with shared activities and improvement of character ([IX.12](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.9.ix.html)). The same passage allows that bad companions can reinforce bad pursuits, rather than making all company beneficial ([IX.12](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.9.ix.html)).

The claim Aristotle makes here is not that company is good for a person but that company shapes a person, and the direction of the shaping depends on what the companions approve. The mould friends take from each other is the one they approve of, so the question a friendship raises is not whether it is pleasant but what character it is quietly forming.

Aristotle's earlier argument grounds the complete form in what is stable in a person rather than in what the friend supplies at the moment. A friendship of usefulness ends when the use ends, and a friendship of pleasure ends when the pleasure changes; the friendship of good men is augmented by their companionship and outlasts the things that brought them together ([VIII.3](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.8.viii.html)). The practical reading is that a person can ask of any close friendship what it is grounded in, and the answer will say a great deal about how long it has left.
