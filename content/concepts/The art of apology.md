The art of apology is Desmond Tutu's account, in No Future Without Forgiveness (1999), of what it takes for a wrongdoer to say the two hardest words and mean them. The book came out of his chairing of South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission, where amnesty required perpetrators to make a full disclosure of what they had done, and its chapters treat apology as a skill the country had to learn together. His most quoted line on its difficulty:

> "It is perhaps the most difficult thing in the world—in almost every language the most difficult words are, 'I am sorry.'" ([Tutu, No Future Without Forgiveness](https://ia902902.us.archive.org/8/items/no-future-without-forgiveness-desmond-tutu/No%20Future%20Without%20Forgiveness%20(Desmond%20Tutu)%20.pdf))

### What an apology has to do

Tutu sets a demanding bar: "when a relationship has been damaged or when a potential relationship has been made impossible," the perpetrator "should acknowledge the truth and be ready and willing to apologize." Then the sequence: "If the wrongdoer has come to the point of realizing his wrong, then one hopes there will be remorse, or at least some contrition or sorrow. This should lead him to confess the wrong he has done and ask for forgiveness." ([Tutu](https://ia902902.us.archive.org/8/items/no-future-without-forgiveness-desmond-tutu/No%20Future%20Without%20Forgiveness%20(Desmond%20Tutu)%20.pdf)). The apology is also not the end of the work: "Confession, forgiveness, and reparation, wherever feasible, form part of a continuum" ([Tutu](https://ia902902.us.archive.org/8/items/no-future-without-forgiveness-desmond-tutu/No%20Future%20Without%20Forgiveness%20(Desmond%20Tutu)%20.pdf)). The book's spirit is ubuntu: the wrongdoer and the wronged remain members of one household, and the apology is the door back into it.
