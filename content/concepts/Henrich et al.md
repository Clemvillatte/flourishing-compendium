“Henrich et al” — 2010.

The 2010 reference is Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine and Ara Norenzayan's review "The Weirdest People in the World?" (Behavioral and Brain Sciences 33:61-83, with open peer commentary on pages 83-135). It argues that psychology's findings come mostly from a narrow slice of humanity and may not generalise to the species.

### The argument

> "Behavioral scientists routinely publish broad claims about human psychology and behavior in the world's top journals based on samples drawn entirely from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, and Democratic (WEIRD) societies." (Henrich, Heine & Norenzayan 2010, [PubMed 20550733](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20550733/))

The authors then ask whether researchers can assume little variation across populations, or that these "standard subjects" represent the species. Their review of comparative work finds both substantial variability in experimental results across populations and that WEIRD subjects are "frequent outliers". The domains covered include visual perception, fairness, cooperation, spatial reasoning, moral reasoning, self-concepts and the heritability of IQ. The abstract concludes that "there are no obvious a priori grounds for claiming that a particular behavioral phenomenon is universal based on sampling from a single subpopulation".

### How narrow the database is

The manuscript cites an analysis of the top journals in six sub-disciplines of psychology from 2003 to 2007 (Arnett 2008): 68% of subjects came from the United States, and 96% from Western industrialized countries. The authors add that this means "96% of psychological samples come from countries with only 12% of the world's population", and that most American samples in a leading social-psychology journal were undergraduates in psychology courses (67%). ([author manuscript, UBC](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~ara/Manuscripts/Weird_People_BBS_Henrichetal.pdf))

### What it does not say

The paper does not claim that WEIRD findings are wrong. It says they cannot be assumed to be universal without comparative evidence. The authors also close with proposals for reorganising behavioural science, so the review is a call for broader sampling, not a demonstration that any particular finding fails elsewhere. The original appeared with commentary pages in the same journal issue, so critics' replies are part of the record.
