John Berry's acculturation theory holds that when people navigate two cultures, they do so along two independent dimensions — maintaining the heritage culture and seeking contact with the host society — whose crossing yields four strategies: integration, assimilation, separation and marginalization. In psychology, "the best known conceptualization of acculturation is Berry's model of acculturation strategies," assessed by "two independent, orthogonal measures that address the acquisition of the new culture and the retention of the original culture" ([PMC systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6997899/)). The four strategies follow: "marginalization (low affiliation with both cultures); separation (high origin-culture affiliation, low new-culture affiliation); assimilation (high new-culture affiliation, low origin-culture affiliation); and integration (high affiliation with both cultures)" ([PMC systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6997899/)).

The bidimensionality is the theory's core move. "In contrast to unidimensional models of acculturation, which tend to equate acculturation with assimilation, Berry's framework recognizes that identification with one's heritage culture exists on a separate continuum from identification with the host culture" ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5003760/)). One can be both, not either.

The strategies carry unequal health consequences. A systematic review of migrant mental health found "marginalisation had the worst effects on mental health of the migrant populations while integration had the most positive effects," with marginalization more than tripling the likelihood of anxiety-related symptoms compared to integration ([PMC systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9461568/)). Consistent with this, "integration or biculturalism was found to be the most advantageous or 'healthiest' acculturation orientation in relation to mental health" ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4346023/)).

The compendium's application: integration protects health, and the strategies are shaped by the receiving society as much as by individual choice ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8168756/)) — acculturation can be designed, not just endured.
