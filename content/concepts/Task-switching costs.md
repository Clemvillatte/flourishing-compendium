Task-switching costs are the performance penalties that appear when a person changes from one task to another. In his review in Trends in Cognitive Sciences, Stephen Monsell writes: "Subjects' responses are substantially slower and, usually, more error-prone immediately after a task switch. This 'switch cost' is reduced, but not eliminated, by an opportunity for preparation" ([Monsell 2003](http://epa.psy.ntu.edu.tw/perception/pdf/Monsell_2003.pdf)).

### The classic experiment

Rogers and Monsell's 1995 study had participants switch between two tasks on every second trial. One task classified a digit as even or odd, and the other classified a letter as consonant or vowel. As the interval before the next stimulus lengthened to 0.6 seconds, "the substantial cost to performance of this predictable task-switch fell", so participants could prepare in part. However, "even with 1.2 s available for preparation, a large asymptotic reaction time (RT) cost remained, but only on the 1st trial of the new task" ([Rogers & Monsell 1995, Oxford Research Archive](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:6180113d-b831-419b-8716-46652110880d)).

A later paper on head-injury patients summarises the preparation effect: switch costs fell from over 200 ms to about half that value as the preparation interval increased from 150 ms to 600 ms ([PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1779821/)).

### What the cost is taken to reflect

A study from the University of Granada describes the switch cost as "the behavioral manifestation of the extra processes involved in reconfiguring the task-set needed in order to perform the new task" ([Granada task-set reconfiguration study](https://www.ugr.es/~setchift/docs/a17-Exploring_task-set_reconfiguration.pdf)). Researchers also use the size of the cost as an inverse measure of cognitive flexibility ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10769152/)).

### Scope

The costs measured are in milliseconds, in simple laboratory tasks. They do not give a fixed penalty for everyday work, and these sources do not test whether single-tasking improves wellbeing. The second reference in my earlier note, Rubinstein and colleagues (2001), was not consulted.
