Steelmanning is the practice of constructing the strongest form of an opponent's argument before answering it — the deliberate opposite of the straw man, in which a weak version is substituted and knocked down. The modern term is defined as "the practice of applying the rhetorical principle of charity through addressing the strongest form of the other person's argument, even if it is not the one they explicitly presented" ([Wikipedia, "Straw man", Steelmanning section](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straw_man)). The practice itself is far older than the word: Mill argues for it in On Liberty, citing Cicero as his example.

> "The greatest orator, save one, of antiquity, has left it on record that he always studied his adversary's case with as great, if not with still greater, intensity than even his own. What Cicero practised as the means of forensic success, requires to be imitated by all who study any subject in order to arrive at the truth." (Mill, On Liberty, chapter 2, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/34901/pg34901.txt))

Mill's reason is epistemic, not courteous. The steel-manned case is the only one worth answering, because a view defeated in its weak form is not defeated at all:

> "He who knows only his own side of the case, knows little of that. His reasons may be good, and no one may have been able to refute them. But if he is equally unable to refute the reasons on the opposite side; if he does not so much as know what they are, he has no ground for preferring either opinion." (On Liberty, chapter 2, same source)

The steelman is built before the critique: hear the opposing reasons "from persons who actually believe them", in their "most plausible and persuasive form", and feel the whole force of the difficulty they raise. Only then, Mill says, does one possess the portion of truth that meets and removes it.
