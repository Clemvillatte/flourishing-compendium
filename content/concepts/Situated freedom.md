Situated freedom is the existentialist name for a two-sided fact: a person is never free in a vacuum, and never determined by their circumstances alone. The term belongs chiefly to Sartre's Being and Nothingness and to Merleau-Ponty's Phenomenology of Perception, where it names the idea that freedom is always exercised somewhere, inside a situation one did not choose.

Sartre's formulation of the paradox:

> "Thus I am absolutely free and absolutely responsible for my situation. But I am never free except in situation." (Sartre, Being and Nothingness, trans. Barnes, 1992, p. 653, quoted in [Helsinki thesis on early Sartre](https://helda.helsinki.fi/server/api/core/bitstreams/da456c81-9afa-4efc-a7e6-d3f7c904a3f9/content))

The same book states the dependence from both sides: "there is freedom only in a situation, and there is a situation only through freedom," and that human-reality "everywhere encounters resistance and obstacles which it has not created, but these resistances and obstacles have meaning only in and through the free choice which human-reality is" (Being and Nothingness, p. 629, quoted in the same thesis). The givens are real; their meaning as obstacles comes from what one is trying to do.

Merleau-Ponty pressed the point further against Sartre: freedom is not a series of absolute re-beginnings but a momentum in time:

> "The very notion of freedom demands that our decision should plunge into the future, that something should have been done by it" (Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, p. 508, quoted in the same thesis)

A decision does not start from nothing; the next instant inherits from what came before, even when it is not necessitated by it.
