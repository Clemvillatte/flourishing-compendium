“Experiential avoidance research” — Hayes et al., 2011.

Experiential avoidance describes unwillingness to remain in contact with internal experiences such as bodily sensations, emotions, thoughts, memories and behavioral predispositions, together with steps to alter their form or frequency or the contexts that occasion them. A 2018 study frames it from a contextual transdiagnostic approach, examining it alongside behavioral activation in emotional distress.

> "Experiential avoidance is defined as the 'phenomenon that occurs when a person is unwilling to remain in contact with particular private experiences (e.g., bodily sensations, emotions, thoughts, memories, behavioral predispositions) and takes steps to alter the form or frequency of these events and the contexts that occasion them' (Hayes et al., 1996, p. 1154)." ([Fernández-Rodríguez et al., Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6129770/))

The article's introduction summarizes earlier work describing short-term relief from avoidance alongside longer-term worsening of distress. It presents experiential avoidance as a hypothesized vulnerability factor for emotional distress.

In the study's own data, from 240 analyzed participants recruited at Spanish community health centres ([Fernández-Rodríguez et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6129770/)), higher self-reported avoidance was associated with greater emotional distress, and the group with both anxiety and depression reported the greatest avoidance together with the least behavioral activation. The BADS Avoidance/Rumination subscale had the numerically highest AUC among the measures tested for distinguishing participants with and without clinically relevant emotional distress in this sample.
