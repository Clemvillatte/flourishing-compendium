Behavioral inhibition theory Gray is Jeffrey Gray's account of the two motivational systems his Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory places under personality: a behavioural inhibition system (BIS) that responds to conflict and threat with caution and anxiety, and a behavioural activation system (BAS) that pursues reward. The original formulation:

> "The behavioural inhibition system (BIS) was postulated to be sensitive to conditioned aversive stimuli (i.e. signals of both punishment and the omission/termination of reward) relating to Anx, but also to extreme novelty, high-intensity stimuli, and innate fear stimuli (e.g. snakes, blood), which are more related to fear." ([Pickering & Corr, "J.A. Gray's Reinforcement Sensitivity Theory of Personality"](https://research.gold.ac.uk/id/eprint/6343/1/9781412946513-Ch11.pdf))

### What the theory carries

The BIS's job, in the 2000 revision by Gray and McNaughton, narrowed to conflict: "The BIS is responsible, not, as in the 1982 version, for mediating reactions to conditioned aversive stimuli and the special class of innate fear stimuli, but for the resolution of goal conflict in general (e.g. between BAS-approach and FFFS-avoidance, as in foraging situations)." Its output is the whole anxiety package: "The BIS generates the emotion of anxiety, which entails the inhibition of prepotent conflicting behaviours, the engagement of risk assessment processes, and the scanning of memory and the environment to help resolve concurrent goal conflict." The counterpart system is defined symmetrically: "The behavioural approach system (BAS) was postulated to be sensitive to conditioned appetitive stimuli... activated by the presentation of stimuli associated with reward and the termination/omission of signals of punishment." Trait differences in the sensitivities of the two systems are, on this theory, what anxiety and impulsivity are made of. The compendium principle Y105 carries the applied reading: know the three strands of a reaction — the approach pull, the avoidance pull, and the conflict between them — before judging the person. A compendium exercise drawn from the theory is to take one hesitation and ask whether it is fear of punishment, hope of reward, or both at once, since the third case is the one the BIS exists for. What the theory does not give: a valuation of inhibition; the system is a mechanism, and its sensitivity can be adaptive or crippling depending on the environment.
