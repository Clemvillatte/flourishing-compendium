Early intervention research is the study of what changes when psychological problems are treated before their patterns consolidate. Its demographic backbone is the National Comorbidity Survey Replication, a nationally representative US survey of 9,282 adults, whose age-of-onset findings remain the standard citation for why early matters. The authors' conclusion, in their own words:

> "About half of Americans will meet the criteria for a DSM-IV disorder sometime in their life, with first onset usually in childhood or adolescence. Interventions aimed at prevention or early treatment need to focus on youth." (Kessler et al., [Arch Gen Psychiatry 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15939837/), abstract)

### Why the timing counts

The survey's central numbers are about onset, not treatment: "Median age of onset is much earlier for anxiety (11 years) and impulse-control (11 years) disorders than for substance use (20 years) and mood (30 years) disorders. Half of all lifetime cases start by age 14 years and three fourths by age 24 years" ([same abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15939837/)). The design is retrospective: adults reporting when their disorders began, which bounds what the study can show. What it establishes is the schedule on which disorders arrive; the case for treating them early follows from that schedule.

The word to watch is "easier." The survey shows early onset; whether early treatment is easier is a separate claim, argued in prevention trials rather than settled by this data.
