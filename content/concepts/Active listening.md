Active listening is a way of listening in which the listener restates what the speaker has said, in the listener's own words, to check that the meaning has been grasped. The term and the practice come from a 1957 booklet by Carl Rogers and Richard Farson, written for supervisors at the University of Chicago's Industrial Relations Center.

> "Basically, it requires that we get inside the speaker, that we grasp, from his point of view, just what it is he is communicating to us. More than that, we must convey to the speaker that we are seeing things from his point of view." (Rogers and Farson, [Active Listening (1957), excerpt PDF](https://wholebeinginstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/Rogers_Farson_Active-Listening.pdf))

### What the listener does

The booklet treats paraphrase as a test, not a performance. "You can do this by reflecting in your own words what the speaker seems to mean by his words and actions. His response to this will tell you whether or not he feels understood." Parroting the speaker's words back proves only hearing, not understanding; the restatement must satisfy the speaker. The authors' rule of thumb: "assume that you never really understand until you can communicate this understanding to the others satisfaction."

### What the evidence shows

A 2014 experiment compared trained listener responses in brief first conversations: 115 participants talked with 10 confederates taught to respond with active listening messages, advice, or simple acknowledgements ([Weger, Castle Bell, Minei and Robinson, 2014](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/scopus2010/9657/)). Participants who received active listening felt more understood than those who received advice or simple acknowledgements. For conversational satisfaction and the listener's social attractiveness, active listening and advice both outperformed simple acknowledgements, with small effect sizes, and did not differ from each other. The study concerns initial interactions; it does not show active listening beating advice on every outcome.
