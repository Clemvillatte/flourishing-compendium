“Sorrow's fruit” — Kahlil Gibran, The Prophet, “On Joy and Sorrow,” as literary parallel.

Sorrow's fruit is a literary image from Kahlil Gibran's The Prophet (1923): in the chapter on joy and sorrow, grief and delight are drawn as inseparable, drawn from the same well. The chapter refuses to rank the two against each other: they come together, sit together, weigh together.

> "Your joy is your sorrow unmasked. And the selfsame well from which your laughter rises was oftentimes filled with your tears. And how else can it be? The deeper that sorrow carves into your being, the more joy you can contain." (Kahlil Gibran, The Prophet, "On Joy and Sorrow", [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/58585/pg58585.txt))

### What the image does

Gibran pictures joy and sorrow as inseparable. His cup and lute images suggest that the same life can contain both pain and delight. This is a literary image, and the page offers it as such: it is a way of seeing grief, and the compendium's principle SP095 carries the practical edge it points to, converting suffering into understanding at the moment of loss.
