“Sorrow's fruit” — Kahlil Gibran, The Prophet, “On Joy and Sorrow,” as literary parallel.

Sorrow's fruit is the idea that grief is not the opposite of what delighted you but its shadow, and that what sorrow carves in a person becomes the vessel that joy later fills. The compendium's literary source is Kahlil Gibran's The Prophet (1923), where the chapter on joy and sorrow refuses to rank the two against each other: they come together, sit together, weigh together.

> "Your joy is your sorrow unmasked. And the selfsame well from which your laughter rises was oftentimes filled with your tears. And how else can it be? The deeper that sorrow carves into your being, the more joy you can contain." (Kahlil Gibran, The Prophet, "On Joy and Sorrow", [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/58585/pg58585.txt))

### What the image does

The chapter works by craft metaphors: the cup is fired in the potter's oven, the lute is hollowed with knives. Pain, in this picture, is the making of the instrument, and the reader grieving a loss is told to look again into the heart, where the weeping and the delight turn out to be for the same thing. The compendium's principle SP095 carries the practical edge: convert suffering into understanding at the moment of loss, not by skipping the grief but by asking what it is the grief is made of.
