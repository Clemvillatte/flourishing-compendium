"Spiritual joy" — Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics I.

Spiritual joy, in the compendium's Aristotelian frame, is the gladness that belongs to a life going well: the felt side of eudaimonia, activity in accordance with virtue, rather than a mood injected from outside. Aristotle defines happiness as exactly that kind of working, and the Nicomachean Ethics argues it cannot be amusement, since happiness is what we "choose always for its own sake, and never with a view to anything" further. The felt side is in the text too: the good man "takes pleasure in the actions which accord with Virtue and is annoyed at those which spring from Vice", and Book X insists that "Happiness stands not in such pastimes but in the Workings in the way of Excellence".

> "The question may be determined also by a reference to our definition of Happiness, that it is a working of the soul in the way of excellence or virtue of a certain kind:" (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book I, D.P. Chase translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt); the source sentence continues "and of the other goods, some we must have to begin with...")

### What the joy is made of

Because happiness is a working, joy here is a by-product of activity rather than a target to be aimed at directly. The compendium's principle B118 carries this into the Buddhist register, where gladness (piti) is treated as part of the path rather than a distraction from it: B118 reads a sutta that lists rapture among the factors that raise a sluggish mind. For a writer on flourishing, the page's essay angle is this: joy that lasts is tied to what one is doing with one's soul, not to what one is consuming; the Ethics denies that happiness lies in pastimes and bodily pleasures, which "any ordinary person, or even a slave, might enjoy".
