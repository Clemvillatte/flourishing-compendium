Abraham Maslow set out the hierarchy of needs in "A Theory of Human Motivation" (Psychological Review, 50, 370-396, 1943). Its central claim is that "human needs arrange themselves in hierarchies of pre-potency. That is to say, the appearance of one need usually rests on the prior satisfaction of another, more pre-potent need. Man is a perpetually wanting animal." ([Maslow, 1943, Classics in the History of Psychology](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Maslow/motivation.htm))

### The levels in Maslow's words

Physiological needs come first. Once they are met, "at once other (and 'higher') needs emerge": safety, then "the love and affection and belongingness needs", then esteem, a need for "self-respect, or self-esteem, and for the esteem of others". At the top is self-actualisation, "the desire to become more and more what one is, to become everything that one is capable of becoming", a term Maslow credits to Kurt Goldstein. His shorthand: "What a man can be, he must be."

### The hierarchy is not all-or-none

Maslow wrote that the order is "not nearly as rigid as we may have implied", and noted exceptions, such as people "in whom, for instance, self-esteem seems to be more important than love". He also rejected the idea that "a need must be satisfied 100 per cent before the next need emerges". Instead, "most members of our society who are normal, are partially satisfied in all their basic needs and partially unsatisfied in all their basic needs at the same time". His illustration, with figures he calls "arbitrary", was that the average citizen is satisfied "perhaps 85 per cent in his physiological needs, 70 per cent in his safety needs, 50 per cent in his love needs, 40 per cent in his self-esteem needs, and 10 per cent in his self-actualization needs".

### A large cross-national test

Tay and Diener analysed Gallup World Poll data from 123 countries. They found that "need fulfillment was consistently associated with SWB across world regions", but also that "the associations of SWB with the fulfillment of specific needs were largely independent of whether other needs were fulfilled" ([Tay and Diener, 2011, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21688922/)).

Diener said: "Our findings suggest that Maslow's theory is largely correct", with the departure that "a person can report having good social relationships and self-actualization even if their basic needs and safety needs are not completely fulfilled." ([University of Illinois news bureau](https://news.illinois.edu/researchers-look-for-ingredients-of-happiness-around-the-world/)). A summary of the study notes the data are cross-sectional, so causal direction is uncertain ([Positive Psychology News](https://positivepsychologynews.com/news/bridget-grenville-cleave/2011062718378)).

### What this means for the essays

The empirical record backs the claim that these needs matter everywhere. It does not back a strict order in which each must be filled before the next counts. Maslow's own text is more cautious than the popular ladder.
