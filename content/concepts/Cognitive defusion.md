Cognitive defusion is the ACT practice of seeing thoughts as thoughts — mental events, constructions passing through awareness — rather than as commands, facts, or the self. Where cognitive change strategies argue with a thought's content, defusion changes one's *relationship* to it: “I'm a failure” becomes “I'm having the thought that I'm a failure,” and the thought loses its authority ([Drexel Defusion Scale — “the ability to achieve psychological distance from internal experiences”](https://drexel.edu/~/media/Files/psychology/labs/innovation/Forman%20Herbert%202012%20Drexel%20Defusion%20Scale.ashx); critical review of defusion and decentering](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5103165/)). It parallels the Stoic separation of impression from judgement ([[S045 — Examine every impression]]; [[S047 — See impressions as what they are — appearances, not realities]]) and of the Buddhist raft parable — views are tools, to be used and set down ([[B014 — Use views as tools, not possessions (the raft)]]; [[B127 — Dismantle the raft when you've crossed]]).

The mechanism: language fuses with experience by default — the word “failure” *is* the experience until attention steps back ([[CO011 — Recognise that thinking can be fused with its content — you can believe your thoughts]]; [[B074 — Thoughts are not facts]]). Defusion inserts the gap: noticing the thought as a thought, without disputing it — often by thanking the mind, singing it, or labelling the process (“there's the catastrophing again”) ([[P159 — Defuse — see thoughts as thoughts]]; [[E006 — Practise the epoché in daily life]]; [[Y018 — Label fiction as fiction]]).

The distinction to hold: defusion is not reappraisal. [[Cognitive reappraisal]] replaces the thought's content with a better reading; defusion leaves the content untouched and loosens its grip — useful when the thought is sticky, when disputing feeds it, or when there is no time ([[K061 — Recognize thoughts' nature rather than fighting their content]]; [[D016 — Distrust names and labels]]).

An example at its smallest: the same sentence written twice on a card — “I am worthless” and “I am having the thought that I am worthless” — and one minute spent noticing how differently the two land.

The cross-tradition cognates are exact: [[Anatta]] and [[Self-as-context]] are the Buddhist and ACT accounts of the observing perspective defusion trains, and [[Right concentration]] is the practice through which thoughts are watched arising and passing without being followed.
