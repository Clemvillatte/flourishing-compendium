Plato's account of the good inner life in the Republic: the soul has parts, like the city has classes, and the just person is the one whose parts stop interfering with each other. The seed's phrase, the harmonized city-soul, points at the analogy the book is built on.

### The just man as his own law

> "...for the just man does not permit the several elements within him to interfere with one another, or any of them to do the work of others,—he sets in order his own inner life, and is his own master and his own law, and is at peace with himself; and when he has bound together the three principles within him, which may be compared to the higher, lower, and middle notes of the scale, and the intermediate intervals—when he has bound all these together, and is no longer many, but has become one entirely temperate and perfectly adjusted nature, then he proceeds to act..." — Plato, [Republic IV, 443d-e (Jowett trans., Project Gutenberg)](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1497/1497-0.txt)

The sentence that follows makes the standard explicit: whatever "preserves and cooperates with this harmonious condition" is just and good action, and whatever impairs it is unjust action, with ignorance presiding over that ([Republic IV](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1497/1497-0.txt)). Order is not imposed from outside by rule-following; Jowett's rendering says justice concerns "not with the outward man, but with the inward, which is the true self and concernment". The image is musical: three principles bound like the notes of a scale, many becoming one.
