The concept says that reappraisal works by changing the appraisal, not by tampering with the feeling, so the person has to be able to tell the two apart. The seed sentence is "changing the appraisal changes the felt response (Gross & John, 2003; Webb et al., 2012)." Both papers were opened. They support the seed sentence, and the title's stronger claim, that effectiveness depends on separating feeling from appraisal, is tested by neither. The nearest evidence for that is labelled below.

### What reappraisal is

Gross and John's five studies found that people who habitually use reappraisal "experience and express greater positive emotion and lesser negative emotion" than suppressors, and that reappraisal "is related positively to well-being" ([Gross and John, 2003, J Pers Soc Psychol 85(2):348-362](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12916575/)). That is evidence on habitual use and outcomes, not on separating feeling from appraisal.

### Where the appraisal is aimed

Webb, Miles and Sheeran's meta-analysis of 306 experimental comparisons found that how reappraisal is aimed matters: "reappraising the emotional response proved less effective (d+ = 0.23) than reappraising the emotional stimulus (d+ = 0.36) or using perspective taking (d+ = 0.45)" ([Webb et al., 2012, Psychological Bulletin 138(4):775-808](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22582737/)). This is the closest finding to the page title: changing how one construes the situation outperformed working on the response itself. The authors do not describe it as separating feeling from appraisal, so that reading is mine.

### Knowing which feeling it is

Barrett, Gross, Christensen and Benvenuto argued that knowing precisely what one feels "helps to inform us about the significance of the immediate situation" and helps in deciding "what, if anything, we should do about changing how we feel." In a 14-day diary study, negative emotion differentiation was positively related to regulation, particularly at high emotional intensity, though the authors caution the analyses "were correlational, and therefore no causal connection... can be assumed" ([Barrett et al., 2001, Cognition and Emotion 15(6):713-724](https://www.affective-science.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/01MaprelationDiffReg.pdf)).

A later study by Kalokerinos and colleagues found that emotion differentiation was not consistently related to which strategies people chose, and in some analyses was negatively associated with reappraisal use. It did find that "low differentiation inhibited strategy effectiveness": among low differentiators, strategies were associated with increased negative emotion ([Kalokerinos et al., 2019](https://ppw.kuleuven.be/okp/_pdf/Kalokerinos2019DTRLN.pdf)). This is the strongest support for the idea that reappraisal depends on discerning the feeling, and it does not isolate reappraisal.

### In the compendium

AF002 (Distinguish the feeling from its interpretation) cites this page as its psychology parallel. The studies opened here support that changing the appraisal can change the response, and that clearer emotion differentiation goes with better regulation. Neither shows that separating feeling from appraisal is a condition for reappraisal to work, so AF002's wording is best read as a practice suggestion.
