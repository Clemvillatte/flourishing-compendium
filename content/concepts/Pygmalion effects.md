high expectations elicit better performance (Rosenthal and Jacobson, 1968).

The Oak School experiment: teachers were told that certain randomly chosen pupils were on the brink of an intellectual spurt. Eight months later those pupils had gained more IQ than their classmates, and the book's own summary is careful about the size of the claim.

> "The results of the experiment we have described in some detail provide further evidence that one person's expectations of another's behavior may come to serve as a self-fulfilling prophecy. When teachers expected that certain children would show greater intellectual development, those children did show greater intellectual development." (Rosenthal and Jacobson 1968, ch. 7, [excerpt at Tufts](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.tufts.edu/dist/d/804/files/2017/02/Pygmalion-in-the-Classroom.pdf))
>
> "In the year of the experiment, the undesignated control-group children gained over eight IQ points while the experimental-group children, the special children, gained over twelve." (Rosenthal and Jacobson 1968, ch. 7, [excerpt at Tufts](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/sites.tufts.edu/dist/d/804/files/2017/02/Pygmalion-in-the-Classroom.pdf))

The book's own caveat: "For the basic year of the experiment, the self-fulfilling prophecy was in evidence primarily at the lower grade levels; it is difficult to be certain why that was the case."
