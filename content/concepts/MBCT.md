Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) is an eight-week group program that combines mindfulness training with ideas from cognitive therapy to stop depression from coming back. It was built for people who have recovered from repeated episodes of depression. The original trial abstract states its aim plainly:

> "This study evaluated mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT), a group intervention designed to train recovered recurrently depressed patients to disengage from dysphoria-activated depressogenic thinking that may mediate relapse/recurrence."
>
> (Teasdale, Segal, Williams, Ridgeway, Soulsby and Lau, "Prevention of relapse/recurrence in major depression by mindfulness-based cognitive therapy", Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology 68(4), 2000, pp. 615-623)

### Origin
MBCT grew out of Jon Kabat-Zinn's mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR), an eight-week program started in 1979 at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center. As the founders' own account puts it:

> "Zindel Segal, Mark Williams and John Teasdale adapted the MBSR program so it could be used especially for people who had suffered repeated bouts of depression in their lives."
>
> ([MBCT — about](https://mbct.co.uk/about-mbct/))

The three set out their approach in the 2002 book Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy for Depression: A New Approach to Preventing Relapse (Guilford Press). The first controlled test was the 2000 trial above: 145 recovered, recurrently depressed patients, randomized to treatment as usual with or without MBCT, followed for 60 weeks.

### Core claims
The theory behind the program is a claim about how relapse happens, and a claim about what interrupts it:

> "Mindfulness helps us to enter an alternative mode of mind that includes thinking but is bigger than thinking."
>
> "It teaches us to shift mental gears, from the mode of mind dominated by critical thinking (likely to provoke and accelerate downward mood spirals) to another mode of mind in which we experience the world directly, non-conceptually, and non-judgementally."
>
> ([MBCT — how mindfulness helps](https://mbct.co.uk/about-mbct/))

The trial result, kept distinct from the theory: for patients with three or more previous episodes of depression (77% of the sample), MBCT significantly reduced risk of relapse or recurrence; for patients with only two previous episodes, it did not (Teasdale et al. 2000).

### How the theory explains relapse
This is a mechanistic theory in program form. Ordinary low mood reactivates the old depressogenic thinking patterns laid down during past episodes; the person slides into rumination without noticing, and the rumination deepens the mood. MBCT trains the skill of catching that slide early. The founders' site describes the vulnerable state as a kind of tunnel vision, and what the practice does about it:

> "Mindfulness practice helps us to see more clearly these patterns of mind; and to learn how recognise when our mood is beginning to go down. This means we can 'nip it in the bud' much earlier than before."
>
> ([MBCT — about](https://mbct.co.uk/about-mbct/))

The evidence base is now strong for relapse prevention in recurrent depression. An individual-patient-data meta-analysis of nine randomized trials (1258 participants with data) found patients receiving MBCT had a reduced risk of depressive relapse within 60 weeks compared with those who did not (hazard ratio 0.69), and a reduced risk against active treatments including antidepressants (hazard ratio 0.79); 229 of 596 who received MBCT relapsed, against 327 of 662 who did not (Kuyken et al. 2016). The benefit is prevention of relapse in recurrent depression, not treatment of an active episode.

### In everyday life
The compendium's reader meets MBCT where the founders place it: in the small, early moment. A person who has been depressed before notices the first dip on a grey Monday: a flatness, a flicker of the old "what's the point". The MBCT-trained move is not to argue with the thought but to register the dip as the start of a known spiral, feel the breath, and attend to what is actually present, so the spiral is "nipped in the bud" while it is still small. The founders also note the reverse side of the tunnel:

> "Mindfulness teaches us a way in which we can get back in touch with the experience of being alive – learning to savour again the simple pleasures that may have been available to us all along but which we took for granted."
>
> ([MBCT — about](https://mbct.co.uk/about-mbct/))
