The research behind journaling as self-reflection is the expressive-writing tradition that Pennebaker began, now called experimental disclosure in the literature. The meta-analytic verdict, across 146 randomized studies:

> "Results of random effects analyses indicate that experimental disclosure is effective, with a positive and significant average r-effect size of .075." (Frattaroli 2006, Psychological Bulletin 132(6): 823-865, [abstract, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17073523/))

The field's own definition of what is being tested:

> "Disclosing information, thoughts, and feelings about personal and meaningful topics (experimental disclosure) is purported to have various health and psychological consequences (e.g., J. W. Pennebaker, 1993)." (Frattaroli 2006, [abstract, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17073523/))

The honest reading of the effect size matters for the compendium, because the practice earns its place as a modest, near-free intervention: it asks for a pen and twenty minutes, it has been tested in randomised designs across health and student populations, and the moderator findings, including for the writing instructions themselves, mean the how is not a detail. The seed's Smyth reference belongs to the same line of work as the earlier fixed-effects meta-analyses the abstract names. the abstract's own framing of the two earlier meta-analyses notes they "suggest that experimental disclosure has a positive and significant effect" while being limited in generalizability, and the new pooled estimate is small. Writing about meaningful personal experience is a real, small, reliable effect, not a cure, and it depends on moderators the meta-analysis went on to identify.

**Plain words** Writing down what happened and what you felt about it, honestly, for a while, does something measurable to health and mood — something small but real, repeatable across many studies.
