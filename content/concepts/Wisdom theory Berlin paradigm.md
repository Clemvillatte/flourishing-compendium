**Wisdom theory Berlin paradigm** — Baltes — wisdom is expert knowledge about the fundamental pragmatics of life: life planning, management and review.

The Berlin Wisdom Paradigm is the research program Paul Baltes and Ursula Staudinger ran at the Max Planck Institute for Human Development, which turned an ancient philosophical ideal into something psychologists could score. Its definition, in the program's own summary:

> wisdom in this paradigm is defined as an expert knowledge system concerning the fundamental pragmatics of life. These include knowledge and judgment about the meaning and conduct of life and the orchestration of human development toward excellence while attending conjointly to personal and collective well-being. (Baltes and Staudinger, "Wisdom: A Metaheuristic (Pragmatic) to Orchestrate Mind and Virtue Toward Excellence," American Psychologist 55(1), 2000, p. 122, [open PDF, Max Planck Society repository](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2102711_4/component/file_2102710/content))

The phrase doing the heavy lifting is "fundamental pragmatics of life," and the authors gloss it directly:

> With fundamental pragmatics, we mean knowledge and judgment about the essence of the human condition and the ways and means of planning, managing, and understanding a good life. (ibid., p. 124)

### How wisdom is measured

The paradigm's method follows from treating wisdom as expertise. People think aloud about hard life problems, and their responses are scored:

> Measurement includes think-aloud protocols concerning various problems of life associated with life planning, life management, and life review. Responses are evaluated with reference to a family of 5 criteria: rich factual and procedural knowledge, lifespan contextualism, relativism of values and life priorities, and recognition and management of uncertainty. (ibid., p. 122)

The problems are deliberately hard. One standard task reads: "Someone receives a telephone call from a good friend who says that he or she cannot go on like this and has decided to commit suicide. What might one/the person take into consideration and do in such a situation?" (ibid., p. 126). Another reads, in full: "A 15-year-old girl wants to get married right away. What should one/she consider and do?" (ibid., p. 126). Appendix B of the article prints excerpts of a high- and a low-rated response to that marriage question; the high-rated one opens: "Perhaps in this instance, special life circumstances are involved, such that the girl has a terminal illness." (ibid., p. 136) It goes on to consider other cultures, historical periods, and value systems. The scoring itself is done by "a select panel of judges, who are extensively trained and calibrated in applying the criteria," rating each protocol "in light of the five wisdom-related criteria using a 7-point scale" (ibid., p. 126). The paradigm measures general wisdom, a person's insight into life in general; the later Bremen paradigm was developed to assess personal wisdom along an analogous set of criteria ([Dong, Weststrate and Fournier 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10336627/)). The article closes by characterizing wisdom as a "cognitive and motivational metaheuristic (pragmatic) that organizes and orchestrates knowledge toward human excellence in mind and virtue, both individually and collectively" ([Baltes and Staudinger 2000, p. 122](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11392856/)).
