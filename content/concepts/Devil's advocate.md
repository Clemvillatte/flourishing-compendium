The devil's advocate was a real office, not a figure of speech. In the Roman Catholic Church it named the Promoter of the Faith, the official whose job was to argue against declaring someone a saint. Sixtus V formally established the office in 1587, and John Paul II significantly reduced its role in 1983 when he revised the canonization procedures with Divinus Perfectionis Magister ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/devils-advocate)). The office was an institutional safeguard: the strongest case against sainthood had to be made, on the record, before the case for it could win.

> "Devil's advocate, a former office in the Roman Catholic Church, the Promoter of the Faith (Latin: promotor fidei), who critically examined the life of and miracles attributed to an individual proposed for beatification or canonization." ([Encyclopaedia Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/devils-advocate))

### Why the devil

The name came from the job description. The promoter's "presentation of facts included everything unfavourable to the candidate in order to uncover any character flaws or misrepresentations of their life of heroic sanctity" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/topic/devils-advocate)). The counterweight, arguing for the candidate, was the advocatus Dei, God's advocate. After the 1983 reform, Britannica notes, the promotor fidei "subsequently held little sway over the proceedings," and the phrase migrated into ordinary use: it now "describes anyone who champions a less accepted cause solely for the sake of argument" (same source).

The idea survives the office. An argument that has survived a determined opponent deserves more trust than one that has never met one, which is why the idiom keeps its usefulness even where the Church post no longer functions as it did.
