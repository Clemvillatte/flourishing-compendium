Plotinus's Enneads treats seeing as a transformation of the seer. His tractate On Beauty (I.6) argues that the soul cannot perceive the divine and the beautiful while remaining what it was.

> "No eye ever saw the sun without becoming sun-like, nor can a soul see beauty without becoming beautiful." (Plotinus, Enneads I.6.9, Armstrong translation, [Internet Archive, Loeb vol. 1](https://archive.org/details/plotinus-in-seven-volumes.-vol.-1-loeb-440))

### What the light requires

The tractate's instruction is preparation: "one must come to the sight with a seeing power made akin and like to what is seen". Those who come blear-eyed or unpurified see nothing, even if shown what is there. The passage gives the phrase its Plotinian ground: the light is a likeness formed in the one who looks.

The chapter carries the demand one step higher:

> "You must become first all godlike and all beautiful if you intend to see God and beauty." (Enneads I.6.9, Armstrong, [Internet Archive, Loeb vol. 1](https://archive.org/details/plotinus-in-seven-volumes.-vol.-1-loeb-440))

The chapter then describes the soul coming in its ascent to intellect, where it knows the Forms and recognises their beauty. Plotinus places the Good beyond this stage, holding beauty as a screen before it. His account connects the ability to perceive beauty with the condition of the soul doing the perceiving.

### A Daoist cousin

The Daodejing closes chapter 52 with light and return imagery of its own, and the compendium's D065 turns that verse into a modern prompt: check what is directing an action before continuing.

> "Who uses well his light,
> Reverting to its (source so) bright,
> Will from his body ward all blight" (Legge, [DDJ 52](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

The pairing is the compendium's own reading: Plotinus works out what the seeing asks of the seer, while the Daodejing verse speaks of using the light and returning to its source. The two pages sit side by side in the compendium's Daoist and Plotinian principles rather than merging into one doctrine.
