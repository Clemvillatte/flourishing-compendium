In the retina, lateral inhibition occurs when responses from surrounding photoreceptor cells inhibit a photoreceptor's light-evoked activity ([Thoreson & Mangel, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3401171/)). H. K. Hartline received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1967 partly for the discovery of antagonistic connections between neighboring photoreceptors in the horseshoe crab, Limulus ([Thoreson & Mangel, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3401171/)).

> "He and his colleagues found that the light-evoked activity of a single photoreceptor is inhibited by the responses of surrounding photoreceptor cells. This lateral inhibition creates a center-surround organization to the receptive field whereby light falling on the center excites the cell and light falling on surrounding regions inhibits the cell. Lateral or surround inhibition improves spatial discrimination and the detection of edges and generates the psychophysical phenomenon known as Mach bands (Ratliff and Hartline, 1959; Ratliff, 1965)." (Thoreson & Mangel, [Lateral interactions in the outer retina](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3401171/), Progress in Retinal and Eye Research 31(5), 2012, §1 Introduction)

### What the inhibition buys

Thoreson and Mangel describe lateral or surround inhibition as improving spatial discrimination and edge detection and generating Mach bands ([Thoreson & Mangel, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3401171/)). Ernst Mach inferred sensory inhibition in vision in 1865 from the illusory bands that now carry his name ([Wikipedia, Lateral inhibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateral_inhibition)). The mechanism is not exclusive to sight: lateral inhibition occurs primarily in visual processes but also in tactile, auditory and even olfactory processing ([Wikipedia, Lateral inhibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateral_inhibition)).
