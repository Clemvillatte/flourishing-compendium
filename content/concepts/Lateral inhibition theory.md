Lateral inhibition theory explains sensory contrast through neighbour suppression: an excited neuron reduces the activity of its neighbours, so boundaries between stimulus regions are sharpened rather than smeared. The theory's founding evidence is the horseshoe crab Limulus, where H. K. Hartline received the 1967 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine partly for the discovery of antagonistic connections between neighboring photoreceptors ([Thoreson & Mangel, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3401171/)).

> "He and his colleagues found that the light-evoked activity of a single photoreceptor is inhibited by the responses of surrounding photoreceptor cells. This lateral inhibition creates a center-surround organization to the receptive field whereby light falling on the center excites the cell and light falling on surrounding regions inhibits the cell. Lateral or surround inhibition improves spatial discrimination and the detection of edges and generates the psychophysical phenomenon known as Mach bands (Ratliff and Hartline, 1959; Ratliff, 1965)." (Thoreson & Mangel, [Lateral interactions in the outer retina](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3401171/), Progress in Retinal and Eye Research 31(5), 2012, pp. 407-441)

### What the inhibition buys

The perceptual payoff is edge emphasis: at a brightness border, cells on the brighter side suppress their neighbours on the darker side more than the reverse, exaggerating the difference so the edge stands out ([Thoreson & Mangel, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3401171/)). Ernst Mach inferred sensory inhibition in vision in 1865 from the illusory bands that now carry his name ([Wikipedia, Lateral inhibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateral_inhibition)). The mechanism is not exclusive to sight: lateral inhibition occurs primarily in visual processes but also in tactile, auditory and even olfactory processing ([Wikipedia, Lateral inhibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateral_inhibition)).
