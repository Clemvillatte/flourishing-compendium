### Definition

Sympathy, in Adam Smith's classic account, is the human resonance with other people's fortunes: the capacity by which their joy and misery become, in some measure, ours. The Theory of Moral Sentiments opens with it:

> How selfish soever man may be supposed, there are evidently some principles in his nature, which interest him in the fortune of others, and render their happiness necessary to him, though he derives nothing from it, except the pleasure of seeing it. Of this kind is pity or compassion, the emotion which we feel for the misery of others, when we either see it, or are made to conceive it in a very lively manner. ([Smith, Theory of Moral Sentiments, opening](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/67363/pg67363.txt))

Smith's term is broader than the modern one: sympathy covers any fellow-feeling, with the joyful as much as the miserable, and it works by imagination. We place ourselves in the other's case.

### Origin

For Smith, sympathy is the raw material of moral judgment. We cannot feel directly what others feel, so we judge conduct by whether the spectator can go along with it, and the whole machinery of conscience in his theory is the internalised spectator. Scheler later distinguished genuine feeling-with from mere emotional contagion and from vicarious feeling, and the modern empathy literature keeps his distinctions in service.

### A real-life example

A friend wins something you wanted. The flicker of envy is immediate; the wider feeling that follows, being glad anyway because it is hers, is sympathy doing its slower work. Nothing about the facts changed between the two moments. The imagination turned toward her side of them.
