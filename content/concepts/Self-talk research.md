Self-talk research studies what people say to themselves — its content, its functions, and its measurable effects on action. The original entry study is Hardy, Hall and Hardy's 2005 quantification of athletes' self-talk; a 2011 meta-analysis pooled the intervention findings.

> "That the content of athlete self-talk was generally positive, covert and abbreviated lends support to the application of Vygotsky's (1986) verbal self-regulation theory to the study of self-talk in sport" (Hardy, Hall & Hardy, 2005, [abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16195042/)).

### What the field found

Hardy, Hall and Hardy surveyed athletes with the Self-Talk Use Questionnaire: 295 athletes across sports and levels in Study 1, 164 recreational volleyball players in Study 2, with self-talk more used in competition than practice and by individual-sport more than team-sport athletes. The meta-analysis of Hatzigeorgiadis and colleagues pooled 32 studies and reported "a positive moderate effect size (ES = .48)," with the effect moderated by task and training: "self-talk interventions were more effective for tasks involving relatively fine, compared with relatively gross, motor demands, and for novel, compared with well-learned, tasks," "instructional self-talk was more effective for fine tasks than was motivational self-talk," and "interventions including self-talk training were more effective than those not including self-talk training" (Hatzigeorgiadis et al., 2011, [abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26167788/)).
