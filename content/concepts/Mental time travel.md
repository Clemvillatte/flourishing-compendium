“Mental time travel” — Suddendorf and Corballis, 1997.

Thomas Suddendorf and Michael Corballis coined the term for a capacity they argued marks "a discontinuity between ourselves and other animals":

> Mental time travel comprises the mental reconstruction of personal events from the past (episodic memory) and the mental construction of possible events in the future.

The two directions are one system on this account. Remembering a past picnic and imagining tomorrow's meeting draw on the same machinery, and the machinery is constructive rather than photographic. As Corballis puts it in a later restatement, "even episodic memory itself is better regarded as a construction than as a replay, and not always accurate". What the capacity buys is not a faithful archive but raw material for scenarios.

The evolutionary claim came with a boundary. The 1997 paper leaned on what they called the Bischof-Köhler hypothesis, the idea that "animals other than humans cannot anticipate future need or drive states, and are therefore bound to a present that is defined by their current motivational state". On their telling, an animal can act for a future it currently wants, but only humans act for a need they do not yet feel, like packing an umbrella for a rainless morning.

That boundary has since been contested. Reviewing the food-caching birds, Nicola Clayton and colleagues report that "western scrub-jays form integrated, flexible, trial-unique memories of what they hid, where and when" and that "these birds can adjust their caching behaviour in anticipation of future needs", concluding that "some animals can travel mentally backwards into the past, and forwards into the future". The discontinuity, if real, is a matter of degree.

### The language link

The capacity also mattered to how the authors read speech. Corballis argues that "mental time travel provides the basis for the generative and creative aspects of language, allowing us to communicate about past and future, and indeed tell stories that need have no basis in reality". On this view, displacement, the power to talk about what is not present, grows out of the power to think about what is not present.
