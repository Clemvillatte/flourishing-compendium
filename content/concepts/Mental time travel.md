“Mental time travel” — Suddendorf and Corballis, 1997.

Thomas Suddendorf and Michael Corballis described mental time travel as a capacity they argued marks a discontinuity between ourselves and other animals ([Clayton et al., 2003](https://bec.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/108/archive/papers/Clayton_4.25.07c.pdf)). Their 1997 definition, quoted in [Clayton, Bussey and Dickinson's Box 1](https://bec.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/108/archive/papers/Clayton_4.25.07c.pdf):

> Mental time travel comprises the mental reconstruction of personal events from the past (episodic memory) and the mental construction of possible events in the future.

Their definition brings remembering personal events and imagining possible futures under a common heading ([Clayton et al., 2003](https://bec.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/108/archive/papers/Clayton_4.25.07c.pdf)). The remembering side is constructive ([Corballis, 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2019.00217/pdf)). As [Corballis, 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2019.00217/pdf) puts it in a restatement:

> These are constructive acts—even episodic memory itself is better regarded as a construction than as a replay, and not always accurate.

Remembering supplies material for imagined scenarios ([Corballis, 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2019.00217/pdf)).

The evolutionary claim came with a boundary ([Clayton et al., 2003](https://bec.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/108/archive/papers/Clayton_4.25.07c.pdf)). The 1997 paper leaned on what they called the Bischof–Köhler hypothesis, again quoted in [Clayton and colleagues' Box 1](https://bec.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/108/archive/papers/Clayton_4.25.07c.pdf):

> ...animals other than humans cannot anticipate future need or drive states, and are therefore bound to a present that is defined by their current motivational state. We shall refer to this as the Bischof–Köhler hypothesis...

On their telling, an animal can act for a future it currently wants, but only humans act for a need they do not yet feel ([Clayton et al., 2003](https://bec.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/108/archive/papers/Clayton_4.25.07c.pdf)).

That boundary has been contested since ([Clayton et al., 2003](https://bec.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/108/archive/papers/Clayton_4.25.07c.pdf)). Reviewing the food-caching birds, [Nicola Clayton, Timothy Bussey and Anthony Dickinson's abstract](https://bec.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/108/archive/papers/Clayton_4.25.07c.pdf) reports:

> ...western scrub-jays form integrated, flexible, trial-unique memories of what they hid, where and when. Moreover, these birds can adjust their caching behaviour in anticipation of future needs.

They conclude that some animals can travel mentally backwards into the past, and forwards into the future, and suggest that some animals have elements of both episodic-like memory and future planning ([Clayton et al., 2003](https://bec.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/108/archive/papers/Clayton_4.25.07c.pdf)).

### The language link

The capacity also mattered to how [Corballis, 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2019.00217/pdf) reads speech. He argues:

> I argue in this article that mental time travel provides the basis for the generative and creative aspects of language, allowing us to communicate about past and future, and indeed tell stories that need have no basis in reality.

On this view, displacement, the power to talk about what is not present, grows out of the power to think about what is not present ([Corballis, 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2019.00217/pdf)).
