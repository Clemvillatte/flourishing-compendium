Pre-performance routines (PPRs) are the fixed sequences of breath, cue word, and timing that athletes run before executing a skill. The evidence base is a 2021 meta-analysis by Rupprecht, Tran, and Gröpel, published in the International Review of Sport and Exercise Psychology:

> The meta-analysis included 112 effect sizes from pre–post and experimental designs in low-pressure and pressurised conditions. Results showed a significant but small effect of PPRs on sport performance in pre–post designs (SMC* = 0.31, 95% CI [0.18, 0.44]) and moderate-to-large effects in experimental designs, both under low-pressure (Hedges' g = 0.64, 95% CI [0.45, 0.83]) and pressurised conditions (Hedges' g = 0.70, 95% CI [0.24, 1.16]). These effects were not moderated by the type of PPR, age, gender, skill level, or intervention characteristics.

(Rupprecht, Tran, & Gröpel, "The effectiveness of pre-performance routines in sports," [Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1750984X.2021.1944271))

The conclusion line is blunt: "Overall, the meta-analytic results support the benefits of the PPR intervention in practice regardless of the type of routine." The point under pressure is prevention: "the typical aim of using a PPR under pressure is to prevent performance failure or 'choking under pressure.'" The mechanism is attention — routines "reduce arousal in high-pressure situations," "ensur[e] consistency in the execution of skills," and "reduc[e] athlete self-awareness."

The compendium's [[D070 — Let stillness precede mastery]] is the contemplative form of the same move: what you do in the seconds before the act is part of the act.
