The bias names a specific reading error: when a provocation is ambiguous, some children fill the gap with hostile intent, and the fill predicts retaliation. Kenneth Dodge's group tested the mechanism across cultures in a 2015 PNAS study:

> "In every ecological group, in those situations in which a child attributed hostile intent to a peer, that child was more likely to report that he or she would respond with reactive aggression than in situations when that same child attributed benign intent." ([Dodge et al., 2015, PNAS 112(30): 9310-9315](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4522743/))

### The study

The participants were "1,299 children (mean age at year 1 = 8.3 y; 51% girls) from 12 diverse ecological-context groups in nine countries worldwide, followed across 4 y." In year 3 each child faced "10 hypothetical vignettes depicting an ambiguous provocation toward the child" and was asked to attribute the provocateur's intent, benign or hostile, and to predict a response, nonaggression or reactive aggression. Across children, hostile attributional bias scores predicted higher mother- and child-rated chronic aggressive behavior problems, even controlling for prior aggression.

### What it means

The bias is not a general suspicion of others. It shows up where the evidence runs out, in the bump from behind whose meaning must be guessed. The study measured predicted responses to hypothetical vignettes, and, separately, chronic aggressive behavior problems as rated by mothers and children. The authors' abstract notes that the findings point to potential interventions, while the study design does not itself test them.

I pair the finding with its contemplative counterweights: the empty boat principle, the principle on giving up the doer's claim, and the self-serving bias principle.
