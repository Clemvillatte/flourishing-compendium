“Solidarity” — Camus, The Rebel; Rorty, Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity; Scholz, Political Solidarity.

Solidarity is a bond of unity among people in the face of a shared adversity. The three works the seed cites each build it differently: Camus from revolt, Rorty from imagination, Scholz from a chosen commitment against injustice. This page reports each as the opened sources present it.

### Rorty: created by imagination

Rorty's introduction states the position as a hope. Solidarity, in his utopia, is a goal to be made, not a fact to be uncovered.

> "In my utopia, human solidarity would be seen not as a fact to be recognized by clearing away 'prejudice' or burrowing down to previously hidden depths but, rather, as a goal to be achieved. It is to be achieved not by inquiry but by imagination, the imaginative ability to see strange people as fellow sufferers. Solidarity is not discovered by reflection but created. It is created by increasing our sensitivity to the particular details of the pain and humiliation of other, unfamiliar sorts of people." (Rorty, Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity, Introduction, [UNLV copy](https://cdclv.unlv.edu/pragmatism/rorty_intro_irony.pdf))

### Scholz: a commitment against injustice

The Stanford Encyclopedia entry presents Scholz's view as political and oppositional. Scholz writes that political solidarity "arises in opposition to something" and "opposes injustice, oppression, tyranny, and social vulnerabilities". The entry's reading of that is that solidarity "is always geared toward overcoming injustice; it is essentially political." On that view a natural disaster can inspire strong bonds but not political solidarity, and participants owe each other positive moral obligations. ([Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/solidarity/), citing Scholz 2008, p. 54)

### Camus: where revolt leads

In The Rebel, Camus sets a limit on rebellion that rests on solidarity. Ingrid Anderson's article, which cites the Bower translation, quotes him:

> "any rebellion which claims the right to deny or destroy this solidarity loses simultaneously its right to be called rebellion and becomes in reality an acquiescence in murder." (Camus, The Rebel, as quoted in [Anderson 2016](https://www.jffp.org/ojs/jffp/article/download/788/749); Anderson gives the page as 30)

The quotation comes second-hand through Anderson, whose footnote cites Camus only by page; it has not been checked against a copy of The Rebel.

### Contested ground

The Stanford entry lists objections to solidarity itself. One is Shklar's warning that "ideologies of solidarity" attract people who find liberalism unsatisfying, as the entry summarizes it. Others are the charges that it promotes false beliefs among members and treats outsiders unfairly. The entry also gives the replies available to defenders. ([Stanford Encyclopedia](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/solidarity/))
