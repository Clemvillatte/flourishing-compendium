Cohen and Wills's account distinguishes two proposed relationships between social support and health ([Cohen, 1992, “Social Support, Stress, and Health”](https://garfield.library.upenn.edu/classics1992/A1992JA16900001.pdf)). The main-effect hypothesis concerns benefits regardless of stress level; the stress-buffering hypothesis concerns protection from the adverse effects of stressors ([same retrospective](https://garfield.library.upenn.edu/classics1992/A1992JA16900001.pdf)).

> Our own work suggested that different types of measures reflected different social and psychological processes and that careful categorization of studies by measure type might bring order to the literature.
>
> Sheldon Cohen, “Social Support, Stress, and Health,” [Current Contents, July 13, 1992](https://garfield.library.upenn.edu/classics1992/A1992JA16900001.pdf), reflecting on the 1985 review with Thomas A. Wills.

### What support measures capture

Cohen reports that the review found buffering patterns when studies measured perceived availability of support, and main effects when they measured numbers of social relationships ([1992 retrospective](https://garfield.library.upenn.edu/classics1992/A1992JA16900001.pdf)). He interprets these measurement differences as reflecting different social and psychological processes ([same retrospective](https://garfield.library.upenn.edu/classics1992/A1992JA16900001.pdf)).
