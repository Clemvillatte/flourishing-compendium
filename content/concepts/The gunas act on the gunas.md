“The gunas act on the gunas” — BG 3.27-3.28, the tradition's own formulation.

### What it is

The Bhagavad Gita's own statement of non-doership, and the compendium's anchor for it. The tradition holds that the field of nature acts on itself: "All activities are carried out by the three modes of material nature. But in ignorance, the soul, deluded by false identification with the body, thinks of itself as the doer" (Bhagavad Gita 3.27, trans. Mukundananda, [Holy Bhagavad Gita](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/3/verse/27)).

### What it carries

The follow-up verse states the perception that dissolves the doer's claim: "illumined persons distinguish the soul as distinct from guṇas and karmas. They perceive that it is only the guṇas (in the shape of the senses, mind, and others) that move among the guṇas (in the shape of the objects of perception), and thus they do not get entangled in them" (Bhagavad Gita 3.28, trans. Mukundananda, [Holy Bhagavad Gita](https://www.holy-bhagavad-gita.org/chapter/3/verse/28)). Sight sees; the hand takes; habit decides; each is a strand of nature responding to other strands, and the claim of a central author is an overlay, not a finding.

The Y104 principle gives up the doer's claim, and this is the tradition's precise mechanics for it: renunciation of authorship does not mean renunciation of action, since the gunas act regardless; it means renunciation of the ahaṅkāra, the I-making, that signs nature's acts. The practice is subtractive: watch the impulse arise, watch the field respond, and keep the signature line blank.
