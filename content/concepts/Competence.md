“Competence” — Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.

- Bandura, 1977
- Deci & Ryan, 2000
- Ryan & Deci, 2017
- Deci and Ryan, 2000

Competence is the felt sense of being able to affect outcomes, to interact effectively with one's environment rather than be pushed around by it. Robert White gave the concept its modern psychological form in 1959, arguing that drive theory could not explain the exploratory, skill-building behaviour of mammals and humans:

> As used here, competence will refer to an organism's capacity to interact effectively with its environment. (Robert W. White, "Motivation Reconsidered: The Concept of Competence," Psychological Review 66(5), 1959, p. 297, [archive.org PDF](https://ia800108.us.archive.org/view_archive.php?archive=/24/items/wikipedia-scholarly-sources-corpus/10.1037%252Fh0034974.zip&file=10.1037%252Fh0040934.pdf))

### What self-determination theory does with it

Self-determination theory treats competence as one of three innate psychological needs, alongside autonomy and relatedness: Ryan and Deci describe "the innate needs for competence, autonomy, and relatedness" (Contemporary Educational Psychology 25(1), 2000, p. 57, [open PDF](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_IntExtDefs.pdf)). On their account competence is not mere self-esteem or pride in outcomes; it is the satisfaction that comes from exercising and extending one's capacities at an optimal challenge.

### The catch

Feeling effective is not enough on its own. Ryan and Deci's cognitive evaluation theory adds a condition that has practical weight: competence only fuels motivation when the person experiences the activity as self-chosen.

> CET further specifies that feelings of competence will not enhance intrinsic motivation unless they are accompanied by a sense of autonomy (Ryan and Deci, "Intrinsic and Extrinsic Motivations: Classic Definitions and New Directions," Contemporary Educational Psychology 25(1), 2000, p. 58, [open PDF](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_IntExtDefs.pdf))

A pianist drilled by a controlling teacher may play flawlessly and feel nothing but relief. The Aristotle link is real but narrower than the modern sense: Nicomachean Ethics II grounds excellence (aretē) in habituation, doing the excellent thing repeatedly until one can do it well, which is competence's moral-craft ancestor rather than its definition.
