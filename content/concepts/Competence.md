“Competence” — a modern psychological concept (White, 1959); the [[T002 — Aristotle|Aristotle]] reference is to his account of skill built by practice.

- Bandura, 1977
- Deci & Ryan, 2000
- Ryan & Deci, 2017
- Deci and Ryan, 2000

Competence is the capacity to interact effectively with one's environment. Robert White gave the concept its modern psychological form in 1959, proposing that drive theory could not account for the exploratory, skill-building behaviour of mammals and humans:

> As used here, competence will refer to an organism's capacity to interact effectively with its environment. (Robert W. White, "Motivation Reconsidered: The Concept of Competence," Psychological Review 66(5), 1959, p. 297, [archive.org PDF](https://ia800108.us.archive.org/view_archive.php?archive=/24/items/wikipedia-scholarly-sources-corpus/10.1037%252Fh0034974.zip&file=10.1037%252Fh0040934.pdf))

Aristotle, in the Nicomachean Ethics, describes learning through practice: "the things we have to learn before we can do them, we learn by doing them, e.g. men become builders by building and lyreplayers by playing the lyre" (Book II, W.D. Ross translation, [MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.ii.html)).

### What self-determination theory does with it

Self-determination theory treats competence as one of three innate psychological needs, alongside autonomy and relatedness: Ryan and Deci describe "the innate needs for competence, autonomy, and relatedness" (Contemporary Educational Psychology 25(1), 2000, p. 57, [open PDF](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_IntExtDefs.pdf)). White defines a capacity; SDT's discussion centres on perceived competence and need satisfaction during action.

### The autonomy condition

Cognitive evaluation theory, SDT's subtheory for intrinsic motivation, proposes that perceived competence enhances intrinsic motivation only alongside a sense of autonomy:

> CET further specifies that feelings of competence will not enhance intrinsic motivation unless they are accompanied by a sense of autonomy or, in attributional terms, by an internal perceived locus of causality (IPLOC; deCharms, 1968). (Ryan and Deci, "Intrinsic and Extrinsic Motivations: Classic Definitions and New Directions," Contemporary Educational Psychology 25(1), 2000, p. 58, [open PDF](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_IntExtDefs.pdf))

By way of illustration: a pianist drilled by a controlling teacher may play accurately yet experience the practice as relief rather than satisfaction.
