Analytic and holistic cognition are two habits of attention and explanation documented across cultures ([Nisbett et al., 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11381831/)). Richard Nisbett and colleagues proposed in 2001 that the patterns arise from differing social practices, though the authors describe the social-origins claim as speculation ([Nisbett et al., 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11381831/)).

> "The authors find East Asians to be holistic, attending to the entire field and assigning causality to it, making relatively little use of categories and formal logic, and relying on 'dialectical' reasoning, whereas Westerners are more analytic, paying attention primarily to the object and the categories to which it belongs and using rules, including formal logic, to understand its behavior." — Nisbett, Peng, Choi and Norenzayan, abstract, Psychological Review, 2001 ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11381831/))

The analytic style pays attention primarily to the object and the categories it belongs to, using rules to understand its behaviour ([Nisbett et al., 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11381831/)). The holistic style attends to the entire field and assigns causality to it, making relatively little use of categories and formal logic ([Nisbett et al., 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11381831/)).

The two styles differ in what they treat as the thing to be explained, which is why the difference shows up as a difference in causality rather than as a difference in intelligence. An analytic reader asks what the object is and what rules govern things of that kind; a holistic reader asks what the field is doing and what the object is doing inside it. Neither question is a misreading of the other; they are questions about different units, and the unit chosen is what determines what can count as an answer.

The social-origins claim is the part the authors themselves flag as speculation, which is worth keeping attached to the finding rather than dropped from it. The claim is that the patterns arise from differing social practices, and the authors describe that origin as something they are proposing rather than something they have shown. A person applying the concept can therefore take the two styles as documented without taking the explanation for them as settled, and can read their own habitual unit of explanation as one among two rather than as the natural one.
