“Confucian duty” — Confucius, Analects.

Duty in the Analects has two anchors: what is right (yi), and the family one is born into. The first is stated in a single line: "The noble man is aware of fairness, the inferior man is aware of advantage" (4:16). The second is the root from which the rest of virtue grows:

> "The noble man concerns himself with the fundamentals. Once the fundamentals are established, the proper way appears. Are not filial piety and obedience to elders fundamental to the actualization of fundamental human goodness?" (1:2) ([Analects, A. Charles Muller translation](http://www.acmuller.net/con-dao/analects.html))

The duty to parents is not mere support, and Confucius is blunt about the difference:

> "Nowadays filial piety means being able to feed your parents. But everyone does this for even horses and dogs. Without respect, what's the difference?" (2:7)

It is also not blind obedience. The Analects asks for attention to the parents' condition, "Your parents' age should not be ignored. Sometimes it will be a source of joy, and sometimes it will be a source of apprehension" (4:21), and it licenses respectful disagreement:

> "When you serve your mother and father it is okay to try to correct them once in a while. But if you see that they are not going to listen to you, keep your respect for them and don't distance yourself from them. Work without complaining." (4:18)

Where the duty bites hardest, it diverges sharply from duty as a Western ethic of rules would shape it. Told of a land where a son testifies against his father who stole a sheep, Confucius answers:

> "The righteous men in my land are different from this. The father conceals the wrongs of the son, and the son conceals the wrongs of the father. This is the correct way!" (13:18)

The point is not that theft is fine; it is that duty is learned first in the bonds one does not choose, and a life of duties must keep its roots. The compendium's principle [[MO069 — Recognise motivation and culture — motivational ideals vary]] uses this as one of its exhibits: an ideal of duty anchored in relationship rather than rule, contrasted with [[The categorical imperative]]'s demand that duty hold regardless of relation.
