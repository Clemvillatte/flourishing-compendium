Conservation of Resources (COR) theory is Stevan E. Hobfoll's 1989 model of stress: stress is not a thing that happens to a person but a relation between a person and their resources. People invest in and defend a stock of valued resources, and stress arises when those resources are threatened, lost, or fail to return after investment. The founding paper's abstract states the model:

> "This resource-oriented model is based on the supposition that people strive to retain, project, and build resources and that what is threatening to them is the potential or actual loss of these valued resources." (Hobfoll, 1989, abstract, [PubMed record, PMID 2648906](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2648906/))

### Origin

Hobfoll, then at Kent State University, published the model in American Psychologist in 1989 as "a new attempt at conceptualizing stress". The paper's stated target was the state of stress research: the abstract charges that existing conceptualizations were "too phenomenological and ambiguous, and consequently, not given to direct empirical testing" (same record). A resource-oriented model was meant to make stress measurable: count what people have, watch what threatens it.

### Core claims

The theory's central claim, as the abstract puts it, is that threat is about resources:

> "what is threatening to them is the potential or actual loss of these valued resources" (Hobfoll, 1989, abstract, [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2648906/))

Later summaries of the theory add its best-known asymmetries, stated in the theory's own terms by Hobfoll and colleagues: loss is more salient than gain, and loss begets further loss, so people who have lost resources become more vulnerable to the next loss (Hobfoll, Halbesleben, Neveu & Westman, Conservation of Resources Theory, Handbook of Stress and Health chapter).

### How the theory explains

A resource theory explains by accounting: behaviour becomes an investment strategy. Working late to keep a job, keeping a friendship warm, maintaining health, guarding a reputation: all are resource defence, and a stressor is anything that draws the balance down. The model deliberately reframes stress appraisal away from purely subjective judgment toward what can be observed and counted, which is what made it productive in burnout and disaster research. Its scope limit is the mirror of its strength: it treats the person as a portfolio, and phenomena that resist resource framing (meaning-making in loss, for instance) need supplementation from other models.

### In everyday life

A person juggling a new baby, a sick parent and a work deadline is not merely "stressed out": on this theory they are watching several resource accounts drain at once, and the exhaustion is the sum of the losses plus the cost of defending what remains. The practical reading follows the theory's logic: audit which resources are actually threatened before concluding that everything is.
