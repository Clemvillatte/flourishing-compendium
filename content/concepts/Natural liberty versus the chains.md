"Natural liberty versus the chains" names a contrast from Rousseau's Social Contract. The book opens with it:

> Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains. One thinks himself the master of others, and still remains a greater slave than they. How did this change come about? I do not know. What can make it legitimate? That question I think I can answer. (Rousseau, Social Contract I.1, Cole translation)

The opening sentence is usually read as a complaint about political authority, and it is. But Rousseau also treats the chains as something people carry inside them. In Book I, Chapter 8, he sets natural liberty against civil liberty:

> What man loses by the social contract in his natural liberty and an unlimited right to everything he tries to get and succeeds in getting; what he gains is civil liberty and the proprietorship of all he possesses. If we are to avoid mistake in weighing one against the other, we must clearly distinguish natural liberty, which is bounded only by the strength of the individual, from civil liberty, which is limited by the general will. (Rousseau, Social Contract I.8, Cole translation)

He then adds an inner version of the same contrast:

> We might, over and above all this, add, to what man acquires in the civil state, moral liberty, which alone makes him truly master of himself; for the mere impulse of appetite is slavery, while obedience to a law which we prescribe to ourselves is liberty. (Rousseau, Social Contract I.8, Cole translation)

Read together, the passages give the concept its shape. Natural liberty, on Rousseau's definition, is bounded only by the strength of the individual; it is the freedom of a being that can take whatever it can get, and its limit is force. Civil liberty is limited by the general will, and moral liberty is obedience to a law one prescribes to oneself. The chains he describes include the impulse of appetite, which he calls slavery outright. The compendium's principles use the phrase for this whole contrast: unbounded wanting on one side, self-given order on the other.
