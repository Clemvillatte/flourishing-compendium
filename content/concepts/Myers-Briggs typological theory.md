The Myers-Briggs framework, built by Isabel Briggs Myers and her mother Katherine Cook Briggs ([Religious Studies Project](https://www.religiousstudiesproject.com/response/questioning-the-utility-of-myers-briggs-by-negeen-ghasedi/)), turns Carl Jung's psychological types into a questionnaire. Pittenger's review describes it as "a type theory that emphasizes the 16 unique categories of personality created by the four type pairs (Extraversion-Introversion, Sensing-Intuition, Thinking-Feeling, and Judging-Perceiving)", where the first three pairs derive from Jung's 1921 theory ([Pittenger, Cautionary comments regarding the MBTI, 2005, PDF copy](https://www.discprofiling.co.nz/i/ComparisonReferencePapers/Cautionary_comments_regarding_the_Myers_Briggs_Typ.pdf)). A critical review summarises the Jungian base: Jung "posited that personalities are primarily either extraverted or introverted and then defined by four paired 'functions': sensing vs. intuition and feeling vs. thinking" ([Stein and Swan, 2019, Evaluating the validity of MBTI theory, PDF](https://swanpsych.com/publications/SteinSwanMBTITheory_2019.pdf)).

### The theory's central claim

Stein and Swan examine the claim that people have a "true type" delineated across four dichotomies. They find the theory "falters on rigorous theoretical criteria in that it lacks agreement with known facts and data, lacks testability, and possesses internal contradictions". Their points include that Jungian theory "is not grounded in empirical data" and that the typing process "allows and bases reports of validity on self-verification", which introduces "circular evidence of validity".

### Evidence on the instrument

Pittenger's 1993 abstract concludes that "there is insufficient evidence to support the tenets of and claims about the utility of the test" ([Pittenger, 1993, The Utility of the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator, abstract on SciSpace](https://scispace.com/papers/the-utility-of-the-myers-briggs-type-indicator-7w8etcgs8t)). A copy of the paper adds that "the patterns of data do not suggest that there is reason to believe that there are 16 unique types of personality" ([Scribd copy](https://www.scribd.com/document/993815466/Pittenger-UtilityMyersBriggsType-1993)). His 2005 article says the MBTI does measure personality-related constructs, but "whether it measures the constructs identified by its underlying theory is not clear". It cites a 14-month retest with reliabilities from r = .48 for the thinking-feeling scale to r = .73 for extraversion-introversion.

A contrary reading exists. A meta-analysis by Capraro and Capraro (2002), as described on Wikipedia, found that the MBTI and its scales "yielded scores with strong internal consistency and test-retest reliability estimates, although variation was observed", but only 7% of the 210 studies reported on reliability directly ([Wikipedia, MBTI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myers%E2%80%93Briggs_Type_Indicator)). Stein and Swan note the publisher claims later versions fixed retest problems, a claim they call "difficult (and costly...) to verify independently".

### Relation to the Big Five

Stein and Swan say the MBTI's dichotomy definitions "differ drastically from dimensions that have been refined via decades of research, namely the Big Five".

### Using it in essays

The seed note, popular despite weak psychometrics, matches the critical literature, but it leaves out the publisher-side findings above. Use the framework to discuss Jungian typology and the history of personality testing, not as evidence of fixed types.
