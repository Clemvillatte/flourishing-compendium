Theory of valuation is John Dewey's 1939 monograph in the International Encyclopedia of Unified Science, and the name of its program: valuations are not cries of approval or disgust but judgments about the direction of action, and they can be tested like any other judgment. Dewey's own statement of the book's modest scope:

> "A theory of valuation as theory can only set forth the conditions which a method of formation of desires and interests must observe in concrete situations." ([Dewey, Theory of Valuation, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/stream/theoryofvaluatio032168mbp/theoryofvaluatio032168mbp_djvu.txt))

### The core claim

Dewey dissolves the split between valuing a means and valuing an end. His two working sentences:

> "Propositions in which things (acts and materials) are appraised as means enter necessarily into desires and interests that determine end-values." ([Dewey, Theory of Valuation](https://archive.org/stream/theoryofvaluatio032168mbp/theoryofvaluatio032168mbp_djvu.txt))

> "Any survey of the experiences in which ends-in-view are formed, and in which earlier impulsive tendencies are shaped through deliberation into a chosen desire, reveals that the object finally valued as an end to be reached is determined in its concrete makeup by appraisal of existing conditions as means." ([Dewey, Theory of Valuation](https://archive.org/stream/theoryofvaluatio032168mbp/theoryofvaluatio032168mbp_djvu.txt))

In other words, what a person finally wants is shaped by what they judge the situation makes possible; and the test of the want is what happens: "Observation of results obtained, of actual consequences in their agreement with and difference from ends anticipated or held in view, thus provides the conditions by which desires and interests (and hence valuations) are matured and tested" ([Dewey, Theory of Valuation](https://archive.org/stream/theoryofvaluatio032168mbp/theoryofvaluatio032168mbp_djvu.txt)).

### In the compendium

- [[P080 — Value is what fulfills conditions of flourishing]] carries the practical edge: on this theory, to ask whether something is good is to ask whether it fulfills the conditions of the flourishing it is part of, and the answer comes from consequences, not from taste.
