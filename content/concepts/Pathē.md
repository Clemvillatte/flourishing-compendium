Pathē (singular pathos) is the Stoic term for the passions: fear, appetite, distress, and delight. The Stoic definition, as reported in the standard sources and stated in the SEP article on Stoicism:

> They [the Stoics] say that passion is impulse which is excessive and disobedient to the dictates of reason, or a movement of soul which is irrational and contrary to nature; and that all passions belong to the soul's commanding-faculty.

(Stoicism, [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/), §4.7)

The four genera are distinguished by their object: "distress, a present perceived bad; fear, a future perceived bad; appetite, a future perceived good; delight (or pleasure), a present perceived good." And the Stoic claim is that a pathos is a judgment: each passion "can be described as an opinion or judgment or weak supposition," with two false elements, an ascription of value to something that is not good or bad, and an assessment of what to do about it.

The sage is not without feeling but with better feelings: "Whereas passions are typically directed at indifferents incorrectly judged to be good or bad, the Sage's good feelings are emotional responses to genuine goods, i.e. to virtue, virtuous actions, and virtuous friends."

The compendium's Stoic band runs on this analysis: the cure for a pathos is not suppression but the correction of the judgment underneath it.
