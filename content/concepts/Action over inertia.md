Action over inertia is the principle that character is built by doing, not by waiting to feel ready. [[T002 — Aristotle|Aristotle]]'s ground for it, in the Nicomachean Ethics:

> "For the things we have to learn before we can do them, we learn by doing them, e.g. men become builders by building and lyreplayers by playing the lyre; so too we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts."
> — [Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.1, trans. W. D. Ross, The Complete Aristotle](https://austria-forum.org/web-books/en/completearistotle00enisds/001766)

The argument reverses the intuition that ability precedes activity. In the crafts, nobody expects to become a builder by study alone: the competence is produced by the very acts it later guides. Aristotle extends the pattern to virtue itself. Just acts make a just person; the doing is the forming.

The clinical parallel is behavioral activation for depression: the withdrawal and inactivity the illness produces are precisely what sustain it, and engaging in small concrete actions — scheduled, specific, achievable — is among the better-supported treatments. The principle works because action changes the conditions that produce feeling, rather than waiting on the feeling to change first.

For practice, the entry's counsel: when inert, do not attempt to move the whole life at once. Choose one small act the done-self would have done, do it, and let the doing count as the training it is.
