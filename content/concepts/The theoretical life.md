The theoretical life is Aristotle's verdict at the end of the Nicomachean Ethics on which life is happiest. The argument's shape is simple: if happiness is activity in accordance with virtue, it should be activity in accordance with the highest virtue, "and this will be the virtue of the best part of us." Whether that part is the intellect or whatever else rules us, "it is the activity of this part of us in accordance with the virtue proper to it that will constitute perfect happiness; and it has been stated already that this activity is the activity of contemplation" ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.7 1177a17, Rackham, Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0054%3Abekker+page%3D1177a)).

### The case for contemplation

Rackham's rendering of the verdict: "And that happiness consists in contemplation may be accepted as agreeing both with the results already reached and with the truth. For contemplation is at once the highest form of activity (since the intellect is the highest thing in us, and the objects with which the intellect deals are the highest things that can be known), and also it is the most continuous, for we can reflect more continuously than we can carry on any form of action" ([NE X.7 1177a21, Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0054%3Abekker+page%3D1177a)). Three further marks complete the case. Contemplation "will be found to possess in the highest degree the quality that is termed self-sufficiency," because "the wise man on the contrary can also contemplate by himself" ([NE X.7 1177a27-34, Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0054%3Abekker+page%3D1177a)). It "may be held to be the only activity that is loved for its own sake: it produces no result beyond the actual act of contemplation" ([NE X.7 1177b1, Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0054%3Abekker+page%3D1177b)). And it fits leisure: "we do business in order that we may have leisure, and carry on war in order that we may have peace" ([NE X.7 1177b4, Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0054%3Abekker+page%3D1177b)), while politics and war are unleisured and chosen for something beyond themselves.

### Higher than the human level

The passage that made the chapter famous follows, and Aristotle does not soften it: "Such a life as this however will be higher than the human level: not in virtue of his humanity will a man achieve it, but in virtue of something within him that is divine" ([NE X.7 1177b26, Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0054%3Abekker+page%3D1177b)). Against those who say a man should have man's thoughts, he answers: "we ought so far as possible to achieve immortality, and do all that man may to live in accordance with the highest thing in him" ([NE X.7 1177b33, Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0054%3Abekker+page%3D1177b)). The book then adds the practical caveat the compendium keeps: this life is not the only happy one, and the political life of practical virtue is happy in a secondary degree. A091's rule, integrate contemplation and action, is the compendium's settlement of that tension.
