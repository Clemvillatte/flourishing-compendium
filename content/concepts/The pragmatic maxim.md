The pragmatic maxim is Charles Sanders Peirce's rule for clarifying ideas: to understand a conception, consider the practical effects we conceive the object of that conception to have — the sum of conceivable practical bearings *is* the concept's meaning ([Wikipedia, "Pragmatic maxim"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatic_maxim); [de Waal on Peirce, Philosophy Now](https://philosophynow.org/issues/43/Charles_Sanders_Peirce_The_Architect_of_Pragmatism)). Published in "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (1878), it is the founding document of pragmatism: no metaphysical deposit beyond the difference a belief would make to practice and experience ([[P020 — Ask for the cash value of your beliefs]] is the compendium's coinage).

The classical demonstration is the diamond: "hard" means, exhaustively, that the diamond would resist scratching — would not yield to other stones — and nothing further hides behind the property. Applied to a disputed concept, the maxim asks: what experiences would differ between the rival views? If nothing differs, the dispute is verbal ([[P029 — Watch how words work, not what they name]]); if something differs, that difference is the whole content of the debate. It is the ancestor of every operational definition in psychology, and the reason the compendium states principles as practices rather than doctrines ([[P079 — Choose acts by their consequences]]).

The distinction to hold: the maxim is not "truth is whatever works" — that crude pragmatism is James's popularisation, which Peirce himself renamed to escape. The maxim is a *criterion of meaning*, not a theory of truth; beliefs are clarified by their practical bearings, and then tested by inquiry ([[A032 — Cultivate good deliberation (euboulia)]]; [[A045 — Deliberate well]]).

An example at its smallest: any nagging abstraction in a plan — "alignment", "readiness" — answered by "what would we see if we had it?"

Its Peircean twin is [[Fallibilism]] — the same inquiry without certainty.
