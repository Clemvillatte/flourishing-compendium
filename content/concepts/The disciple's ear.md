“The disciple's ear” — "Is it not a pleasure to learn and practice?"; Confucius, Analects.

### What it is

The compendium uses this title for the Confucian picture of learning as reception: the student's first act is to listen well, and the teacher's job is to wait for the ear to open. The Analects opens with the pleasure of that reception, "Is it not pleasant to learn with a constant perseverance and application?" (Analects I.1, trans. Legge, [Gutenberg #3330](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3330/pg3330.txt)), and Confucius makes the student's eagerness the condition of teaching: "I do not open up the truth to one who is not eager to get knowledge, nor help out any one who is not anxious to explain himself. When I have presented one corner of a subject to any one, and he cannot from it learn the other three, I do not repeat my lesson" (Analects VII.8, trans. Legge, [Gutenberg #3330](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3330/pg3330.txt)).

### What it carries

The disciple's ear is not passive hearing. Confucius describes a posture of active selection in anyone who wants to learn: "When I walk along with two others, they may serve me as my teachers. I will select their good qualities and follow them, their bad qualities and avoid them" (Analects VII.21, trans. Legge, [Gutenberg #3330](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3330/pg3330.txt)). He also disclaims private knowledge: "I am not one who was born in the possession of knowledge; I am one who is fond of antiquity, and earnest in seeking it there" (Analects VII.19, trans. Legge, [Gutenberg #3330](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3330/pg3330.txt)). The hearing that matters is therefore earned and effortful, and it precedes judgment; the Y085 principle states the same order from the Advaita side, where the teaching must be received and restated accurately before it can be weighed. The everyday form is the student who can repeat the position she intends to dispute, in words the teacher would accept.
