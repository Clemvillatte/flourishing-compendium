“Flexibility as virtue” — Nietzsche, The Gay Science.

The seed gives no section number. Two aphorisms in The Gay Science (The Joyful Wisdom, 1882) fit the label. Neither uses the phrase "flexibility as virtue", and the label is the compendium's summary. The quotations come from the 1910 Thomas Common translation on Project Gutenberg.

### Aphorism 159: every virtue has its time

> "The honesty of him who is at present inflexible often causes him remorse; for inflexibility is the virtue of a time different from that in which honesty prevails." (Nietzsche, The Gay Science, section 159, "Every Virtue has its Time", [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/52881/pg52881.txt))

The claim is about timing. It treats inflexibility as a virtue that belongs to some times and not to others, and it does not say flexibility is always better.

### Aphorism 296: the cost of a fixed reputation

> "A fixed reputation was formerly a matter of the very greatest utility; and wherever society continues to be ruled by the herd-instinct, it is still most suitable for every individual to give to his character and business the appearance of unalterableness,—even when they are not so in reality." (Nietzsche, section 296, "A Fixed Reputation")

Nietzsche says this valuation of constancy "brings all changing, re-learning, and self-transforming into disrepute". He judges it "most injurious to knowledge", because the thinker's good will to declare himself opposed to his former opinions, and to distrust all that wants to be fixed in him, is "condemned and brought into disrepute". He adds: "the petrifaction of opinions has all the honour to itself". Aphorism 297 follows with a related point: "the ability to endure contradiction is a high indication of culture".

### How far this supports the principle

Read together, the two aphorisms support a limited claim: fixity is socially rewarded, it can cost the thinker, and which traits count as virtues depends on the age. They do not set out flexibility as a general virtue or a method for being flexible. The link to the executive-function account is the compendium's own.
