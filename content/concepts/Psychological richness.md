Psychological richness is Oishi and Westgate's proposed aspect of a good life centred on varied, interesting experiences that change a person's perspective ([2022 abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34383524/)). Their account distinguishes it from both happiness and meaning, without treating the three as unrelated ([same abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34383524/)).

> "Unlike happy or meaningful lives, psychologically rich lives are best characterized by a variety of interesting and perspective-changing experiences." (Oishi and Westgate, [abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34383524/)).

> "We present empirical evidence that happiness, meaning, and psychological richness are related but distinct and desirable aspects of a good life, with unique causes and correlates. In doing so, we show that a nontrivial number of people around the world report they would choose a psychologically rich life at the expense of a happy or meaningful life, and that approximately a third say that undoing their life's biggest regret would have made their lives psychologically richer." (Oishi and Westgate, [abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34383524/))

The authors' framing sets the concept against a dichotomy: psychological science has typically taken a good life in terms of hedonic or eudaimonic well-being, and the paper's claim is that the dichotomy leaves something out. The predictors the paper reports are not the predictors of the other two: people leading psychologically rich lives tend to be more curious, think more holistically, and lean more politically liberal ([Oishi and Westgate, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34383524/)).

The thing the dichotomy leaves out is variety of a specific kind. A rich life is not one with more good days in it but one in which the days change what a person can see, and the change is the part the other two categories have no place for. The regret finding is the practical half of the claim: about a third of respondents said that undoing their life's biggest regret would have made their lives psychologically richer, which reads a regret not as a loss of pleasure or of purpose but as a road not taken that would have widened the person ([Oishi and Westgate, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34383524/)). The concept does not tell a person to seek change for its own sake, only that a life arranged to minimise change will not be rich, whatever else it is.

## Facets

- **Related but distinct**: richness is not simply another label for a happy life or a meaningful one ([Oishi and Westgate, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34383524/)).
- **A preference, not a universal ranking**: the authors report that some respondents would choose richness at the expense of happiness or meaning; that does not say everyone should make the same choice ([abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34383524/)).
