Anxiety about the future, in Kierkegaard's treatment, is anxiety's characteristic direction: The Concept of Anxiety (1844) locates it in possibility, where we feel a "sympathetic antipathy" or an "antipathetic sympathy" in which we are both drawn towards and repelled by some possibility ([Lippitt & Evans, "Søren Kierkegaard", SEP, §2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)).

> "Anxiety is not merely a pathological condition which is a symptom of disease, but part of the human condition itself. It is an awareness of freedom." — [Lippitt & Evans, "Søren Kierkegaard", SEP, §2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)

### The dizziness of freedom

The book's famous image explains why the future is anxiety's home terrain. Looking down into one's own possibility produces the dizziness; the cure Haufniensis recommends is not the elimination of anxiety but learning to be anxious properly ([Lippitt & Evans, "Søren Kierkegaard", SEP, §2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)). The entry notes that this looking down produces anxiety, the state that precedes sin, and that a morally free action always comes from a "leap" and cannot be seen as necessary ([Lippitt & Evans, "Søren Kierkegaard", SEP, §2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)).

> "anxiety is the dizziness of freedom, which emerges when the spirit wants to posit the synthesis and freedom looks down into its own possibility, laying hold of finiteness to support itself. (CA 61/SKS 4, 365)" — [Kierkegaard, The Concept of Anxiety, quoted in Lippitt & Evans, SEP, §2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)

> "Whoever has learned to be anxious in the right way has learned the ultimate" (CA 155) — [Kierkegaard, The Concept of Anxiety, quoted in Lippitt & Evans, SEP, §2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)
