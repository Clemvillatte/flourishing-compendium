Dialectical thinking, in the cross-cultural psychology literature, is naive dialecticism: a set of lay beliefs about how the world works, found more strongly in East Asian cultures. Spencer-Rodgers and colleagues define it:

> "Naïve dialecticism refers to a set of East Asian lay beliefs characterized by tolerance for contradiction, the expectation of change, and cognitive holism." (Spencer-Rodgers, Boucher, Mori, Wang and Peng, [The Dialectical Self-Concept: Contradiction, Change, and Holism in East Asian Cultures](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2811254/), Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin, 2009)

Its three main concepts: "The theory of change asserts that the universe is unpredictable, dynamic, and in constant flux. The theory of contradiction holds that two ostensibly contradictory propositions may both be true simultaneously (yin–yang). The third feature of naïve dialecticism is holism, or the notion that the part cannot be understood except in relation to the whole." (same source) In the studies behind that paper, "Contradictory self-knowledge was more readily available (Study 1) and simultaneously accessible (Study 2) among East Asians (Japanese and Chinese) than among Euro-Americans."
