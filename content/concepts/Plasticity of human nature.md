John Dewey's Human Nature and Conduct (1922) treats human nature as shaped by habit and custom, and plasticity as the capacity to change. In Part One he writes that "the plasticity of the young presents a temptation to those having greater experience and hence greater power which they rarely resist. It seems putty to be molded according to current designs. That plasticity also means power to change prevailing custom is ignored" (Dewey, Human Nature and Conduct, Part One, [Project Gutenberg text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/41386/pg41386.txt)).

> "Social conditions rather than an old and unchangeable Adam have generated wars; the ineradicable impulses that are utilized in them are capable of being drafted into many other channels." (Dewey, Human Nature and Conduct, Part Two, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/41386/pg41386.txt))

### Docility and originality

Dewey's point is that plasticity is learning capacity, not mere pliancy. He says "to be truly docile is to be eager to learn all the lessons of active, inquiring, expanding experience." Later he complains that "plasticity and originality have been opposed to each other," although "the most precious part of plasticity consists in ability to form habits of independent judgment and of inventive initiation." For him, "it demands a more complete and intense docility to form flexible easily re-adjusted habits than it does to acquire those which rigidly copy the ways of others" (same book).

### Limits of the claim

Dewey is arguing against the idea that war, or any one social arrangement, is fixed by an unchanging native force: "to suppose there is some one unchanging native force which generates war is as naive as the usual assumption that our enemy is actuated solely by the meaner of the tendencies named and we only by the nobler." His argument is philosophical and social. It is not an empirical measurement of how much an adult's character can change. It also does not rely on neuroplasticity research, which the compendium treats on a separate page.
