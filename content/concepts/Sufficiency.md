In Daodejing 33, someone satisfied with their lot is called rich. Chapter 44 pairs contentment with knowing when to stop. ([Legge translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

> "He who is satisfied with his lot is rich; he who goes on acting with energy has a (firm) will." (Daodejing 33.1, Legge translation, [Project Gutenberg ebook #216](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

> "Who is content  
> Needs fear no shame.  
> Who knows to stop  
> Incurs no blame.  
> From danger free  
> Long live shall he." (Daodejing 44, section 3, Legge translation, [Project Gutenberg ebook #216](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

Chapter 44 opens by asking the reader to weigh life against fame and wealth, then criticizes attachment to fame and large stores. ([Legge translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

### What the two chapters share

Both chapters refuse the arithmetic of more. Chapter 33 names sufficiency a form of wealth that does not compound, and chapter 44 makes the same move with a question: keeping life and losing the rest, or keeping the rest and losing life, which brings sorrow nearer? The contentment at issue is an active measurement: knowing what the living actually requires and stopping there, and the shame the verse says contentment fears none of is the comparison that would restart the count. The measure is taken in advance rather than after the loss, since chapter 44 asks its question while the reader still holds all three things, and that timing is what makes the answer practical instead of elegiac.
