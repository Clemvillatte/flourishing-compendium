Non-seeking is Shunryu Suzuki's term for practice without a "gaining idea", the wish that meditation or practice will buy you something. In Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind he sets the rule and its limit in one breath:

> "We say our practice should be without gaining ideas, without any expectations, even of enlightenment. This does not mean, however, just to sit without any purpose. This practice free from gaining ideas is based on the Prajna Paramita Sutra." (Suzuki, Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind, "No Dualism", [Stanford PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/~funn/ZenMindBeginners.pdf))

### What the rule asks

The point is not inaction. Suzuki is explicit that the practice still does something; it just refuses to make the doing conditional on a reward. Two passages carry it:

> "As long as you think, 'I am doing this,' or 'I have to do this,' or 'I must attain something special,' you are actually not doing anything. When you give up, when you no longer want something, or when you do not try to do anything special, then you do something. When there is no gaining idea in what you do, then you do something." (Suzuki, Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind, "Nothing Special", [Stanford PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/~funn/ZenMindBeginners.pdf))

> "If you continue this simple practice every day you will obtain a wonderful power. Before you attain it, it is something wonderful, but after you obtain it, it is nothing special. It is just you yourself, nothing special." (Suzuki, Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind, "Nothing Special", [Stanford PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/~funn/ZenMindBeginners.pdf))

The chapter's summary line: "The most important thing is to forget all gaining ideas, all dualistic ideas. In other words, just practice zazen in a certain posture. Do not think about anything. Just remain on your cushion without expecting anything. Then eventually you will resume your own true nature. That is to say, your own true nature resumes itself" ([Stanford PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/~funn/ZenMindBeginners.pdf)).

The passages read together make a specific claim about what the gaining idea does to a practice. A person thinking "I am doing this" is described by Suzuki as doing nothing, which is not a description of idleness but of the relation between the thinking and the doing: the thought makes the doing an instrument of something else. The "nothing special" of the second passage is the same point made in the other direction, since the power the practice obtains turns out to be nothing but the person, which is what makes it not a prize to be collected. What the passages do not supply is a recommendation to sit without purpose, and Suzuki's own sentence rules that out in its first breath.
