Virtue ethics is one of the three major approaches in normative ethics, and the one that puts character first. The Stanford Encyclopedia entry by Rosalind Hursthouse and Glen Pettigrove identifies it as "the one that emphasizes the virtues, or moral character, in contrast to the approach that emphasizes duties or rules (deontology) or that emphasizes the consequences of actions (consequentialism)" ([Hursthouse & Pettigrove 2003](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-virtue/)). The contrast shows up in a concrete case: "A utilitarian will point to the fact that the consequences of doing so will maximize well-being, a deontologist to the fact that, in doing so the agent will be acting in accordance with a moral rule such as 'Do unto others as you would be done by' and a virtue ethicist to the fact that helping the person would be charitable or benevolent" ([Hursthouse & Pettigrove 2003](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-virtue/)).

What marks the theory is not its subject matter but its architecture: "Whereas consequentialists will define virtues as traits that yield good consequences and deontologists will define them as traits possessed by those who reliably fulfil their duties, virtue ethicists will resist the attempt to define virtues in terms of some other concept that is taken to be more fundamental" ([Hursthouse & Pettigrove 2003](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-virtue/)). A virtue itself is "an excellent trait of character... a disposition, well entrenched in its possessor—something that, as we say, goes all the way down, unlike a habit such as being a tea-drinker—to notice, expect, value, feel, desire, choose, act, and react in certain characteristic ways" ([Hursthouse & Pettigrove 2003](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-virtue/)).

The tradition runs from Aristotle and Plato in the West, and can be traced back to Mencius and Confucius in the East. Almost any modern version draws on three Greek concepts: arête (excellence or virtue), phronesis (practical or moral wisdom), and eudaimonia, "usually translated as happiness or flourishing" ([Hursthouse & Pettigrove 2003](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-virtue/)). The eudaimonist form holds that "a virtue is a trait that contributes to or is a constituent of eudaimonia and we ought to develop virtues... precisely because they contribute to eudaimonia" ([Hursthouse & Pettigrove 2003](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-virtue/)). The modern revival in Anglo-American philosophy is usually dated to Elizabeth Anscombe's 1958 article "Modern Moral Philosophy," with Philippa Foot, John McDowell, Alasdair MacIntyre and Rosalind Hursthouse building out a third way between natural science and cultural relativism. Contemporary variants include agent-based theories (Michael Slote, Linda Zagzebski), exemplarism, target-centered accounts (Christine Swanton), and Platonistic forms ([Hursthouse & Pettigrove 2003](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-virtue/)).

The best-known objections are application (no codifiable decision procedure), cultural relativism, conflict between virtues, self-effacement, egoism, and situationism, the claim from social psychology that character traits as such do not exist. The entry's reply to situationism is that the studies do not address the multi-track disposition a virtue is supposed to be ([Hursthouse & Pettigrove 2003](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-virtue/)). The approach treats the development of character itself as the route to a good life, not as a means to satisfying rules or outcomes.
