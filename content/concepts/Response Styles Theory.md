Nolen-Hoeksema's 1991 abstract proposes: "I propose that the ways people respond to their own symptoms of depression influence the duration of these symptoms. People who engage in ruminative responses to depression, focusing on their symptoms and the possible causes and consequences of their symptoms, will show longer depressions than people who take action to distract themselves from their symptoms. Ruminative responses prolong depression because they allow the depressed mood to negatively bias thinking and interfere with instrumental behavior and problem-solving" ([Nolen-Hoeksema, Responses to depression and their effects on the duration of depressive episodes, J Abnorm Psychol 100(4), 569-582, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1757671/)).

### Three mechanisms

A later review by Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco and Lyubomirsky says rumination "enhances the effects of depressed mood on thinking", "interferes with effective problem solving", and "interferes with instrumental behavior, leading to increases in stressful circumstances". Nolen-Hoeksema and Davis (1999) added that chronic ruminators "will lose social support" ([Rethinking Rumination, Perspectives on Psychological Science 2008, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x)). The theory's alternative is distraction: "thoughts and behaviors that help divert one's attention away from one's depressed mood", such as going for a run or concentrating on a project, while excluding "dangerous or self-destructive activities" like heavy drinking.

### What later research changed

The seed's "longer and deeper episodes" needs a caveat. The 2008 abstract states: "rumination appears to more consistently predict the onset of depression rather than the duration, but rumination interacts with negative cognitive styles to predict the duration of depressive symptoms". It adds that, "contrary to original predictions, the use of positive distractions has not consistently been correlated with lower levels of depressive symptoms in correlational studies, although dozens of experimental studies show positive distractions relieve depressed mood". Their conclusion: "Inducing rumination prolongs the experience of negative mood in dysphoric individuals, but self-reported rumination does not reliably predict duration of episodes of major depression." They also warn that chronic distraction without reappraisal or problem solving "may morph into avoidance".

### Other evidence

A study of 397 adolescents found that response styles predicted concurrent and six-week follow-up mood beyond other cognitive variables, and that only rumination predicted future depressed mood ([Response styles and negative affect among adolescents, Cognitive Therapy and Research](http://link.springer.com/10.1007/BF02229241)). The same study found that girls reported more rumination, though controlling for response style did not eliminate the gender difference in depression.
