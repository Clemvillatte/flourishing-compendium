Social baseline theory (SBT) holds that the human brain treats the company of other people as its default condition, so being with trusted others costs less effort than managing alone. The 2011 statement of the theory is by Lane Beckes and James A. Coan (the seed credits Coan, 2011). It starts from economy of action, the principle that "all organisms must take in more energy than they expend".

### What the theory claims

The authors use "baseline" in two senses. In the first, the brain assumes close proximity to social resources.

> "...close proximity to social resources is the baseline assumption of the human brain." (Beckes & Coan 2011, p. 976, [PDF](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/55917f64e4b0cd3b4705b68c/t/5cd2617f9353680001816713/1557291392068/beckes.coan.2011.pdf))

The consequence is that other people lower the cost of living. The abstract says SBT suggests that their presence helps individuals "to conserve important and often metabolically costly somatic and neural resources through the social regulation of emotion". The paper names two routes, risk distribution (threats are spread across a group) and load sharing (effort is shared between people).

### The evidence they cite

Beckes and Coan describe a study by Coan, Schaefer and Davidson (2006) in which women faced the threat of a shock alone, holding a stranger's hand, or holding their partner's hand. In their account, women in the highest-quality relationships showed the least threat-related brain activity. They argue that proximity to a familiar relational partner has benefits beyond simple risk distribution, because energy conservation then also depends on "trust and interdependence".

### Limits and neighbours

The authors flag the status of their own claims: "some of the specific ideas discussed below are speculative and remain to be tested." They also treat SBT as related to attachment theory but different, since its mechanisms of risk distribution and load sharing are distinct from Bowlby's.
