Expectation-disconfirmation is a model of how satisfaction is formed. It comes from Richard Oliver's 1980 paper in the Journal of Marketing Research, whose abstract proposes "consumer satisfaction as a function of expectation and expectancy disconfirmation" ([Oliver 1980](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/002224378001700405)).

### The model

Oliver treats expectations as a reference point. In his words, "outcomes poorer than expected (a negative disconfirmation) are rated below this reference point, whereas those better than expected (a positive disconfirmation) are evaluated above this base." He borrows Helson's adaptation-level idea for this, and concludes that "Satisfaction, then, can be seen as an additive combination of the expectation level and the resulting disconfirmation" ([Oliver 1980](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/002224378001700405)). Satisfaction then feeds a revised attitude and a later intention.

### What the study found

The test was a two-stage field study of a federal flu vaccination programme, with questionnaires before the vaccine was available and again at the end of the flu season. The paper reports that disconfirmation was unrelated to pre-exposure measures and was related to satisfaction in all four samples (residents and students, vaccinated and not), with the greatest impact on satisfaction among the vaccinated in both of those samples. It also reports that "the adaptation level effect is remarkably resistant to extinction", since the seven-month gap between surveys makes simple recall an unlikely explanation ([Oliver 1980](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/002224378001700405)).

### Limits the author names

Satisfaction and disconfirmation were measured with the same instrument, so shared method variance is possible. The sample came from one community, the response rate was not high, and the resident sample leaned towards higher-income white respondents. The paper adds that the attribute-level approach "did not yield more encouraging results" than the overall better-or-worse-than-expected scales ([Oliver 1980](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/002224378001700405)).

### Scope

The compendium's earlier one-line summary, "satisfaction tracks performance minus expectation", is a simplification. In the model, expectation itself also enters satisfaction, and the disconfirmation judgement is a separate rating of whether things were better or worse than expected. The evidence is consumer and health-decision data, not a test of expectations about relationships or wellbeing.
