The negativity bias is the finding that bad events have more psychological impact than good ones of similar size. The Baumeister review opens with the evidence: "The greater power of bad events over good ones is found in everyday events, major life events (e.g., trauma), close relationship outcomes, social network patterns, interpersonal interactions, and learning processes". It adds that "bad information is processed more thoroughly than good", "the self is more motivated to avoid bad self-definitions than to pursue good ones", and "bad impressions and bad stereotypes are quicker to form and more resistant to disconfirmation than good ones" ([Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Finkenauer and Vohs, 2001, Bad is Stronger than Good, Review of General Psychology 5(4), 323-370, abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1037/1089-2680.5.4.323)).

### The claim in the authors' words

The authors state it with a hedge: "When equal measures of good and bad are present, however, the psychological effects of bad ones outweigh those of the good ones." They say bad does not always triumph, because "good may prevail over bad by superior force of numbers: Many good events overcome the psychological effects of a single bad one" (quoted in [Meaning Lab, Cody Kommers](https://codykommers.substack.com/p/bad-v-good)). That blog also gives the examples: marital interaction studies where positive aspects "have almost no predictive power" while negative aspects do, and the hot stove effect in learning.

The abstract also says diagnosticity and salience explain some findings, but "the greater power of bad events is still found when such variables are controlled", and "hardly any exceptions (indicating greater power of good) can be found".

### A philosophical challenge

A philosophical critique says Baumeister et al. are taken to have offered the canonical formulation of the negativity bias, and that their review is the paper "most often cited" on it. The critique argues the bias "should be rejected" because the hypothesis "is never adequately clarified" ([Rethinking the Negativity Bias, Review of Philosophy and Psychology](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13164-018-0382-7)). That is one author's view; the critique's abstract proposes "alternative hedonic hypotheses".

### Scope of the seed claim

The seed says negative events elicit "stronger and faster reactions". The abstract supports stronger, and speed in impression formation ("quicker to form"). I did not open Rozin and Royzman (2001), so no claim here rests on it.
