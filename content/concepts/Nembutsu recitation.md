Nembutsu recitation is the Pure Land Buddhist practice of calling on Amida Buddha's name. The sayings of Shinran (1173-1262) recorded by his disciple Yuien-bo in the Tannisho describe what the recitation is, in the 1928 English translation of Tosui Imadate:

> "When the thought is awakened in your heart to say the nembutsu, believing that your rebirth in the Pure Land is attained through the inconceivable power of Amida's Original Vow, you come to share in his grace which embraces all beings forsaking none. You should know that Amida's Vow makes no discrimination as regards the person, old or young, good or bad, only requiring that one has faith." ([Tannisho, chapter I, trans. Imadate, 1928](https://archive.org/details/thetannisho))

### Not a technique for earning salvation

On the page of the Tannisho the recitation is emphatically not a practice one performs to qualify for the Pure Land. Shinran's recorded answer to followers who crossed ten provinces to ask him about the way of rebirth was that he knew no way other than the nembutsu, and that his sole reason for saying it lay in his teacher's instruction that "the only condition of salvation by Amida is to say the nembutsu" ([chapter II, same text](https://archive.org/details/thetannisho)). The most quoted saying in the collection reverses the ordinary moral calculation:

> "Even a good man is reborn in the Pure Land, and how much more so with a wicked man!" ([chapter III, same text](https://archive.org/details/thetannisho))

The reasoning given is that whoever relies on his own power and good deeds has no wish to invoke the Other Power, while one burdened with passions, having no means of escape by austerities, is exactly the being Amida's Vow was made for ([chapter III, same text](https://archive.org/details/thetannisho)).
