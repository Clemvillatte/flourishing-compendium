The construct was introduced in Clance and Imes's 1978 paper, from their clinical work with over 150 high-achieving women. The opening definition:

> "The term impostor phenomenon is used to designate an internal experience of intellectual phonies, which appears to be particularly prevalent and intense among a select sample of high achieving women." (Clance and Imes 1978, Psychotherapy: Theory, Research and Practice 15(3): 241-247, [author's own PDF](https://paulineroseclance.com/pdf/ip_high_achieving_women.pdf))

The paper's core observation is that the evidence does not touch the belief:

> "However, despite their earned degrees, scholastic honors, high achievement on standardized tests, praise and professional recognition from colleagues and respected authorities, these women do not experience an internal sense of success." (Clance and Imes 1978, [author's own PDF](https://paulineroseclance.com/pdf/ip_high_achieving_women.pdf))

And the belief itself, as the abstract states it: "Despite outstanding academic and professional accomplishments, women who experience the imposter phenomenon persists in believing that they are really not bright and have fooled anyone who thinks otherwise." The paper also records the typical success story these women told themselves: success attributed to hard work, luck, knowing the right people, being in the right place at the right time, or interpersonal assets such as charm, rather than to ability. The paper itself explores four factors that maintain impostor feelings over time, and the later research tradition has carried the construct into perfectionism, overwork and burnout research.

Subsequent measurement work, including Clance's own scale and later reviews, has broadened the construct beyond its original sample while keeping the core definition: evidence-resistant, internally felt phoniness. The compendium keeps the original paper's wording as the anchor.

**Plain words** Some people can hold a stack of evidence that they are good at what they do and still privately believe they have fooled everyone. Each new success goes onto the stack without changing the belief underneath.
