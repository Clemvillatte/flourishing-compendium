Aristotle's claim, in the first book of the Nicomachean Ethics, that happiness must be judged over a whole life and not by a moment of it. The requirement is stated as a qualification on the definition of the human good: a life counts, and one day does not.

> "But we must add 'in a complete life.' For one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day; and so too one day, or a short time, does not make a man blessed and happy." (Aristotle, [Nicomachean Ethics I, trans. W. D. Ross](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html))

The issue is pressed further in the same book, through the old advice of Solon:

> "Must no one at all, then, be called happy while he lives; must we, as Solon says, see the end?" (Aristotle, [ibid.](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html))

Aristotle works the problem honestly: to postpone the verdict until death is "a paradox, that when he is happy the attribute that belongs to him is not to be truly predicated of him because we do not wish to call living men happy, on account of the changes that may befall them" ([ibid.](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html)). His resolution is that happiness is an activity, so the judgment of a life as a whole is what the complete life is for — the ending completes the pattern rather than creating it.
