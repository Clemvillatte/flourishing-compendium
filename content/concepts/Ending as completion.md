Aristotle's claim, in the first book of the Nicomachean Ethics, that happiness must be judged over a whole life and not by a moment of it. He qualifies his account of happiness with the requirement "in a complete life" ([Nicomachean Ethics I, trans. W. D. Ross](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html)):

> "But we must add 'in a complete life.' For one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day; and so too one day, or a short time, does not make a man blessed and happy." (Aristotle, [Nicomachean Ethics I, trans. W. D. Ross](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html))

The issue is raised again in the same book, through the old advice of Solon:

> "Must no one at all, then, be called happy while he lives; must we, as Solon says, see the end?" (Aristotle, [ibid.](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html))

Aristotle presses the problem further: to postpone the verdict until death is "a paradox, that when he is happy the attribute that belongs to him is not to be truly predicated of him because we do not wish to call living men happy, on account of the changes that may befall them" ([ibid.](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html)). These passages make a complete life relevant to happiness while questioning the requirement to wait until death before calling someone happy ([ibid.](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html)).
