Heart-rate-variability biofeedback (HRVB) is a training method in which a person sees their own heart rhythm live and learns to breathe so as to enlarge its natural swings. Lehrer and Gevirtz describe it: "The procedure consists of feeding back beat by beat heart rate data during slow breathing maneuvers such that the participant tries to maximize RSA, create a sine-wave-like curve of peaks and valleys, and match RSA to heart rate patterns." Respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA) is "the heart pattern that occurs when heart rate increases during inhalation and decreases during exhalation" ([Lehrer and Gevirtz, 2014, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4104929/)). Their example screen shows the shift "from normal breathing to 7 breaths per minute breathing".

### How it is thought to work

The seed note for this page credits vagal tone. Lehrer and Gevirtz are more specific: "The most supported possible mechanism is the strengthening of homeostasis in the baroreceptor", with "the effect on the vagal afferent pathway to the frontal cortical areas" proposed more recently. So vagal pathways are one candidate, and the baroreflex account has more support in their review.

### What the trials show

Goessl, Curtiss and Hofmann's meta-analysis pooled 24 studies with 484 participants. Compared with control conditions, biofeedback gave "Hedges' g = 0.83", and the authors conclude that HRV biofeedback "is associated with a large reduction in self-reported stress and anxiety" but add "more well-controlled studies are needed" ([Psychological Medicine, 2017, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28478782/)). Lehrer and Gevirtz themselves say that "few areas have extensive support by way of controlled studies", though "the overall picture seems to be very promising".

### A counter-signal

A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of remote HRVB found that it "is effective for reducing depression symptoms and increasing HRV, but not for perceived stress". It adds that there was "no significant effect of HRV-B on anxiety however only three studies examined this outcome and two of them were nonrandomized and had a high risk of bias" ([Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10484-025-09750-w)). The two reviews differ in scope: the first covers all delivery formats, the second only remote ones.

### What the essays can say

HRVB is a trainable breathing-and-feedback method with solid support for self-reported stress and anxiety in the pooled studies, with open questions about mechanism and about remote delivery.
