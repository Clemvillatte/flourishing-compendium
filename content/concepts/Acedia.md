In [Praktikos 12](https://evagriusponticus.net/cpg2430/cpg2430.eng.1990.dysinger.html), Evagrius describes acedia through a monk's aversion to his place and work and his urge to abandon his cell. The chapter opens:

> "The demon of acedia, which is also called the noonday demon, is the most burdensome of all the demons. It besets the monk at about the fourth hour (10 am) of the morning, encircling his soul until about the eighth hour (2 pm)." (Evagrius Ponticus, Praktikos 12, translated by Luke Dysinger, [Guide to Evagrius Ponticus](https://evagriusponticus.net/cpg2430/cpg2430.eng.1990.dysinger.html))

The chapter's symptoms read as a psychology of restlessness: the sun seems to stop, the monk keeps looking out of the window, hates his place and his work, and the demon "employs, as they say, every [possible] means to move the monk to abandon his cell and give up the race" ([Praktikos 12, Dysinger translation](https://evagriusponticus.net/cpg2430/cpg2430.eng.1990.dysinger.html)).

### How it ends in Evagrius

The chapter does not end in defeat. Evagrius writes that "No other demon follows on immediately after this one but after its struggle the soul is taken over by a peaceful condition and by unspeakable joy" ([Praktikos 12, Dysinger translation](https://evagriusponticus.net/cpg2430/cpg2430.eng.1990.dysinger.html)). The monk is not asked to flee the restlessness or to defeat it by force. The chapter treats it as a struggle to be gone through, with peace on the far side.

### Aquinas on sorrow about the good

Thomas Aquinas, using the Latin tradition's word "sloth", gives a definition that moves the emphasis from the cell to the heart. He writes that "sloth is sorrow about spiritual good in as much as it is a Divine good", and that the sorrow is evil "if it so oppresses man as to draw him away entirely from good deeds" (Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 35, [New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3035.htm)). Read this way, acedia is a loss of gladness in what one most cares about, not laziness as such. An objection in the same question cites Gregory's list of its "daughters", among them "faint-heartedness, despair, sluggishness in regard to the commandments"; it is the objector's quotation, and not Aquinas's own reply.

### What it is not

Acedia is not simply boredom or a work-life problem, and it is not a clinical diagnosis. The compendium links it to modern pictures of burnout and amotivation as a human resemblance, not an identity. The sources above are monastic and scholastic, and they describe the experience as those authors saw it.
