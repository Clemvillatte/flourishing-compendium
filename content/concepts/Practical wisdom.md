“Practical wisdom” — [[T002 — Aristotle|Aristotle]], Nicomachean Ethics VI.5; Confucius, The Analects. Aristotle's own account in Book VI:

> "the man to whom we should give the name of Good in Counsel, simply and without modification, is he who in the way of calculation has a capacity for attaining that of practical goods which is the best for Man. Nor again does Practical Wisdom consist in a knowledge of general principles only, but it is necessary that one should know also the particular details, because it is apt to act, and action is concerned with details." (Nicomachean Ethics VI, [Chase translation, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt))

### What practical wisdom is not

Practical wisdom exceeds the application of rules. Aristotle is explicit that the circumstances of action vary so much that no set of rules, however complicated, can solve every practical problem. It is also inseparable from character: phronesis and ethical virtue require each other. Modern attempts to formalise phronesis (such as the Aristotelian Phronesis Model) are reconstructions, not Aristotle's own theory.

### Practical implications

Practical wisdom is the skill that generalises: for any domain, whether health, work or relationships, the expert practitioner reads the situation and chooses the response the situation actually needs, and knowing the most rules is what beginners do while that perception is forming. It is acquired by the same route as any expertise: repetition with feedback, across varied cases, over time. This is analogous to modern deliberate practice and to the research on coping flexibility, though the connection is interpretive rather than a demonstrated equivalence. The practical implication for a flourishing practice is to treat rules as training wheels: support while the perception forms, then ride without them.
