Melioration theory is a theory of choice developed by Richard Herrnstein and colleagues. Herrnstein and Prelec's abstract states it: melioration "implies that choices distributed over a period of time may be reliably and predictably suboptimal, in terms of the person's own preferences". The paper applies the theory to "'pathological' consumption patterns" and argues one "should find a general underinvestment in those activities that exhibit increasing average returns to rate of consumption, and an overinvestment in activities that have an addiction-like interaction between value and rate" ([Herrnstein and Prelec, 1991, Journal of Economic Perspectives, AEA](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.5.3.137)).

### The core rule

Melioration was first proposed to explain the matching law. One statement of the rule: "subjects compare local rates of reinforcement from concurrently available alternatives and shift in a relatively continuous manner towards the higher one" (Herrnstein and Vaughan, 1980, p. 164, quoted in [Rationality, Melioration, and Law-of-Effect Models for Choice, Duke repository](https://dukespace.lib.duke.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/93aa164d-d2a5-4e39-b210-33f89a8de4b6/content)). Another source defines it as "the process of comparing the rates of return and shifting toward the alternative that is currently yielding the better return" (Herrnstein and Prelec 1991, p. 361, quoted in [Rutgers PDF on the law of effect](https://ruccs.rutgers.edu/images/personal-charles-r-gallistel/publications/DeconLawEffect.pdf)). The neural-model paper adds that on concurrent variable-interval schedules melioration produces matching, "because an increase (decrease) in the selection of one option decreases (increases) the return from that option", so that an equilibrium is reached "when the returns from the two alternatives are equal" ([Biophysically Based Neural Model of Matching Law Behavior, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6674121/)).

### Why it can be suboptimal

Sims and colleagues describe melioration as "choosing a lesser, local gain over a greater longer term gain" and "a kind of temporal myopia" ([Melioration as Rational Choice, Psychological Review 2013, PDF](https://www2.bcs.rochester.edu/sites/robbie/SimsNethJacobsGray_PsychRev2013.pdf)). They add that it "deviates from rational choice by ignoring the consequences of actions on future utility". Vaughan's 1981 pigeon work arranged conditions where maximising and melioration pulled apart; a replication abstract says pigeons "allocated more time to a locally richer alternative even when doing so resulted in suboptimal choice" ([Melioration revisited, Journal of the Experimental Analysis of Behavior](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/jeab.327)). In humans, volunteers paid for choices often "disregarded the internalities and were instead guided by the current yields", and "the tendency toward melioration was only partially counteracted by explicit instructions on how to maximize earnings" ([Herrnstein et al., 1993, Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/bdm.3960060302)).

### Critiques and open questions

A critical paper in the Duke repository argues that "melioration is in fact defined not as a process but as an outcome", and that "the matching-law data on which melioration rests are not local and dynamic but global and static". The neural-model paper quotes Williams (1988): "the most fundamental problem faced by melioration theory is the specification of the method by which local reinforcement rates are calculated".

### Reading the addiction link

The seed note says melioration "traps addiction". The 1991 abstract supports a narrower claim: that activities with an "addiction-like interaction between value and rate" invite overinvestment. Use that wording.
