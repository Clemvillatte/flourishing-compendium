The seed line says indignation at injustice motivates prosocial action, citing Batson and colleagues (2007). That paper says something different. Its abstract defines moral outrage as "anger at the violation of a moral standard" and separates it from "personal anger at being harmed and empathic anger at seeing another for whom one cares harmed". Across two experiments the authors "found evidence of personal anger and empathic anger, but little evidence of moral outrage" ([Batson et al., 2007, European Journal of Social Psychology 37(6), 1272-1285, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.434)).

### What Batson's team found

Participants "perceived unfair treatment of another, even another for whom they had not been induced to feel empathy, to be as unfair as participants perceived unfair treatment of themselves." But "the appraisal conditions that evoked anger were unfair treatment of self and unfair treatment of a cared-for other, not unfairness per se. In the absence of empathic concern, unfair treatment of another evoked little anger" (Wiley abstract). A Japanese paper summarising the Batson et al. experiment reports that all participants judged an unequal lottery distribution morally unjust, but strong anger was reported only when the participant was the one shortchanged ([J-STAGE paper, in Japanese](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jjesp/54/2/54_1315/_pdf)).

So the claim that moral outrage drives prosocial action rests on other work. This page should not attribute it to Batson et al.

### Outrage in digital media

Crockett (2017) offers a framework: "Moral outrage is triggered by stimuli that call attention to moral norm violations. These stimuli evoke a range of emotional and behavioural responses that vary in their costs and constraints. Finally, expressing outrage leads to a variety of personal and social outcomes". She argues that digital media "may exacerbate the expression of moral outrage by inflating its triggering stimuli, reducing some of its costs and amplifying many of its personal benefits", and that online networks "may diminish the social benefits of outrage by reducing the likelihood that norm-enforcing messages reach their targets" ([Crockett, Moral outrage in the digital age, Nature Human Behaviour](https://static1.squarespace.com/static/538ca3ade4b090f9ef331978/t/674ce0ece3507b134dbb4940/1733091565297/Crockett_2017_NHB.pdf)).

The MAD model "posits that people have group-identity-based motivations to share moral-emotional content, that such content is especially likely to capture our attention, and that the design of social-media platforms amplifies our natural motivational and cognitive tendencies to spread such content" ([The Decision Lab, quoting Brady, Crockett and Van Bavel, 2020](https://thedecisionlab.com/insights/society/social-media-and-moral-outrage)). Brady and Crockett (2023) add that users "learn over time to express more outrage" and may come to believe "others are more outraged than they actually are" ([Norm Psychology in the Digital Age](https://sage.cnpereading.com/doi/10.1177/17456916231187395)).

### How to use this

Treat outrage as a mixed signal. The Batson et al. data suggest much felt anger tracks harm to self or to someone cared about, while the digital-media work shows it can be amplified for reasons unrelated to justice.
