Social attention is the way one person's attention is guided by what other people are attending to, most clearly through where they look. The seed's citation, Frischen, Bayliss and Tipper's 2007 review in Psychological Bulletin, opens its abstract like this:

> "During social interactions, people's eyes convey a wealth of information about their direction of attention and their emotional and mental states." ([Frischen, Bayliss and Tipper, 2007, Psychol Bull 133(4):694-724, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17592962/))

### What the review covers

The abstract says the review gives an overview of research on the perception of gaze direction and its influence on how the other person is perceived, and on gaze-following behaviour such as joint attention "in infant, adult, and clinical populations." It gives particular focus to the gaze-cueing paradigm, "used to investigate the mechanisms of joint attention" ([Frischen et al., 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17592962/)).

### Gaze cueing in practice

A 2017 study by Strachan and colleagues, summarising earlier work, says that when the eyes of a face move to another point in space "our own attention follows their gaze," giving enhanced processing for objects in that area and poorer processing elsewhere. The same paper reports that people show cueing effects "even when they know that gaze direction is uninformative," citing Driver et al. (1999) ([Strachan et al., 2017, Visual Cognition](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/119885/1/Strachan_etal_17_Vis_Cog.pdf)). I did not open Driver et al. directly, so that claim rests on Strachan's description of it.

### In the compendium

The principles listed (R070, AT062, K355, K248 and others) use social attention to talk about attentiveness to people and about how digital environments capture attention. The sources opened here establish that attention is shaped by others' gaze in laboratory tasks. They do not test attentiveness as a virtue, synchrony practice, or the effects of social media feeds, and those links are the compendium's own. Only the abstract of Frischen et al. (2007) was opened, not the full review.
