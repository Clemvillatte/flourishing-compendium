“Existence precedes essence” — Sartre, Existentialism Is a Humanism; Sartre, Existentialism is a Humanism.

Sartre gave this slogan its best-known statement in the lecture Existentialism Is a Humanism, delivered in Paris at the end of 1945. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes that the lecture was an accessible version of Being and Nothingness (1943) and responded to Marxist and Christian critics, and that Sartre makes the remark "in both Being and Nothingness and Existentialism is a Humanism" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Jean-Paul Sartre](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sartre/)). The quotations here are from the lecture, read in the English text on the Marxists Internet Archive.

### The paper-knife contrast

Sartre starts from a made object. A paper-knife has a purpose and a method of production that exist before the thing does.

> "Let us say, then, of the paperknife that its essence – that is to say the sum of the formulae and the qualities which made its production and its definition possible – precedes its existence." (Sartre, [Existentialism Is a Humanism](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/sartre/works/exist/sartre.htm))

He adds that thinking of God as creator usually means thinking of "a supernal artisan", so that the conception of a person in God's mind is comparable to the paper-knife in the artisan's mind.

### The claim itself

> "What do we mean by saying that existence precedes essence? We mean that man first of all exists, encounters himself, surges up in the world – and defines himself afterwards." (Sartre, same lecture)

On this view, "there is no human nature, because there is no God to have a conception of it", and "Man is nothing else but that which he makes of himself." Sartre calls this the first principle of existentialism.

### What follows from it

The consequence he draws is responsibility without excuse: "one will never be able to explain one's action by reference to a given and specific human nature; in other words, there is no determinism – man is free, man is freedom." He puts the same idea as "man is condemned to be free", condemned because he did not create himself yet is at liberty. He also argues that choosing is never private: what we choose for ourselves we choose in effect for everyone, so "Our responsibility is thus much greater than we had supposed".

### Limits and care

Sartre's own argument for the slogan rests on atheism: there is no God to hold a conception of human beings. He says that Christian existentialists such as Jaspers and Marcel also hold that existence comes before essence, so the phrase names a shared starting point and not a single doctrine. The lecture is a philosophical argument, not an empirical finding, and the compendium should not present it as one. The Stanford Encyclopedia also notes the dictum seems to commit Sartre to denying a pre-existing transcendental ego, as in Husserl.
