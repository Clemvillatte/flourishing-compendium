Modesty norms research studies the cultural expectation to downplay one's achievements and what following that norm does to the person. A study by Huachuan Du and Eva Jonas in the Scandinavian Journal of Psychology locates the norm:

> "Modesty has long been known as an important social norm in Eastern cultures, such as China, Japan, and Korea." ([Du & Jonas, Scand J Psychol 56(1), 2015, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25346396/))

### What following the norm does

The study tested whether compliance with the modesty norm can maintain self-esteem, as terror management theory would predict. The findings ran the other way:

> "In Study 1, following the modesty norm (i.e., explicit self-effacement) led to decreased implicit self-esteem, however, this was only the case if mortality was salient. In Study 2, violating the modesty norm (i.e., explicit self-enhancement) increased implicit self-esteem - however - again, this was only the case when mortality was salient." ([same record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25346396/))

The abstract's conclusion is direct: "These findings indicate that self-esteem cannot be maintained through compliance with the modesty norm." The two studies were conducted with Chinese participants, and the effect appeared only when mortality was made salient, so the result is scoped to that collectivistic setting and that existential condition.
