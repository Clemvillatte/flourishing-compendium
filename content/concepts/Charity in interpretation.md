Charity in interpretation is Donald Davidson's name for a constraint on understanding rather than a virtue exercised in it: to make sense of another speaker at all, one must read them as mostly right. His formulation:

> "Charity is forced on us; whether we like it or not, if we want to understand others, we must count them right in most matters." (Davidson, "On the Very Idea of a Conceptual Scheme", [PAPA 1974](https://projekt.ht.lu.se/fileadmin/user_upload/sol/ovrigt/projekt_ccs/Davidson.pdf))

### What the charity carries

The reasoning is not kindness but logic. Interpretation solves for belief and meaning at once, and with no shared world to start from there is no foothold; a reading that made the speaker mostly wrong would be a reading that made them mostly noise. Hence the working rule: "We make maximum sense of the words and thoughts of others when we interpret in a way that optimizes agreement (this includes room, as we said, for explicable error, i.e. differences of opinion)." Charity is deliberately an optimum, not a maximum: total agreement is as uninterpretable as total error, and the point is to make disagreement mean something. Davidson is explicit: "The method is not designed to eliminate disagreement, nor can it: its purpose is to make meaningful disagreement possible." The compendium principle CO037 carries the applied warning on the other side of the coin: the fundamental attribution error blames persons rather than situations, and charity is the standing corrective of reading the person in a way that could be right. A compendium exercise drawn from the principle is to restate an opponent's view in a form they would accept before criticising it, since a refutation of a reading no one holds persuades no one. What the charity does not give: an obligation to agree; it fixes what was said, and the disagreement can then begin.
