The expanding self is a cross-tradition figure: the self described not as a fixed unit but as something that grows past its own boundaries, whether by including more of the world or by discovering layers within. This entry collects its three main echoes: the nested selves of the Taittiriya Upanishad, Walt Whitman's expanding cosmos, and the psychological self-expansion model of Arthur and Elaine Aron.

> "Different from this, which consists of the essence of food, is the other, the inner Self, which consists of breath. ... Different from this, which consists of breath, is the other, the inner Self, which consists of mind. ... Different from this, which consists of mind, is the other, the inner Self, which consists of understanding. ... Different from this, which consists of understanding, is the other inner Self, which consists of bliss." (Taittirīya Upaniṣad 1.12.1, [Bṛhat open library translation](https://www.brhat.in/openlibrary/special/taittiriya-upanishad/1-12-1))

### The three echoes

The Upanishad's sequence moves inward. Each self fills the one before it and is "different from" it: the food-self is filled by the breath-self, the breath-self by the mind-self, the mind-self by the understanding-self, and the understanding-self by the bliss-self, of which "Brahman is the seat (the support)" ([Taittirīya Upaniṣad 1.12.1](https://www.brhat.in/openlibrary/special/taittiriya-upanishad/1-12-1)). Whitman's lines move outward: "Wider and wider they spread, expanding, always expanding, / Outward and outward and forever outward" ([Whitman, Song of Myself, 45](https://poets.org/poem/song-myself-45)). The "they" are the astronomical systems the speaker sees through his scuttle, and this entry's comparison treats that outward spread as the outward counterpart of the Upanishad's inward nesting. The psychological version supplies a motive and a mechanism: "The self-expansion model (originating in Aron & Aron, 1986; and tested and refined in many studies) proposes that we have evolved with two primary motivations: survival and self-expansion (growth, increasing knowledge and abilities, status, and so forth)", and "a major way people experience self-expansion is through close relationships in which the other is, to some extent, 'included in the self', so that one feels they have access to the other's knowledge, resources, and so forth as part of oneself" ([Aron and Tomlinson, Self-Expansion Activities with a Partner as One Path to Well-Being](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13203665/)).

### How the echoes differ

The three accounts are not interchangeable. The Upanishad's expansion is contemplative and metaphysical, a sequence of inner selves each filling the last. Whitman's is poetic, an image of cosmic spread addressed to the self's place in it. The Arons' is motivational, a construct with a mechanism, the inclusion of others in the self. This entry juxtaposes these accounts as a comparison; it does not claim that they describe the same process.
