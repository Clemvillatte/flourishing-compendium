The expanding self is a cross-tradition figure: the self described not as a fixed unit but as something that grows past its own boundaries, whether by including more of the world or by discovering layers within. This entry collects its three main echoes: the nested selves of the Taittiriya Upanishad, Walt Whitman's expanding cosmos, and the psychological self-expansion model of Arthur and Elaine Aron.

> "Different from this, which consists of the essence of food, is the other, the inner Self, which consists of breath. ... Different from this, which consists of breath, is the other, the inner Self, which consists of mind. ... Different from this, which consists of mind, is the other, the inner Self, which consists of understanding. ... Different from this, which consists of understanding, is the other inner Self, which consists of bliss." (Taittiriya Upanishad, Ananda Valli, [translation in the Bṛhat open library](https://www.brhat.in/openlibrary/special/taittiriya-upanishad/1-12-1))

### The three echoes

The Upanishad's sequence moves inward. Each self fills the one before it and is "different from" it: the food-self is filled by the breath-self, the breath-self by the mind-self, the mind-self by the understanding-self, and the understanding-self by the bliss-self, whose "seat (the support)" is Brahman ([Taittiriya Upanishad](https://www.brhat.in/openlibrary/special/taittiriya-upanishad/1-12-1)). Whitman's version moves outward, in Section 45 of Song of Myself: "Wider and wider they spread, expanding, always expanding, / Outward and outward and forever outward" ([Whitman, Song of Myself, 45](https://poets.org/poem/song-myself-45)). The psychological version supplies a mechanism and a motive: "The self-expansion model (originating in Aron & Aron, 1986; and tested and refined in many studies) proposes that we have evolved with two primary motivations: survival and self-expansion (growth, increasing knowledge and abilities, status, and so forth)", and "a major way people experience self-expansion is through close relationships in which the other is, to some extent, 'included in the self', so that one feels they have access to the other's knowledge, resources, and so forth as part of oneself" ([Aron and Tomlinson, Self-Expansion Activities with a Partner as One Path to Well-Being](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13203665/)).

### How the echoes differ

The three accounts are not interchangeable. The Upanishad's expansion is contemplative and metaphysical: each layer is a sheath around a self that is not any of them. Whitman's is poetic and democratic: the self grows by absorbing the world's particulars. The Arons' is motivational and measurable: expansion increases potential efficacy, and its loss when a relationship stops growing is studied as a predictor of boredom and infidelity. What they share is only the shape, a boundary that moves.
