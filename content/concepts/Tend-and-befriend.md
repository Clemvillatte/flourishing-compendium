Tend-and-befriend is a model of the human stress response developed by Shelley Taylor and her colleagues, first laid out in a 2000 Psychological Review paper. The abstract states the claim: "Although fight-or-flight may characterize the primary physiological responses to stress for both males and females, we propose that, behaviorally, females' responses are more marked by a pattern of 'tend-and-befriend'" ([Taylor et al. 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10941275/)). The two halves are defined in the same sentence: "Tending involves nurturant activities designed to protect the self and offspring that promote safety and reduce distress; befriending is the creation and maintenance of social networks that may aid in this process" ([Taylor et al. 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10941275/)).

The model was built as a correction to Cannon's fight-or-flight account, which had guided the science of stress for decades. Taylor and Master explain the gap: "Although this viewpoint is consistent with most animal research, it ignores one of human beings' most basic ways of fending off threats, namely the fact that they live in small groups that afford joint protection" ([Taylor & Master, chapter](https://taylorlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2014/11/2010_Affiliative-Responses-to-Stress.pdf)). In their words, "under stressful times human beings tend to their offspring to protect them from harm and affiliate with others for successful joint efforts against threats" ([Taylor & Master, chapter](https://taylorlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2014/11/2010_Affiliative-Responses-to-Stress.pdf)).

The proposed mechanism draws on the attachment-caregiving system: the 2000 paper proposes that oxytocin, working together with female reproductive hormones and endogenous opioid peptide mechanisms, may underlie the pattern ([Taylor et al. 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10941275/)). In Taylor and Master's account, because estrogen modulates oxytocin, those effects are thought to be stronger in females than in males ([Taylor & Master, chapter](https://taylorlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2014/11/2010_Affiliative-Responses-to-Stress.pdf)). They also report two meta-analyses finding women more likely than men to seek and use social support across a broad array of stressors, and add a boundary: "the difference in magnitude in affiliative responses to stress between men and women is relatively small, and clearly men's survival has been beneficially affected by affiliative responses to threat, just as women's survival has" ([Taylor & Master, chapter](https://taylorlab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2014/11/2010_Affiliative-Responses-to-Stress.pdf)).

The model adds an affiliative response to the stress repertoire rather than replacing fight-or-flight: the original abstract allows that fight-or-flight characterizes the primary physiological responses to stress for both males and females, and limits the tend-and-befriend claim to behavior ([Taylor et al. 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10941275/)).
