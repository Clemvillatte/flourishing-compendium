Neti neti is an Upaniṣadic formula for speaking of the Self or Brahman by denying every description offered for it. The earliest firmly identified use is Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 2.3.6, which one teaching page renders "Now, therefore, the teaching is: neti neti" ([Asivana Yoga, Neti Neti](https://asivanayoga.com/blogs/yoga-mantras/neti-neti)). A fuller form, "sa esa neti nety atma", "explicitly identifies Atman as the subject of the formula" (same page). A glossary gives the translation of 4.5.15 as: "This self is That which has been described as 'Not this, not this.' It is imperceptible, for It is never perceived; undecaying, for It never decays; unattached, for It is never attached" ([Vedaboys, Source and Meaning of Neti Neti](https://www.vedaboys.com/what-is-the-meaning-of-neti-neti/)).

### What the words say

The same teaching page explains the grammar: the phrase combines na ("not") with iti ("thus"), so it is closer to "not so, not so", and "not this, not that" is "an interpretive rendering". It also notes that the phrase "does not literally say 'I am not this,' because it contains no word for 'I.'" That matters for the seed line, which turns the formula into a procedure of subtracting what you take yourself to be. The text itself rejects the adequacy of a description of Atman or Brahman; the first-person subtraction is a later way of using it.

### Not a plain negation

The page adds that the formula "does not offer a positive definition" but "does not straightforwardly mean that nothing exists", since in 2.3.6 it is followed by the designation satyasya satyam, "the truth of truth".

### Two readings

According to Wikipedia, Shankara reads neti neti as negating all empirical attributes and forms, with Brahman beyond distinctions and everything else illusion. Ramanuja reads it "not as a complete negation but as an acknowledgment of limitations of human language and conceptualization when describing Brahman" ([Wikipedia, Brihadaranyaka Upanishad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brihadaranyaka_Upanishad)). Verse numbering also differs by recension: the Kanva recension has the Atman description at 3.9.26 and in 4.2.4, 4.4.22 and 4.5.15 (Asivana Yoga).
