Anguish (angoisse) is Sartre's name for the reflective awareness of one's own freedom, and his canonical illustration is the vertigo at the edge of a precipice. The passage, in Hazel Barnes's translation:

> "Vertigo is anguish to the extent that I am afraid not of falling over the precipice, but of throwing myself over. A situation provokes fear if there is a possibility of my life being changed from without; my being provokes anguish to the extent that I distrust myself and my own reactions in that situation."

([Sartre, Being and Nothingness, trans. Barnes, p. 29, quoted in the Scribd lecture PDF](https://www.scribd.com/document/1031374178/Lecture-6-Beauvoir-and-Sartre-on-Freedom).)

### The distinction from fear

Sartre is explicit that anguish and fear are phenomenologically distinct: "anguish is distinguished from fear in that fear is fear of beings in the world whereas anguish is anguish before myself" ([Sartre on Anxiety, studylib excerpt of BN pp. 65-67](https://studylib.net/doc/6886825/sartre-on-anxiety)). A situation provokes fear when it acts on the person; anguish arises when the person distrusts their own future conduct. The artillery example gives the felt version: "anguish is born in him when he tries to foresee the conduct with which he will face the bombardment, when he asks himself if he is going to be able to 'hold up'" ([same](https://studylib.net/doc/6886825/sartre-on-anxiety)).

The formula that ties it to freedom: "Anguish is precisely my consciousness of being my own future, in the mode of not-being" ([BN, p. 31, quoted in the Scribd lecture](https://www.scribd.com/document/1031374178/Lecture-6-Beauvoir-and-Sartre-on-Freedom)). And in the gambler case: "After having patiently built up barriers and walls, after enclosing myself in the magic circle of a resolution, I perceive with anguish that nothing prevents me from gambling" ([BN, trans. Barnes, pp. 65-67](https://studylib.net/doc/6886825/sartre-on-anxiety)).

### The compendium's use

Sartre holds the experience as informative rather than pathological. The anguish is a disclosure: what it reveals is that no past resolution and no external constraint is what stands between the person and the act, and that gap is what the tradition calls freedom. The gambler's anguish at the gaming table and the vertigo at the cliff edge are the same discovery in two registers — the past self's decisions do not bind, and the future self's conduct is not yet determined by anything but choice.
