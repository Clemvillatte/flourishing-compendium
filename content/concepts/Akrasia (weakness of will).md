Akrasia (ἀκρασία) is the ancient Greek term for weakness of will. In the modern discussion, akrasia is action contrary to one's present better judgment ([Stroud & Svirsky, "Weakness of Will", SEP, §3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/weakness-will/)). Aristotle treats it in Book VII of the Nicomachean Ethics; as [Kraut, "Aristotle's Ethics", SEP, §7](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/) glosses the Greek, akrasia is "incontinence", literally "lack of mastery".

> "In his dialogue the Protagoras, Socrates denies even the possibility of akrasia (weakness of will, incontinence)." — [Stroud & Svirsky, "Weakness of Will", SEP, §1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/weakness-will/)

### Aristotle's partial agreement

Aristotle opens his treatment by reporting that Socrates held there is no akrasia, and calling that thesis one that clearly conflicts with how things appear ([Kraut, "Aristotle's Ethics", SEP, §7](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/)). Yet his own analysis lands close to Socrates: the person who acts against reason does not have what is thought to be unqualified knowledge; in a way he has knowledge, and in a way he does not ([Kraut, "Aristotle's Ethics", SEP, §7](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/)). The agreement is partial, because Aristotle insists on the power of the emotions to rival, weaken or bypass reason ([Kraut, "Aristotle's Ethics", SEP, §7](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/)).

> An akratic person goes against reason as a result of some pathos (“emotion”, “feeling”). — [Kraut, "Aristotle's Ethics", SEP, §7](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/)

### The modern debate

R.M. Hare (1963) defended Socrates' conclusion: for Hare, any apparent case of akrasia must in fact be one in which the agent is actually unable to do the better act, or one in which the agent does not genuinely evaluate it as better, even if he says he does ([Stroud & Svirsky, "Weakness of Will", SEP, §1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/weakness-will/)). Donald Davidson (1970) answered with the analysis that set the terms of the later debate, offering this characterization of incontinent action ([Stroud & Svirsky, "Weakness of Will", SEP, §2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/weakness-will/)).

> "In doing b an agent acts incontinently if and only if: (a) the agent does b intentionally; (b) the agent believes there is an alternative action a open to him; and (c) the agent judges that, all things considered, it would be better to do a than to do b." — [Davidson 1970, as characterized in Stroud & Svirsky, SEP, §2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/weakness-will/)

Richard Holton (1999) later argued that the ordinary phenomenon is something else again: not every abandonment of a plan counts, but weakness of will is a particular kind of failure to follow a previously formed intention ([Stroud & Svirsky, "Weakness of Will", SEP, §3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/weakness-will/)). On his reading, the state the weak-willed person betrays is not an evaluative judgment but an intention ([Stroud & Svirsky, "Weakness of Will", SEP, §3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/weakness-will/)).

> "Weakness of will as the untutored understand it is not akrasia (if we reserve that term for action contrary to one's better judgment), but rather a certain kind of failure to stick to one's plans." — [Stroud & Svirsky, SEP, §3, presenting Holton's view](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/weakness-will/)

Aristotle's own treatment adds a distinction modern readers can use: he separates impetuosity (acting on passion without deliberating at all) from weakness (deliberating, choosing, then yielding to passion anyway) ([Kraut, "Aristotle's Ethics", SEP, §7](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/)).
