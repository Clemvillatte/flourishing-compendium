Akrasia (ἀκρασία) is the ancient Greek term for weakness of will: acting against one's own better judgment, knowing which action is better and doing the other anyway. Aristotle treats it at length in Book VII of the Nicomachean Ethics under the heading of incontinence, translating the Greek literally as "lack of mastery". The puzzle it poses was posed before him. In Plato's Protagoras, Socrates denies the phenomenon is even possible.

> "In his dialogue the Protagoras, Socrates denies even the possibility of akrasia (weakness of will, incontinence)." — [Stroud & Svirsky, "Weakness of Will", SEP, §1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/weakness-will/)

### Socrates' denial and its critics

Socrates' reasoning was that no one errs willingly: if someone truly judged an act best, they would do it, so apparent cases must be ignorance in disguise. Aristotle took him to be partly right and partly wrong: the akratic person does in some sense act on a false belief that her act is good, yet she genuinely knows what is best, because she goes against reason as a result of feeling (pathos). The modern debate revived the same question. R.M. Hare (1963) rejected Socrates' conclusion that akrasia is impossible, holding that akrasia involves making at least two judgments simultaneously, made under different descriptions. Donald Davidson (1970) developed this into the standard modern definition, whose conditions the SEP entry reconstructs as follows.

> "In doing b an agent acts incontinently if and only if: (a) the agent does b intentionally; (b) the agent believes there is an alternative action a open to him; and (c) the agent judges that, all things considered, it would be better to do a than to do b." — [Davidson 1970, quoted in Stroud & Svirsky, SEP, §5](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/weakness-will/)

### A narrower modern reading

Richard Holton (2009) proposed that the ordinary phenomenon deserves its own analysis: abandoning one's previously formed resolutions, rather than acting against a present all-things-considered judgment. On this view akrasia, properly speaking, is only part of what people call weakness of will.

> "Weakness of will as the untutored understand it is not akrasia (if we reserve that term for action contrary to one's better judgment), but rather a certain kind of failure to stick to one's plans." — [Holton 2009, quoted in Stroud & Svirsky, SEP, §9](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/weakness-will/)

Aristotle's own treatment adds a distinction modern readers can use: he separates impetuosity (acting on passion without deliberating at all) from weakness (deliberating, choosing, then yielding to passion anyway).
