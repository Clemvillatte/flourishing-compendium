The James-Lange theory is one of the earliest theories of emotion in modern psychology. William James set out its core in "What is an Emotion?" (Mind, 1884): the perception of an event triggers bodily changes directly, and the feeling of those changes as they occur is the emotion. The theory carries Carl Lange's name as well, as a second 19th-century scholar who proposed a similar account ([Wikipedia, James-Lange theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James%E2%80%93Lange_theory)).

> "The hypothesis here to be defended says that this order of sequence is incorrect, that the one mental state is not immediately induced by the other, that the bodily manifestations must first be interposed between, and that the more rational statement is that we feel sorry because we cry, angry because we strike, afraid because we tremble, and not that we cry, strike, or tremble, because we are sorry, angry, or fearful, as the case may be." (James, [What is an Emotion?](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/emotion.htm), Mind 9, 1884, pp. 188-205)

### What the theory inverts

James's thesis in his own words is "that the bodily changes follow directly the PERCEPTION of the exciting fact, and that our feeling of the same changes as they occur IS the emotion" ([James, 1884](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/emotion.htm)). On this account the body is not the aftermath of an emotion but its substance: the running comes first and the fear is the felt registration of the running. The proposal breaks with the common-sense order James describes, in which we meet a bear, are frightened, and run ([James, 1884](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/emotion.htm)).

The theory has been criticized and modified over its history, and modern theorists generally treat the experience of emotion as modulated by physiological feedback together with other information rather than consisting solely of bodily changes ([Wikipedia, James-Lange theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James%E2%80%93Lange_theory)).
