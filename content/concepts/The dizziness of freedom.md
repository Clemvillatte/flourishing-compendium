The dizziness of freedom is Søren Kierkegaard's image for anxiety, introduced in The Concept of Anxiety (1844) under the pseudonym Vigilius Haufniensis. Its point is a distinction: fear has a definite object, while anxiety is a response to possibility itself, to the open field of one's own choices.

> "Anxiety may be compared with dizziness. He whose eye happens to look down into the yawning abyss becomes dizzy. But what is the reason for this? It is just as much in his own eyes as in the abyss . . . Hence, anxiety is the dizziness of freedom." (Kierkegaard, The Concept of Anxiety, 1844, quoted in [Marino, The Danish Doctor of Dread](https://web.stanford.edu/~jsabol/existentialism/NYT_Kierkegaard.pdf), New York Times, 2012)

### What the image claims

The book is explicit that anxiety is "altogether different from fear and similar concepts that refer to something definite". Its texture is double: "anxiety is a sympathetic antipathy and an antipathetic sympathy", a simultaneous attraction and repulsion toward one's own possibility. Kierkegaard's scholar Gordon Marino glosses the picture: "the ledge that we both want and do not want to look over runs along the abyss of our own possibilities". The dizziness comes from the looker's own eyes, not the abyss: nothing external forces the fall, and nothing external forbids it. Marino's summary of the payoff: "it is in our anxiety that we come to understand feelingly that we are free".

### The honest limit

The Concept of Anxiety is subtitled "a simple psychologically orienting deliberation on the dogmatic issue of hereditary sin", and the dizziness passage sits inside an argument about sin's possibility, not inside clinical psychology. Kierkegaard treats anxiety as a disclosure of freedom to be educated through, not a disorder to remove, a framing later existentialists borrowed while dropping the theology.
