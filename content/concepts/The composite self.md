In Reasons and Persons, Parfit argues that a person is nothing over and above a living body, a brain, and the connected series of experiences, thoughts and actions that make up that life. The label "the composite self" is the compendium's; the view it names is Parfit's Reductionism, and its rival is what he calls the further fact, the belief that behind the series stands a separate, persisting subject.

> "When I believed that my existence was a further fact, I seemed imprisoned in myself. My life seemed like a glass tunnel, through which I was moving faster every year, and at the end of which there was darkness. When I changed my view, the walls of my glass tunnel disappeared. I now live in the open air." (Parfit, Reasons and Persons, 1984, p. 281, as quoted in [Korsgaard, "Personal Identity and the Unity of Agency", Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037c-60a9-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content))

### What the change felt like

Parfit reports the loss of the further fact as liberating rather than depressing. The passage continues in the fuller quotation given by the Cambridge carrier: "There is still a difference between my life and the lives of other people. But the difference is less. Other people are closer. I am less concerned about the rest of my own life, and more concerned about the lives of others." (Parfit, 1984, p. 281, as quoted in [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/2CFB063593764399E687EE69BE7ADDA0/S0031819123000049a.pdf/grief-and-the-inconsolation-of-philosophy.pdf)) The self being composite, the boundary between one life and other lives is a matter of degree, and concern can cross it more easily.
