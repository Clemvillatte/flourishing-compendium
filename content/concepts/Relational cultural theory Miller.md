**Relational cultural theory Miller** — Miller, Jordan — growth-fostering relationships are the core of human development; disconnection is the source of pathology.

Relational-cultural theory (RCT) is a developmental and therapeutic framework that starts from Jean Baker Miller's work and was extended by Judith Jordan and colleagues. The page title is a garbled form of the theory's name. Its main source is the opening chapter of Jordan's Relational-Cultural Therapy (3rd ed., APA, 2024), so it is the theory as one of its own developers states it.

### The core premise

> "In contrast, relational–cultural theory is built on the premise that, throughout the lifespan, human beings grow through and toward connection." (Jordan 2024, Introduction, [APA sample pages](https://www.apa.org/pubs/books/3842146-sample-pages.pdf))

Jordan contrasts this with mainstream Western theories that picture development as a path from dependence to independence. RCT, she writes, challenges prevailing developmental theories "which frame independence as the hallmark of mature development". It sees the ideal of psychological separation as "illusory and defeating" and says that "increasing relationship differentiation, rather than separation from sustaining relationships, is the route of development".

### What a growth-fostering relationship looks like

> "Growth-fostering relationships are characterized by 'the five good things': (a) zest, an increase in energy; (b) increased knowledge and clarity about one's own experience, the other person, and the relationship; (c) creativity and productivity; (d) a greater sense of worth; and (e) a desire for more connection (Miller & Stiver, 1997)." (Jordan 2024)

Mutual empathy is the mechanism she stresses. For empathy to facilitate change, "each person must see, know, and feel the responsiveness of the other person"; it involves mutual impact, mutual care and mutual responsiveness.

### Disconnection

Not all disconnection is harmful. "Acute disconnections occur in all relationships and by themselves are not harmful", and if reworked so both people feel respected, they can build trust.

> "Chronic disconnections, however, are the source of what most people call pathology and result from repeatedly encountering nonempathic responses. At the extreme, they result from humiliation, violation, abuse, and emotional neglect." (Jordan 2024)

RCT also points beyond the individual. Jordan says therapists need to take into account "the disconnections created by stratified social organizations and marginalization" as well as individual suffering (her examples include racism, heterosexism and classism). She adds that RCT is not "a compendium of harmonious and cozy relationships": she attributes to Miller the view that "good conflict" is necessary for change.

### Status of the evidence

Jordan notes that the theory was "initially ignored then treated as 'dangerous,'" and that "significant research is being carried out to test various aspects and applications". That is a developer's description, so treat it as the theory's own account of itself. An independent overview by Comstock and colleagues (2008) presents growth-fostering relationships as essential to human development and psychological well-being and links RCT to multicultural and social-justice counselling competencies ([abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/j.1556-6678.2008.tb00510.x)).
