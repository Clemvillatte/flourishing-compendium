Self-silencing theory is Dana Jack's account of how people, beginning with the depressed women she interviewed, suppress their own thoughts, feelings and needs to keep close relationships safe, and what that suppression costs. Jack and Afiya Ali's later summary of the founding interviews states the pattern in the women's own terms.

> "The women detailed how they began to silence or suppress certain thoughts, feelings, and actions that they thought would contradict their partner's wishes. They did so to avoid conflict, to maintain a relationship, and/or to ensure their psychological or physical safety." (Jack & Ali, introductory essay to Silencing the Self Across Cultures, [excerpted by Oxford University Press](https://blog.oup.com/2010/03/silencing-the-self/))

### Origin

Jack set out the theory in Silencing the Self: Women and Depression (Harvard University Press, 1991), drawing on a longitudinal study of clinically depressed women, and with Diana Dill published the 31-item Silencing the Self Scale in 1992 to measure the relational schemas the theory describes.

### Core claims

The theory's causal claim, again in the authors' words:

> "When followed, these self-silencing relational schemas create vulnerability to depression by directing women to defer to the needs of others, censor self-expression, repress anger, inhibit self-directed action, and judge the self against a culturally defined 'good woman.'" (same essay)

A recent study defines the construct under study: "Self-silencing is defined as the tendency to limit self-expression or speaking, often to avoid conflict, preserve harmony, or maintain relationships" (Scott et al., [The wear and tear of racism: Self-silencing from the perspective of young Black women](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10421612/), SSM Qualitative Research in Health, 2023, citing Jack & Ali 2010).

### The four schemas

The theory is organised around four measured dimensions: divided self, "the internal split that occurs as women construct a false self in their intimate relationships"; externalized self-perception, "evaluating oneself based on societal standards imposed by gender roles, culture, and media rather than personal beliefs and perspectives"; silencing the self, "suppressing self-expression due to the perception of separation or conflict as a threat"; and care as self-sacrifice, "consistently prioritizing the desires and expectations of others over one's own" (Aras & Demir-Kaya, [The mediating role of gaslighting as a psychological violence between self-silencing and psychological distress](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13079800/), Scientific Reports, 2026).

### In everyday life

The women's descriptions carry the cost: "They described how silencing their voices led to a loss of self and a sense of being lost in their lives. They also conveyed their shame, desperation, and anger over feelings of entrapment and self-betrayal" (Jack & Ali, same essay).
