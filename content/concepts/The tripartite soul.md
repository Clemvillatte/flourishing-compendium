The tripartite soul is Plato's division of the soul into three elements: reason, spirit, and appetite. The seed names the Republic, book IV; the compendium also carries the same material under the heading "the divided soul", which points to the same book.

### Plato's ground

The argument runs by cases in which the soul is visibly at war with itself. Socrates tells the story of Leontius:

> "Leontius, the son of Aglaion, coming up one day from the Piraeus, under the north wall on the outside, observed some dead bodies lying on the ground at the place of execution. He felt a desire to see them, and also a dread and abhorrence of them; for a time he struggled and covered his eyes, but at length the desire got the better of him..." (Plato, Republic IV, trans. Benjamin Jowett, [Project Gutenberg #1497](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1497/pg1497.txt))

The moral is drawn immediately:

> "The moral of the tale is, that anger at times goes to war with desire, as though they were two distinct things." (same book)

And when appetite overpowers reason, the third element takes sides:

> "...in this struggle, which is like the struggle of factions in a State, his spirit is on the side of his reason..." (same book)

The conclusion is put as a question the dialogue then answers: "we have alighted upon an easy question—whether the soul has these three principles or not?" (book IV, same translation), and the individual is assumed to have "the same three principles in his own soul which are found in the State".
