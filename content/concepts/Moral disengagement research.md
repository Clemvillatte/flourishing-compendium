moral self-sanctions can be selectively disengaged from inhumane conduct (Bandura, 1999).

Bandura's review locates moral agency in self-regulatory mechanisms rooted in personal standards linked to self-sanctions. Those mechanisms govern conduct only when activated, and the review's subject is the many psychosocial maneuvers by which they are switched off. The disengagement may center on:

- cognitive restructuring of inhumane conduct into a benign or worthy one, by moral justification, sanitizing language and advantageous comparison
- disavowal of a sense of personal agency, by diffusion or displacement of responsibility
- disregarding or minimizing the injurious effects of one's actions
- attribution of blame to, and dehumanization of, those who are victimized

> "The self-regulatory mechanisms governing moral conduct do not come into play unless they are activated, and there are many psychosocial maneuvers by which moral self-sanctions are selectively disengaged from inhumane conduct." (Bandura, 1999, Personality and Social Psychology Review 3(3), 193–209, [abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15661671/))

The review's conclusion follows from the number of these mechanisms:

> "Given the many mechanisms for disengaging moral control, civilized life requires, in addition to humane personal standards, safeguards built into social systems that uphold compassionate behavior and renounce cruelty." (Bandura, 1999, [abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15661671/))

### Where the switch is thrown

Bandura's Figure 1 places the mechanisms at four points in the chain between moral standards and conduct: how the conduct itself is described, who is seen as acting, which consequences get counted, and how the victim is regarded ([Bandura, 1999, full-text PDF, pp. 2–3](https://www.antoniocasella.eu/nume/Bandura_1999.pdf)). A person can hold the same standards throughout; what changes is which part of the causal story is left available for self-censure to work on.

### Moral justification and sanitizing language

In moral justification, detrimental conduct is made personally and socially acceptable by portraying it as serving socially worthy or moral purposes; Bandura notes that people do not ordinarily engage in harmful conduct until they have justified to themselves the morality of their actions ([p. 3](https://www.antoniocasella.eu/nume/Bandura_1999.pdf)). Naming does related work, and the effect has been measured:

> "Euphemizing is an injurious weapon. People behave much more cruelly when assaultive actions are verbally sanitized than when they are called aggression." (Bandura, 1999, [full-text PDF](https://www.antoniocasella.eu/nume/Bandura_1999.pdf), p. 4)

### Diffusion of responsibility

Moral control also weakens when responsibility for detrimental behaviour is spread across many hands. Most enterprises require the services of many people, each performing subdivided jobs that seem harmless in themselves; once activities are routinized into detached subfunctions, people shift their attention from the morality of what they are doing ([p. 7](https://www.antoniocasella.eu/nume/Bandura_1999.pdf)). On this account large-scale harm can be produced by considerate people, each doing a small, well-described part.

Bandura's mechanisms describe a further figure: someone who keeps the standards and switches them off. For my comparison with Aristotle's separation of the incontinent person from the vicious one, see [[A059|Distinguish incontinence from vice]]; for the comparison with cleverness as finding effective means to a goal already set, see [[A037|Distinguish cleverness from practical wisdom]].
