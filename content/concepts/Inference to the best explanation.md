“Inference to the best explanation” — Harman, "The Inference to the Best Explanation".

Gilbert Harman presented the label in 1965 for a form of inference he thought already everywhere in use. His thesis: "enumerative induction should not be considered a warranted form of nondeductive inference in its own right," and the appearances of it are really something else, "the inference to the best explanation." The term, he notes, "corresponds approximately to what others have called 'abduction,' 'the method of hypothesis,' 'hypothetic inference,' 'the method of elimination,' 'eliminative induction,' and 'theoretical inference'" ([Harman, 1965, Philosophical Review 74(1): 88-95](https://www.fitelson.org/125/Harman_IBE.pdf)).

### The core passage

His formulation of the inference itself:

> "In making this inference one infers, from the fact that a certain hypothesis would explain the evidence, to the truth of that hypothesis. In general, there will be several hypotheses which might explain the evidence, so one must be able to reject all such alternative hypotheses before one is warranted in making the inference. Thus one infers, from the premise that a given hypothesis would provide a "better" explanation for the evidence than would any other hypothesis, to the conclusion that the given hypothesis is true." ([Harman, 1965, p. 89](https://www.fitelson.org/125/Harman_IBE.pdf))

The examples are everyday rather than formal: "When a detective puts the evidence together and decides that it must have been the butler, he is reasoning that no other explanation which accounts for all the facts is plausible enough or simple enough to be accepted. When a scientist infers the existence of atoms and subatomic particles, he is inferring the truth of an explanation for various data which he wishes to account for."

### The catch

The warrant is comparative, which is the concept's weak point as much as its power: the inference only works once the alternatives are rejected, and what counts as "better" is left in scare quotes.
