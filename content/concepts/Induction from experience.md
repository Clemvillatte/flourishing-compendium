Hume's problem, stated in the Enquiry, is that experience cannot justify the step from past to future without assuming the very thing to be proved:

> "For all inferences from experience suppose, as their foundation, that the future will resemble the past, and that similar powers will be conjoined with similar sensible qualities. If there be any suspicion that the course of nature may change, and that the past may be no rule for the future, all experience becomes useless, and can give rise to no inference or conclusion." (Hume, An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, sec. IV, [Project Gutenberg #9662](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9662))

His answer to how we nevertheless manage is not reason but habit:

> "Custom, then, is the great guide of human life. It is that principle alone which renders our experience useful to us, and makes us expect, for the future, a similar train of events with those which have appeared in the past." (Hume, An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, sec. V, [Project Gutenberg #9662](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9662))

The result is a working trust in induction that is not a proof: nature has kept behaving, habit has kept expecting it to, and the expectation has kept paying. Hume's eggs make the point: nothing so like as eggs, yet nobody expects the same taste in all of them until a long course of uniform experiments. A firm reliance, in his words, is only attained after a long course of uniform experiments in any kind, which is why experience helps gradually rather than instantly, and why one vivid case can outweigh a quiet thousand in the mind that uses it.

The Enquiry's practical upshot for anyone reasoning from experience is to know which faculty is doing the work: expectation, not proof.

**Plain words** You cannot prove the future will be like the past; you just cannot help expecting it, and so far that expectation has worked. Induction is a habit with a good record, not a certainty with a licence.
