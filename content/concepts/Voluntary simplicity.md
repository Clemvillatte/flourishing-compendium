Voluntary simplicity is the deliberate choice to live with less. Thoreau urges it in Walden:

> "Our life is frittered away by detail. An honest man has hardly need to count more than his ten fingers, or in extreme cases he may add his ten toes, and lump the rest. Simplicity, simplicity, simplicity! I say, let your affairs be as two or three, and not a hundred or a thousand; instead of a million count half a dozen, and keep your accounts on your thumb nail." (Thoreau, Walden, "Where I Lived, and What I Lived For", [Project Gutenberg #205](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/205))

The same chapter gives the measure of the choice: "a man is rich in proportion to the number of things which he can afford to let alone" ([Walden](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/205)).

### Why Thoreau simplified

The purpose is stated a few pages on, in the same book: "I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately, to front only the essential facts of life, and see if I could not learn what it had to teach, and not, when I came to die, discover that I had not lived" (Thoreau, Walden, "Where I Lived, and What I Lived For", [Project Gutenberg #205](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/205)). Simplicity is the means and deliberate living the end. He adds that he did not wish "to practise resignation, unless it was quite necessary", so the aim is aliveness, not self-denial.

His argument in the opening chapter is about the effect of things on a life. He judges most luxuries, and many of the so-called comforts, to be "positive hindrances to the elevation of mankind", and holds that "the wisest have ever lived a more simple and meagre life than the poor" (Walden, "Economy"). Thoreau's claim about the ancient philosophers is a literary assertion and not an empirical one, so this page treats it as his view.

### What it is not

Voluntary simplicity is not poverty and not austerity for its own sake. The key word is voluntary: a choice made to free attention for what matters, as the passage on living deliberately states.
