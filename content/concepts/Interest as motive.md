“Interest as motive” — Dewey, Democracy and Education.

Dewey's claim is that interest is not a garnish on learning but its engine. For Dewey, interest connects a learner's present activities and capacities with an end that matters to them; his educational proposal is to find material that engages those activities, rather than attach pleasant incentives to otherwise disconnected work.

> "To be interested is to be absorbed in, wrapped up in, carried away by, some object. To take an interest is to be on the alert, to care about, to be attentive. We say of an interested person both that he has lost himself in some affair and that he has found himself in it. Both terms express the engrossment of the self in an object." — [John Dewey, Democracy and Education, ch. X, §1 (1916), full text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/852/852-h/852-h.htm)

### What follows for education

Dewey draws the practical consequence against both old camps: the remedy "is not in finding fault with the doctrine of interest, any more than it is to search for some pleasant bait that may be hitched to the alien material." Sugar-coating is not interest, because interest is what an activity has when it connects to "present powers." His summary line gives the theory in one sentence: "Interest means that one is identified with the objects which define the activity and which furnish the means and obstacles to its realization" (ch. X summary). On this view, interest is not applied to a task from outside; it is what the task already has when it grows from what the learner can already do.
