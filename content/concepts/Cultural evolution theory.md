Cultural evolution theory is the research program that studies culture with the tools of evolutionary thinking. In the Stanford Encyclopedia's description, "researchers in the field of cultural evolutionary theory pursue an eclectic program of investigation that lies at the intersection of cognitive science, anthropology, and evolutionary biology", a tradition "initiated in the 1970s and early 1980s by Cavalli-Sforza & Feldman (1981) on the one hand, and Boyd & Richerson (1985, 2005; Richerson & Boyd 2005) on the other" ([SEP, Cultural Evolution](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/evolution-cultural/)). What makes the program cultural is "its focus on the various ways in which humans—and other organisms—learn from other members of their species" (SEP). A standard definition of the thing that evolves comes from Richerson and Boyd:

> "Culture, they say, is information capable of affecting individuals' behavior that they acquire from other members of their species through teaching, imitation, and other forms of social learning." — Richerson and Boyd 2005, p. 5, as rendered in the SEP entry's narration ([link](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/evolution-cultural/))

The theory's reach extends to language; Darwin endorsed Max Müller's view that "a struggle for life is constantly going on amongst the words and grammatical forms in each language", and asserted that "the survival or preservation of certain favoured words in the struggle for existence is natural selection" (SEP). The Encyclopedia's verdict on the program's subject matter: "The case in favor of some cultural evolutionary theory is irresistible" — the interesting questions are "precisely how theories of cultural evolution should be fashioned, and how they should be related to more traditional understandings of organic evolution" (SEP).
