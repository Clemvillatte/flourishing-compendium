“The shattered self” — Herman, Trauma and Recovery.

The shattered self is my label for what trauma does to a person's fundamental assumptions, including the assumption of one's own worth. Herman's formulation, as quoted in a peer-reviewed phenomenological study of trauma:

> "traumatic events destroy the victim's fundamental assumptions about the safety of the world" (Herman, 1992/1997, p. 51, quoted in [Ratcliffe, Ruddell & Smith, 2014, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4166378/))

The same study places Herman's account alongside Janoff-Bulman's three beliefs, the third of which is the self:

> "An explicitly cognitive approach, which construes these assumptions as 'cognitive schemas' or fundamental beliefs, is adopted by Janoff-Bulman (1992, pp. 5–6), who identifies three such beliefs as central to one-place trust: 'the world is benevolent;' 'the world is meaningful;' and 'the self is worthy.'" ([Ratcliffe, Ruddell & Smith, 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4166378/))

### What the shattering is

The study the page rests on argues the shattering runs deeper than a list of overturned beliefs. A person may already know, in the abstract, that people seriously harm each other. What goes is not that knowledge but a felt, taken-for-granted style of anticipation. The authors put it this way:

> "What is eroded, we suggest, is a habitual, non-propositional style of anticipation, which cannot be exhaustively characterized in terms of however many propositional attitudes of the form 'x believes that p.'" ([Ratcliffe, Ruddell & Smith, 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4166378/))

The felt change shows up as more than a set of beliefs revised. The study ties loss of interpersonal trust to loss of trust in one's own abilities, and to a sense that meaningful action has become impossible — a substantial personality change, not a shift in opinion. What goes, on this reading, is the world's quality of being livable at all, not a belief about it.

### The trajectory of repair

Herman's stages of recovery are summarised in the same source:

> "Herman (1992/1997) describes three broad stages of recovery: a localized sense of safety is first nurtured, after which the person can attempt to construct a narrative around what has happened, and finally there is reengagement with communal life." ([Ratcliffe, Ruddell & Smith, 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4166378/))

The study adds a caution worth carrying: recovery does not mean regaining the old unreflective trust. The person "can come to relate to others and to the world more generally in a way that is compatible with moving forward into an open future" — a different stance, not a restoration of the earlier one.
