The reserve clause is a Stoic device for acting wholeheartedly while refusing to stake your happiness on the outcome. The Greek term is hupexhairesis, and [[T014 — Marcus Aurelius|Marcus Aurelius]] employs it as a technical term, pursuing his aims "with a 'reserve clause'; it converts into material for itself any obstacle that it meets with, just as fire when it gets the mastery of what is thrown upon it" (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4.1, trans. Hays, quoted in [Donald Robertson](https://donaldrobertson.name/2013/02/02/action-with-a-reserve-clause-in-marcus-aurelius/)). The clause is a small verbal habit with a large payoff: add "fate permitting" to every intention, and a blocked outcome becomes material rather than injury.

### How it works

The mechanism rests on the [[The dichotomy of control|dichotomy of control]]. Effort is up to you; the result rarely is. So the Stoic "should pursue external goals with the caveat: 'Fate permitting'" ([Donald Robertson](https://donaldrobertson.name/2013/02/02/action-with-a-reserve-clause-in-marcus-aurelius/)). Marcus attributes the full rule to Epictetus: impulses should be "with a 'reserve clause'; that they have in view our neighbour's welfare; that they are proportionate to a thing's value" (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 11.37, quoted in [Donald Robertson](https://donaldrobertson.name/2013/02/02/action-with-a-reserve-clause-in-marcus-aurelius/)). The same idea appears in the Encheiridion: through reason we may "pursue our ends 'with reservation' (Encheiridion 2), mindful that god may not bring them to fruition" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epictetus/)). In George Long's older rendering, the ruling faculty "doth prosecute it with exception and reservation; so that whatsoever it is that falls out contrary to its first intentions, even that afterwards it makes its proper object" (Marcus Aurelius, [Meditations 4.1](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2680/pg2680.txt)). That last move is the whole trick: the obstacle itself becomes the next object of rational pursuit.

### Why the Stoics needed it

The doctrine of preferred indifferents creates an apparent paradox: the Stoic energetically pursues health, reputation, and success while denying they are goods. The reserve clause dissolves the tension. A selection "can be correct even if Zeus decides not to produce the indifferent that the agent pursues: like excellent dancing, virtuous selection of indifferents does not require the acquisition of any object external to the agent in order to count as successful" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/)). One can recognize "that a thing is without ultimate value and still act vigorously in pursuit of it, when doing so is in accordance with one's rational character" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/epictetus/)). Failure therefore costs nothing that was ever yours. This is the hinge between [[Premeditatio malorum]], which rehearses loss in advance, and [[Amor fati]], which affirms whatever arrives.
