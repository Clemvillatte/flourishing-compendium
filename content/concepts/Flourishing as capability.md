Flourishing as capability is Martha Nussbaum's reading of what a good life requires, developed in Creating Capabilities (2011) from the capability approach she built with Amartya Sen. The question the approach asks is what people are really able to do and to be, not what they happen to do or feel. On this reading, flourishing is a set of real opportunities: someone who has food but cannot leave the house, or who is fed but cannot think, is not flourishing however content they seem.

### The central capabilities

Nussbaum's version names ten "central human capabilities": life; bodily health; bodily integrity; senses, imagination and thought; emotions; practical reason; affiliation; other species; play; and control over one's environment ([Robeyns and Byskov, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/)). The list is justified by a dignity threshold: each capability is needed in order for a human life to be "not so impoverished that it is not worthy of the dignity of a human being" (Nussbaum 2000: 72). Practical reason, the ability "to form a conception of the good and to engage in critical reflection about the planning of one's life," and affiliation, the ability to live with and toward others, are the two Nussbaum treats as architectonic (Creating Capabilities, pp. 33-34).

### Thresholds, not trade-offs

The theory is a floor, not a ceiling. Nussbaum argues the ten capabilities, being incommensurable, cannot be traded off against one another, and that the state should provide each citizen a minimum level of each; she calls the result "a partial and minimal account of social justice" (2006: 71) that specifies thresholds governments should guarantee ([Robeyns and Byskov, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/)). The list is deliberately open-ended and revisable. For the compendium's purposes the bridge to eudaimonia is direct: Aristotle's functioning-well becomes, in Nussbaum's hands, a set of guaranteed opportunities to function.
