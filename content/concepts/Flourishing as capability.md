This compendium heading refers to Martha Nussbaum's version of the capability approach, set out in her book Creating Capabilities (2011). The approach evaluates people's real opportunities to do and be things they have reason to value. A capability is an opportunity; a functioning is a doing or being that has actually been achieved, and the distinction keeps available choices separate from the choices a person makes ([Robeyns and Byskov, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/)).

### The central capabilities

Nussbaum's list groups the prescribed capabilities under ten "central human capabilities": life; bodily health; bodily integrity; senses, imagination and thought; emotions; practical reason; affiliation; other species; play; and control over one's environment ([Robeyns and Byskov, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/)). The Stanford Encyclopedia explains her justification in terms of a human life being:

> "not so impoverished that it is not worthy of the dignity of a human being" (Nussbaum, 2000, p. 72, quoted in [Robeyns and Byskov, SEP §3.3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/))

### Thresholds, not trade-offs

Nussbaum argues that governments should guarantee a minimum level of each capability, that the ten capabilities, being incommensurable, cannot be traded off against one another, and that her account remains a partial and minimal theory of social justice specifying those thresholds rather than everything justice might require above them ([Robeyns and Byskov, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/capability-approach/)). The list is open-ended and revisable by her own description.
