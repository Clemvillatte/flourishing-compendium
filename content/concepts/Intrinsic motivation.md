Intrinsic motivation means doing an activity for its inherent satisfaction rather than for a separable consequence ([Ryan and Deci 2000, p. 56](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_IntExtDefs.pdf)); Ryan and Deci contrast this with extrinsic motivation, which pursues a separable outcome ([Ryan and Deci 2000, p. 60](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_IntExtDefs.pdf)).

> "Intrinsic motivation is defined as the doing of an activity for its inherent satisfactions rather than for some separable consequence. When intrinsically motivated a person is moved to act for the fun or challenge entailed rather than because of external prods, pressures, or rewards." (Ryan and Deci 2000, "Intrinsic and Extrinsic Motivations," Contemporary Educational Psychology 25, p. 56, [selfdeterminationtheory.org](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_IntExtDefs.pdf))

### A healthy-state tendency

The theory describes the tendency through exploration and play: "From birth onward, humans, in their healthiest states, are active, inquisitive, curious, and playful creatures, displaying a ubiquitous readiness to learn and explore, and they do not require extraneous incentives to do so" ([Ryan and Deci 2000, "Intrinsic and Extrinsic Motivations," p. 56](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_IntExtDefs.pdf)). The companion review calls it "the inherent tendency to seek out novelty and challenges, to extend and exercise one's capacities, to explore, and to learn" (Ryan and Deci 2000, American Psychologist 55, p. 70, [author-hosted PDF](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf)). The review also reports an apparent decline across school grades: "In schools, for example, it appears that intrinsic motivation becomes weaker with each advancing grade" ([Ryan and Deci 2000, "Intrinsic and Extrinsic Motivations," p. 60](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_IntExtDefs.pdf)).

### The undermining effect

The paper's rewards discussion is a 2000 summary of a research program that began with demonstrations that extrinsic rewards can undermine intrinsic motivation; Ryan and Deci's review explains the undermining effect in terms of rewards shifting the perceived locus of causality toward an external source ([Ryan and Deci 2000](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_IntExtDefs.pdf)). The review's meta-analytic verdict is that "virtually every type of expected tangible reward made contingent on task performance does, in fact, undermine intrinsic motivation" ([Ryan and Deci 2000, "Intrinsic and Extrinsic Motivations," p. 59](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_IntExtDefs.pdf)). The compendium's rewards principle [[MO062 — Learn to use rewards wisely — reinforcement, not bribery]] frames the practical skill as using rewards "as celebration of progress rather than as the reason for the behaviour."
