The fractured self is R. D. Laing's phenomenological description, in The Divided Self (1960), of the person who cannot take his own being for granted: split from the world, split from himself, and split from his body. Laing's contrast is between two ways of experiencing one's being. The secure person experiences himself as "real, alive, whole, and, in a temporal sense, a continuous person" (Laing, The Divided Self, 1990 ed., p. 39, quoted in [Gustafsson & Krickel-Choi, 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/1354066120927073)). The person Laing writes about is on the other side of that line:

> "They experience themselves as split in a double sense: from the world and from themselves" (Laing, 1990, p. 17, quoted in [Gustafsson & Krickel-Choi, 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/1354066120927073)).

### What the description carries

Laing's stated aim in the book was to make the process of going mad understandable, and the fractured self is his account of its starting position: a self whose identity and autonomy are always in question, which may lack the experience of its own temporal continuity, and which may feel more insubstantial than substantial. The defensive structures Laing describes, the inner self sealed off from a compliant outer self, are attempts to preserve a being that feels perpetually threatened; my principle SE069 carries the clinical sign: recognise identity disturbances, times when the self feels fragmented. The description also has a caution built in: Laing's account is phenomenology, an attempt to describe experience from inside, not a validated causal model of schizophrenia, and the psychiatric mainstream did not follow him. A compendium exercise drawn from the concept is to notice in oneself or another the state of feeling unreal or discontinuous, and to treat it as experience to be steadied rather than as fact about the person. What the account does not give: treatment evidence; the therapeutic programme Laing later built at Kingsley Hall was never trialled.
