The need to belong is the hypothesis, argued by Baumeister and Leary in a 1995 Psychological Bulletin review, that the drive for social attachment is not a preference or a learned habit but a fundamental human motivation. Their one-line specification: "The need is for frequent, nonaversive interactions within an ongoing relational bond" ([Baumeister and Leary 1995, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7777651/)). Two ingredients, both required: frequent, nonaversive interactions, held inside a bond that persists.

### The evidence they surveyed

The review surveyed the empirical literature, and the abstract records the verdicts. "Consistent with the belongingness hypothesis, people form social attachments readily under most conditions and resist the dissolution of existing bonds." Belongingness "appears to have multiple and strong effects on emotional patterns and on cognitive processes," while "Lack of attachments is linked to a variety of ill effects on health, adjustment, and well-being." Even "several seeming counterexamples turned out not to disconfirm the hypothesis," and the evidence the abstract mentions on satiation, substitution, and behavioral consequences is "likewise consistent with the hypothesized motivation" ([Baumeister and Leary 1995, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7777651/)). Their conclusion, stated in the abstract's final sentence: "Existing evidence supports the hypothesis that the need to belong is a powerful, fundamental, and extremely pervasive motivation" ([Baumeister and Leary 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7777651/)).

### What follows

If belonging is a need rather than a taste, two practical consequences follow, and the compendium's principles carry both. First, treating belonging as optional misreads the mind: "[[SO029 — Treat belonging as a need, not a preference]]" is the rule the research licenses. Second, on this reading the operative variable is the bond with its frequent, nonaversive interactions, so the question for practice is whether one's relationships actually run on interactions of that kind ([Baumeister and Leary 1995, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7777651/)). Relatedness in self-determination theory names a similar need, offered here as a comparison; ubuntu gives the belonging idea an older philosophical formulation, the claim that personhood itself is constituted with others.
