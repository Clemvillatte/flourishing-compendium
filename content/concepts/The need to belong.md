The need to belong is the hypothesis, argued by Baumeister and Leary in a 1995 Psychological Bulletin review, that the drive for social attachment is not a preference or a learned habit but a fundamental human motivation. Their one-line specification: "The need is for frequent, nonaversive interactions within an ongoing relational bond" ([Baumeister and Leary 1995, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7777651/)). Two ingredients, both required: contact that happens often and contact that hurts nobody, held inside a bond that persists.

### The evidence they surveyed

The review worked through five criteria for a fundamental motivation, and the abstract records the verdict on each. "Consistent with the belongingness hypothesis, people form social attachments readily under most conditions and resist the dissolution of existing bonds." Belongingness "appears to have multiple and strong effects on emotional patterns and on cognitive processes," while "lack of attachments is linked to a variety of ill effects on health, adjustment, and well-being." Even "seeming counterexamples turned out not to disconfirm the hypothesis," and the evidence on satiation and substitution behaved the way a true need should ([Baumeister and Leary 1995, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7777651/)). Their conclusion, stated in the abstract's final sentence: "Existing evidence supports the hypothesis that the need to belong is a powerful, fundamental, and extremely pervasive motivation" ([Baumeister and Leary 1995](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7777651/)).

### What follows

If belonging is a need rather than a taste, two practical consequences follow, and the compendium's principles carry both. First, treating belonging as optional misreads the mind: "[[SO029 — Treat belonging as a need, not a preference]]" is the rule the research licenses. Second, the need is satisfied or starved by relationship quality, not by headcount; the criterion is interactions inside an ongoing bond, so a responsive partner can feed the need while a crowd cannot ([Baumeister and Leary 1995, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7777651/)). Self-determination theory later folded the finding into its own taxonomy as relatedness, one of three basic psychological needs; ubuntu gives it the older philosophical formulation, the claim that personhood itself is constituted with others.
