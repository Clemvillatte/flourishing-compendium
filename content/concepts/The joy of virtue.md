“The joy of virtue” — Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.

### What it is

The compendium uses this title for a claim Aristotle makes in Nicomachean Ethics X: virtue is not endured for the sake of something else; it is enjoyed. The good person's pleasures track what is actually good, because "in all such matters that which appears to the good man is thought to be really so... and virtue and the good man as such are the measure of each thing, those also will be pleasures which appear so to him, and those things pleasant which he enjoys" (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.5, trans. Ross, [MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.10.x.html)).

### What it carries

The joy is a consequence of alignment, not a bonus paid for good behavior: "to each man the activity in accordance with his own disposition is most desirable, and, therefore, to the good man that which is in accordance with virtue" (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.6, trans. Ross, [MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.10.x.html)). Acting justly, courageously or generously is the activity most native to the person who has those dispositions, so it is also the activity that most satisfies. Aristotle's conclusion is blunt about substitutes: "Happiness, therefore, does not lie in amusement" (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.6, trans. Ross, [MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.10.x.html)).

The SP055 principle prefers the affects of reason on the ground that they are stronger, and this is why: an appetite's pleasure fades with the appetite, while the pleasure of an activity one is good at returns with every exercise of it. The practical test is experiential: a person who gives time, help or attention as a mere duty is doing the act without the disposition, and the disposition, not the act, is where the joy lives.
