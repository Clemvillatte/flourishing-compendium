**Cognitive transformation theory of desistance** — Giordano — leaving crime requires cognitive shifts: recognising new possibilities and reinterpreting one's past.

Cognitive transformation theory is Peggy Giordano, Stephen Cernkovich and Jennifer Rudolph's account of how people stop offending, published in 2002 from life-history interviews with men and women who had been delinquent as adolescents. Its point of departure is the older sociology that treats marriage, work and other "turning points" as the causes of going straight. The theory does not deny those things help, but it puts the thinking first: the external events only change a person when they set off changes in outlook that the person partly does themselves. The paper distinguishes four such shifts:

> "Conceptually, we distinguish four types of intimately related cognitive transformations. The first, and arguably the most fundamental, is a shift in the actor's basic openness to change."

> "A fundamental premise is that both exposure to a hook and one's attitude toward it are important elements of successful change." ([Giordano, Cernkovich and Rudolph 2002, American Journal of Sociology 107(4):990-1064, open PDF](https://faculty.washington.edu/matsueda/courses/517/Readings/Giordano%20et%20al%202002.pdf))

The environment enters through what the authors call "hooks for change", and the name is deliberate:

> "We wish to emphasize the actor's own role in creatively and selectively appropriating elements in the environment (we will refer to these elements as 'hooks for change'), including, but not limited to, such positive influences as a spouse."

> "We might refer to potentially prosocial features of the environment as catalysts, change agents, causes, or even turning points (Laub and Sampson 2001; Maruna 2001), but we prefer to call them 'hooks for change' for two reasons. First, consistent with Mead's notion of opening the door to certain stimuli and closing it to others, we wish to emphasize the actor's own role in latching onto opportunities presented by the broader environment." (same paper)

The third shift builds a new self to live in:

> "Thus, a third type of cognitive transformation occurs when actors are able to envision and begin to fashion an appealing and conventional 'replacement self' that can supplant the marginal one that must be left behind." (same paper)

And the fourth seals it:

> "The fourth type of cognitive change (the capstone) involves a transformation in the way the actor views the deviant behavior or lifestyle itself."

> "Thus, the desistance process can be seen as relatively complete when the actor no longer sees these same behaviors as positive, viable, or even personally relevant." (same paper)

The theory matters beyond criminology because it is a worked example of how change in a life happens: an opening of attention, a latching onto something in the world, a new self-image, and finally a change in what the old life even means.
