“Joy” — Spinoza, Ethics III.

Joy, on this page, is the affect Spinoza calls laetitia. His Ethics defines it by a change rather than a state:

> “Pleasure is the transition of a man from a less to a greater perfection.” (Spinoza, Ethics III, Definitions of the Emotions II, [Elwes translation](https://sacred-texts.com/phi/spinoza/ethics/eth03.htm); the Latin term is laetitia, which Elwes renders “pleasure” and later translations such as Curley’s render “joy”)

The definition makes joy a crossing rather than a possession. A person settled at their level of power feels nothing about it; the affect appears only while the level changes, upward for joy and downward for its opposite. Spinoza separates a local form from a spread one: pleasure referred to the whole of body and mind together he calls merriment (hilaritas), and the compendium’s cheerfulness principle keeps that word. At the end of the definitions he reduces the whole family to three roots: “that they all spring from desire, pleasure, or pain, or, rather, that there is nothing besides these three” (Ethics III, concluding note to the definitions, Elwes).

Spinoza’s own explanation of why he wrote “transition”:

> “I say transition: for pleasure is not perfection itself. For, if man were born with the perfection to which he passes, he would possess the same, without the emotion of pleasure.” (Ethics III, Definitions of the Emotions II, explanation)

The scholium to III.11, in the wording of the Stanford Encyclopedia’s quotation of the Curley translation:

> “By Joy, therefore, I shall understand in what follows that passion by which the mind passes to a greater perfection. And by Sadness, that passion by which it passes to a lesser perfection.” (Spinoza, Ethics III, Proposition 11 Scholium, quoted in [LeBuffe, Spinoza’s Psychological Theory, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/spinoza-psychological/))

### Joy as finish, not goal

Aristotle treats the companion term, pleasure, as something that arrives at the end of an activity rather than a target:

> “And Pleasure perfects the Working. But Pleasure does not perfect it in the same way as the Faculty and Object of Perception do... Furthermore, Pleasure perfects the act of Working not in the way of an inherent state but as a supervening finish, such as is bloom in people at their prime.” (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.4, [Chase translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/8438/8438-h/8438-h.htm))

Nietzsche, in the aphorism the seed names, makes the same move from sensation to affirmation, and adds a shareable direction. The New Year passage ties joy to what a person can say yes to, and the later aphorism names the social form of it, the capacity to join in another’s good fortune rather than only their losses:

> “I want more and more to perceive the necessary characters in things as the beautiful:—I shall thus be one of those who beautify things. Amor fati: let that henceforth be my love! ... And all in all, to sum up: I wish to be at any time hereafter only a yea-sayer!” (Nietzsche, The Gay Science 276, [Common translation](https://americanliterature.com/author/friedrich-nietzsche/book/the-gay-science/book-fourth-sanctus-januarius))

> “I want to teach them that which at present so few understand, and the preachers of fellowship in sorrow least of all:—namely, fellowship in joy!” (Nietzsche, The Gay Science 338, same page)
