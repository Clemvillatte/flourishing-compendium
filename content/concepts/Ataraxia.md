Ataraxia (Greek ἀταραξία) means freedom from mental disturbance — tranquillity, untroubledness, or serenity of mind. The word names a central condition of the good life in Epicurean philosophy, and James Warren notes that Epicureans and Sextan Pyrrhonists both "promote something which they call ataraxia as the goal of life". Epicurus, in the Letter to Menoeceus, identifies pleasure as the highest human good, but defines true pleasure as "freedom from pain in the body and trouble in the mind" — aponia and ataraxia respectively ([Epicurus Today](https://epicurustoday.com/02-key-sources/notataraxia/)). Principal Doctrine 3 elaborates: "The magnitude of pleasure reaches its limit in the removal of all that is painful" ([Epicurus Today](https://epicurustoday.com/02-key-sources/notataraxia/)). The standard framing, found in the Epicureanism overview, is that "the ultimate goal then of Epicurean ethics was to reach a state of aponia and ataraxia" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicureanism)).

The mechanism is therapeutic. In the Letter to Menoeceus, philosophy secures the "health of the soul", and sober reasoning and the examination of choices and avoidances serve to banish the beliefs that cause the greatest disturbances of the soul ([Epicurus Today](https://epicurustoday.com/02-key-sources/notataraxia/)).

The scholarly debate over whether ataraxia or pleasure is the telos is real. Scholars including Nussbaum, Warren, Konstan, Purinton, O'Keefe, Rosenbaum, Rist and Burton are quoted in the debate, with positions ranging from ataraxia as the goal itself to ataraxia as one constituent of the pleasured life ([Epicurus Today](https://epicurustoday.com/02-key-sources/ataraxiamisreading/)).

## What it is NOT


Ataraxia is not the same as hedonic pleasure, and it is not a state of numbness or apathy. On the reading defended in the Epicurus Today article, ataraxia is the condition of a mind in full pleasure, not a third state above pleasure and pain. Nor is ataraxia the same as happiness in the Aristotelian sense: Aristotle's [[Eudaimonia|eudaimonia]] is activity in accordance with virtue, whereas ataraxia is a condition — a way of being undisturbed rather than a way of acting. Epicurus's own texts do not present ataraxia as the ultimate goal but as a constituent of a life already pleasured; scholars debate whether the telos is pleasure, ataraxia, or both.

## Practical implications


Ataraxia can be compared, as an interpretive analogy, to the modern psychological construct of low negative affectivity and to cognitive therapy's targeting of catastrophising. In habit terms it suggests a diagnostic: if a proposed habit or goal increases chronic mental disturbance (rumination, anxiety, intrusive worry), it is misaligned with the underlying structure of well-being. Techniques that reduce reactivity to outcomes, such as Buddhist equanimity or modern reappraisal, can be read as aiming at a similar target, though the traditions differ in their grounds. The concept is useful precisely because it names the thing being optimised (calm) rather than the technique for getting there.
