Ataraxia (Greek ἀταραξία) means freedom from mental disturbance: tranquillity, untroubledness, or serenity of mind. The word names a central condition of the good life in Epicurean philosophy. Epicurus, in the Letter to Menoeceus, identifies pleasure as the highest human good, but defines true pleasure as:

> "freedom from pain in the body and from trouble in the mind" (Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, [Epicurus Today](https://epicurustoday.com/02-key-sources/notataraxia/))

aponia and ataraxia respectively. Principal Doctrine 3 elaborates:

> "The magnitude of pleasure reaches its limit in the removal of all that is painful" (Epicurus, Principal Doctrine 3, [Epicurus Today](https://epicurustoday.com/02-key-sources/notataraxia/)).

The standard framing, found in the Epicureanism overview, is that "the ultimate goal then of Epicurean ethics was to reach a state of aponia and ataraxia" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicureanism)).

### The therapeutic mechanism

In the Letter to Menoeceus, philosophy secures the health of the soul: "For no one can come too early or too late to secure the health of his soul." Sober reasoning and the examination of choices and avoidances serve to banish the beliefs that cause the greatest disturbances of the soul ([Epicurus Today](https://epicurustoday.com/02-key-sources/notataraxia/)).

### A scholarly debate

The scholarly debate over whether ataraxia or pleasure is the telos is real. A catalog of the debate documents scholars across the last several decades characterizing Epicurus's goal as ataraxia, tranquility, or katastematic pleasure rather than pleasure plain and simple: Nussbaum (1994), Warren (2002, 2004), Konstan, Purinton (1993), O'Keefe (2010), Rosenbaum (1990), Rist (1972) and Burton are all catalogued, with positions ranging from ataraxia as the goal itself to ataraxia as one constituent of the pleasured life ([Epicurus Today, Tranquillism](https://epicurustoday.com/02-key-sources/ataraxiamisreading/)).

## What it is NOT

Ataraxia is not the same as hedonic pleasure, and it is not a state of numbness or apathy. On the reading defended in the Epicurus Today article, ataraxia is the condition of a mind in full pleasure, not a third state above pleasure and pain: "It is not a separate goal that stands above pleasure." Nor is ataraxia the same as happiness in the Aristotelian sense: Aristotle's [[Eudaimonia|eudaimonia]] is activity in accordance with virtue, whereas ataraxia is a condition, a way of being undisturbed rather than a way of acting.

## Practical implications

Ataraxia can be compared, as an interpretive analogy, to the modern psychological construct of low negative affectivity and to cognitive therapy's targeting of catastrophising. In habit terms it suggests a diagnostic: if a proposed habit or goal increases chronic mental disturbance (rumination, anxiety, intrusive worry), it is misaligned with the underlying structure of well-being. Techniques that reduce reactivity to outcomes, such as [[Equanimity|Buddhist equanimity]] or modern reappraisal, can be read as aiming at a similar target, though the traditions differ in their grounds. The concept is useful precisely because it names the thing being optimised (calm) rather than the technique for getting there.
