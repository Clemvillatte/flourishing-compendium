Bibliotherapy research is the study of structured reading as a treatment: a standardized cognitive-behavioural programme taken home in book form and worked through more or less independently, with therapist contact only supportive. The founding meta-analysis is Cuijpers (1997) on unipolar depression:

> "The mean effect size of studies comparing bibliotherapy with a waiting list control group (6 studies, 7 comparisons) was 0.82 (95% confidence interval, CI: 0.50, 1.15)." ([Cuijpers 1997, via the NCBI review record](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK67070/))

### What the research carries

The comparison with therapy is the striking part: "Four studies (5 comparisons) compared bibliotherapy with individual therapy. The mean effect size was -0.10 (95% CI: -0.48, 0.28), which indicated that there were no important differences between individual therapy and bibliotherapy." The review's conclusion carries its own caution: "Bibliotherapy was an effective treatment modality in unipolar depression, which was no less effective than individual or group therapy. However, this conclusion was based on a limited number of studies in which bibliotherapy and other forms of therapy differed considerably from each other." The samples were also media-recruited, so the generalisation to clinical settings is a live limit. For a compendium built on reading, the finding is nevertheless a license: structured reading is not a weaker cousin of help, it is one of the tested forms help takes. My principle S123 carries the applied reading: read and reflect on philosophical texts daily, since the evidence supports reading as an active ingredient rather than a pleasant adjunct. A compendium exercise drawn from the research is to choose one structured book programme for a current difficulty and complete it as an experiment, drop-out being the main thing the studies warn against. What the research does not give: evidence that any book helps; the tested programmes were specific, structured and cognitive-behavioural in content.
