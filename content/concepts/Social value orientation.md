Social value orientation (SVO) is a measurable difference between people in how much weight they give to another person's outcomes when they choose how to share resources. Murphy, Ackermann and Handgraaf describe the idea this way:

> "Within the SVO framework it is assumed that people vary in their motivations or goals when evaluating different resource allocations between themselves and another person." ([Murphy, Ackermann and Handgraaf, 2011, Judgment and Decision Making 6(8):771-781](http://ryanomurphy.com/resources/Murphy-Ackermann-Handgraaf-2011.pdf))

They call SVO "the magnitude of the concern people have for others" and note that the usual assumption of narrow self-interest "is itself a particular SVO, namely a perfectly individualistic orientation" ([Murphy et al., 2011](http://ryanomurphy.com/resources/Murphy-Ackermann-Handgraaf-2011.pdf)).

### The four types

In their slider measure a person makes a series of choices that divide money between self and another person, and the average allocations are converted into an angle. Per the paper, altruists have an angle above 57.15 degrees, prosocials between 22.45 and 57.15, individualists between -12.04 and 22.45, and competitive types below -12.04. The examples they give of what each type pursues are: individualistic, maximising one's own payoff; competitive, maximising the difference between one's own and the other's payoff; prosocial, maximising joint payoffs. Optional secondary items separate joint maximisation from inequality aversion ([Murphy et al., 2011](http://ryanomurphy.com/resources/Murphy-Ackermann-Handgraaf-2011.pdf)).

### What the measure predicts

A meta-analysis by Balliet, Parks and Joireman of 82 studies found a "significant and small to medium effect size" (r = .30) for the link between SVO and cooperation in social dilemmas. The effect was larger when participants were not paid (r = .39) than when they were paid (r = .23) ([Balliet, Parks and Joireman, 2009](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/soss_research/748/)). A later reanalysis describes the same meta-analysis as 48 reports covering 82 laboratory studies that used experimental games such as the prisoner's dilemma, and reports the combined effect as indicating "that prosocials cooperate more than proselfs" ([reanalysis in Judgment and Decision Making](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/judgment-and-decision-making/article/is-there-evidence-of-publication-biases-in-jdm-research/FE91A27169A2EDF9C914E60A3CD31DD6)).

### In the compendium

The principles listed (for example S030, BE056 and D117) concern acting for others and not stirring envy. The sources above show that SVO is a laboratory measure of allocation choices, and that it correlates modestly with cooperation in games. They do not show that SVO changes with practice or character development, and they do not test real-world kindness. The seed citation to Van Lange et al. (2007) was not opened for this revision. I did not locate the original Messick and McClintock (1968) paper, so the origin of the construct is not described here.
