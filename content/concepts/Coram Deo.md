“Coram Deo” — the Reformation's phrase, as the cross-tradition echo.

Coram Deo is Latin for "before the face of God," and it names a whole manner of living: to do whatever one does in the awareness that one stands in God's presence. The phrase itself is a summary; the theology under it is Calvin's, and its charter passages are in the Institutes' chapters on the Christian life. There the premise is ownership before it is duty:

> "But if we are not our own, but the Lord's, it is plain both what error is to be shunned, and to what end the actions of our lives ought to be directed. We are not our own; therefore, neither is our own reason or will to rule our acts and counsels. We are not our own; therefore, let us not make it our end to seek what may be agreeable to our carnal nature. We are not our own; therefore, as far as possible, let us forget ourselves and the things that are ours. On the other hand, we are God's; let us, therefore, live and die to him (Rom. xiv. 8.) We are God's; therefore, let his wisdom and will preside over all our actions. We are God's; to him, then, as the only legitimate end, let every part of our life be directed." ([Calvin, Institutes of the Christian Religion, Book III, ch. 7, Beveridge translation](https://www.bluffton.edu/courses/humanities/2/calvin.htm))

The aim is not groveling but reorientation, and Calvin says so in a sentence modern readers often find surprising:

> "O how great the proficiency of him who, taught that he is not his own, has withdrawn the dominion and government of himself from his own reason that he may give them to God!" (same text)

The doctrine has two practical edges. One is the consecration of everything, including ordinary work, since "we are consecrated and dedicated to God, and, therefore, should not henceforth think, speak, design, or act, without a view to his glory" (same text) — the claim behind the Reformation's famous dignity of every calling, from the cobbler's bench to the pulpit. The other edge is watchfulness without terror: the presence one lives before is not surveillance but care, on the model of the Psalmist's "the eyes of the Lord are upon the righteous" (Ps. 34:15, in the Institutes' treatment of providence). The seed of the same awareness appears throughout the universe itself: in creation, Calvin writes, God "daily place[s] himself in our view, that we cannot open our eyes without being compelled to behold him" (Institutes, Book I, ch. 5).

The compendium's principle [[X017 — Practice watchfulness as the constant presence of the Beloved]] is the cross-tradition echo named in the seed: the same before-a-face awareness, kept in other vocabularies by other traditions. Its neighbor [[Vocation as calling]] carries the Reformation consequence: work done before God is work that matters.
