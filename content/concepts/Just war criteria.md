Just war criteria are the tradition's answer to the question whether any war can be lawful, and the classic answer is Aquinas's three requirements in Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 40:

> First, the authority of the sovereign by whose command the war is to be waged. Secondly, a just cause is required, namely that those who are attacked, should be attacked because they deserve it on account of some fault. Thirdly, it is necessary that the belligerents should have a rightful intention, so that they intend the advancement of good, or the avoidance of evil.

(Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 40, a. 1, [English Dominican Province translation, New Advent](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/3040.htm))

Aquinas's treatment builds on Augustine, and the SEP article on Augustine quotes the passage the tradition runs on: "if you remember that you are a human being, you will grieve that these things are necessary" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/augustine/)). The war is fought in mourning, not triumph; the necessity is the thing to be grieved.

The criteria are usually read in two groups. Jus ad bellum asks whether it is permissible to go to war at all: legitimate authority, just cause, right intention, and, in the modern codification, last resort and proportionality. Jus in bello asks how a war, once under way, may be fought: discrimination between combatants and civilians, and proportionality in means. The compendium's principles work both edges. [[D076 — Enter conflict reluctantly — with a mourning heart]] runs on the Augustinian grief; [[D131 — Treat weapons as a last resort]] runs on last resort.
