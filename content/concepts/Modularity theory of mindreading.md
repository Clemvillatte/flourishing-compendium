The modularity theory of mindreading holds that understanding other minds rests on an innate, specialised mechanism. In Alan Leslie's account: "Our ability to understand the thoughts and feelings of other people does not initially develop as a theory but as a mechanism. The 'theory of mind' mechanism (ToMM) is part of the core architecture of the human brain, and is specialized for learning about mental states" ([Leslie, Friedman and German, 2004, Trends in Cognitive Sciences, Rutgers PDF](https://ruccs.rutgers.edu/images/archive/personal-alan-leslie/publications/Leslie%20Friedman%20German%202004.pdf)). The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy describes the mechanism as taking "information about the past and present behavior of other people" and computing "their probable psychological states", with outputs in the form of M-representations ([IEP, Theory of Mind](https://iep.utm.edu/theomind/)).

### Two stages: ToMM and selection

The theory has two parts. ToMM "kick-starts belief-desire attribution but effective reasoning about belief contents depends on a process of selection by inhibition", which "develops slowly through the preschool period and well beyond" (Leslie et al., 2004). In a false-belief task the child must first inhibit the answer based on the object's real location. Scholl and Leslie write that normally developing 3-year-olds "have an intact ToMM, but fail the false-belief task because of immature selection processing" ([Minds, Modules, and Meta-Analysis, Rutgers PDF](https://ruccs.rutgers.edu/images/personal-alan-leslie/publications/Scholl%20&%20Leslie%202001.pdf)). The seed phrase "developing in stages" fits this, though the stages concern selection processing more than the module.

### The autism hypothesis

Baron-Cohen, Leslie and Frith asked in 1985 whether autistic children lack a theory of mind. Their paper states: "Even though the mental age of the autistic children was higher than that of the controls, they alone failed to impute beliefs to others" ([Does the autistic child have a theory of mind?, Rutgers PDF](https://ruccs.rutgers.edu/images/archive/personal-alan-leslie/publications/Baron-Cohen%20Leslie%20&%20Frith%201985.pdf)). The IEP reports that 85% of typically developing children and 86% of children with Down syndrome passed the Sally and Ann task, but only 20% of the autistic children predicted that Sally would look in the basket. In the Scholl and Leslie account, autistic individuals fail "despite intact selection processing", an impairment "specific to theory of mind".

### Limits

The seed says impairment "yields autism". The sources say less. Leslie himself hedges the first half: "In the limit, the ToMM may be modular". He states the second as a hypothesis: "the ToMM is damaged in autism (Kanner's syndrome), resulting in the core signs of that neurodevelopmental disorder" ([Leslie, 2000 chapter, Rutgers PDF](https://ruccs.rutgers.edu/images/archive/tech_rpt/tr-61/Leslie_2000b_NEW_COGNITIVE_NEUROSCIENCES.pdf)). A chapter from the Autism Research Centre says Leslie's computational analysis "has been widely accepted", but "the innate modularity claim is more controversial" ([Cognitive Neuroscience of Autism, ARC PDF](https://docs.autismresearchcentre.com/papers/2000_SBC_Cognitive-neuroscience-of-autism.pdf)). Leslie, Friedman and German note that ToMM "may be modular", while selection processing "appears to be penetrable to knowledge and instruction". The Encyclopedia.com entry adds that passing the false-belief task attests to a theory of mind but failing does not show its absence, because younger children "may fail due to their inability to inhibit improper responses" ([Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/medicine/anatomy-and-physiology/anatomy-and-physiology/theory-mind)).
