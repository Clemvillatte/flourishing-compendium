Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT, said as a single word, not as initials) belongs to what its developers call the third generation of behavior therapy, alongside Dialectical Behavior Therapy and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf)). The paper that lays out the model states that "ACT is an approach to psychological intervention defined in terms of certain theoretical processes, not a specific technology," and continues: "In theoretical and process terms we can define ACT as a psychological intervention based on modern behavioral psychology, including Relational Frame Theory, that applies mindfulness and acceptance processes, and commitment and behavior change processes, to the creation of psychological flexibility" ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf)). The approach grew out of a decades-long attempt to rebuild behavior analysis around human language and cognition.

The overall goal of the model is psychological flexibility: "the ability to contact the present moment more fully as a conscious human being, and to change or persist in behavior when doing so serves valued ends" ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf)). The model identifies six core processes: acceptance, cognitive defusion, being present, self as context, values, and committed action. Acceptance is taught as the alternative to experiential avoidance, and the paper defines it fully: "Acceptance involves the active and aware embrace of those private events occasioned by one's history without unnecessary attempts to change their frequency or form, especially when doing so would cause psychological harm" ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf)). Defusion works on function rather than content: "Cognitive defusion techniques attempt to alter the undesirable functions of thoughts and other private events, rather than trying to alter their form, frequency or situational sensitivity" ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf)). Values are "chosen qualities of purposive action that can never be obtained as an object but can be instantiated moment by moment," and committed action means "larger and larger patterns of effective action linked to chosen values" ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf)).

The pathological core the model names is the fusion-avoidance pattern: "Cognitive fusion supports experiential avoidance, which is the attempt to alter the form, frequency, or situational sensitivity of private events even when doing so causes behavioral harm" ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf)). A later unified-model review states: "ACT focused on six processes (acceptance, defusion, self, now, values, and action) that bear on a single overall target (psychological flexibility)" ([Hayes et al. 2012](https://www.apa.org/education-career/ce/acceptance-commitment.pdf)). On outcomes, that review reports the earlier meta-analysis of 21 randomized trials with an average between-group effect size of d = .66 at post-treatment, and a survey of 66 studies of specific ACT components finding an average effect size of g = .68 for the components against inactive controls ([Hayes et al. 2012](https://www.apa.org/education-career/ce/acceptance-commitment.pdf)). The paper's stance on inner pain is one sentence: "Acceptance (and defusion) in ACT is not an end in itself. Rather acceptance is fostered as a method of increasing values-based action" ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf)).

## Related concepts
