Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT, said as a single word, not as initials) belongs to what its developers call the third generation of behavior therapy, alongside Dialectical Behavior Therapy and Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy. The approach grew out of a decades-long attempt to rebuild behavior analysis around human language and cognition. Its developers define it by processes rather than techniques:

> "ACT is an approach to psychological intervention defined in terms of certain theoretical processes, not a specific technology. In theoretical and process terms we can define ACT as a psychological intervention based on modern behavioral psychology, including Relational Frame Theory, that applies mindfulness and acceptance processes, and commitment and behavior change processes, to the creation of psychological flexibility" ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf)).

The single target of those processes is psychological flexibility:

> "ACT targets each of these core problems with the general goal of increasing psychological flexibility – the ability to contact the present moment more fully as a conscious human being, and to change or persist in behavior when doing so serves valued ends." ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf))

### The six processes

The model identifies six core processes: acceptance, cognitive defusion, contact with the present moment, self-as-context, values, and committed action. Acceptance is taught as the alternative to experiential avoidance:

> "Acceptance involves the active and aware embrace of those private events occasioned by one's history without unnecessary attempts to change their frequency or form, especially when doing so would cause psychological harm" ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf)).

Defusion works on the function of a thought rather than its content:

> "Cognitive defusion techniques attempt to alter the undesirable functions of thoughts and other private events, rather than trying to alter their form, frequency or situational sensitivity" ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf)).

Values are "chosen qualities of purposive action that can never be obtained as an object but can be instantiated moment by moment," and committed action means "larger and larger patterns of effective action linked to chosen values" ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf)).

### What goes wrong

The pathological core the model names is the fusion-avoidance pattern:

> "Cognitive fusion supports experiential avoidance, which is the attempt to alter the form, frequency, or situational sensitivity of private events even when doing so causes behavioral harm" ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf)).

Inner pain itself is not the enemy on this account; the attempt to control it is. The paper is explicit that acceptance is instrumental: "Acceptance (and defusion) in ACT is not an end in itself. Rather acceptance is fostered as a method of increasing values-based action" ([Hayes et al. 2006](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf)).

### Evidence status

A later unified-model review states the shape of the model in one line: "ACT focused on six processes (acceptance, defusion, self, now, values, and action) that bear on a single overall target (psychological flexibility)" ([Hayes et al. 2012](https://www.apa.org/education-career/ce/acceptance-commitment.pdf)). That review reports the earlier meta-analysis of 21 randomized trials, with an average between-group effect size of d = .66 at post-treatment, and a survey of 66 studies of specific ACT components finding an average weighted effect size of g = .68 against inactive controls ([Hayes et al. 2012](https://www.apa.org/education-career/ce/acceptance-commitment.pdf)). The authors' own summary of the evidence in the 2006 paper is measured: "There are not enough well-controlled studies to conclude that ACT is generally more effective than other active treatments across the range of problems examined, but so far the data are promising" ([Hayes et al. 2006, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16300724/)).
