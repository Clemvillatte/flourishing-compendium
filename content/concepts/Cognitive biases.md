Cognitive biases are the systematic, predictable errors produced by the mind's shortcuts. The founding paper's abstract states the program ([Tversky & Kahneman 1974, Science 185:1124-1131](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124)):

> "This article described three heuristics that are employed in making judgments under uncertainty: (i) representativeness, which is usually employed when people are asked to judge the probability that an object or event A belongs to class or process B; (ii) availability of instances or scenarios, which is often employed when people are asked to assess the frequency of a class or the plausibility of a particular development; and (iii) adjustment from an anchor, which is usually employed in numerical prediction when a relevant value is available."
> — [Tversky & Kahneman 1974, "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases", Science 185:1124-1131 (journal abstract)](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124)

The same abstract carries the two-sided verdict — the shortcuts are economical, and the errors are lawful: "These heuristics are highly economical and usually effective, but they lead to systematic and predictable errors" ([Tversky & Kahneman 1974](https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.185.4157.1124)). The clinical facet, in anxiety, is the threat-related attentional bias, established meta-analytically ([Bar-Haim et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17201568/)):

> "Overall, the results show that the bias is reliably demonstrated with different experimental paradigms and under a variety of experimental conditions, but that it is only an effect size of d = 0.45. Although processes requiring conscious perception of threat contribute to the bias, a significant bias is also observed with stimuli outside awareness. The bias is of comparable magnitude across different types of anxious populations (individuals with different clinical disorders, high-anxious nonclinical individuals, anxious children and adults) and is not observed in nonanxious individuals."
> — [Bar-Haim, Lamy, Pergamin, Bakermans-Kranenburg & van IJzendoorn 2007, "Threat-related attentional bias in anxious and nonanxious individuals: a meta-analytic study", Psychological Bulletin 133(1):1-24 (PubMed abstract)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17201568/)

The meta-analysis covered "172 studies (N = 2,263 anxious, N = 1,768 nonanxious)" ([Bar-Haim et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17201568/)). The two sources bracket the concept: heuristics are not malfunctions but efficient shortcuts whose errors are predictable enough to study — and the anxiety literature adds that a bias can also mark a clinical state, selectively, and with moderate effect.
