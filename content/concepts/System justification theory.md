System justification theory is a social-psychological account of why people defend the social, economic and political arrangements they live under, even when those arrangements hurt them. John Jost and Mahzarin Banaji proposed it in 1994 in the British Journal of Social Psychology, defining system justification as the "process by which existing social arrangements are legitimized, even at the expense of personal and group interest" (Jost and Banaji 1994, p. 2, quoted in Jost, Banaji and Nosek 2004).

> "The third is 'system justification,' and it captures social and psychological needs to imbue the status quo with legitimacy and to see it as good, fair, natural, desirable, and even inevitable." (Jost, Banaji and Nosek, [A decade of system justification theory: accumulated evidence of conscious and unconscious bolstering of the status quo](https://banaji.sites.fas.harvard.edu/research/publications/articles/2004_Jost_PP.pdf), Political Psychology 25(6), 2004)

### What the theory claims

The 1994 paper distinguished three justification motives that can pull against each other: ego justification (a favorable self-image), group justification (a favorable image of one's own group, which the 2004 review calls the primary focus of social identity theory) and system justification (legitimacy for the larger order). The distinctive claim is the third motive: among members of disadvantaged groups it can outweigh the other two, so people rationalize the very arrangements that disadvantage them. The review puts the point directly: "those who suffer the most from the system are also those who have the most to explain, justify, and rationalize". It frames this as a way of reducing ideological dissonance and defending against threats to the system's legitimacy. On this account, stereotypes and political ideologies partly serve that need.
