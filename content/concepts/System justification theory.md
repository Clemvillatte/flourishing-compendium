System justification theory is a social-psychological account of why people defend the social, economic and political arrangements they live under, even when those arrangements hurt them ([Jost, Banaji and Nosek 2004](https://banaji.sites.fas.harvard.edu/research/publications/articles/2004_Jost_PP.pdf)). John Jost and Mahzarin Banaji introduced the theory in 1994, defining system justification as the "process by which existing social arrangements are legitimized, even at the expense of personal and group interest" (Jost and Banaji 1994, p. 2, quoted in Jost, Banaji and Nosek 2004) ([Jost, Banaji and Nosek 2004](https://banaji.sites.fas.harvard.edu/research/publications/articles/2004_Jost_PP.pdf)).

### What the theory claims

Jost and Banaji (1994) distinguished three justification tendencies that can pull in different directions for members of disadvantaged groups: ego justification (a favorable self-image), group justification (a favorable image of one's own group) and system justification (legitimacy for the larger order) ([Jost, Banaji and Nosek 2004](https://banaji.sites.fas.harvard.edu/research/publications/articles/2004_Jost_PP.pdf)). The review describes the third motive this way:

> "The third is 'system justification,' and it captures social and psychological needs to imbue the status quo with legitimacy and to see it as good, fair, natural, desirable, and even inevitable." (Jost, Banaji and Nosek, [A decade of system justification theory: accumulated evidence of conscious and unconscious bolstering of the status quo](https://banaji.sites.fas.harvard.edu/research/publications/articles/2004_Jost_PP.pdf), Political Psychology 25(6), 2004, section "Ego, Group, and System Justification Motives")

Whether system justification wins out is presented as conditional, not guaranteed ([Jost, Banaji and Nosek 2004](https://banaji.sites.fas.harvard.edu/research/publications/articles/2004_Jost_PP.pdf)). In the review's words, "members of disadvantaged groups are likely to engage in social change only when ego justification and/or group justification motives overcome the strength of system justification needs and tendencies" (section "Ego, Group, and System Justification Motives") ([Jost, Banaji and Nosek 2004](https://banaji.sites.fas.harvard.edu/research/publications/articles/2004_Jost_PP.pdf)). The review's strongest version of the claim draws on cognitive dissonance research: "If there is a motive to justify the system in order to reduce ideological dissonance and defend against threats to the system's legitimacy, it follows that those who suffer the most from the system are also those who have the most to explain, justify, and rationalize" (section "Enhanced System Justification Among the Disadvantaged") ([Jost, Banaji and Nosek 2004](https://banaji.sites.fas.harvard.edu/research/publications/articles/2004_Jost_PP.pdf)). The authors present this as a theoretical argument within their account ([Jost, Banaji and Nosek 2004](https://banaji.sites.fas.harvard.edu/research/publications/articles/2004_Jost_PP.pdf)).
