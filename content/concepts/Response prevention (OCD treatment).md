Response prevention, also called ritual prevention, is the half of exposure and response prevention (ERP) in which the person with obsessive-compulsive disorder agrees not to carry out the compulsion. Edna Foa's review in Dialogues in Clinical Neuroscience defines it this way:

> "Ritual prevention involves instructing the patient to abstain from the ritualizing that they believe prevents the feared disaster or reduces the distress produced by the obsession (eg, washing hands after touching the floor and fearing contracting a disease)." (Foa, 2010, [Dialogues Clin Neurosci](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3181959/))

### Where it came from

The same review says the "first real breakthrough" came in 1966, when Meyer described two patients treated with prolonged exposure to distressing objects and situations "combined with strict prevention of rituals." It adds that Meyer and colleagues went on to treat more patients and found the programme "highly successful in 10 of 15 cases, and partially effective in the remaining patients" ([Foa, 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3181959/)).

### What a trial of the parts found

Foa and colleagues randomly assigned patients with contamination obsessions and washing rituals to exposure only, ritual prevention only, or both, each delivered as 15 daily 120-minute sessions over three weeks. Patients in all three conditions improved. The combined group did better on almost every symptom measure, at post-treatment and at follow-up. The two single-component groups differed in kind: exposure patients reported lower anxiety when confronting feared contaminants, while ritual-prevention patients reported larger drops in urges to ritualize ([Foa, 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3181959/)).

### In the compendium

The seed sentence says that blocking compulsions is essential for exposure to work. The trial above supports a milder reading: adding ritual prevention to exposure gave a better outcome than exposure alone, and exposure alone still produced improvement. It is also one trial in one symptom group. The seed citations (Foa et al., 2007; Abramowitz et al., 2019) were not opened for this revision, so they are kept as listed but not relied on here. The principles below that use the term, BE029 and BE071, apply it to compulsive behaviour in general. The source covers treatment of diagnosed OCD, not everyday habits.
