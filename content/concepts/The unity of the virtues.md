The unity of the virtues is the thesis, argued by Socrates in Plato's *Protagoras* and refined by Aristotle in *Nicomachean Ethics* VI, that the virtues are not separate talents but one trained capacity seen from different angles. Socrates' version was radical: all the virtues are knowledge — courage, temperance, justice and piety are names for knowing what is good in different situations, so no one can have one virtue fully without having them all ([Stanford Encyclopedia, “Plato's Shorter Ethical Works: the Protagoras”](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2022/entries/plato-ethics-shorter/); [Cambridge, “The Unity of Virtue”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/social-philosophy-and-policy/article/abs/unity-of-virtue/FB7BAE5306153BD998EB4F2FA71C7E12)).

Aristotle kept the insight and made it psychological: the virtues are unified by [[Phronesis]] — practical wisdom is the faculty that sees the mean in each domain, and because that seeing is one skill, a person genuinely practical about courage will also be practical about honesty ([[A047 — Let practical wisdom unify the virtues]]). The apparent counter-cases — the brave coward, the honest man who can't resist temptation — are cases of natural capacity, not virtue: boldness without justice is just force, and clear-sightedness without temperance turns manipulative ([[A037 — Distinguish cleverness (deinotes) from practical wisdom]]).

The practical reading for character work: virtues are trained together or not at all — each practice reinforces the same underlying judgment ([[A040 — Practise the unity of the virtues]]; [[K256 — The unity-of-virtue drill]]). Integrity is the everyday face of the doctrine: consistency between thought, word, and deed is only possible when one faculty is doing the judging ([[A030 — Practice integrity — consistency between thought, word, and deed]]; [[S011 — Recognise the three topoi — the three disciplines]] distributes the same training across Stoic practice).

An example at its smallest: one honest admission made when it costs something trains courage, temperance and honesty in a single act — which is why the compendium rehearses virtues rather than reading about them ([[K250 — Virtue rehearsal]]; [[K255 — Virtue journaling]]).
