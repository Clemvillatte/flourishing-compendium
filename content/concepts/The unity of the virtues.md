The unity of the virtues is the thesis, argued by Socrates in Plato's Protagoras and reworked by Aristotle in Nicomachean Ethics VI, that the virtues come together rather than standing as separate talents. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says the Protagoras "introduces the doctrines of the unity of virtue and the impossibility of acrasia", the idea that it is impossible to know what is right and still do wrong ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/plato-ethics-shorter/)). Socrates' version made virtue a kind of knowledge.

### Aristotle's version

Aristotle keeps the unity and drops the identification with knowledge. In Ross's translation he says Socrates "was wrong" in thinking all the virtues were forms of practical wisdom, but "right" in saying they imply it. What he holds is:

> "It is clear, then, from what has been said, that it is not possible to be good in the strict sense without practical wisdom, nor practically wise without moral virtue." (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.13, Ross translation, [MIT Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.6.vi.html))

He then answers the objection that someone might be naturally equipped for one virtue and not another:

> "This is possible in respect of the natural virtues, but not in respect of those in respect of which a man is called without qualification good; for with the presence of the one quality, practical wisdom, will be given all the virtues." (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.13, Ross translation, [MIT Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.6.vi.html))

So the unity is carried by [[Phronesis]], the one faculty that judges well in every domain. Natural dispositions can come apart. Virtue "in the strict sense" cannot.

### Reading it for practice

The practical consequence is that training the virtues separately has a limit. Courage without justice, or honesty without kindness, is a natural disposition working without the faculty that makes it a virtue. Practising judgment across domains strengthens each of them, and that is the aim of the compendium's drills.

### What it is not

It is not the claim that everyone who is good at one thing is good at everything, or that a person must be perfect to count as virtuous. It says what virtue in the strict sense involves, not how many people reach it.
