The redemptive self is Dan McAdams's name for the life-story pattern his research found among highly generative American adults. In his own retrospective account:

> "Taken together, the five themes I have articulated above—early advantage, suffering of others, moral steadfastness, redemption sequences, and prosocial goals—comprise an overall life-narrative pattern, which I call the redemptive self (McAdams, 2006)."
> — [McAdams 2022, "Narrative Identity and the Redemptive Self: An Intellectual Autobiography, with Occasional Critique", Narrative Works 11(1) (Erudit PDF)](https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/nw/2022-v11-nw09057/1108951ar.pdf)

The stories "often begin with childhood scenes that juxtapose two contrasting themes: (1) I enjoyed an advantage early in life (was lucky, fortunate, chosen for distinction) and (2) I witnessed suffering, pain, oppression, or the like in the lives of people around me, or in the world in general" ([McAdams 2022](https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/nw/2022-v11-nw09057/1108951ar.pdf)). McAdams also describes redemption sequences: "often, good things follow the bad, or else negative events lead to insights about the self, or lead to growth, recovery, advancement, liberation, or some other redemptive meaning in the life narrative" ([McAdams 2022](https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/nw/2022-v11-nw09057/1108951ar.pdf)). The pattern is cultural rather than universal: it is "a story about how to live a good life at midlife in America, a story that many Americans recognize and aspire to", and one that "can squeeze out rivals that might work better for those people whose lives depart from conventional norms, expectations, and timetables" ([McAdams 2022](https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/nw/2022-v11-nw09057/1108951ar.pdf)).
