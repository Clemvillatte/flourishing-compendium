Identity — the answer to "who am I": whether a fixed essence (ātman), a construction of aggregates (anattā), a narrative (Ricoeur), or a relation ([[Ubuntu|Ubuntu]]). The graph's traditions offer every option.

The word "identity" covers two questions, and the compendium's four options answer different ones. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy separates them clearly, and the sources below follow that split.

### Two questions

> "Outside of philosophy, the term 'personal identity' commonly refers to properties to which we feel a special sense of attachment or ownership." (Olson, [Personal Identity, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/identity-personal/), section 1)

Olson calls the question of what determines this sense of self the characterization question. The persistence question is different: "What does it take for a person to persist from one time to another—to continue existing rather than cease to exist?" He adds that an account of it is "an account of our persistence conditions". Answers include psychological-continuity views, animalism and the bundle view.

### Narrative (Ricoeur)

Narrative identity addresses the question "who?" The Stanford Encyclopedia entry on Ricoeur explains that personal identity "can be considered in terms of a narrative identity: what story does a person tell about his or her life, or what story do others tell about it?" It describes his distinction between two kinds of identity: "Idem identity is the identity of something that is always the same which never changes, ipse identity is sameness across and through change." ([Pellauer & Dauenhauer, Paul Ricoeur, section 5](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ricoeur/))

### No fixed self (Buddhist)

The Stanford Encyclopedia's account of the Buddha says that the teaching of non-self (anātman) names the conceit "that there is an 'I' and a 'mine'" as the ignorance behind suffering. The Buddha's argument from impermanence runs: if there were a self it would be permanent, none of the five psychophysical elements is permanent, so there is no self. It notes a disagreement in scholarship: some interpreters hold that the Buddha leaves open a non-empirical self, while classical Indian philosophers understood him to have denied the self altogether. ([Siderits, Buddha, sections 1-3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/buddha/)) The same entry describes the person as "conventionally true", a useful fiction that causes existential suffering "past a certain point—namely the point at which we take it too seriously".

### Relational (Ubuntu)

Ubuntu itself was not opened for this page. The Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on African ethics, which does not use the word, quotes Menkiti on a related idea: personhood "is the sort of thing which has to be attained, and is attained in direct proportion as one participates in communal life". ([Gyekye, African Ethics, section 4](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/african-ethics/)) This supports a relational view of personhood in African thought, but it should not be treated as a full account of Ubuntu.

### What this page does not claim

These four answers are not rivals on one question. The Ricoeur and Ubuntu readings speak mainly to the characterization question, and the Buddhist non-self argument is aimed at the persistence question and at the sense of an owning "I". The seed's claim that the graph's traditions offer "every option" is a statement about the compendium, not a survey of the field.
