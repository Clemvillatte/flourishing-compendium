Mental simulation research is the line of work showing that imagining a goal helps only when you imagine the steps rather than the trophy. The clearest experiment is Pham and Taylor's 1999 study, in which college freshmen mentally simulated, for 5 to 7 days before a midterm, either the process for doing well on the exam (good study habits) or a desired outcome (getting a good grade) or both. The abstract:

> Results indicated that process simulation enhanced studying and improved grades; the latter effect was mediated by enhanced planning and reduced anxiety.

(Pham & Taylor, "From Thought to Action: Effects of Process- Versus Outcome-Based Mental Simulations on Performance," PSPB 25(2), 1999, [abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167299025002010))

The theoretical distinction the paper draws is between simulations of desired goals (outcome simulation) and simulation of the steps leading to a desired goal (process simulation). Outcome simulation gives you the feeling; process simulation gives you the schedule.

The compendium's [[P039 — Deliberate by rehearsal, not by verdict]] runs on this finding, and [[K275 — Morning premeditation]] is the practice kernel it supports: rehearse the morning's possible encounters as steps, not as triumphs.
