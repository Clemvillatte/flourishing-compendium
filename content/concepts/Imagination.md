"Imagination"

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy opens its entry with a definition that turns on what imagination is not:

> "To imagine is to represent without aiming at things as they actually, presently, and subjectively are. One can use imagination to represent possibilities other than the actual, to represent times other than the present, and to represent perspectives other than one's own." ([Munro, Liao and Gendler, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/imagination/))

### Kinds of imagining

The entry sorts the uses of the word into families. Neil Van Leeuwen's influential distinction separates attitude imagining, regarding some content as fictional rather than believing it true, from imagistic imagining, representing something in mental imagery. Walton separates spontaneous from deliberate imagining; Kind and Kung distinguish transcendent uses, which let one look beyond the world as it is, from instructive uses, which teach one about that world.

### Why it matters here

Imagination is the faculty through which the future gets represented before it arrives. The entry notes the psychological work on "mental time travel," the similarities between remembering the past and imagining the future, and the everyday use of imagistic imagination in practical deliberation. The compendium's psychological entry [[Episodic future thinking]] follows that line. The philosophical principles carry the older warnings: [[SP014 — Expect to imagine others as like yourself]] marks the projection error built into perspective-taking, and [[SP038 — Treat superstition as fear's theology]] marks what happens when fear scripts the scenes.
