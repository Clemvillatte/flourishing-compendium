The noticing-wandering-returning cycle is the repeating sequence of events inside a session of breath-focused meditation: attention rests on the breath, drifts into mind wandering, is caught by a moment of noticing, and is deliberately returned. The cycle itself trains the attentional return mechanism (Hasenkamp et al., 2012) — my framing; the study behind it measured the cycle's brain signatures, not training outcomes. The laboratory study that gave the cycle its four intervals put it this way:

> "This model proposes four intervals in a cognitive cycle: mind wandering, awareness of mind wandering, shifting of attention, and sustained attention." (Hasenkamp, Wilson-Mendenhall, Duncan & Barsalou, 2012, [NeuroImage, 59(1), 750–760](https://barsaloulab.org/Online_Articles/2012-Hasenkamp_et_al-NeuroImage-meditation_time_course.pdf), abstract)

### The cycle in the authors' words

The paper describes the loop from the practitioner's side: "When attempting to sustain focus on an object, such as the breath, an individual inevitably experiences mind wandering. At some time during mind wandering, the practitioner becomes aware that his/her mind is not on the object, at which point he/she disengages from the current train of thought and shifts attention back to the object, where it stays focused again for some period of time." The four states were labelled MW, AWARE, SHIFT and FOCUS, and the authors add: "The subjective experience of these states is a cyclical process that iterates repeatedly throughout a session of FA meditation. Thus, the practice of FA meditation is not a single cognitive state, except perhaps in very advanced practitioners." The instruction the cycle comes from is the classic one, which Hasenkamp and colleagues cite from the Buddhist tradition's meditation manuals: "During FA practice, an individual attempts to maintain focus on a single object (e.g., the breath), bringing attention back to the object whenever the mind wanders (Gunaratana, 2002; Wallace, 2006)."

### What the scan showed

Fourteen meditation practitioners performed twenty minutes of breath-focused meditation in an fMRI scanner, pressing a button whenever they realised the mind had wandered. Wandering was frequent even here: "the average number of button presses during the meditation task was 15.5 (s.d. 7.4), representing an average of one reported mind wandering event every 80 seconds over the 20-minute meditation session" (Behavioral Data, 3.1). The authors reported different patterns of activity across the intervals: "Analyses revealed activity in brain regions associated with the default mode during mind wandering, and in salience network regions during awareness of mind wandering. Elements of the executive network were active during shifting and sustained attention. Furthermore, activations during these cognitive phases were modulated by lifetime meditation experience" (abstract). The frequency of wandering itself did not track experience: "The number of button presses did not correlate significantly with practice time (r = -0.14, p = 0.64)".

### Why the return is the skill

The trained move is not the absence of wandering but the noticing and the return. The paper's background, citing Lutz and colleagues (2008), frames the practice the same way: "The practice of focused attention (FA) meditation is intended to help the practitioner enhance awareness of his/her cognitive states while developing attentional control." What the study does not show: that a faster return causes improved well-being. The outcomes measured were brain activity and button presses, not training gains.
