First impressions bias is the tendency to let an early, quick judgment of a person set the course of everything we later learn about them. Two strands of research stand behind it, and they are separate findings: how fast an impression forms, and how much weight the first information carries.

### How fast: a hundred milliseconds

Willis and Todorov showed participants unfamiliar faces for different lengths of time. In their words, "Judgments made after a 100-ms exposure correlated highly with judgments made in the absence of time constraints, suggesting that this exposure time was sufficient for participants to form an impression." More time did not raise the correlations for attractiveness, likeability, trustworthiness, competence or aggressiveness. It did raise confidence, which led them to suggest that "additional time may simply boost confidence in judgments" ([Willis & Todorov, 2006, Psychological Science 17(7): 592-598](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16866745/)). The abstract measures consistency between quick and unhurried judgments, not whether the judgments are accurate, so it says nothing about whether the impressions are right.

### How heavy: the order of information

Asch (1946) read two lists to separate groups. The lists "are identical with regard to their members, differing only in the order of succession". One began with intelligent and industrious and ended with envious; the other ran in reverse. The first produced an impression of an able person with some shortcomings, while the second "impresses the majority as a 'problem,' whose abilities are hampered by his serious difficulties." Some of the same qualities were read differently across conditions: impulsiveness and criticalness were interpreted in a positive way in the first and took on a negative colour in the second. Asch's own explanation: "the first terms set up in most subjects a direction which then exerts a continuous effect on the latter terms" ([Asch, 1946, Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology 41: 258-290, hosted copy](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/personality/1946-asch.pdf)).

The effect was seen in "a majority" of cases, and Asch notes it is "not observed in all subjects." The experiment used a short spoken list of traits and a small number of participants, so it supports the idea of a primacy effect without giving a size.

### Why this matters for the wiki's themes

The research is about perception of others, not about the self or about suffering as such. It matters for essays on wisdom because a quick judgment tends to feel like knowledge. Together the studies show how quickly an impression forms and how an early one can colour what follows.
