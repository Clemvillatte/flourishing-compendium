Positive attention training is a family of laboratory methods that try to shift where a person's attention goes, toward positive or rewarding information and away from negative information, and then see whether emotional responses change. The seed cites Wadlinger and Isaacowitz (2011) as the main review. Its abstract states the case and its limit together:

> "We argue that attention may be an invaluable tool for promoting emotion regulation. Accordingly, we present evidence that selective attention to positive information reflects emotion regulation, and that regulating attention is a critical component of the emotion regulatory process." (Wadlinger and Isaacowitz, Personality and Social Psychology Review 15(1):75-102, [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2970710/))

The abstract adds that "attentional regulation can be successfully trained through repeated practice," and that "additional interdisciplinary research is needed to bolster these nascent findings" ([Wadlinger and Isaacowitz](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2970710/)).

### Two studies the review describes

In Wadlinger and Isaacowitz's own 2008 experiment (N = 55), participants practised a dot-probe task in which the target consistently appeared where a positive word, or for the control group a neutral word, had been. Afterwards, those trained toward positive information "looked significantly less at the negative images" in a stress task, measured by eye tracking, and the neutral group did not ([Wadlinger and Isaacowitz, 2008, Emotion 8(1):121-126](http://www.gruberpeplab.com/teaching/psych231_fall2013/documents/231_WadlingerIsaacowitz2008.pdf)).

The review also summarises Johnson (2009). Undergraduates who were told to keep their attention on happy faces in a dot-probe task showed "almost three times significantly lower state frustration scores" after a second anagram stress task than a no-goal group. Those better at attending to happy faces persisted longer on the stress task ([Wadlinger and Isaacowitz](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2970710/)). I read this study only through the review, not in the original.

### What the evidence does not show

The seed sentence says this training improves mood and reduces depressive symptoms. The sources opened do not support that wording. The two studies above used student volunteers and measured gaze or momentary frustration, not clinical depression. The review itself says "what remains to be seen is whether these skills transfer to more general tasks and how long the effects last." It also notes that positive stimuli tend to be lower in arousal than negative ones, so people with depression or anxiety "may have difficulty in orienting their attention towards this type of positive information" ([Wadlinger and Isaacowitz](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2970710/)).

A separate case-series study of six variants of attention bias modification for depression, with 30 students with mild to moderate depressive symptoms over four sessions, reported that "none of the investigated versions of ABM had a consistent effect on attentional bias" and concluded that "it is unlikely that any of these ABM versions will have a specific effect on symptoms in controlled studies" ([Kruijt, Putman and Van der Does, 2013, ISRN Psychiatry](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3658414/)). The review is more optimistic about meditation, which it says appears "to be among the most effective training methodologies in enhancing emotional well-being" ([Wadlinger and Isaacowitz](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2970710/)).

### In the compendium

AT021, AT051, AF063 and the other principles listed ask the reader to turn attention toward what is good. The laboratory work is a narrower claim: brief training can shift gaze and momentary reactions in healthy volunteers. Whether a daily practice of this kind lowers depression is not established by these sources.
