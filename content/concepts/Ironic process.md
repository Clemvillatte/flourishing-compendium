Wegner's ironic-process account describes how attempts at thought suppression can backfire ([Najmi and Wegner, Hidden Complications of Thought Suppression](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037c-bc4b-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content)). It distinguishes an effortful operating process that redirects attention from a monitoring process that watches for the unwanted thought:

> "The theory holds that two processes are involved in suppression: an effortful and conscious operating process that diverts attention away from unwanted thoughts, and an effortless and unconscious ironic monitoring process that both maintains vigilance for occurrences in awareness of the unwanted thought, and triggers further action of the operating process if the unwanted thought appears in awareness. These two processes work hand in hand to ensure that unwanted thoughts remain outside of awareness. Ironically, however, by maintaining vigilance for the unwanted thought, the monitoring system helps assure that the unwanted thought never becomes dormant." (Najmi and Wegner, [Hidden Complications of Thought Suppression, Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037c-bc4b-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content))

The white bear experiments found the rebound effect:

> "In the original thought suppression experiments, Wegner et al. (1987) found that after a period of thought suppression, people instructed to stop suppressing the thought and instead to begin thinking about it reported more intrusions of the thought than occurred without prior suppression." (Najmi and Wegner, [Hidden Complications of Thought Suppression, Harvard DASH](https://dash.harvard.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/7312037c-bc4b-6bd4-e053-0100007fdf3b/content))
