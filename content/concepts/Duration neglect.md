Duration neglect is the finding that how long an experience lasted has surprisingly little effect on how people rate it afterwards. Memory seems to keep a few moments and drop the length.

In the best-known demonstration, Fredrickson and [[T022 — Kahneman, Daniel|Kahneman]] (1993) had people watch pleasant and aversive film clips of different lengths and intensities, rating their feelings in real time and then rating each clip as a whole. The authors report that "[e]ffects of film duration on retrospective evaluations were small, entirely explained by changes in real-time affect and further reduced when made from memory." They suggest that these evaluations "appear to be determined by a weighted average of 'snapshots' of the actual affective experience, as if duration did not matter" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8355141/)).

### The cold-water study

Kahneman and colleagues (1993) gave volunteers two painful trials with a hand in 14 °C water. The short trial lasted 60 seconds. In the long trial, the hand stayed in for the same 60 seconds and then 30 seconds more while the water was warmed to 15 °C, still painful but less so for most people. Asked which trial to repeat, "a significant majority chose to repeat the long trial, apparently preferring more pain over less." The authors conclude that duration "plays a small role in retrospective evaluations of aversive experiences", which are "often dominated by the discomfort at the worst and at the final moments of episodes" ([abstract, Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x)).

### Why it matters for suffering

If remembered quality and lived quality can come apart, then a choice made from memory may not track how much someone actually went through. The studies above show the gap in short laboratory episodes; this page does not claim that it holds for months of grief or chronic illness.
