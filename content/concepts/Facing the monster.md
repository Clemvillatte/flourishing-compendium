“Facing the monster” — Kierkegaard; Kierkegaard, The Concept of Anxiety.

The monster is what one is drawn toward and repelled by at once. Kierkegaard gives the phenomenon its cleanest description in children's play: the pull toward "the adventurous, the monstrous, and the mysterious" is not a taste for danger but the first stirring of freedom, feeling its own possibilities.

> "The anxiety that is posited in innocence is in the first place no guilt [...]. In observing children, one will the discover this anxiety more particularly as a seeking for the adventurous, the monstrous, and the enigmatic [...]. He who becomes guilty through anxiety is indeed innocent, for it was not he himself but anxiety, a foreign power, that laid hold of him [...]. And yet he is guilty, for he sank in anxiety [...]. There is nothing in the world more ambiguous [...]. That anxiety makes its appearance is the pivot upon which everything turns." — [Kierkegaard, The Concept of Anxiety (Thomte trans.), quoted in Lundsgaard-Leth, trópos VIII(2) (2015)](https://ojs.unito.it/index.php/tropos/article/download/7983/6709/)

### Why one goes toward it

Kierkegaard's "sympathetic antipathy and antipathetic sympathy" names the double movement: the thing is wanted and not wanted in the same glance. The ambiguity he marks — innocent, because a foreign power laid hold of one; guilty, because one sank — is the reason the facing cannot be skipped. What is only fled stays in charge of the fear; what is faced becomes available for use. The exposure-therapy parallels in the seed links work from the same premise, arrived at clinically: approach what you fear, and the fear completes itself instead of renewing itself.
