“Facing the monster” — Kierkegaard; Kierkegaard, The Concept of Anxiety.

The monster is what one is drawn toward and repelled by at once. Kierkegaard gives the phenomenon its description in children's play: a seeking that shows up as a pull toward "the adventurous, the monstrous, and the enigmatic."

> "The anxiety that is posited in innocence is in the first place no guilt [...]. In observing children, one will the discover this anxiety more particularly as a seeking for the adventurous [Eventyrlige], the monstrous [Uhyre], and the enigmatic [...]. He who becomes guilty through anxiety is indeed innocent, for it was not he himself but anxiety, a foreign power, that laid hold of him [...]. And yet he is guilty, for he sank in anxiety [...]. There is nothing in the world more ambiguous [...]. That anxiety makes its appearance is the pivot upon which everything turns." — Kierkegaard, The Concept of Anxiety (Thomte trans.), as quoted in [Lundsgaard-Leth, trópos VIII(2) (2015), 162–163](https://ojs.unito.it/index.php/tropos/article/download/7983/6709/)

### Why one goes toward it

The passage's double movement, a seeking that is also a fear, is what the page title calls facing the monster. The ambiguity Kierkegaard marks, innocent because a foreign power laid hold of one and guilty because one sank, is why the facing cannot be skipped. What is only fled keeps its charge. The original entry's links place the idea beside the clinical principle of approaching what one fears.
