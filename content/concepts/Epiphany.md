The word epiphany does not appear in William James's The Varieties of Religious Experience. The book's nearest discussion is of conversion, and Lectures IX and X are the place to read how James treats a sudden inner change. He defines the topic broadly: "To be converted, to be regenerated, to receive grace, to experience religion, to gain an assurance, are so many phrases which denote the process, gradual or sudden, by which a self hitherto divided, and consciously wrong inferior and unhappy, becomes unified and consciously right superior and happy, in consequence of its firmer hold upon religious realities" ([James, Lecture IX](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/621/pg621.txt)).

### Two types

Following Starbuck, James describes two ways such change happens: "a conscious and voluntary way and an involuntary and unconscious way", giving "the volitional type" and "the type by self-surrender". In the volitional type, "the regenerative change is usually gradual, and consists in the building up, piece by piece, of a new set of moral and spiritual habits. But there are always critical points here at which the movement forward seems much more rapid" ([Lecture IX](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/621/pg621.txt)). James quotes Starbuck's comparison with an athlete who "sometimes awakens suddenly to an understanding of the fine points of the game" and a musician who "may suddenly reach a point at which pleasure in the technique of the art entirely falls away".

### A natural process

James asks whether instantaneous conversion is a miracle, or whether "the whole phenomenon of regeneration, even in these startling instantaneous examples, possibly" is "a strictly natural process". He explains the shifts partly by "explicitly conscious processes of thought and will, but as due largely also to the subconscious incubation and maturing of motives deposited by the experiences of life. When ripe, the results hatch out, or burst into flower" ([Lecture X](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/621/pg621.txt)). Whatever one's religious view, he writes, "we should still have to write down man's liability to sudden and complete conversion as one of his most curious peculiarities."
