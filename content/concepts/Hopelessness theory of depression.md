The hopelessness theory of depression was set out by Lyn Abramson, Gerald Metalsky and Lauren Alloy in "Hopelessness depression: A theory-based subtype of depression" (Psychological Review 96(2), 358-372, 1989) ([PhilPapers record](https://philpapers.org/rec/ABRHDA)). A 2015 review summarises the causal chain: "the hopelessness theory predicts that the interaction between negative cognitive styles and negative life events engenders a sense of hopelessness. This hopelessness, in turn, was hypothesized to be sufficient by itself to bring about depression." ([Liu, Kleiman, Nestor and Cheek, Clinical Psychology: Science and Practice, 2015, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4689589/))

### Three inferential tendencies

The review describes negative inferential styles as three tendencies in response to a negative event: "(1) inferring stable and global causes (rather than unstable and specific causes) for the event, (2) inferring negative consequences of the event, and (3) inferring negative self-characteristics." These styles are diatheses, meaning vulnerabilities: they "by themselves should not be associated with increased likelihood for developing hopelessness and depression". Risk rises "only in the presence of negative life events". The review calls the hopelessness theory "a diathesis-stress model of depression", in which negative events act as "occasion setters" (same review).

### A proposed subtype

The theory also proposes "a distinct cognitively mediated subtype of depression, hopelessness depression". The review notes that one version of the proposed criteria requires at least two weeks of hopelessness with at least five of 11 symptoms, and that the symptom profile "has been revised since the initial formulation".

### What the evidence shows

The review finds "general support" for the link between negative inferential styles and depression through an interaction with negative life events. It calls the diathesis-stress interaction "the aspect of the hopelessness theory that has received the most empirical support thus far". The gaps are also stated:

- Most work concerns "generally mild depressive symptoms in non-clinical samples".
- Support for hopelessness as a mediator is "mixed", and the subtype is described as "perhaps the one part of the theory for which the evidence base is currently most tentative".
- "The most central aspect of the hopelessness theory remains largely unexamined": whether the style-by-event interaction predicts new depressive episodes over and above past depression and gender.
- Nearly all studies measured negative inferential styles by self-report.

A separate set of four studies found that negative attributional style "was more related" to hopelessness depression symptoms than to endogenous depression symptoms, "consistent with hopelessness theory" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11153969/)).

### Caution for the essays

The theory describes a risk pathway in a research model, not a diagnosis a reader can apply to themselves. The review also warns of "frequent conceptual confusion" between this theory and the earlier reformulated learned helplessness theory.
