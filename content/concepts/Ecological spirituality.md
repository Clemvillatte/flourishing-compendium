Ecological spirituality here is the compendium's name for a family of ideas in which spiritual life is lived in relation to the living Earth. It cites two authors, Thomas Berry and Gary Snyder, whose approaches differ. The Oxford Bibliographies entry on religion and ecology introduces Berry as a "cultural historian and 'geologian'" who imagines cosmic evolution as a "new story" in which "humanity becomes conscious of itself as a disruptive ecological force and works to transform itself into a benign presence on the Earth". In The Great Work (1999) he identifies this transition as the "Great Work" of our time ([Oxford Bibliographies: Religion and Ecology](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62344/reference-article/554375653)).

### Berry: the Great Work

A chapter on Berry and the cosmic common good quotes him: "The Great Work now ... is to carry out the transition from a period of human devastation of the Earth to a period when humans would be present to the planet in a mutually beneficial manner." The chapter adds that Berry sees every creature as having a threefold nature, which it calls differentiation, subjectivity and communion ([Oxford chapter on Thomas Berry](https://academic.oup.com/book/10369/chapter/158156781)). The same chapter treats wildness, "that which is uncontrolled by human dominance", as important to this account.

### Snyder: practising the wild

An Oxford reference entry on Snyder says that, unlike the transcendentalists who "perceived nature as symbolic and looked through it to find spiritual truths", Snyder "emphasizes how the spiritual takes shape within the real, physical, and imaginative interactions between elements that make up the ecology of one's place." It describes The Practice of the Wild (1990) as "more than a simple environmental treatise", exploring "the ethical and imaginative dimensions of wilderness", and notes that Snyder "borrows frequently from the thirteenth-century Buddhist philosopher Dogen" ([Oxford entry on Gary Snyder](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61883/chapter/547862884)).

### Scope

Berry writes from a Christian and cosmological standpoint and Snyder from Buddhist and place-based practice, so the shared label hides real differences. This page relies on scholarly summaries, and neither The Great Work nor The Practice of the Wild was read in full. The summaries describe views and do not test whether the practices improve wellbeing.
