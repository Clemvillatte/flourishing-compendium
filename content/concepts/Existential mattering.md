Existential mattering is the sense that your life counts in the larger scheme of things. George and Park (2016) treat it as one of three components of meaning in life, alongside comprehension and purpose. In the wording of [Ostafin, Papenfuss and Vervaeke (2022)](<https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10615806.2021.1994556>), quoting George and Park (2016, p. 206), mattering "consists of individuals feeling that 'their existence is of significance, importance, and value to the world.'"

### The three parts

The same article summarises the framework this way: researchers "have started to converge on a conceptualization of life meaning as involving both an overall sense of whether life is meaningful and three components that contribute to a sense of a meaningful life, including comprehension (coherence), purpose, and mattering (significance)."

Using George and Park's terms, comprehension means feeling "that one's life makes sense, things seem clear in one's life, things in one's life fit together well, and things are as they ought to be". Purpose means being "motivated by valued life goals" and feeling "pulled and directed toward their goals".

### How it is measured

George and Park's Multidimensional Existential Meaning Scale has 15 items, and Ostafin and colleagues use it with a subscale for each part. The mattering example item is "Even considering how big the universe is, I can say that my life matters" ([Ostafin et al., 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10615806.2021.1994556)).

### Scope

The page relies on a secondary account of George and Park's wording. Their original article was not available for checking, and the Ostafin study uses the framework for a different question, namely how meaning relates to fear of the unknown. It does not test existential mattering as a route to flourishing.
