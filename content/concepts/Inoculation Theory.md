Inoculation theory, originated by William McGuire in the 1960s, proposes that people can be "psychologically 'vaccinated' against persuasive attacks", just as they can be immunised against viral contagions ([Traberg, Roozenbeek and van der Linden, 2022, Annals of the American Academy of Political and Social Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/00027162221087936)). McGuire and Papageorgis reasoned that exposing people to "a weakened dose of an impending persuasive attack (and preemptively refut[ing] that attack)" should work better than the usual approach of bolstering an attitude "with more supportive facts", which they likened to a "healthy diet" (same paper).

### The original experiments

A Science news feature recounts McGuire's test. He used four cultural truisms, such as the belief that one should brush one's teeth after every meal. Confronting students with counterarguments could "drastically decrease" belief in a truism. But "if the students first read an essay that laid out the counterarguments and refuted them, their belief didn't erode nearly as much". McGuire argued that people raised in an "aseptic" belief environment are vulnerable when "suddenly exposed to a massive dose" of attack ([Science news feature](https://www.science.org/content/article/can-people-be-inoculated-against-misinformation)).

### Two components

An inoculation message has two parts. The threat component makes people aware that a persuasive attack is imminent. Refutational preemption, also called prebunking, gives people "tools or arguments to refute future persuasion attempts" (Traberg et al.). A report in Science Advances states the same two parts as "a forewarning that induces a perceived threat of an impending attack on one's attitudes" and exposure to a weakened dose "that contains a preemptive refutation" ([Roozenbeek et al., 2022, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9401631/)).

### Evidence

A review cites a meta-analysis by Banas and Rains (2010) finding that inoculations "are generally effective at increasing attitudinal resistance against unwanted persuasion, with a mean effect size of d = 0.43 (considered a moderate effect size)" ([Countering Misinformation: Evidence, Knowledge Gaps..., OSF PDF](https://files.osf.io/v1/resources/b52um/providers/osfstorage/63297bd56c2401097a50924a?action=download&direct&version=4)). In seven preregistered studies (six randomised controlled studies with 6,464 participants and a YouTube field study with 22,632), short videos "improve manipulation technique recognition", "boost confidence in spotting these techniques", and "improve the quality of their sharing decisions" (Roozenbeek et al., 2022). Traberg and colleagues add that inoculation can give a "blanket of protection": protection extends to related attitudes that were not treated.

### Cautions

Most of the newer evidence concerns misinformation, not everyday persuasion. The Science feature notes that McGuire believed inoculation only worked if people's beliefs had never been challenged before. The trials above are short-term online studies; the sources I opened do not report long-run effects.
