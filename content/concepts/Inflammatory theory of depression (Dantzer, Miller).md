The theory's two anchor papers state the chain. Robert Dantzer and colleagues described how the immune system talks to the brain:

> "In response to a peripheral infection, innate immune cells produce pro-inflammatory cytokines that act on the brain to cause sickness behaviour. When activation of the peripheral immune system continues unabated, such as during systemic infections, cancer or autoimmune diseases, the ensuing immune signalling to the brain can lead to an exacerbation of sickness and the development of symptoms of depression in vulnerable individuals." (Dantzer, O'Connor, Freund, Johnson and Kelley, [From inflammation to sickness and depression: when the immune system subjugates the brain, Nature Reviews Neuroscience 9 (2008), pp. 46-56, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18073775/))

### The evolutionary turn

Andrew Miller and Charles Raison's later review carries the theory to its modern form:

> "However, in modern times, such interactions between inflammation and the brain appear to drive the development of depression and may contribute to non-responsiveness to current antidepressant therapies." (Miller and Raison, [The role of inflammation in depression: from evolutionary imperative to modern treatment target, Nature Reviews Immunology 16 (2016), pp. 22-34, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26711676/))

Their abstract frames the same crosstalk as behaviour that once had survival value, avoidance and alarm in the presence of pathogens and predators, and they detail the mechanisms by which the innate and adaptive immune systems interact with neurotransmitters and neurocircuits to influence the risk for depression ([Miller and Raison, 2016, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26711676/)). Dantzer's line already carried the vulnerability point: the development of depressive symptoms happens in vulnerable individuals, and inflammation might increase the risk of major depressive episodes much like the more traditional psychosocial factors ([Dantzer et al., 2008, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18073775/)).
