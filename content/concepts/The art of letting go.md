“The art of letting go” — Buddhism.

The art of letting go is the Buddhist practice of releasing what has served its purpose: possessions, roles, goals, views, even the teachings themselves once they have done their work. Its root image is the Buddha's raft simile. A man builds a raft of grass and twigs to cross a dangerous river; once across, he would be a fool to hoist it on his back and carry it everywhere. The raft deserves gratitude, not ownership. Letting go, in this picture, is not rejection; it is finishing a relationship with a thing at the right time.

> "In the same way, monks, I have taught the Dhamma compared to a raft, for the purpose of crossing over, not for the purpose of holding onto. Understanding the Dhamma as taught compared to a raft, you should let go even of Dhammas, to say nothing of non-Dhammas." (MN 22, Alagaddupama Sutta, [Thanissaro Bhikkhu translation, AccessToInsight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.022.than.html))

### What the practice trains

The exercise is to hold things the way the traveler holds the raft: fully, while crossing. Modern goal research describes the same skill from the outside: people who can disengage from unattainable goals and re-engage with new ones fare better than people who cling, on measures of wellbeing and health. The compendium's principle MO058 carries that consequence. What the simile adds is the inner stance: gratitude for what carried you, and no complaint at leaving it on the shore.
