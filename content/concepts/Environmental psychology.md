Environmental psychology studies how physical settings shape behavior and well-being: how places are perceived and become objects of attachment, and what exposure to natural settings does for attention and mood. A sample of its experimental findings is Bratman and colleagues' PNAS study:

> We show in healthy participants that a brief nature experience, a 90-min walk in a natural setting, decreases both self-reported rumination and neural activity in the subgenual prefrontal cortex (sgPFC), whereas a 90-min walk in an urban setting has no such effects on self-reported rumination or neural activity.
>
> (Bratman, Hamilton, Hahn, Daily and Gross, "Nature experience reduces rumination and subgenual prefrontal cortex activation", PNAS 112(28), 2015, [pnas.org](https://www.pnas.org/doi/abs/10.1073/pnas.1510459112))

The study targeted rumination, which the abstract describes as "a maladaptive pattern of self-referential thought that is associated with heightened risk for depression and other mental illnesses." The authors write that the study "reveals a pathway by which nature experience may improve mental well-being" and that it suggests "that accessible natural areas within urban contexts may be a critical resource for mental health in our rapidly urbanizing world" (Bratman et al. 2015).
