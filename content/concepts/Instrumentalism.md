“Instrumentalism” — Dewey, The Quest for Certainty.

Dewey's instrumentalism holds that ideas are instruments of action, and that their worth is decided by what they do. The thesis in The Quest for Certainty:

> "The test of ideas, of thinking generally, is found in the consequences of the acts to which the ideas lead, that is, in the new arrangements of things which are brought into existence." ([Dewey, The Quest for Certainty, ch. V](https://archive.org/stream/questforcertaint029410mbp/questforcertaint029410mbp_djvu.txt))

### Ideas at work

The chapter grounds the doctrine in operations. Thought, he writes, can begin a plan, "but it takes actual operations to which the plan, as the fruit of thought, gives instrumental guidance to make a building out of separate bricks, or to transform an isolated sensory quality into a significant clue to knowledge of nature." Our conceptions and ideas "are designations of operations to be performed or already performed," and their value "is determined by the outcome of these operations." The polemical edge is aimed at spectator theories of knowledge: "If we see that knowing is not the act of an outside spectator but of a participant inside the natural and social scene, then the true object of knowledge resides in the consequences of directed action" (ch. VIII).

### What it rules out

An idea that never passes into action has, intellectually, nothing to its credit; the value of ideas is determined, on this account, by the operations to which they lead. My principles translate the doctrine: an idea's meaning is its practical effects, ideas are tools rather than mirrors of reality, and concepts that no longer earn their keep should be retired.
