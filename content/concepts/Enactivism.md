Enactivism is the name for a family of views in cognitive science that treat cognition as something a living body does in its environment, rather than something a brain does with internal representations of a pre-given world. The term comes from The Embodied Mind (1991) by Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson and Eleanor Rosch, whose summary of the program reads:

> "In a nutshell, the enactive approach consists of two points: (1) perception consists in perceptually guided action and (2) cognitive structures emerge from the recurrent sensorimotor patterns that enable action to be perceptually guided." ([Varela, Thompson and Rosch, 1991, p. 173](https://monoskop.org/images/2/21/Varela_Thompson_Rosch_The_Embodied_Mind_Cognitive_Science_and_Human_Experience_1991.pdf))

### Origin

The Embodied Mind was published by MIT Press in 1991, written by a neuroscientist, a philosopher and a psychologist, and it crossed cognitive science with European phenomenology and Buddhist philosophy. Its target was the representational core of cognitivism, the assumption that cognition consists in problem solving on the basis of representations ([Varela et al., 1991, ch. 9](https://monoskop.org/images/2/21/Varela_Thompson_Rosch_The_Embodied_Mind_Cognitive_Science_and_Human_Experience_1991.pdf)). The Stanford Encyclopedia now presents enactivism as the view that "cognition emerges from or is constituted by sensorimotor activity," with three strands currently distinguished: autopoietic enactivism, sensorimotor enactivism and radical enactivism ([SEP, Embodied Cognition](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/Win2023/entries/embodied-cognition/)).

### Core claims

On what "embodied" adds:

> "By using the term embodied we mean to highlight two points: first, that cognition depends upon the kinds of experience that come from having a body with various sensorimotor capacities, and second, that these individual sensorimotor capacities are themselves embedded in a more encompassing biological, psychological, and cultural context." ([Varela, Thompson and Rosch, 1991, p. 173](https://monoskop.org/images/2/21/Varela_Thompson_Rosch_The_Embodied_Mind_Cognitive_Science_and_Human_Experience_1991.pdf))

On what enaction is:

> "Cognition in its most encompassing sense consists in the enactment or bringing forth of a world by a viable history of structural coupling." ([Varela, Thompson and Rosch, 1991, p. 205](https://monoskop.org/images/2/21/Varela_Thompson_Rosch_The_Embodied_Mind_Cognitive_Science_and_Human_Experience_1991.pdf))

The book closes its evolutionary chapter with an image from the path: cognitive capacities are linked to histories that are lived, "much like paths that exist only as they are laid down in walking" (p. 205).

### How it explains

The theory is organismic rather than computational in form. Where cognitivism explains cognition by operations on internal symbols, enactivism explains it by the coupling between an organism and its world: perception is an activity the whole animal performs, and the world it meets is shaped by that history of interaction. The strands disagree among themselves; the Stanford Encyclopedia notes that sensorimotor enactivism's appeal to mediating know-how is seen by radical enactivists as a brand of cognitivism ([SEP, Embodied Cognition](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/Win2023/entries/embodied-cognition/)).

### In everyday life

Illustrative scenario: a person reaches for a cup while looking elsewhere and their hand adjusts mid-course to the handle's position. The enactive reading is that perceiving the cup is partly knowing what the visual field will do as the head and hand move. The scenario illustrates perceptually guided action; it is not a reported experiment.
