threats to freedom motivate their restoration (Brehm, 1966).

The theory posits that people hold specific behavioral freedoms: "freedoms are beliefs individuals have about the ways in which they may act", and "anything that makes exercising a freedom more difficult represents a threat to freedom" ([Reynolds-Tylus, 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2019.00056/full)). When a freedom is threatened or eliminated, a motivational state is aroused that provokes individuals to seek to restore the threatened or lost freedom, and the magnitude of reactance aroused is hypothesized to be positively correlated with the importance of the threatened freedom. The compendium's linked principles work both directions of the effect: notice the push-back hand in yourself, and don't press others to the point of rebellion.

The state shows itself in behaviour. Direct restoration may include "directly engaging in an admonished behavior (e.g., consuming alcohol after exposure to an anti-drinking message), or by resisting an advocated behavior (e.g., refusing a flu vaccination after exposure to a pro-flu vaccination message)"; this is the boomerang effect, "perhaps the most intuitive manifestation of freedom restoration" ([Reynolds-Tylus, 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2019.00056/full)). Indirect routes include increasing one's liking of the threatened choice, derogating the source of the threat, denying the threat, or "exercising a related freedom to regain feelings of control and choice". Reactance is operationalized as "an amalgam of anger and negative cognitions".

The arousal is also manageable from the message side. "The use of more implicit, autonomy supporting, and choice-enhancing language has been shown to diminish reactance arousal", and restoration postscripts, brief closing statements that the decision is the recipient's own choice, cut the threat; providing two behavioral options rather than one "resulted in significantly less freedom threat and subsequent reactance" ([Reynolds-Tylus, 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2019.00056/full)).

> "Psychological reactance" is "the motivational state that is hypothesized to occur when a freedom is eliminated or threatened with elimination" (Brehm and Brehm, 1981, p. 37, quoted in [Reynolds-Tylus, 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2019.00056/full)).

> "Reactance is a motivational state that provokes individuals to seek to restore their threatened or lost freedom (Brehm, 1966)." (Reynolds-Tylus, 2019, [Frontiers in Communication](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2019.00056/full))
