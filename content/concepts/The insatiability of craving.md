The insatiability of craving is Schopenhauer's structural claim about desire: wanting does not end with getting, because wanting keeps regenerating itself. In the Haldane-Kemp translation of The World as Will and Idea (the online text preserves some typographic quirks of the scanned edition):

> "All willing arises from want, therefore from deficiency, and therefore from suffering, The satisfaction of a wish ends it; yet for one wish that is satisfied there remain at least ten which are denied." (Schopenhauer, The World as Will and Idea, trans. Haldane and Kemp, [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_World_as_Will_and_Idea/Volume_1/Third_Book))

### No object closes the loop

The passage continues: the desire lasts long, the demands are infinite, the satisfaction short. "But even the final satisfaction is itself only apparent; every satisfied wish at once makes room for a new one; both are illusions; the one is known to be so, the other not yet." The image that follows is Schopenhauer's own: "No attained object of desire can give lasting satisfaction, but merely a fleeting gratification; it is like the alms thrown to the beggar, that keeps him alive to-day that his misery may be prolonged till the morrow."

The passage closes with the mythological images, and a sentence the psychology would later keep finding on its own:

> "Therefore, so long as our consciousness is filled by our will, so long as we are given up to the throng of desires with their constant hopes and fears, so long as we are the subject of willing, we can never have lasting happiness nor peace" (Schopenhauer, The World as Will and Idea, trans. Haldane and Kemp, [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_World_as_Will_and_Idea/Volume_1/Third_Book))

### The Buddhist parallel

Schopenhauer knew the resonance was real, and the Stanford Encyclopedia marks it: "In a manner reminiscent of traditional Buddhism, he recognizes that life is filled with unavoidable frustration and acknowledges that the suffering caused by this frustration can itself be reduced by minimizing one's desires" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Schopenhauer](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/schopenhauer/)). The Buddhist side singles out the same engine: the Buddha "singles out craving (tanha) as the dominant and most pervasive cause, 'the origin of suffering'" (Bhikkhu Bodhi, [The Way to the End of Suffering](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/bodhi/wayToEnd.html)); the second noble truth associates suffering's origin with craving, "literally 'thirst'" ([Britannica, Four Noble Truths](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Four-Noble-Truths)).

### Later echoes in psychology

Two modern findings restate the structure. The hedonic treadmill: "as a person makes more money, expectations and desires rise in tandem, resulting in no permanent gain in happiness" ([Wikipedia, Hedonic treadmill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedonic_treadmill)). And addiction science, where wanting splits from liking: "In human drug addicts, 'wanting' becomes dissociated with 'liking' as the desire to use an addictive drug increases, while the pleasure obtained from consuming it decreases due to drug tolerance" ([Wikipedia, Dopamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dopamine)).
