**Groupthink theory** — Janis — cohesive groups prioritise harmony over realistic appraisal, producing defective decisions.

Groupthink is the term Irving Janis gave in 1972 to the way a tight-knit group can talk itself into a bad decision: the pull of agreement inside the group grows strong enough to crowd out an honest look at the alternatives. The word itself was coined earlier, by William H. Whyte in Fortune magazine in 1952; Janis turned it into a studied theory of decision-making failure, built from cases such as the Bay of Pigs invasion ([Wikipedia, "Groupthink"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groupthink)).

> "I use the term groupthink as a quick and easy way to refer to the mode of thinking that persons engage in when concurrence-seeking becomes so dominant in a cohesive ingroup that it tends to override realistic appraisal of alternative courses of action." (Janis 1972, quoted in [Wikipedia, "Groupthink"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groupthink))

### What the theory claims

Janis drew the term deliberately from Orwell's Newspeak vocabulary, because the connotation is intended: "the term refers to a deterioration in mental efficiency, reality testing and moral judgments as a result of group pressures." His cases were government policy fiascoes, and his remedy was procedural rather than moral: leaders who invite dissent, outside review, and devil's advocacy, so that wanting the group to hold together stops doing the group's thinking for it.

The theory's own standing is mixed: later reviews found the evidence for the full antecedent-symptom-failure chain weaker than Janis claimed, while the core observation, that cohesion plus concurrence-seeking can suppress dissent, remains widely used.
