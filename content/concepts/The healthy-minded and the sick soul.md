“The healthy-minded and the sick soul” — James, The Varieties of Religious Experience.

### What it is

The compendium's title for the pair of temperaments William James sets against each other in Lectures IV-VII of The Varieties of Religious Experience. The healthy-minded temperament is "the temperament which has a constitutional incapacity for prolonged suffering" (James, Lectures VI-VII, [Gutenberg #621](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/621/pg621.txt)). Its counterpart, the sick soul, is treated across those two lectures as the other way a human being can be built.

### What it carries

James frames the pair through a quotation: "'God has two families of children on this earth'... 'the once-born and the twice-born'" (Francis W. Newman, quoted in James, Lectures IV-V, [Gutenberg #621](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/621/pg621.txt)). The happiness motive James starts from is general, not the property of one temperament: "How to gain, how to keep, how to recover happiness, is in fact for most men at all times the secret motive of all they do, and of all they are willing to endure" (James, Lecture IV, [Gutenberg #621](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/621/pg621.txt)). The lectures that follow work out "the contrast between the two ways of looking at life" (James, Lecture VIII, [Gutenberg #621](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/621/pg621.txt)) that these two types embody.

The compendium connects this with P103, which accepts restlessness as the human condition: James's two temperaments give that acceptance a documented shape, since a temperament is not an error one could simply correct. A person reading James can check whether their own experience is being offered as evidence about everyone's, which the two-temperament frame treats as a category mistake rather than an argument to be won.
