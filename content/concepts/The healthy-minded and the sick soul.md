“The healthy-minded and the sick soul” — James, The Varieties of Religious Experience.

### What it is

The compendium's title for the pair of temperaments William James sets against each other in Lectures IV-VII of The Varieties of Religious Experience. The healthy-minded temperament has "a constitutional incapacity for prolonged suffering, and in which the tendency to see things optimistically is like a water of crystallization in which the individual's character is set" (James, Lectures VI-VII, [Gutenberg #621](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/621/pg621.txt)). The sick soul is its counterpart: a temperament that registers the world's evil as a primary fact and cannot be argued out of it.

### What it carries

James frames the pair through a quotation he approves: "God has two families of children on this earth... the once-born and the twice-born" (Francis W. Newman, quoted in James, Lectures IV-V, [Gutenberg #621](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/621/pg621.txt)). The once-born see no gulf to be delivered from; the twice-born pass through division, melancholy and failure to reach a stability that includes what they suffered. James refuses to grade the two from the outside, treating each as a genuine way the human machine can be built. The healthy-minded ask, with the whole hedonistic school, "How to gain, how to keep, how to recover happiness, is in fact for most men at all times the secret motive of all they do, and of all they are willing to endure" (James, Lecture IV, [Gutenberg #621](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/621/pg621.txt)), while the sick soul treats that program as a verdict on the seeker, not the world.

The P103 principle accepts restlessness as the human condition, and James supplies its pedigree: the restlessness is not a defect of the once-born's optimism failing to install, it is the native temperature of half the species. A person who knows which temperament they carry can stop treating their own experience as the measure of everyone's, which is James's practical point: "the contrast between the two ways of looking at life" (James, Lecture VIII, [Gutenberg #621](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/621/pg621.txt)) is a fact about people, not an argument to be won.
