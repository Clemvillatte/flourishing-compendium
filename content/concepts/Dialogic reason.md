Dialogic reason is the idea that understanding and good judgement arise between people in exchange, not inside a single isolated mind. The compendium ties it to two thinkers, Martin Buber and Jürgen Habermas, who approach it in different ways. The two Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries consulted here describe each.

### Buber: the between

The entry on Buber describes I and Thou as "a sustained meditation on the twofold 'primordial words' I-It and I-Thou." I-It is "the root of all subject-object relations, the reduction of the other as it occurs to a shrunken and abstract self." Against it Buber sets the I-Thou relation, in which "the emphasis is on the 'between' of Self and Other." The entry reads this as "emphasizing 'betweenness' and communication over the monological agency of the sovereign self," and says the I-Thou "cannot be reduced to a concept" because it points to an actual encounter ([Zank and Braiterman, SEP: Martin Buber](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/buber/)).

### Habermas: reaching understanding

The entry on Habermas explains that he contrasts communicative action with instrumental and strategic action. In its words, "instrumental and strategic actions are oriented towards success" while "communicative action is oriented towards consensus or reaching understanding/agreement." Speakers, it says, "make validity claims (to truth, rightness and truthfulness)" that their listeners can take up ([Finlayson and Rees, SEP: Jürgen Habermas](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/habermas/)).

The same entry records a dispute. It says "most commentators agree that Habermas fails to establish the irreducibility thesis by argument," meaning the claim that communicative rationality cannot be reduced to instrumental rationality, although some think the distinction is still useful ([SEP: Habermas](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/habermas/)).

### Scope

Buber's dialogue is an encounter between a self and a Thou. Habermas's is a rational procedure of making and testing claims. They share a rejection of the solitary subject, but they are not one theory, and the label "dialogic reason" is the compendium's pairing. Neither book was read in full for this page.
