“The two-factor model of well-being” — Keyes, 2002.

The two-factor model is Corey Keyes' answer to a measurement question with practical stakes: is mental health just the low end of mental illness, or its own thing? Working with confirmatory factor analyses of depression symptoms (CESD subscales) and psychological well-being scales, he found the data fit two correlated but separate latent factors — one for the presence and absence of mental illness, one for the presence and absence of mental health. His summary of what that means:

> In short, mental health is not merely the absence of mental illness; it is not simply the presence of high levels of subjective well-being. Mental health is best viewed as a complete state consisting of the presence and the absence of mental illness and mental health symptoms. (Keyes, "The Mental Health Continuum: From Languishing to Flourishing in Life," Journal of Health and Social Behavior 43(2), 2002, p. 210, [open PDF](https://thefpr.org/wp-content/uploads/Keyes_flourishing.pdf))

### The continuum it supports

On this foundation Keyes built the mental health continuum. Its top category, flourishing, is defined by the criteria he set:

> To be flourishing in life, individuals must exhibit a high level (high = upper tertile) on one of the two measures of emotional well-being and high levels on six of the 11 scales of positive functioning. (ibid., p. 210)

The bottom category, languishing, mirrors the criteria with low levels. The two-factor structure is what lets the model treat "not depressed" and "flourishing" as different achievements — the finding that the risk of a major depressive episode was nearly six times greater among languishing than flourishing adults (ibid., p. 207) shows languishing carries its own risk, not merely the absence of illness. For the compendium's hedonic/eudaimonic distinction (PP001), the model is evidence that well-being has structure: feeling good and functioning well are separable dimensions, and flourishing is defined by both.
