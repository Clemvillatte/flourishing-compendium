Attentional bias research asks whether what attention selects — particularly toward threat-related information — contributes to emotional vulnerability, or merely accompanies it. The field's question is causal, and the study that addressed it directly was run by Colin MacLeod, Elizabeth Rutherford, Lyn Campbell, Greg Ebsworthy and Lin Holker, published in the Journal of Abnormal Psychology in 2002.

> "Although it is well-established that vulnerability to negative emotion is associated with attentional bias toward aversive information, the causal basis of this association remains undetermined. Two studies addressed this issue by experimentally inducing differential attentional responses to emotional stimuli using a modified dot probe task, and then examining the impact of such attentional manipulation on subsequent emotional vulnerability. The results supported the hypothesis that the induction of attentional bias should serve to modify emotional vulnerability, as revealed by participants' emotional reactions to a final standardized stress task. These findings provide a sound empirical basis for the previously speculative proposal that attentional bias can causally mediate emotional vulnerability, and they suggest the possibility that cognitive-experimental procedures designed to modify selective information processing may have potential therapeutic value." ([MacLeod, Rutherford, Campbell, Ebsworthy and Holker, 2002](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11866165/), abstract)

### What the studies did

Rather than measuring attention and anxiety together and inferring a link, the two studies manipulated attention itself. Participants' attentional responses to emotional stimuli were experimentally induced in different directions using a modified dot probe task; the effect on emotional vulnerability was then measured through participants' emotional reactions to a standardized stress task. Because the attentional difference was produced by design, the change in emotional vulnerability could be read as a consequence of it.

### What the field took from it

The abstract's closing sentence points to the applied program this research suggested: procedures "designed to modify selective information processing may have potential therapeutic value." The 2002 finding is the study that made the causal question experimental rather than correlational.
