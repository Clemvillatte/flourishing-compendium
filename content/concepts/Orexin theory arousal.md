Orexins, also called hypocretins, are two neuropeptides "secreted exclusively by a small population of neurons in the lateral hypothalamus"; a review in PMC says evidence has "solidified" that they are "both necessary to maintain and sufficient to induce wakefulness" ([The brain hypocretins and their receptors, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3791851/)). The Nature Reviews Neuroscience abstract describes a neuropeptide system, "centred in the dorsolateral hypothalamus, that regulates arousal states, influences feeding and is implicated in the sleep disorder narcolepsy" ([The hypocretins: Setting the arousal threshold, Nature Reviews Neuroscience](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn808)).

### Stabilising wakefulness

Orexin neurons "are activated during wakefulness, whereas during sleep they are inhibited" and "regulate monoaminergic and cholinergic nuclei in the brain stem to regulate sleep and wakefulness" ([The neural circuit of orexin (hypocretin), Nature Reviews Neuroscience](https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn2092)). An optogenetics review describes the system as an integrator of homeostatic functions with a non-redundant function as an "arousal stabilizer", while noting that studies "cannot conclude whether the activity of Hcrt cells accompanies wakefulness or drives the transition" ([Optogenetic Control of Hypocretin (Orexin) Neurons and Arousal, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5047484/)).

### Motivation

The same Nature review says orexin neurons link to the arcuate nucleus (feeding) and the dopaminergic reward system in the ventral tegmental area, and that findings indicate that orexin neurons link energy balance, emotion, reward systems and arousal. A PMC review on breathing says orexins play roles in "maintenance of wakefulness, energy homeostasis, and reward-seeking behaviour" ([Hypothalamic orexins/hypocretins as regulators of breathing, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5767117/)). So "motivation" in the seed is better read as reward-seeking and energy balance linked to arousal.

### Narcolepsy

In humans with narcolepsy, hypocretin concentrations "are severely reduced and hypocretin neurons are reduced in number or missing altogether, indicating that human narcolepsy results from degeneration of these neurons, possibly as a result of an autoimmune process" (Nature Reviews Neuroscience, Setting the arousal threshold). The seed's "their loss causes narcolepsy" fits narcolepsy type 1, which a Wiley review describes as "caused by a deficiency of orexin" ([Orexin Deficiency in Narcolepsy, Brain and Behavior](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/brb3.70984)). It does not cover all cases: an older PMC article reports that "low or undetectable levels of hypocretin are found in most patients but some have normal or raised levels" ([The hypocretin/orexin system, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1279673/)).

### Caution

This entry is a neurobiology finding, not a full theory of arousal. One PMC review says the "mechanism of action of the orexin system is not well defined" and is a focus of future study ([A review of physiological functions of orexin, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10313292/)).
