"The unexamined I" names a structural fact about self-inspection rather than a moral failing: the self that examines is not among the things it examines. In Nishida Kitarō's account of experience, the split between the one who experiences and what is experienced is a late arrival. "The moment of seeing a color or hearing a sound" is prior not only to the thought that the color or sound is the activity of an external object or that one is sensing it, but also to the judgment of what the color or sound might be (Nishida 1911c: 3, as quoted in the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nishida-kitaro/)). Pure experience, the "one and only reality," differentiates into subject and object, and the individual is formed out of it; the "I" that introspection reports is therefore a product of reflection, not its starting point. Nishida's later work reformulated the point as "seeing without a seer, hearing without a hearer." His theory of self-awareness adds the reason the examiner cannot be caught: "the most basic form of consciousness inherently reflects or mirrors itself within itself, so that there is no difference between that which reflects and what is reflected" — knower and known are the same, and, as the entry notes, talk of self-awareness "does not imply the pre-given existence of some personal self that at times may be self-conscious." The reflexivity "allows it to take itself into account without making it another object." When the mind turns to examine the self, it finds an object, a memory, a role, a feeling, and whatever it finds is not the finding itself. The tradition of self-grasping diagnosis in the Buddhist materials makes the same observation from the other side, treating the grasped self as the error to be seen through rather than the thing to be known.
