"The unexamined I" names a structural fact about self-inspection rather than a moral failing: the self that examines is not among the things it examines. Nishida Kitarō built his philosophy from this fact. In his first book he argued that experience in its basic form precedes the split between the one who experiences and what is experienced; "the moment of seeing a color or hearing a sound" comes before any thought that the color is out there or that one is sensing it (Nishida 1911c: 3, quoted in the [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nishida-kitaro/)). The "I" that introspection reports is therefore a product of reflection, not its starting point. Nishida's later work sharpened the point into a formula: experience prior to subject and object becomes "seeing without a seer, hearing without a hearer" (quoted in the same entry). The seer that cannot be seen is the standing puzzle. When the mind turns to examine the self, it can only find an object, a memory, a role, a feeling, and whatever it finds is by definition not the finding itself. The examination multiplies contents while the examiner recedes, which is why a person can become more self-conscious and yet no better acquainted with the one who is aware. The companion's second layer draws on this in its account of how suffering is maintained: the self that is taken as a fixed thing to be defended, compared, or improved is precisely the unexamined one, a construction mistaken for the seeing. The practical consequence is not to stop examining but to change what the examination assumes; the tradition of self-cherishing diagnosis in the Buddhist materials makes the same move, treating the grasped self as the error to be seen through.
