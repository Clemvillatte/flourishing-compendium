Deep ecology is a school of environmental philosophy founded by the Norwegian philosopher and climber Arne Næss. Brennan and Lo's Stanford Encyclopedia entry says it "was born in Scandinavia, the result of discussions between Næss and his colleagues Sigmund Kvaløy and Nils Faarlund," three friends who shared "a passion for the great mountains" ([SEP, "Environmental Ethics", §3.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-environmental/)).

### Shallow and deep

Næss (1973) called the "fight against pollution and resource depletion" the shallow ecology movement, whose central objective is "the health and affluence of people in the developed countries." The deep ecology movement, "in contrast, endorses 'biospheric egalitarianism', the view that all living things are alike in having value in their own right, independent of their usefulness to others" ([SEP, §3.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-environmental/)).

### A relational self

The entry highlights a second feature, Næss's rejection of "atomistic individualism". He holds that picturing a person as an individual with a separate essence "radically separates the human self from the rest of the world", which, in the entry's summary of his argument, "leads to selfishness towards other people" and also "induces human selfishness towards nature." His alternative is a relational "total-field image" in which organisms are "knots" in the biospherical net. The entry explains that "by identifying with nature I can enlarge the boundaries of the self beyond my skin," so that "self-realization" becomes "the realization of a wider ecological Self." Næss "maintains that the deep satisfaction that we receive from identification with nature and close partnership with other forms of life in nature contributes significantly to our life quality" ([SEP, §3.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-environmental/)).

### How the position changed

The entry notes that biospheric egalitarianism "was modified in the 1980s to the weaker claim that the flourishing of both human and non-human life has value in itself, without any commitment to these values being equal." Deep ecology then "ceased to be a specific doctrine" and became a "platform" of eight points on which different traditions, "whether indigenous, Christian, Buddhist, Daoist, process philosophy, or whatever", could agree. It also records a criticism: Næss "failed to explain in any detail how to make sense of the idea that oysters or barnacles, termites or bacteria could have interests of any morally relevant sort at all" ([SEP, §3.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-environmental/)).
