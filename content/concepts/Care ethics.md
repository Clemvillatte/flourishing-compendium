## In plain words

Care ethics is a normative ethical theory that "holds that moral action centers on interpersonal relationships and care or benevolence as a virtue" ([Wikipedia, Ethics of care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics_of_care)). Where theories of justice ask what is fair in the abstract, care ethics asks who is in front of you and what they need.

## Where it comes from

The tradition grew out of Carol Gilligan's work on moral development; Gilligan, "considered the originator of the ethics of care, criticized the application of generalized standards as 'morally problematic, since it breeds moral blindness or indifference'" ([Wikipedia, Ethics of care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics_of_care)). The contrast with the older theories is drawn in their guiding questions: "consequentialist and deontological ethical theories emphasize generalizable standards and impartiality, ethics of care emphasize the importance of response to the individual," reflected in "their different moral questions: 'what is just?' versus 'how to respond?'" ([Wikipedia, Ethics of care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics_of_care)). Nel Noddings developed it into a relational ethics of caring, in which ethical caring is distinguished from natural caring done out of inclination ([Wikipedia, Ethics of care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics_of_care)).

## How it works

The framework's assumptions: "persons are understood to have varying degrees of dependence and interdependence; other individuals affected by the consequences of one's choices deserve consideration in proportion to their vulnerability; and situational details determine how to safeguard and promote the interests of individuals" ([Wikipedia, Ethics of care](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics_of_care)). Joan Tronto's four phases give care's moral structure: attentiveness, because "care requires a recognition of others' needs in order to respond to them"; responsibility ("In order to care, we must take responsibility ourselves"); competence; and responsiveness.

## What it explains

Care ethics explains the moral weight of the work that keeps people alive — parenting, nursing, teaching — which impartialist theories struggled to value. It also explains why good care fails: inattention to needs, responsibility diffused, care without skill.

## Everyday life

The practice asks three questions before any rule: who here is vulnerable, what does this specific person need, and what am I actually able to do well. Attentiveness comes first, because a need not noticed cannot be met.
