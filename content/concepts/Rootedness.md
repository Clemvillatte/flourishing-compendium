"Rootedness" names a need of the human soul before it names a virtue. Simone Weil put the claim at the front of The Need for Roots (1949): to be rooted is perhaps the most important and least recognized of the soul's needs. Her working definition is given in terms of participation rather than place alone:

> "A human being has roots by virtue of his real, active, and natural participation in the life of a community which preserves in living shape certain particular treasures of the past and certain particular expectations for the future." (Simone Weil, The Need for Roots, excerpted at [The Examined Life](https://theexaminedlife.org/library/the-need-for-roots))

What makes the participation "living" is the double direction: a rooted person receives a community's treasures of the past and holds its expectations for the future. The definition emphasizes real, active participation in a community, which is more than residing in a place; the continuity a rooted person belongs to runs in both temporal directions.

### Root shock

Mindy Thompson Fullilove offers a separate account of the harm associated with displacement. Studying American neighborhoods torn apart by urban renewal, she named the injury "root shock": "the traumatic stress reaction to the loss of all or part of one's emotional ecosystem" (Fullilove, 2004, p. 15). The image is botanical, and a twentieth-anniversary essay co-authored by Fullilove states its premise plainly: "Just as the health of the plant is dependent on its relationship – its rootedness – to its environment, so the health of each person is affected by their belonging to place and community." ([UCSF Health & Aging](https://healthandaging.ucsf.edu/news/root-shock-twenty-years-displacement-%E2%80%93-its-harms-and-remedies))
