"Rootedness" names a need of the human soul before it names a virtue. Simone Weil put the claim first in The Need for Roots (1949; trans. Arthur Wills, 1952): to be rooted is perhaps the most important and least recognized of the soul's needs, and she gave the need a working definition in terms of participation rather than place alone:

> "A human being has roots by virtue of his real, active, and natural participation in the life of a community which preserves in living shape certain particular treasures of the past and certain particular expectations for the future." (Simone Weil, The Need for Roots, excerpted at [The Examined Life](https://theexaminedlife.org/library/the-need-for-roots))

Roots on this definition grow through place, birth, occupation, and one's social surroundings. What makes the participation "living" is the double direction: a rooted person receives a community's treasures of the past and holds its expectations for the future. Rootedness is therefore not nostalgia and not staying put; it is the active share in a continuity that runs in both temporal directions.

### Root shock

The modern clinical descendant of the idea is Mindy Thompson Fullilove's. Studying the displacement of American neighborhoods by urban renewal, she named the injury "root shock": "the traumatic stress reaction to the loss of all or part of one's emotional ecosystem" (Fullilove, 2004, p. 15). The image is botanical, and a twentieth-anniversary essay co-authored by Fullilove states its premise plainly: "Just as the health of the plant is dependent on its relationship – its rootedness – to its environment, so the health of each person is affected by their belonging to place and community." Displacement costs a person more than housing; it costs the web of attachments that made the environment their own, the detailed knowledge of places and people by which a person knows who they are.
