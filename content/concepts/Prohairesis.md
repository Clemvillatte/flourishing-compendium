Prohairesis (Greek: προαίρεσις) is Aristotle's term for deliberate choice: the decision reached after deliberation, which reveals character. The word was first introduced into philosophy by Aristotle in the Nicomachean Ethics ([Wikipedia, Prohairesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prohairesis)). His formula for it, in Ross's translation:

> "The object of choice being one of the things in our own power which is desired after deliberation, choice will be deliberate desire of things in our own power; for when we have decided as a result of deliberation, we desire in accordance with our deliberation." (Aristotle, [Nicomachean Ethics III.3, trans. Ross](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.3.iii.html))

### What it is not

Book III.2 works by subtraction: choice is not appetite or anger, not wish, and not opinion. Appetite is shared with irrational creatures, and the incontinent man acts with appetite but not with choice ([NE III.2](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.3.iii.html)). Wish can aim at impossibilities, like immortality, but "no one chooses such things, but only the things that he thinks could be brought about by his own efforts" ([NE III.2](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.3.iii.html)). Opinion can be true or false; choice is distinguished by badness or goodness, and it is choice, not opinion, that makes people men of a certain character ([NE III.2](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.3.iii.html)).

The word later moved to the centre of Stoic teaching: for Epictetus, prohairesis is the faculty that distinguishes human beings, and the only thing fully in our power ([Wikipedia, Prohairesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prohairesis)). The compendium's principles use the Aristotelian sense: A054 (Choose deliberately) builds on the subtraction passage, and S056 lists the concept among its philosophy parallels.
