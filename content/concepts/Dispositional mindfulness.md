Dispositional mindfulness is mindfulness treated as a trait: how much a person tends to be attentive and aware of present experience in everyday life, whether or not they meditate. It is measured by questionnaire.

Brown and Ryan (2003) developed the Mindful Attention Awareness Scale for this purpose. Their abstract says it "measures a unique quality of consciousness that is related to a variety of well-being constructs, that differentiates mindfulness practitioners from others, and that is associated with enhanced self-awareness." An experience-sampling study found that "both dispositional and state mindfulness predict self-regulated behavior and positive emotional states," and a clinical study with cancer patients showed that "increases in mindfulness over time relate to declines in mood disturbance and stress" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12703651/)).

### More than one thing

Baer and colleagues (2006) compared five existing mindfulness questionnaires and found "five clear, interpretable facets of mindfulness". They also found that the factor structure "may vary with meditation experience," and that the facets "have incremental validity in the prediction of psychological symptoms" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16443717/)). The practical lesson is that one score can hide different qualities.

### In the compendium

- [[B027 — Develop right mindfulness (samma sati)]] sets out the Buddha's instruction in the Satipatthana Sutta. These questionnaires measure a modern psychological construct and were not designed to test that teaching, so the two are parallel and not identical.

### Limits

Most of the evidence in these abstracts is correlational or comes from self-report scales. The abstracts support associations, and they do not by themselves show that being a more mindful person causes better well-being.
