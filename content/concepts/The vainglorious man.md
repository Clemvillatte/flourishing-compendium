"The vainglorious man" is the companion's heading for Oronte in Molière's *The Misanthrope*, the courtier who deals in esteem. He arrives to offer "my tribute of unlimited esteem," professing an "ardent wish, long-cherished, / To be among the number of your friends," and reasons that "a zealous friend, and one / Of my condition, can't well be rejected" (Molière, *The Misanthrope*, Act I, scene 2, trans. Page; [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Misanthrope_(Moli%C3%A8re))). His friendship is offered as a transaction in standing: he mentions his access to the king, "if you need any favour from the court," and he brings the sonnet he wrote, "And ask you whether I'd best publish it," noting "It took me but a quarter-hour to write it." The courtly world supplies the applause he seeks, Philinte cooing over each stanza, "I'm charmed already with this little taste," while Alceste mutters, "Vile flatterer! You're praising rubbish." Oronte then invokes the agreement that Alceste speak "in all sincerity," and Alceste gives the diagnosis: "This is a ticklish subject always, sir; / We're fond of being flattered for our wit." His counsel is that "any gentleman should always keep / In stern control this writing itch we're seized with... For, through this eagerness to show our works, / 'Tis likely we shall cut a foolish figure." Read as the companion's literary comparison, the scene shows a man who asks for criticism while expecting praise: the request for sincerity and the appetite for applause sit in the same speech, and the scene lets a reader watch the two claims collide in one person.
