"The vainglorious man" is the companion's heading for Oronte in Molière's *The Misanthrope*, the courtier who deals in esteem and starves without it. He arrives at Alceste's rooms to offer "my tribute of unlimited esteem," professing an "ardent wish, long-cherished, / To be among the number of your friends," and reasons that "a zealous friend, and one / Of my condition, can't well be rejected" (Molière, *The Misanthrope*, Act I, scene 2, trans. Page; [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Misanthrope_(Moli%C3%A8re))). His friendship is a transaction in standing: he mentions his access to the king, "if you need any favour from the court," and he brings the sonnet he wrote, "to ask you whether I'd best publish it," noting "It took me but a quarter-hour to write it." The scene's machinery is the point. Oronte does not want a reader; he wants a mirror, and the courtly world supplies one, Philinte cooing over each stanza, "I'm charmed already with this little taste," while Alceste mutters, "Vile flatterer! You're praising rubbish." Oronte then invokes the agreement that Alceste speak "in all sincerity," and Alceste gives the diagnosis: "This is a ticklish subject always, sir; / We're fond of being flattered for our wit." His counsel is that "any gentleman should always keep / In stern control this writing itch we're seized with... For, through this eagerness to show our works, / 'Tis likely we shall cut a foolish figure." The vainglory is not the pride itself but its economy: the man has outsourced his worth to the applause of a room he does not respect, so that a quarter-hour's verse must be published to keep the ledger current, and one honest reader registers as an enemy.
