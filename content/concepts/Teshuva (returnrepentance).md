Teshuva is the Jewish practice of return. The word comes from the root shuv, to turn back, and the tradition reads repentance as turning rather than groveling: the person who has gone wrong turns around and walks home. Maimonides gives the practice its classic form in the Mishneh Torah, Laws of Repentance 2:2, and his definition is striking for how much of it is forward-facing:

> "What constitutes Teshuvah? That a sinner should abandon his sins and remove them from his thoughts, resolving in his heart, never to commit them again... Similarly, he must regret the past... He must verbally confess and state these matters which he resolved in his heart." (Maimonides, [Mishneh Torah, Repentance 2:2](https://www.sefaria.org/Mishneh_Torah%2C_Repentance.2.2?lang=bi), Sefaria translation)

### The steps in order

Maimonides' definition has the repentant person leave the sin behind and regret it, and then say out loud what has been resolved in the heart. The resolution carries the weight, because the future is what the turning is for, and he adds a demanding standard: the repentant person must reach the level where "He who knows the hidden will testify concerning him that he will never return to this sin again." How that standard is read, and how strictly the tradition applies it, is discussed in the surrounding chapters of the Laws of Repentance.

The compendium pairs the practice with repair behaviours and with self-forgiveness: the turn is toward the relationship or the life one left, and the past's unchangeability is met with regret and amends rather than endless self-punishment.
