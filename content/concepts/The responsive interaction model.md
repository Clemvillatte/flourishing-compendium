The responsive interaction model is Harry Reis and Phillip Shaver's account, in "Intimacy as an Interpersonal Process" (1988), of how closeness is actually built: not by the sharing alone but by what the other person does with it. The chapter's abstract states the sequence:

> "The process begins when one person expresses personally revealing feelings or information to another. It continues when the listener responds supportively and empathically. For an interaction to become intimate, the discloser must feel understood, validated, and cared for." (Reis & Shaver, 1988, [Handbook of Personal Relationships, ch. 20](https://depts.washington.edu/uwcssc/sites/default/files/Reis%20&%20Shaver,%201988.pdf))

### What the model carries

The weight of the model falls on the response: "In our model, B's response to A's expression is as important as A's initial disclosure in determining whether an interaction will become intimate." Disclosure is only the opening move; the interaction becomes intimate when the speaker perceives the reply as understanding, validating and caring, a perception later named perceived partner responsiveness. The model is transactional, each person's behaviour depending on the other's response and on their own motives, needs and goals, which is why intimacy can grow or stall in a single conversation. The compendium principle PP029 carries the practice: the quality of one's response is half the event, and listening well is the working half. A compendium exercise drawn from the model is to take one disclosure from someone this week and audit the response it received, understood, validated, cared for, or none of these. What the model does not give: a guarantee that responsiveness is enough; the model describes the mechanism of felt intimacy, not the repair of relationships whose trust is already broken.
