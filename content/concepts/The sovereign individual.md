“The sovereign individual” — Nietzsche, Beyond Good and Evil.

Note on the original entry attribution: the passage is located in On the Genealogy of Morals, Second Essay, section 2; the original entry's Beyond Good and Evil citation appears to misplace it. The passage, in the Gutenberg edition:

> "If, however, we place ourselves at the end of this colossal process, at the point where the tree finally matures its fruits, when society and its morality of custom finally bring to light that to which it was only the means, then do we find as the ripest fruit on its tree the sovereign individual, that resembles only himself, that has got loose from the morality of custom, the autonomous 'super-moral' individual (for 'autonomous' and 'moral' are mutually-exclusive terms),—in short, the man of the personal, long, and independent will, competent to promise, and we find in him a proud consciousness (vibrating in every fibre), of what has been at last achieved and become vivified in him, a genuine consciousness of power and freedom, a feeling of human perfection in general." (Nietzsche, [On the Genealogy of Morals II.2, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/52319/52319-h/52319-h.htm))

### The paradoxical task

The Second Essay opens with the question (section 1):

> "The breeding of an animal that can promise—is not this just that very paradox of a task which nature has set itself in regard to man?" (Nietzsche, [On the Genealogy of Morals II.1, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/52319/52319-h/52319-h.htm))

The sovereign individual is what this breeding task is for. Section 2 calls what precedes him "the long history of the origin of responsibility", built on what the text names "an actual memory of the will". His word can be relied on because his will is his own.
