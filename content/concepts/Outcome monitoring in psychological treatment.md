Outcome monitoring, also called routine outcome monitoring or measurement-based care, is the practice of measuring a client's progress during psychotherapy and giving that information back to the clinician. Lambert, Whipple and Kleinstäuber define the scope of their review as "the impact of measuring, monitoring, and feeding back information on client progress to clinicians while they deliver psychotherapy," and say such systems "typify attempts to enhance routine care by assisting psychotherapists in recognizing problematic treatment response and increasing collaboration between therapist and client to overcome poor treatment response" ([Lambert et al., 2018, Psychotherapy, PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30335463/)).

> "Feedback practices reduced deterioration rates and nearly doubled clinically significant/reliable change rates in clients who were predicted to have a poor outcome." (Lambert, Whipple & Kleinstäuber, 2018, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30335463/))

### How strong the evidence is

The same abstract reports 24 studies, of which "two-thirds ... found that ROM-assisted psychotherapy was superior to treatment-as-usual offered by the same practitioners," with effect sizes that "ranged from small to moderate." A later meta-analysis by Rognstad and colleagues found a significant overall effect of measurement feedback systems on outcomes (d = 0.14) and a larger one for patients flagged as not on track (d = 0.29), and concluded that these systems "seem to have a small positive effect on treatment outcomes" ([Rognstad et al., 2022, Administration and Policy in Mental Health, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9931854/)). The gain is modest on average and concentrated among clients who are not improving.
