“Sparse speech” — Laozi, Daodejing 23.

Sparse speech is the Daoist discipline of saying little. The Daodejing's image in chapter 23 is meteorological: nature's loudest events are brief, a violent wind not lasting a whole morning and a sudden rain not a whole day. The chapter opens by linking abstaining from speech with obeying the spontaneity of one's nature, and the page's reading is that talk that blusters belongs with the short-lived spasms the chapter says even Heaven and Earth cannot sustain.

> "Abstaining from speech marks him who is obeying the spontaneity of his nature. A violent wind does not last for a whole morning; a sudden rain does not last for the whole day. To whom is it that these (two) things are owing? To Heaven and Earth. If Heaven and Earth cannot make such (spasmodic) actings last long, how much less can man!" (Daodejing 23, James Legge translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

### What the restraint is for

Legge links restraint in speech with following one's nature, and his example is the short duration of violent wind and rain. The compendium's principle B172 holds the value this concept grounds; its plain-words reading is the pause before speaking, asking whether what is about to be said serves a purpose. The image is about force and duration rather than silence for its own sake. A squall spends everything at once and is over by morning; the reading this page takes from it is that speech delivered at full volume all day cannot last, and that a person who wants words to keep working spreads them thin.
