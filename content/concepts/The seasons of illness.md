In [Ecclesiastes 3](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/8300), the seasons poem puts birth, death, planting, weeping and healing on one list:

> "All things have their season, and in their times all things pass under heaven. A time to be born and a time to die. A time to plant, and a time to pluck up that which is planted. A time to kill, and a time to heal." (Ecclesiastes 3:1-3, Douay-Rheims translation, [Project Gutenberg #8300](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/8300))

The compendium applies the poem's pattern to the body: conditions of health and illness come in their times, and what a person needs changes with them ([Ecclesiastes 3](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/8300)). The heading is the compendium's own phrase; the poem itself carries no such concept, only the list.

The poem goes on pairing what people usually keep apart:

> "A time to weep, and a time to laugh. A time to mourn, and a time to dance." (Ecclesiastes 3:4, Douay-Rheims translation, [Project Gutenberg #8300](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/8300))

That pairing is where the application to illness does its work. Weeping and laughing are not opposites ranked against each other; they are both on the list, each with its season, and the poem gives neither the last word. For someone in a stretch of sickness, the same holds of treatment and rest, of effort and acceptance. The linked principles use the pattern this way: the body's limits are a fact with its season, chronic illness changes what care and mood need, and maintenance is ongoing adaptation rather than a one-time fix. The verse is not medical advice, and the compendium's principles do not treat it as such; it supplies a shape for expecting change instead of demanding that the body hold still.
