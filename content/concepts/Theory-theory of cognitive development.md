**Theory-theory of cognitive development** — Gopnik — children's concepts develop by theory revision: evidence changes their naive explanatory frameworks.

The theory-theory of cognitive development, advanced by Alison Gopnik and her collaborators, proposes that the same processes drive childhood concept change and science. As a team of critics state the view in their open-access paper: "According to this view, the processes underlying cognitive development in infants and children and the processes underlying scientific cognition are identical" (Faucher, Mallon, Nazer, Nichols, Ruby, Stich & Weinberg, "The Baby in the Lab-Coat", [open PDF](https://ruccs.rutgers.edu/images/labs-rgehc/publications/Baby_in_the_Labcoat.pdf)).

The paper quotes Gopnik's own formulations, which carry the theory's flavor:

> "[T]he moral of [the] story is not that children are little scientists but that scientists are big children." (Gopnik 1996a, p. 486) And: "[E]veryday cognition, on this view, is simply the theory that most of us most of the time have arrived at when we get too old and stupid to do more theorizing.... We might think of our enterprise as scientists as the further revision of the theory by the fortunate, or possibly just childish, few who are given leisure to collect evidence and think about it." (Gopnik & Meltzoff 1997, p. 214, both quoted in the same paper)

On this account, a child's understanding of the physical and mental world is a sequence of theories, each with concepts, explanations and predictions, each revised when evidence resists it. Development is intellectual history in miniature: the infant's first "theory" of objects gives way to a theory that accommodates hidden displacement, and later to a representational theory of mind.

The critics' caution belongs on the page too: Stich and Nichols argue the hypothesis is untenable because it "neglects the many important ways that human minds are designed to operate within a social environment," including the transmission of norms. The compendium's application of the theory is thus framed as: hold frameworks lightly because they were built by a child's evidence, and may be rebuilt by an adult's.
