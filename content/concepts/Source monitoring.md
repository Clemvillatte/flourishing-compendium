Source monitoring is the cognitive process by which a person attributes remembered information to its origin: did I see this, hear it, imagine it, or read it somewhere? The framework comes from Marcia Johnson, Stephen Hashtroudi and D. Stephen Lindsay's 1993 synthesis of the same name. Their argument:

> "source monitoring is based on qualities of experience resulting from combinations of perceptual and reflective processes, usually requires relatively differentiated phenomenal experience, and involves attributions varying in deliberateness." (Johnson, Hashtroudi and Lindsay, [Source monitoring, Psychological Bulletin 114(1), 1993](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8346328/), abstract)

The judgments "evaluate information according to flexible criteria and are subject to error and disruption" (same abstract). Memory does not carry a source label; the source is reconstructed by evaluating the memory's features — its perceptual detail, its match to what is already known — and this reconstruction can go wrong. A source-monitoring error "is a type of memory error where the source of a memory is incorrectly attributed to some specific recollected experience," as when a person learns of an event from a friend and later reports having learned it on the news ([Wikipedia, Source-monitoring error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source-monitoring_error)).

The process runs on two speeds. Heuristic judgements are fast and unconscious, using perceptual and contextual information; systematic judgements are slower, deliberate and effortful, and are deployed when the stakes demand it ([Wikipedia, Source-monitoring error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source-monitoring_error)). Depression, high stress, and damage to the relevant brain areas are among the factors that disrupt the process ([same article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source-monitoring_error)).
