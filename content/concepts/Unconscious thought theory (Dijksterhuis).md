Unconscious thought theory (UTT) is Ap Dijksterhuis and Loran Nordgren's 2006 account of what the mind does with a problem when attention is elsewhere: unconscious thought is a mode with its own strengths, and for complex choices, the authors claim, it can do better work than conscious deliberation. Whether the claim survives testing is taken up below. The abstract states the program:

> "We present a theory about human thought named the unconscious-thought theory (UTT). The theory is applicable to decision making, impression formation, attitude formation and change, problem solving, and creativity. It distinguishes between two modes of thought: unconscious and conscious. Unconscious thought and conscious thought have different characteristics, and these different characteristics make each mode preferable under different circumstances." ([Dijksterhuis & Nordgren, 2006, abstract](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/j.1745-6916.2006.00007.x/))

> "For instance, contrary to popular belief, decisions about simple issues can be better tackled by conscious thought, whereas decisions about complex matters can be better approached with unconscious thought." ([Dijksterhuis & Nordgren, 2006, abstract](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/j.1745-6916.2006.00007.x/))

### Origin

The theory formalized a line of experiments beginning with Dijksterhuis's 2004 studies on unconscious processing of choice information. Its most famous exhibit was the 2006 Science paper on the "deliberation-without-attention effect": that people facing a difficult choice decide better after distraction than after conscious deliberation, an effect the replication literature calls the unconscious thought advantage ([Nieuwenstein et al., 2015](https://www.ejwagenmakers.com/2015/Nieuwenstein%20et%20al.%20JDM%202015.pdf)). UTT gave the effect a vocabulary: two modes of thought, each with its own best circumstances.

### How the theory is supposed to work

The engine room is the capacity principle. A later review summarizes it: "According to UTT (Dijksterhuis & Nordgren, 2006), unconscious processing should be superior to conscious processing in the case of complex decisions for several reasons: (1) unconscious processing has far greater processing capacity (parallel instead of serial processing), (2) conscious processing is constrained by an attentional bottleneck (e.g., Miller, 1956), (3) unconscious processing produces more accurate and reliable weighting of features because conscious weighting is moved around by temporary contextual influences such as the relative accessibility of some features over others, and (4) the limited focus of conscious thought causes it to weight dimensions currently under the attentional spotlight more than other dimensions (another temporary accessibility effect), producing polarization effects (Wilson & Schooler, 1991)" ([Acreche & others, as stated in the UTT review literature, 2011](https://acmelab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/2011_unconscious_thought_theory_and_its_discontents.pdf)). On this account the unconscious is the wider funnel, consciousness the spotlight. The experimental setting is the everyday case: a choice with many attributes, an apartment, a job, a used car, where the theory predicts that reading the information and then doing something unrelated will choose better than conscious deliberation. (Paradigm description, not a reported outcome.)

### The evidence status

The flagship effect has not survived replication well. A 2015 meta-analysis and large-scale replication attempt reported: "we conducted a meta-analysis and a large-scale replication study (N = 399) that met the conditions deemed optimal for replicating the UTA. Consistent with the reliability account, the large-scale replication study yielded no evidence for the UTA, and the meta-analysis showed that previous reports of the UTA were confined to underpowered studies that used relatively small sample sizes" ([Nieuwenstein et al., 2015](https://www.ejwagenmakers.com/2015/Nieuwenstein%20et%20al.%20JDM%202015.pdf)). The paper concludes: "Accordingly, we conclude that there exists no reliable support for the claim that a momentary diversion of thought leads to better decision making than a period of deliberation" ([Nieuwenstein et al., 2015](https://www.ejwagenmakers.com/2015/Nieuwenstein%20et%20al.%20JDM%202015.pdf)). What the evidence bears on is narrow: the momentary-diversion claim, in multi-attribute choice tasks. The everyday maxim "sleep on it" is a cousin of that claim, and these studies do not test actual sleep.
