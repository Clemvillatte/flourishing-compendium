Rorty treats vocabularies as instruments whose usefulness depends on what people are trying to do, rather than as rival mirrors of a single intrinsic description of reality ([Ramberg and Dieleman, Richard Rorty, section 3.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rorty/)).

> "vocabularies as tools to be assessed in terms of the particular purposes they may serve." (Ramberg and Dieleman, [section 3.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rorty/); excerpt).

The point is purpose-relative comparison: asking which tool is best makes little sense before specifying the job ([section 3.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rorty/)). The entry also distinguishes changing beliefs within a vocabulary from changing the descriptions through which possible claims become interesting, and rejects reading Rorty as giving language causal priority over the world ([sections 2.2 and 3.5](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/rorty/)).

The instrumental reading is not a dismissal of the vocabularies themselves but a claim about how to compare them. A vocabulary is the set of descriptions through which things can show up as interesting, and the descriptions are what a person can be better or worse equipped with for a given purpose. The distinction between changing a belief inside a vocabulary and changing the vocabulary itself is what keeps the claim from collapsing into relativism: moving between vocabularies is not a move that could be assessed from inside either one, since each is the thing being assessed.

The practical reading runs against a habit of treating disagreements as failures of information. Two people with different vocabularies may not be disagreeing about the same thing at all, since what counts as a question is already something the vocabulary supplies. A person applying the concept asks what job a way of talking is doing rather than whether it is the true one, and the question is answerable in a way that the true one is not.
