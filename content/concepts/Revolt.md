Revolt is Camus's third answer to the absurd, after the two he rejects: physical suicide and philosophical suicide. It is the refusal to either end the confrontation or paper it over, and the essay states it as one of the only coherent philosophical positions:

> One of the only coherent philosophical positions is thus revolt. It is a constant confrontation between man and his own obscurity. It is an insistence upon an impossible transparency. It challenges the world anew every second.

(Albert Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus, "Absurd Freedom," trans. Justin O'Brien, [Internet Archive full text](https://archive.org/stream/AlbertCamusTheMythOfSisyphus/Albert%20Camus%20-%20The%20Myth%20Of%20Sisyphus_djvu.txt))

The definition carries its own negative markers: "It is not aspiration, for it is devoid of hope," and it is "the certainty of a crushing fate, without the resignation that ought to accompany it." The essay's most famous line lands the same stance from the other side:

> The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy.

(Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus, "The Myth of Sisyphus," [O'Brien translation](https://www.sccs.swarthmore.edu/users/00/pwillen1/lit/msysip.htm))

My principle [[E056 — Practise revolt]] works the stance into practice; [[E055]] supplies the absurd recognition revolt presupposes. What distinguishes revolt from despair is that nothing is being asked of the world: the absurd is kept alive rather than settled, and the person living it stays in full view of the confrontation rather than escaping it.
