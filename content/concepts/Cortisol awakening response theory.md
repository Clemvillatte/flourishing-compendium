**Cortisol awakening response theory** — the sharp cortisol rise after waking indexes healthy hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal function and stress load.

The cortisol awakening response (CAR) is a burst of cortisol secretion that follows waking in most healthy people. The summary above goes further than the reviews opened for this page, which describe what regulates the CAR and what influences it. One review says its function is still not clarified. Treat it as a research measure that reflects several things, not a verdict on health or stress.

### What it is

> "In humans, the secretion of cortisol from the adrenal glands follows a diurnal cycle with a profound increase after awakening. This increase after awakening, a phenomenon termed the cortisol awakening response (CAR), appears to be a distinct feature of the hypothalamus-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, superimposing the circadian rhythmicity of cortisol secretion." (Fries, Dettenborn & Kirschbaum 2009, [PubMed 18854200](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18854200/))

### What influences it

Fries and colleagues report that the CAR is influenced by gender, health status, health behaviour and stress perception, and that the hippocampus and other structures (amygdala, prefrontal cortex, suprachiasmatic nucleus) appear to take part in regulating it. They also write that "the exact function of the profound cortisol increase after awakening is still not clarified". Their own hypothesis is that anticipating the coming day matters for its size.

### More than an HPA index

Clow and colleagues (2010) argue in the title that the CAR is "more than a measure of HPA axis function". They present evidence for reduced adrenal sensitivity to ACTH before waking, mediated by a pathway from the suprachiasmatic nucleus, and link the CAR to the ending of sleep inertia. ([PubMed 20026350](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20026350/))

### How it is measured

A 2019 study of salivary cortisol in healthy adults found the peak 20 minutes after waking and argued that the usual magnitude measure (the area under the curve) may not capture the pattern. The authors found a simpler "salience" index discriminated healthy controls from people with seasonal affective disorder better than magnitude did, and suggest low magnitude may suit within-person change and low salience may suit lasting between-person differences. ([Evans et al. 2019, PubMed 30731428](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30731428/)) The way the CAR is scored can change what it appears to show.
