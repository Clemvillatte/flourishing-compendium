## Meanings

The phenomenon at stake has been named twice over. When Tedeschi and Calhoun opened the field, they were looking at the aftermath of major life crises. Their later review, whose title became the canonical citation for the growth literature, defined the core experience plainly:

> Posttraumatic growth is the experience of positive change that occurs as a result of the struggle with highly challenging life crises. (Tedeschi & Calhoun, 2004, "Posttraumatic Growth: Conceptual Foundations and Empirical Evidence," Psychological Inquiry 15(1), abstract, [tandfonline.com](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1207/s15327965pli1501_01))

"Stress-related growth" is the term the field attached to the same phenomenon when it shows up under ordinary, drawn-out conditions rather than single seismic events. A later editorial in the growth literature explains the division of labor:

> Other researchers prefer to refer to the positive changes caused by long-term life experiences as "stress-related growth," this term is preferred for chronic life events (Park, 2010). (Senol-Durak, Di Tella & Romeo, 2023, "Editorial: Post-traumatic growth," Frontiers in Psychology, [PMC10411343](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10411343/))

So the difference is not in the direction of change but in its carrier. Post-traumatic growth speaks of crisis: the assumptions break at once, and the rebuilding is rapid and seismic. Stress-related growth speaks of grind: caregiving, chronic illness, long poverty of one kind or another. The change accumulates the way weathering works on rock, and the person may not notice it until long after the fact.

## Why it matters for flourishing

The one warning the literature insists on is that growth here is not compensation. Pain does not convert into gain at a fixed rate, and people who reach for meaning too quickly, as if it were owed to them for having suffered, tend to reach less of it. The growth, when it comes, sits beside the damage:

> It is important to note that PTG does not negate trauma's negative effects but acknowledges that growth can occur alongside pain and suffering. (Senol-Durak, Di Tella & Romeo, 2023, "Editorial: Post-traumatic growth," Frontiers in Psychology, [PMC10411343](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10411343/))

That is the antifragile part of the facet: some systems do gain from disorder, but the gain is a property of the rebuilding, not of the blow. The Cited-in parallels draw the line carefully. Treat problems as invitations, accept suffering as a teacher without seeking it, transform mishaps into the path. None of that counsels looking for trouble; it counsels not wasting the rebuilding that trouble forces.

## Learning it

The practice is mostly retrospective and narrative. The growth shows up in the story a person tells about the hard years: what the struggle made stronger, what it clarified, what relationship it deepened. The honest version keeps both columns. Someone who reports only the growth is performing optimism; someone who reports only the damage has not yet done the recounting. The recounting itself, done slowly and with witnesses, appears to be one of the more reliable ways the change consolidates.
