Creative repetition is the idea that repeating something can renew it instead of copying it. Kierkegaard's short book Repetition (1843), written under the pseudonym Constantin Constantius, is its main source. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy says the book goes on "to distinguish two forms of movement with respect to knowledge of eternal truth: recollection and repetition," and defines repetition as "recollection forwards" ([IEP: Kierkegaard](https://iep.utm.edu/kierkega/)).

### Backward and forward

Recollection follows Plato's anamnesis, "a recovery of a truth already present in the individual." It is "a movement backwards, since it is retrieving knowledge from the past." Repetition, by contrast, puts weight on the action involved: the Danish word Gjentagelse more literally means "taking again" ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/kierkega/)). The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy makes the same contrast, saying that "Socratic recollection is a recuperation of the past" while Christian repetition is a "recollection forwards" ([SEP: Kierkegaard, 2016 edition](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2016/entries/kierkegaard/)).

### The experiment fails

In the book itself, repetition is not achieved. The IEP notes that the narrator "fails in his attempt to repeat a journey to Berlin," and that the "young man" who becomes absorbed in Job "ultimately gets no further than religious poetry", turning out to be a fiction "invented by Constantius as a psychological experiment" ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/kierkega/)). The book shows what repetition is not as much as what it is.

### Scope

In Kierkegaard's own account, repetition is a religious and existential movement. The encyclopedias treat it as a movement of faith that is renewed over time, not as a method for creative work. The compendium's heading "creative repetition" applies the idea to creativity, and neither source makes that application. Kierkegaard's text was not read directly for this page.
