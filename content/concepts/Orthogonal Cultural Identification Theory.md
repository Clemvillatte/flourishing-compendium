A theory of cultural identity: identification with each culture is its own dimension, independent of the others, so one can be high on both, on one, or on neither.

> "A theory of cultural identification is presented indicating that identification with different cultures is orthogonal. Instead of cultures being placed at opposite ends of a continuum, cultural identification dimensions are independent of each other, and increasing identification with one culture does not require decreasing identification with another." — [Oetting & Beauvais (1990), abstract (PubMed, PMID 2101397)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2101397/)

### Origin

The theory comes from research on Native American and Mexican American youth, published by Eugene Oetting and Fred Beauvais in 1990-91 in the International Journal of the Addictions.

### Core claims

The theory's form is a measurement model turned world-view: because the dimensions are independent, any combination is possible. A person may hold a single identity, be strongly bicultural, lean to one culture, or identify weakly with any. The studies behind the abstract reported three findings: identification with Anglo culture tracked having Anglo friends and family acceptance of an Anglo marriage; identification with either culture was "a source of personal and social strength"; and that strength "does not translate automatically into less drug use", which followed the identified culture's own stance on drugs ([Oetting & Beauvais, 1990](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2101397/)).

### In everyday life

Illustrative scenario, not a reported study result: a granddaughter may keep her family's traditions strongly and also move comfortably in the majority culture, and the theory predicts no automatic trade-off between the two.
