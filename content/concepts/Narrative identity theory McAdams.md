**Narrative identity theory McAdams** — McAdams — identity is a life story; redemption sequences and agency themes in the narrative predict generativity and wellbeing.

Dan McAdams's program starts from a structural claim: identity in adulthood is not a list of traits or a set of roles but a story. His compressed definition ([McAdams, 2013](http://www.its.caltech.edu/~squartz/Perspectives%20on%20Psychological%20Science-2013-McAdams-272-95.pdf)):

> The internalized and evolving amalgam of self stories—what is now typically referred to as a narrative identity (McAdams & Pals, 2006)—aims to integrate the reconstructed past, experienced present, and imagined future.

The story-building self arrives on a developmental schedule. "A third layer of selfhood begins to form in the adolescent and emerging adulthood years, when the self as autobiographical author aims to construct a story of the Me, to provide adult life with broad purpose and a dynamic sense of temporal continuity" ([McAdams, 2013](http://www.its.caltech.edu/~squartz/Perspectives%20on%20Psychological%20Science-2013-McAdams-272-95.pdf)). His shorthand for the arrangement: "The I becomes an autobiographical author; the Me becomes the story it tells" ([McAdams, 2013](http://www.its.caltech.edu/~squartz/Perspectives%20on%20Psychological%20Science-2013-McAdams-272-95.pdf)).

### What the story content predicts

The research payoff comes from coding the sequences inside those stories. McAdams, Reynolds, Lewis, Patten and Bowman's 2001 study is the anchor, and its abstract deserves quoting ([McAdams et al., 2001](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167201274008)):

> In the midlife sample, adults scoring high on self-report measures of generativity showed significantly higher levels of redemption and lower levels of contamination sequences. In both samples, redemption sequences in life narrative accounts were positively associated with self-report measures of psychological well-being, whereas contamination sequences predicted low levels of well-being among midlife adults.

A redemption sequence moves from a bad scene to a good one; contamination runs the other way, good scenes turning bad. The association held across two samples, and it survived a strong test: "redemption sequence scores were a stronger predictor of well-being than were ratings of the overall affective quality of life-narrative accounts" ([McAdams et al., 2001](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167201274008)). The shape of the sequence carried information beyond whether the story was happy or sad.

The 2013 retrospective frames the pattern in generativity terms: midlife American adults high in generativity "tend to highlight redemptive episodes in autobiographical memory and tend to see their lives as following a redemptive arc" ([McAdams, 2013](http://www.its.caltech.edu/~squartz/Perspectives%20on%20Psychological%20Science-2013-McAdams-272-95.pdf)). The stories they author are "psychological resources on which they draw" as actors and agents ([McAdams, 2013](http://www.its.caltech.edu/~squartz/Perspectives%20on%20Psychological%20Science-2013-McAdams-272-95.pdf)). These are correlations in American samples, not instructions — but they are unusually stable ones across the program's studies.
