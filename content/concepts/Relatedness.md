“Relatedness” — Deci and Ryan, 2000.

- Deci & Ryan, 2000
- Ryan & Deci, 2017

Relatedness is the felt sense of belonging and connection to other people, groups or a culture. In self-determination theory it is one of three innate psychological needs, alongside competence and autonomy: Ryan and Deci describe "the innate needs for competence, autonomy, and relatedness" (Contemporary Educational Psychology 25(1), 2000, p. 57, [open PDF](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_IntExtDefs.pdf)). It is not the same as liking company; it is the deeper sense of mattering to particular others and being respected by them.

### Why it is more than a comfort

SDT gives relatedness a load-bearing role in motivation. Uninteresting but necessary behaviours get internalized, made one's own, when the people who value them are people one feels connected to:

> This suggests that the groundwork for facilitating internalization is providing a sense of belongingness and connectedness to the persons, group, or culture disseminating a goal, or what in SDT we call a sense of relatedness. (Ryan and Deci, "Intrinsic and Extrinsic Motivations: Classic Definitions and New Directions," Contemporary Educational Psychology 25(1), 2000, p. 64, [open PDF](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_IntExtDefs.pdf))

The classroom finding shows the mechanism in ordinary life: students internalize a classroom's values when they feel respected and cared for by the teacher, and Ryan, Stiller and Lynch (1994) found relatedness to teachers and parents predicted greater internalization of school regulations (ibid., p. 64). Connection is the channel through which other people's values become one's own.

### The Māori and Buddhist parallels

The compendium pairs relatedness with whanaungatanga, the Māori principle of kinship and relational belonging (R026), and with relational autonomy (R077), which keeps connection and self-determination from being framed as opposites.
