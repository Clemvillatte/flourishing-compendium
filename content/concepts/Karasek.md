“Karasek” — 1979.

The 1979 reference is Robert Karasek's paper "Job Demands, Job Decision Latitude, and Mental Strain: Implications for Job Redesign" (Administrative Science Quarterly 24(2):285-308), which introduced the job strain model, later extended with social support (the demand-control-support model of Karasek and Theorell, 1990).

### The model

Karasek's abstract says the model "predicts that mental strain results from the interaction of job demands and job decision latitude". The key finding follows.

> "The consistent finding is that it is the combination of low decision latitude and heavy job demands which is associated with mental strain. This same combination is also associated with job dissatisfaction." (Karasek 1979, [PDF copy of the paper](https://movingforwardnetwork.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/Karasek_Job-Demands-Job-Decision-Latitude-and-Mental-Strain-Implications-for-Job-Redesign_1979.pdf))

In the model, demands are the "instigators of action", such as workload and conflict. Decision latitude is the constraint that "modulates the release or transformation of 'stress' (potential energy) into the energy of action". Job strain occurs when demands are high and latitude is low. The data were nationally representative surveys from Sweden and the United States.

### The practical claim and its caveat

The paper's main implication is that redesigning work to give a broad range of workers more decision latitude "could reduce mental strain, and do so without affecting the job demands" tied to organisational output. Karasek also warns of diminishing returns: at high levels of latitude, symptoms level out or even begin to rise, and a broad increase in decision responsibility for managers "may only increase their strain". For the lowest-status jobs, he reports, the reduction of strain from more latitude is substantial.

### How the model has held up

A 2003 review of longitudinal research on the demand-control-(support) model applied five quality criteria to 45 studies; 19 (42%) scored acceptably on all of them. Those higher-quality studies gave only modest support for the claim that the combination of high demands and low control produces high strain, though there was good evidence for lagged causal effects of work characteristics, especially on self-reported health or well-being. ([de Lange et al. 2003, PubMed 14570524](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14570524/)) The 1979 paper tested the model on national survey data; the review above concerns the later demand-control-(support) version of the model.
