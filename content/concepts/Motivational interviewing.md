(Miller and Rollnick, 2013), works with, not against, a person's own motivation.

## Facets

- **as the alternative** — Miller and Rollnick, 2013

William Miller and Stephen Rollnick's third edition opens with a plain sentence that carries the whole method ([Miller and Rollnick, 2013](https://ia801005.us.archive.org/35/items/NelsonTratadoDePediatria/Motivational%20Interviewing.pdf)):

> Motivational interviewing is a collaborative conversation style for strengthening a person's own motivation and commitment to change.

Every word is doing work. It is "always a collaborative conversation, never a lecture or monologue. It is more a matter of guiding than directing" ([Miller and Rollnick, 2013](https://ia801005.us.archive.org/35/items/NelsonTratadoDePediatria/Motivational%20Interviewing.pdf)). And the motivation at stake is "the person's own motivation" — the method draws out reasons that are already there rather than installing new ones.

### What it works against

The reason the method exists is a helper's instinct the authors named. The context is worth quoting at length ([Miller and Rollnick, 2013](https://ia801005.us.archive.org/35/items/NelsonTratadoDePediatria/Motivational%20Interviewing.pdf)):

> Seeing people head down a wrong path stimulates a natural desire to get out in front of them and say, "Stop! Go back! Don't you see? There is a better way over there!," and it is done with the best of intentions, with one's heart in the right place. We call this the "righting reflex"—the desire to fix what seems wrong with people and to set them promptly on a better course, relying in particular on directing.

The person who argues back digs in, and the argument "both for and against change" gets voiced from the client's own mouth. What MI meets instead is ambivalence: "most people who need to make a change are ambivalent about doing so. They see both reasons to change and reasons not to. They want to change and they don't want to, all at the same time" ([Miller and Rollnick, 2013](https://ia801005.us.archive.org/35/items/NelsonTratadoDePediatria/Motivational%20Interviewing.pdf)).

### Four processes and a spirit

The third edition replaced the old principles with "four broad processes that this approach comprises—engaging, focusing, evoking, and planning" ([Miller and Rollnick, 2013](https://ia801005.us.archive.org/35/items/NelsonTratadoDePediatria/Motivational%20Interviewing.pdf)). Their later retrospective adds that in practice "the processes are more iterative and flexible" ([Miller and Rollnick, 2023](https://motivationalinterviewing.org/sites/default/files/the_evolution_of_motivational_interviewing.pdf)). Underneath sits a spirit with four parts, "practiced: partnership, acceptance, compassion, and evocation" ([Miller and Rollnick, 2013](https://ia801005.us.archive.org/35/items/NelsonTratadoDePediatria/Motivational%20Interviewing.pdf)). The glossary's clinical definition compresses all of it: "A person-centered counseling style for addressing the common problem of ambivalence about change" ([Miller and Rollnick, 2013](https://ia801005.us.archive.org/35/items/NelsonTratadoDePediatria/Motivational%20Interviewing.pdf)).
