“The free market of ideas” — Milton, Areopagitica.

The seed ties the metaphor to Milton's Areopagitica (1644), his speech against the licensing of books. Milton argues for open encounter between opinions, and a text search of the Gutenberg edition finds no passage using that metaphor for ideas. According to [Wikipedia's article on the marketplace of ideas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marketplace_of_ideas), the "free trade in ideas" first appears in Justice Holmes's 1919 dissent in Abrams v. United States, and the phrase "marketplace of ideas" in a concurring opinion by Justice Douglas in United States v. Rumely. The label is therefore later shorthand for Milton's argument. Milton's text was opened in full, and Wikipedia is a secondary source for the later history.

### Truth and open encounter

> "Let her and Falsehood grapple; who ever knew Truth put to the worse, in a free and open encounter? Her confuting is the best and surest suppressing." ([Milton, Areopagitica](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/608))

This is the core of the argument. Milton holds that licensing "misdoubt[s]" the strength of truth, and he trusts truth to win when it is "in the field". That trust is a claim about truth, not a result established by evidence, and the page does not test it.

### Liberty above other liberties

> "Give me the liberty to know, to utter, and to argue freely according to conscience, above all liberties."

### Virtue that has been tried

Milton's argument also concerns the formation of character. A virtue that avoids contact with opposing views is weak:

> "I cannot praise a fugitive and cloistered virtue, unexercised and unbreathed, that never sallies out and sees her adversary but slinks out of the race"

and he adds that "that which purifies us is trial, and trial is by what is contrary". This is the part of the essay closest to the compendium's interest in wisdom development.

### Limits

Milton's case was made for his own time and this page does not examine whether the Areopagitica excepts any views from toleration, so it makes no claim about that. Whether competition between ideas reliably favours truth is a separate question that the sources opened here do not answer.
