## In plain words

Action identification theory is Robin Vallacher and Daniel Wegner's account of what people think they are doing. Its core claim: "Action identification theory holds that an action can be identified by the performer in different ways, and that these various act identities differ in their appropriateness for maintaining the action effectively" ([Vallacher, Wegner & Somoza 1989](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/DANWEGNER/pub/Vallacher,Wegner,&Somoza1989.pdf)). One act can be described as moving fingers or as making music, and the description you hold matters.

## Where it comes from

Vallacher and Wegner laid out the theory in their 1985 book A Theory of Action Identification (Erlbaum) and in a 1987 Psychological Review article, "What do people think they're doing?" The theory grew out of work on how people answer the question "What are you doing?", which the authors treat as an identification of the action, not a mere report of movement.

## How it works

Identities range from low to high level. Higher-level identities "signify why or with what effect the action is performed," while lower-level identities "specify how the action is performed" (Vallacher & Wegner, 1989, p. 660, quoted in [Cruz & Pinto 2019](https://scielo.isciii.es/pdf/rpto/v35n2/1576-5962-rpto-35-2-0123.pdf)). The pull runs upward: "Optimal action identification exists when a personally easy action is identified in relatively high-level terms (i.e., the action's effects and implications) or a personally difficult action is identified in relatively low-level terms (i.e., the action's mechanical details)" ([Vallacher, Wegner & Somoza 1989](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/DANWEGNER/pub/Vallacher,Wegner,&Somoza1989.pdf)).

## What it explains

The theory predicts which identification level will serve performance: in the experiment, the hard task went better under the low-level name and the easy task under the high-level name, suggesting that people tend to manage difficulty by shifting the level at which they name what they are doing ([Vallacher, Wegner & Somoza 1989](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/DANWEGNER/pub/Vallacher,Wegner,&Somoza1989.pdf)).

## Everyday life

The finding suggests a check you can run on your own practice: when an activity keeps breaking down, try naming it at a lower level, the mechanical details, until fluency returns; when it runs smoothly, the higher-level name carries the meaning.
