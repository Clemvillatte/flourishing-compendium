## In plain words

Action identification theory is Robin Vallacher and Daniel Wegner's account of what people think they are doing. Its core claim: "Action identification theory holds that an action can be identified by the performer in different ways, and that these various act identities differ in their appropriateness for maintaining the action effectively" ([Vallacher, Wegner & Somoza 1989](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/DANWEGNER/pub/Vallacher,Wegner,&Somoza1989.pdf)). The same act can be described as moving fingers or as making music, and the description you hold affects how well you do it.

## Where it comes from

Vallacher and Wegner laid out the theory in their 1985 book A Theory of Action Identification (Erlbaum) and in a 1987 Psychological Review article titled "What do people think they're doing?" Their premise is that people answer the question "What are you doing?" with remarkable ease, and that the answer given is an identification of action.

## How it works

Identities range from low to high level. Low-level identities "specify how the action is performed"; high-level identities "signify why or with what effect the action is performed" ([Vallacher & Wegner 1987](https://scispace.com/pdf/what-do-people-think-they-re-doing-action-identification-and-3wpq5rx221.pdf)). There is a natural pull toward meaningful, high-level descriptions, but difficulty pulls the actor back down: "Optimal action identification exists when a personally easy action is identified in relatively high-level terms (i.e., the action's effects and implications) or a personally difficult action is identified in relatively low-level terms (i.e., the action's mechanical details)" ([Vallacher, Wegner & Somoza 1989](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/DANWEGNER/pub/Vallacher,Wegner,&Somoza1989.pdf)).

## What it explains

The theory explains why descriptions change with skill: the beginner must think about mechanics, the expert can think about purpose. In their speech-fluency experiment, subjects made fewer errors and felt more satisfied when an easy task was identified at high level and a difficult task at low level ([Vallacher, Wegner & Somoza 1989](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/DANWEGNER/pub/Vallacher,Wegner,&Somoza1989.pdf)). It also grounds the Behavior Identification Form, a standard measure of preferred level of identification.

## Everyday life

Learning to meditate, the instruction "watch the breath" fails you at the start; "notice the coolness at the nostrils" works, and later the high-level description returns. When a practice collapses, dropping to a lower-level description is often what restores it. This is the theory's practical lesson: match the description to the current difficulty of the act.
