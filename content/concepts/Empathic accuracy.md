"Empathic accuracy" — Ickes, 1993.

## Facets

- **research** — Ickes, 1997

Empathic accuracy is the psychologist William Ickes' construct, introduced in 1993, for how well one person infers what another is actually thinking and feeling. A later definition in the research literature keeps the wording simple:

> "Empathic accuracy (EA; Ickes & Hodges, 2013) is the extent to which people accurately perceive their peers' thoughts, feelings, and other inner mental states." (Sened et al., [2017 meta-analytic review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28394141/), abstract)

The construct is measured rather than assumed: in the standard dyadic-interaction paradigm, two people interact on video and later each reports, at signaled points, what they were thinking and feeling, while their partner guesses those contents; accuracy scores compare the guesses to the target's own reports ([same abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28394141/)). This is why the construct is an accuracy claim and not a warmth claim: a perceiver can care and still be wrong.

What accuracy does in relationships is an empirical question, and the meta-analysis gives it a modest answer. Across 21 studies and 2,739 participants, empathic accuracy and relationship satisfaction showed a small but significant association: "We found a small but significant association between the two (r = .134, p < .05)" ([same abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28394141/)). The link was stronger for reading negative emotions: "EA for negative emotions (one's accuracy when assessing a partner's negative emotions) was more closely related to satisfaction (r = .171, p < .05) than EA for positive ones (r = .068, p > .1)" ([same abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28394141/)).
