The cognitive-behavioural model holds that how a person interprets a situation, more than the situation itself, shapes what they feel and do. Aaron Beck developed it from the 1960s (the seed cites Beck, 1976), and it underlies cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT). The summaries below come from the Beck Institute, a StatPearls review and a meta-review, not from Beck's 1976 book, which was not opened.

### The core claim

> "The cognitive model describes how people's perceptions of, or spontaneous thoughts about, situations influence their emotional, behavioral (and often physiological) reactions." (Beck Institute, [Cognitive Model](https://beckinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Cognitive-Model.pdf))

The same document says distressed people's perceptions "are often distorted and dysfunctional". People can learn to identify and evaluate their automatic thoughts, defined as spontaneously occurring verbal or imaginal cognitions, and also to modify the distorted beliefs, their basic understanding of themselves, their worlds and other people, that give rise to those thoughts.

### Three layers

A StatPearls review of CBT stresses three aspects of cognition: automatic thoughts, cognitive distortions, and underlying beliefs or schemas. Its example is a friend who passes you in a hallway without saying hello. The thought "he hates me" is likely to lower your mood and make you avoidant, while the thought "he is in a hurry" would not. Beliefs sit at two levels, core beliefs (global, rigid and overgeneralised, such as "I am unlovable") and intermediate beliefs (assumptions, attitudes and rules). ([StatPearls](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK470241/))

### What the evidence says

Hofmann and colleagues (2012) reviewed 106 meta-analyses, a representative sample of 269 identified, covering CBT for many problems.

> "In general, the evidence-base of CBT is very strong, and especially for treating anxiety disorders." (Hofmann et al. 2012, [PMC3584580](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3584580/))

They add that "the efficacy of CBT is questionable for some problems", that many meta-analysed studies had small samples or inadequate control groups, and that high-quality studies are still needed. The evidence supports CBT as a treatment; it does not by itself prove that the model's account of how thoughts cause emotions is the mechanism.
