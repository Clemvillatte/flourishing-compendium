Core knowledge theory is Elizabeth Spelke's proposal about the starting equipment of the human mind. On this account, complex adult skills:

> "such as reading and calculation and complex cognitive achievements such as formal science and mathematics may depend on a set of building block systems that emerge early in human ontogeny and phylogeny." — Spelke, "Core Knowledge", American Psychologist 2000 ([PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11280937/))

These systems are specialized and limited:

> "These core knowledge systems show characteristic limits of domain and task specificity: Each serves to represent a particular class of entities for a particular set of purposes." — Spelke 2000 ([PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11280937/))

What distinguishes human cognition, on this account, is combination:

> "By combining representations from these systems, however, human cognition may achieve extraordinary flexibility." — Spelke 2000 ([PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11280937/))

The theory is a hypothesis with a research program attached: studies of infants and nonhuman primates, Spelke argues, "may contribute to understanding unique features of human knowledge" ([Spelke 2000, PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11280937/)). A later overview with Katherine Kinzler gives the proposal its plainest form: "Human cognition is founded, in part, on four systems for representing objects, actions, number, and space", and "it may be based, as well, on a fifth system for representing social partners" ([Spelke and Kinzler 2007](https://siegler.tc.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/5023-Spelke2007.pdf)). These systems "serve to represent inanimate objects and their mechanical interactions, agents and their goal-directed actions, sets and their numerical relationships of ordering, addition and subtraction, and places in the spatial layout and their geometric relationships", and each "has deep roots in human phylogeny and ontogeny, and it guides and shapes the mental lives of adults" ([Spelke and Kinzler 2007](https://siegler.tc.columbia.edu/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/5023-Spelke2007.pdf)).
