This page covers the research on how people adjust to major life changes — marriage, divorce, unemployment, widowhood, disability. The question is what happens to well-being after such events, and whether it returns to where it was before.

### What the studies show

Much of the evidence comes from large panel studies that follow the same people for years. Writing in 2006, Ed Diener, Richard Lucas and Christie Scollon summarized what the German panel data showed:

> "Using the same sample of Germans, we have examined the ways that specific life events influence happiness. In support of the initial adaptation model, people do seem to adapt to some life events. For instance, Lucas et al. (2003) showed that, on average, Germans did not get lasting boosts in happiness after marriage. Instead, they reported short-term increases in happiness that were followed by relatively quick adaptation. However, the extent of adaptation varies for different life events. Lucas et al. (2003) showed that widows and widowers, people who were laid off from work (Lucas, Clark, Georgellis, & Diener, 2004), and individuals who divorced (Lucas, 2005b) all reported long-lasting changes in life satisfaction after these life events. The widows showed the greatest amount of adaptation (at least in terms of the absolute increase from their lowest level of happiness), but even this took about eight years and was not quite complete." ([Diener, Lucas and Scollon, 2006](https://www.its.caltech.edu/~squartz/Dienerbook.pdf), collected-reprints PDF, pp. 109-110)

The same article re-examined the classic Brickman et al. (1978) disability findings and calculated that the spinal cord-injured participants' general happiness was about 0.75 standard deviations below the controls — a difference the authors call large — contrary to the popular reading of that study as showing full adaptation.

### The picture that replaced the treadmill

The article's conclusion is not that people bounce back to nothing, nor that events permanently scar. Its five revisions: set points are not hedonically neutral; people have different set points, partly temperament; the components of well-being can move in different directions; set points can change under some conditions; and individuals differ in their adaptation to events. The average curve in a panel study hides those individual differences.
