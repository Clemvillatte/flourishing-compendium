Stigma research is the sociological and psychological study of how a marked difference becomes a disqualifying social identity. The field's founding book is Erving Goffman's Stigma: Notes on the Management of Spoiled Identity (1963), and a later review of the field summarises his definition:

> "Goffman defines stigma as 'an attribute that is deeply discrediting' (Goffman, 1963, p. 3) and emphasizes two major consequences of stigma: status loss and social rejection." ([Phelan et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4091623/))

### The classic picture

Goffman's stigmatized person is "reduced in our minds from a whole and usual person to a tainted and discounted one", and stigma is "the situation of the individual who is disqualified from full social acceptance" ([Phelan et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4091623/)). The same review carries the field's most-cited later definition, Bruce Link and Jo Phelan's 2001 conceptualization: stigma exists when labeling, negative stereotypes, separation into "us" and "them", status loss and discrimination converge, and it is "entirely contingent on access to social, economic and political power" ([Phelan et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4091623/)).
