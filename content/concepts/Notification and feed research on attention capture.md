This page covers research on whether smartphone notifications and infinite feeds pull attention away from what a person is doing. The best-documented case in the page's evidence is the notification, and the clearest single study is Stothart, Mitchum and Yehnert (2015). Their abstract states: "We found that cellular phone notifications alone significantly disrupted performance on an attention-demanding task, even when participants did not directly interact with a mobile device during the task. The magnitude of observed distraction effects was comparable in magnitude to those seen when users actively used a mobile phone, either for voice calls or text messaging" ([Stothart et al., 2015, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance 41(4), 893-897](https://neurosciencenews.com/attention-cell-phone-notifications-2308/), full abstract reproduced).

> "Although these notifications are generally short in duration, they can prompt task-irrelevant thoughts, or mind wandering, which has been shown to damage task performance." (Stothart et al., 2015, abstract)

### What the study did

Participants were 212 undergraduates who did the Sustained Attention to Response Task and were randomly assigned to a call, text message, or no-notification group. The authors write that they "believe that what underlies this effect is the tendency for cellular notifications to prompt task-irrelevant thoughts, or mind wandering" and note that the mechanism was "not directly assessed in our study" ([Stothart et al., 2015, full text](https://ia600108.us.archive.org/view_archive.php?archive=/24/items/wikipedia-scholarly-sources-corpus/10.1037%252Fh0034974.zip&file=10.1037%252Fxhp0000100.pdf)). For errors, the authors report "a greater increase in the probability of making an error for the text message and call conditions than the no notification condition." On very fast reaction times, the increase "was larger for the call group than the text message group, suggesting that call notifications were more distracting than text notifications" (same paper). The two notification types therefore differ on one of the two measures.

### What this page does not show

The study tested notifications on a lab task with students. It did not test infinite feeds, and it does not measure the fragmenting of daily experience. Those parts of the page's description rest on other studies that have not yet been opened and cited here. The principles below that speak of attentional hijack or design intent (AT059, AT060, AT061) go beyond what this one experiment measured.
