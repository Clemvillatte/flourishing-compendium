## Other formulations in this library

- competitiveness as self-worth contingency predicts poorer mental health (Ryckman et al., 1994)

The construct comes from Karen Horney: hypercompetitiveness is not strong striving but an exaggerated need to compete and win, in which self-worth rides on the outcome. The reported associations of that arrangement are set out below.

> "According to her theory, hyper-competitiveness is an exaggerated, neurotic form of competitiveness. Individuals scoring high on the HCA scale have a very strong need to compete and to win at any cost, because their self-worth is based on competition, thus they feel more powerful by winning a competition and, at the same time, winning makes them feel superior to others." — [Orosz et al., Frontiers in Psychology 9 (2018), full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5972296/), summarising Ryckman et al. (1990, 1994) and Horney (1937)

### Reported associations

The same review records the profile: "This orientation is associated with low self-esteem, low self-actualization, low optimal psychological health, high neuroticism, high aggression, high dominance, and high exhibition scores; higher mistrust, Machiavellianism, dogmatism, and narcissism." In the review's summary, those scoring high on the scale perceive their competitors as enemies and are not afraid to use unfair strategies in order to win. These are associations, not demonstrated causes. The contrast that matters for flourishing is with the self-developmental orientation measured by the same research program, which the review reports was positively associated with resilience and positivity, while the hypercompetitive orientation was unrelated to both.
