## Other formulations in this library

- competitiveness as self-worth contingency predicts poorer mental health (Ryckman et al., 1994)

The construct comes from Karen Horney: hypercompetitiveness is not strong striving but a neurotic arrangement in which winning is the only way the self can feel worth anything. The measurable costs of that arrangement are now well documented.

> "According to her theory, hyper-competitiveness is an exaggerated, neurotic form of competitiveness. Individuals scoring high on the HCA scale have a very strong need to compete and to win at any cost, because their self-worth is based on competition, thus they feel more powerful by winning a competition and, at the same time, winning makes them feel superior to others." — [Orosz et al., Frontiers in Psychology 9 (2018), full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5972296/), summarising Ryckman et al. (1990, 1994) and Horney (1937)

### What it costs

The same review records the profile: "This orientation is associated with low self-esteem, low self-actualization, low optimal psychological health, high neuroticism, high aggression, high dominance, and high exhibition scores; higher mistrust, Machiavellianism, dogmatism, and narcissism." Competitors are perceived as enemies, and unfair strategies are not refused. The contrast that matters for flourishing is with the personal-development orientation measured by the same research program, which correlates with resilience and positivity rather than with their absence. Not daring to be first, in the Taoist formulation, is a description of the exit from this trap rather than a counsel of mediocrity.
