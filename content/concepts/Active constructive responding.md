“Active constructive responding” — Gable, Reis, Impett and Asher, 2004.

Active constructive responding is the practice of meeting another person's shared good news with enthusiasm: asking about it, showing genuine interest, and letting the teller relive the event in the telling. The anchor study is Gable, Reis, Impett and Asher's 2004 four-study paper on what they call capitalization, the process the response belongs to:

> "He called this capitalization, a term that we adopt to refer to the process of informing another person about the occurrence of a personal positive event and thereby deriving additional benefit from it." (Gable, Reis, Impett and Asher, [What do you do when things go right?, JPSP 87(2):228-245](https://www.sas.rochester.edu/psy/people/faculty/reis_harry/assets/pdf/GableReisImpettAsher_2004.pdf), introduction)

### The four response styles

The paper's typology, adapted from Rusbult's model of responses to a partner's negative behavior, crosses two dimensions, active versus passive and constructive versus destructive:

> "we differentiated active–constructive responses (e.g., enthusiastic support), passive–constructive responses (e.g., quiet, understated support), active–destructive responses (e.g., quashing the event), and passive–destructive responses (e.g., ignoring the event)." (same paper)

The measure asks how a partner typically responds, and its active-constructive items are behavioural: "My partner usually reacts to my good fortune enthusiastically" and "My partner often asks a lot of questions and shows genuine concern about the good event."

### What the studies found

Two diary studies (one with 154 students, one with 94 undergraduates keeping ten days of diaries) found that on days people told someone about their most positive event, positive affect and life satisfaction were higher, above and beyond the event's importance and that day's negative events. Two couple studies (59 dating couples; 89 married couples) found that perceiving one's partner as an active-constructive responder predicted relationship quality, intimacy and daily marital satisfaction, and the association held when global marital satisfaction and responses to bad behavior were controlled. The fourth study added a mechanism: positive events that had been shared with more people were better recalled a day later, while sharing negative events made no difference to recall.

The evidence is correlational throughout, and the measure captures perceived responses rather than observed behavior; the paper itself notes that perceptions are proximal and the partner's actual response more distal.
