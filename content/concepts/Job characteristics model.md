The job characteristics model is a theory of work design. Its original version, constructed by Greg R. Oldham and J. Richard Hackman in 1975, proposed five core job characteristics (skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy and feedback) that affect work-related outcomes through three psychological states: experienced meaningfulness, experienced responsibility, and knowledge of results ([Wikipedia, Job characteristics model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Job_characteristics_model)). Hackman and Oldham presented the final form of the theory in their 1980 book Work Redesign.

> "Job characteristics theory is a theory of work design. It provides \"a set of implementing principles for enriching jobs in organizational settings\". The original version of job characteristics theory proposed a model of five \"core\" job characteristics (i.e., skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy, and feedback) that affect five work-related outcomes (i.e., motivation, satisfaction, performance, and absenteeism and turnover) through three psychological states (i.e., experienced meaningfulness, experienced responsibility, and knowledge of results)." ([Wikipedia, Job characteristics model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Job_characteristics_model))

### Evidence and later revision

The theory grew out of 1960s work redesign, which reacted against the simplification of jobs into repetitive tasks after routinized work was found to breed dissatisfaction. In the 1980 revision, Hackman and Oldham added two moderators (knowledge and skill, and context satisfaction), removed absenteeism and turnover as outcomes, and concentrated attention on internal work motivation and other affective outcomes, following weak empirical support for some of the original outcome variables ([Wikipedia, Job characteristics model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Job_characteristics_model)).

A later meta-analytic summary of 259 studies and 219,625 participants found motivational characteristics explained, on average, 34% of the variance in job satisfaction, with social and work-context characteristics adding further increments ([Humphrey, Nahrgang & Morgeson, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17845089/)).
