starting with very small behaviours builds habits (Fogg, 2019; Clear, 2018).

The method rests on BJ Fogg's behavior model, stated in his own words on his model's site:

> "Behavior happens when Motivation, Ability, and a Prompt come together at the same time. When a behavior does not occur, at least one of those three elements is missing." (BJ Fogg, [Fogg Behavior Model](https://behaviormodel.org/), opened)

A peer-reviewed scoping review of the model's use in public health puts the same point in the literature's terms: "The FBM posits that behavior is influenced by the interaction of three core factors: motivation, ability, and prompts. For a behavior to occur, all three elements must align in the same moment—sufficient motivation, the ability to perform the behavior, and an effective prompt." The review adds the model's account of ability: "the Ability Chain, which outlines five elements that can either facilitate or hinder behavior: time, money, physical effort, mental effort, and routine" (Behavioral science meets public health: a scoping review of the Fogg behavior model, [open access](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s12889-025-24525-y)).

The tiny-habits method is the model's practical corollary. Since motivation is the least controllable element, the method designs around it: shrink the behavior until it requires almost no ability, and anchor it to an existing routine that supplies the prompt. The review documents this pattern across the interventions it surveyed: reducing cognitive and physical effort, adjusting routines, and integrating healthy behaviors into existing routines. My principle "make habits small — start tiny" and "stack habits — link new behaviours to existing ones" are this design logic in practice.

My application: change is engineered, not willed. Where a practice keeps failing, the model's diagnostic is which element is missing, and the method's answer is almost always to make the behavior smaller and attach it to something already reliable.
