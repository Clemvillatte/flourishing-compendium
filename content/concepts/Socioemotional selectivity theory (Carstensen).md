Socioemotional selectivity theory is a life-span theory of motivation developed by Laura Carstensen: what people want from their social lives depends on how much time they feel they have left. Its full statement is the 1999 American Psychologist article by Carstensen, Isaacowitz and Charles, and Carstensen restated it for Science in 2006.

> "Socioemotional selectivity theory claims that the perception of time plays a fundamental role in the selection and pursuit of social goals." (Carstensen, Isaacowitz & Charles, [1999, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10199217/))

### Origin

Laura Carstensen developed the theory; the 1999 American Psychologist article with Derek Isaacowitz and Susan Charles is its full statement, and Carstensen restated it for Science in 2006. The quotations on this page are drawn from the two abstracts.

### Core claims

The theory sorts social motives into two families and makes time perception the switch between them:

> "When time is perceived as open-ended, knowledge-related goals are prioritized. In contrast, when time is perceived as limited, emotional goals assume primacy." (Carstensen et al., [1999, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10199217/))

Age matters only as a proxy: "The inextricable association between time left in life and chronological age ensures age-related differences in social goals" — but "the perception of time is malleable, and social goals change in both younger and older people when time constraints are imposed" ([1999, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10199217/)). By 2006 Carstensen put it more strongly: "Gradually, time left becomes a better predictor than chronological age for a range of cognitive, emotional, and motivational variables", and the motivational shift "also appears in other contexts (for example, geographical relocations, illnesses, and war) that limit subjective future time" ([2006, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16809530/)).

### How it explains

The mechanism is motivational: "constraints on time horizons shift motivational priorities in such a way that the regulation of emotional states becomes more important than other types of goals" ([2006, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16809530/)). The theory does not say that every older person is calmer than every younger one; it predicts where goals point, and the age-related emotional findings are a separate empirical question (see [[SE075 — Recognise self and ageing — self continues to develop]]).

### In everyday life

The malleability claim is the practical part: the shift is not locked to birthdays. A relocation, an illness, or any event that shortens the felt future produces the same reordering in a 25-year-old as in a 75-year-old. (Illustrative scenario, not a reported participant or study result.)
