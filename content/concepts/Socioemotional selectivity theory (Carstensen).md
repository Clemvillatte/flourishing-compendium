Socioemotional selectivity theory is a life-span theory of motivation: what people want from their social lives depends on how much time they feel they have left.

> "Socioemotional selectivity theory claims that the perception of time plays a fundamental role in the selection and pursuit of social goals." (Carstensen, Isaacowitz & Charles, [1999, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10199217/))

### Origin

Carstensen, Isaacowitz and Charles presented the theory in their [1999 American Psychologist article](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10199217/), and the [2006 Science piece](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16809530/) restates it.

### Core claims

The theory sorts social motives into two families and makes time perception the switch between them:

> "When time is perceived as open-ended, knowledge-related goals are prioritized. In contrast, when time is perceived as limited, emotional goals assume primacy." (Carstensen et al., [1999, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10199217/))

The theory's claim is about perceived time, not chronological age: "The inextricable association between time left in life and chronological age ensures age-related differences in social goals", but "the perception of time is malleable, and social goals change in both younger and older people when time constraints are imposed" ([1999, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10199217/)). And "Gradually, time left becomes a better predictor than chronological age for a range of cognitive, emotional, and motivational variables", with the motivational shift also appearing "in other contexts (for example, geographical relocations, illnesses, and war) that limit subjective future time" ([2006, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16809530/)).

### How it explains

The mechanism is motivational: "constraints on time horizons shift motivational priorities in such a way that the regulation of emotional states becomes more important than other types of goals" ([2006, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16809530/)). The theory does not say that every older person is calmer than every younger one; it predicts where goals point, and the age-related emotional findings are a separate empirical question (see [[SE075 — Recognise self and ageing — self continues to develop]]).

### In everyday life

The malleability claim is the practical part. Imagine someone preparing to leave a city: with only a few days remaining, they might choose time with close friends over an event offering new acquaintances. This illustrates the theory's limited-time prediction ([1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10199217/), [2006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16809530/) abstracts); it is not a reported study result.
