**Theory of Reasoned Action** — Fishbein, Ajzen — intention to perform a behaviour follows from attitude and subjective norms toward it.

The theory of reasoned action (TRA) "aims to explain the relationship between attitudes and behaviors within human action." Developed by Martin Fishbein and Icek Ajzen from 1967 onward out of social psychology, persuasion models, and attitude research, the theory is built around one hinge: "a person's intention to perform a behavior is the main predictor of whether or not they actually perform that behavior" ([Wikipedia, "Theory of reasoned action"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_reasoned_action)). Intention, in turn, has two determinants. Attitude toward the behavior is "the way people feel towards a particular behavior"; subjective norm is the perceived weight of relevant others, defined by Ajzen as the "perceived social pressure to perform or not perform the behavior." The model is stated as a weighted function, \( BI=(AB)W_{1}+(SN)W_{2} \), where behavioral intention is the sum of attitude and subjective norm, each carrying empirically derived weights that vary by person and situation; direct prior experience with an activity increases the weight of the attitude component. Its predictions hold under three conditions identified by Sheppard and colleagues: the intention measure must match the behavior in specificity; there must be "stability of intentions between time of measurement and performance"; and carrying out the intention must be "under the volitional control of the individual." Under those conditions behavioral intention predicts "any voluntary act, unless intent changes prior to the performance or unless the intention measure does not correspond to the behavioral criterion in terms of action, target, context, time-frame and/or specificity." The evidence base is wide: meta-analytic work on condom use across 96 data sets found "people are more likely to use condoms if they have previously formed the corresponding intentions," with those intentions deriving from attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control; applications extend from exercise intent to whistle-blowing on hazing, renewable-energy purchasing, and vaccination advocacy. The theory's limits are as well documented as its successes. It has no place for habit, so "the habituation of past behavior also tends to reduce the impact that intention has on behavior"; it sets aside personality, emotion, and rules; and it assumes control, which Ajzen himself conceded: "we can never be absolutely certain that we will be in a position to carry out our intentions. Viewed in this light it becomes clear that strictly speaking every intention is a goal whose attainment is subject to some degree of uncertainty." That concession became the theory of planned behavior in 1985, which adds perceived behavioral control as a third predictor of intention, accounting for "times when people have the intention to conduct the behavior, but the actual behavior is thwarted because of subjective and objective reasons."
