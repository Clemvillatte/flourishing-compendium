**Theory of Reasoned Action** — Fishbein, Ajzen — intention to perform a behaviour follows from attitude and subjective norms toward it.

A theory of why people do what they do, built on one hinge: the best predictor of a voluntary act is the person's stated intention to do it, and that intention is built from two things, how the person feels about the act, and how much the people around them seem to approve.

### Origin

Martin Fishbein and Icek Ajzen developed the theory from 1967 onward, drawing on social psychology, persuasion models, and attitude research. The aim was "to explain the relationship between attitudes and behaviors within human action."

### Core claims

> Behavioral intention ... comes as a result of a belief that performing the behavior will lead to a specific outcome.

> *Subjective norms* ... refer to the way perceptions of relevant groups or individuals such as family members, friends, and peers may affect one's performance of the behavior. Ajzen defines subjective norms as the "perceived social pressure to perform or not perform the behavior."

The model is a weighted function, \( BI=(AB)W_{1}+(SN)W_{2} \): behavioral intention is attitude toward the behavior plus subjective norm, each carrying empirically derived weights that vary by person and situation.

### How the prediction holds — and where it breaks

The model's predictions hold under conditions identified by Sheppard and colleagues: the intention measure must match the behavior in specificity; intentions must be stable between measurement and performance; and the act must be "under the volitional control of the individual." Under those conditions, behavioral intention predicts "any voluntary act, unless intent changes prior to the performance or unless the intention measure does not correspond to the behavioral criterion in terms of action, target, context, time-frame and/or specificity." The evidence base is wide: a synthesis of 96 data sets on condom use found that "people are more likely to use condoms if they have previously formed the corresponding intentions," with those intentions deriving from attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control, that last component belonging to the theory's later extension. The limits are documented alongside: the theory "is designed to make broad generalizations, so there is no emphasis on specifics like personality, rules, or emotions"; "the habituation of past behavior also tends to reduce the impact that intention has on behavior"; and it is limited "in terms of being able to predict behaviors that require access to certain opportunities, skills, conditions, and/or resources." Ajzen's own statement marks the boundary: "we can never be absolutely certain that we will be in a position to carry out our intentions. Viewed in this light it becomes clear that strictly speaking every intention is a goal whose attainment is subject to some degree of uncertainty." In 1985 Ajzen extended the theory into the theory of planned behavior, adding perceived behavioral control as a third predictor, to account for "times when people have the intention to conduct the behavior, but the actual behavior is thwarted because of subjective and objective reasons."

### In everyday life

A person who intends to start exercising in the morning has, on this model, an intention built from two sources: their own attitude toward morning exercise and the subjective norm, whether the people whose opinion they count seem to approve or mock. If the intention is stable and the act is under their control, it predicts the behavior. If a roommate's jokes erode the norm, or the gym is closed, the intention weakens or the act fails. The practical use is diagnostic: when a stated intention does not translate into action, check which input is missing: the attitude, the norm, or the control.
