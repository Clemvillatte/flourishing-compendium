The testing effect, also called retrieval practice, is the finding that practicing recall of studied material improves later retention more than restudying it does. Henry Roediger and Jeffrey Karpicke named and measured it in a 2006 experiment with educationally relevant prose passages:

> "Taking a memory test not only assesses what one knows, but also enhances later retention, a phenomenon known as the testing effect."
>
> (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006, [Psychological Science 17(3), abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/))

### The delayed-test result

The striking part is the time course. In the 2006 experiments, students either reread prose passages or took free-recall tests on them, without feedback, then took a final retention test after 5 minutes, 2 days, or 1 week. Rereading won at 5 minutes. Recall won at 2 days and a week, and rereading inflated students' confidence in what they would remember:

> "When the final test was given after 5 min, repeated studying improved recall relative to repeated testing. However, on the delayed tests, prior testing produced substantially greater retention than studying, even though repeated studying increased students' confidence in their ability to remember the material."
>
> (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006)

Their one-line conclusion: "Testing is a powerful means of improving learning, not just assessing it."

### Not rote

A later experiment addressed the worry that retrieval practice only trains recall of strings, not understanding. Jeffrey Karpicke and Janell Blunt had students learn science texts either by retrieval practice or by building concept maps, then tested comprehension:

> "practicing retrieval produces greater gains in meaningful learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping"
>
> (Karpicke and Blunt, 2011, [Science 331, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21252317/))

The advantage held "with test questions that assessed comprehension and required students to make inferences".
