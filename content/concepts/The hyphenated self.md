The hyphenated self is the postcolonial name for an identity held in the join: British-Indian, Korean-American, the self that lives in the hyphen between inherited and adopted affiliations rather than in either alone. Its clearest theoretical statement is Stuart Hall's account of diaspora identity, which refuses both purity and nostalgia:

> "The diaspora experience as I intend it here is defined, not by essence or purity, but by the recognition of a necessary heterogeneity and diversity; by a conception of 'identity' which lives with and through, not despite, difference; by hybridity." (Hall, "Cultural Identity and Diaspora", in the [opened text](https://dl.icdst.org/pdfs/files3/9abb1b80ee9e5795a6d738eee197c052.pdf))

### What the hyphen carries

Hall rejects the picture of identity as a return: "diaspora does not refer us to those scattered tribes whose identity can only be secured in relation to some sacred homeland to which they must at all costs return, even if it means pushing other people into the sea." Instead, identity is a production: "Diaspora identities are those which are constantly producing and reproducing themselves anew, through transformation and difference." The hyphen is therefore not a defect to be resolved but the site where the self is made — a making that continues across a lifetime rather than settling into either side. The compendium principle SE073 carries the practical reading: recognise self and culture conflict in bicultural and marginalised selves, since the strain is structural rather than personal failure. A compendium exercise drawn from the hyphen is to write the two halves of one's own hyphen (national, linguistic, professional, generational) and what each side does not see. What the hyphen does not give: an assurance that in-betweenness is painless; the theory names the production, not the cost of it.
