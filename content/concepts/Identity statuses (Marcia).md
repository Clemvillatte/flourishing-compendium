James Marcia took Erikson's loose talk of an adolescent identity crisis and gave it a measurable structure. In his 1966 study, 86 college men were interviewed, and each was placed in one of four statuses along two dimensions, crisis and commitment:

> "Criteria for inclusion in 1 of 4 identity statuses were the presence of crisis and commitment in the areas of occupation and ideology." (Marcia, [Development and Validation of Ego-Identity Status](https://archive.org/download/marcia1966/marcia1966.pdf), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 3(5), 1966, abstract)

The four statuses are identity achievement (commitment after crisis), moratorium (in crisis, commitments still vague), foreclosure (commitment without crisis), and diffusion (neither crisis nor commitment). The status the compendium watches most closely is foreclosure, and Marcia's description of it has not aged:

> "A foreclosure subject is distinguished by not having experienced a crisis, yet expressing commitment. It is difficult to tell where his parents' goals for him leave off and where his begin. He is becoming what others have prepared or intended him to become as a child." (Marcia, 1966, identity-status definitions section)

The study found these were styles of coping, not rungs on a ladder: a person can occupy any status for a long time. Foreclosure subjects in the study endorsed authoritarian values more strongly and kept unrealistically high goals after failure, where identity-achievement subjects moderated theirs.
