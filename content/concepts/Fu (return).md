Fu (復) is Laozi's word for return, and chapter 16 of the Daodejing makes it the shape the Dao's movement takes: things grow, then each goes back to its root. The chapter is the locus classicus of the idea, and it treats return not as failure but as the completion of a cycle.

> "All things alike go through their processes of activity, and (then) we see them return (to their original state). When things (in the vegetable world) have displayed their luxuriant growth, we see each of them return to its root. This returning to their root is what we call the state of stillness." (Laozi, Daodejing 16, trans. Legge, [Sacred Texts Archive](https://www.sacred-texts.com/tao/taote.htm))

The root is where stillness (jing) is found, and knowing that unchanging rule of return is, on Legge's rendering, what marks intelligence; not knowing it leads to "wild movements and evil issues". The same chapter carries the promise that a life aligned with this pattern endures.

### Beside Nietzsche's recurrence

The compendium sits this idea next to eternal recurrence as a comparison. The two are not the same claim. Nietzsche's thought-experiment asks whether one could will one's life to repeat without end; Laozi observes that things in nature already revert to their source, and that aligning with that rhythm, rather than forcing against it, is the wiser course. The resemblance is in the shape, a circle rather than a line, not in the doctrine.
