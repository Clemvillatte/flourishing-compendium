Cognitive ageing is asynchronous: the abilities do not rise and fall together. Hartshorne and Germine's large-sample study states the pattern:

> "Our results reveal considerable heterogeneity in when cognitive abilities peak: some abilities peak and begin to decline around high school graduation; some abilities plateau in early adulthood, beginning to decline in the 30s; still others do not peak until the 40s or later. These findings motivate a nuanced theory of maturation and age-related decline, where multiple, dissociable factors differentially affect different domains of cognition."
> — [Hartshorne & Germine 2015, "When does cognitive functioning peak? The asynchronous rise and fall of different cognitive abilities across the lifespan", Psychological Science 26(4):433-443 (open access)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4441622/)

The evidence base, from the same abstract: "convergent evidence from 48,537 Web participants and a comprehensive analysis of normative data from standardized IQ and memory tests" ([Hartshorne & Germine 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4441622/)). The study's framing gives the compendium its caution: because peaks are dissociable, "a nuanced theory of maturation and age-related decline" replaces any single decline story — and the seed's contrast between early-peaking speed and late-peaking vocabulary must be read per-ability, not as a general rise-and-fall of "intelligence" ([Hartshorne & Germine 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4441622/)).
