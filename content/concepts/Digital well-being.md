Digital well-being is the study of how digital environments affect well-being. The compendium's seed citations mark two research strands: Verduyn and colleagues' active/passive model of social network site use, and Halfmann and Rieger's work on social pressure and need satisfaction in smartphone use.

The Verduyn review's most-cited distinction separates the two ways of using a social network site. In its words, passive use "refers to the monitoring of other people's lives without engaging in direct exchanges with others" (Verduyn et al., 2017, p. 281, as quoted in Calvin et al., [Computers in Human Behavior Reports](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13341170/), 2025). The model's hypothesis, as the same article summarizes it:

> "Researchers hypothesize that passive use elicits ill-being through instigating feelings of envy and social comparison while active social media use elicits feedback and social connection, subsequently increasing well-being (Verduyn et al., 2017)." (Calvin, Chen and Selkie, 2025)
