The narrative self is the self understood as the protagonist of one's own life story. The [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consciousness-intentionality/consciousness-self.html) entry on consciousness of self sets it against the experiential self:

> "An experienced (or experiential) self so conceived is sometimes contrasted with a 'narrative self' (one's self conceived of as the protagonist in autobiographical accounts)." (Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Consciousness and Intentionality: Consciousness of Self", [supplement](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consciousness-intentionality/consciousness-self.html))

### Strong and weak versions

The same passage notes that the contrast is sharpest where the narrative notion figures in claims that the self is nothing but a fiction spun out of narrative, a "center of narrative gravity" in Dennett's 1991 metaphor. It also reports an objection: a purely narrativist conception of self is at least incomplete, because it misses the basic experience of ourselves as agents (Bayne and Pacherie, 2007).

### What it needs

In the entry on [phenomenological approaches to self-consciousness](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/self-consciousness-phenomenological/), Gallagher and Zahavi follow Owen Flanagan in separating a primary, low-level self-consciousness of experiences as mine from the "much stronger notion" in play when we think about our own narrative self. They add that the latter "presupposes both conceptual knowledge and narrative competence." On that account the narrative self comes after the basic sense of ownership of experience, not before it.
