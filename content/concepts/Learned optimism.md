Learned optimism is Martin Seligman's name for the finding, set out in his 1990 book of that title, that the way people explain setbacks to themselves can be retrained. The book grew out of his learned helplessness research: on the reformulated model, a pessimistic explanatory style construes bad events as internal, stable and global, and the optimistic style as the opposite. The publisher's description of the book states the practical claim:

> "Offering many simple techniques anyone can practice, Dr. Seligman explains how to break an "I–give–up" habit, develop a more constructive explanatory style for interpreting your behavior, and experience the benefits of a more positive interior dialogue." ([Vintage Books, 2006 edition](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/163862/learned-optimism-by-martin-e-p-seligman-phd/))

### Retraining the style

The dimensions come from the reformulated learned helplessness model: "Individuals who habitually construe the causes of bad events as internal, stable, and global ('it's my fault, it's going to last forever, and it's going to undermine everything I do') should, when they experience bad events, be more susceptible to helplessness deficits than those with the opposite style" ([Seligman and Schulman, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 50 (1986), UPenn PDF](https://ppc.sas.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/predictproductivity.pdf)).
