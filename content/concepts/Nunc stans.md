Nunc stans, the "standing now," is the scholastic name for the mode of presence that belongs to eternity rather than to time. The source is Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, Book V, where Lady Philosophy defines eternity to settle the problem of divine foreknowledge and human freedom.

> Eternity is the simultaneous and complete possession of infinite life.

(Boethius, Consolation of Philosophy, Book V, [CCEL translation](https://ccel.org/ccel/boethius/consolation.vi.html))

The contrast with time is structural: "All that lives under the conditions of time moves through the present from the past to the future; there is nothing set in time which can at one moment grasp the whole space of its lifetime." What is eternal "grasps and possesses wholly and simultaneously the fulness of unending life, which lacks naught of the future, and has lost naught of the fleeting past." The payoff for the argument: "just as you can see things in this your temporal present, so God sees all things in His eternal present."

The later scholastics compressed this into the pair nunc stans (the standing now of eternity) versus nunc fluens (the flowing now of time) — the Aquinas 101 treatment of divine eternity notes the formula comes straight from Boethius.

The compendium's [[B012 — Practice only happens now]] works the temporal side of the same insight: for us, the only real point of contact with life is the moving present, and the eternal present is its limit-case.
