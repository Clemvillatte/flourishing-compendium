“Abgeschiedenheit” — Meister Eckhart.

Abgeschiedenheit is the detachment Meister Eckhart places above every other virtue. Writing in early fourteenth-century German, the Dominican argues in his treatise on detachment that love, humility and mercy, however high, each remain tied to creatures in some way, while detachment is free of all creatures ([Treatise on Detachment, DW V:400-436](https://www.ldysinger.com/@texts2/1320_eckhart/04_tract-abgesch.htm)). The treatise also distinguishes this detachment from outward penance: fasting and bodily discipline act on the surface of a person, while detachment works from the innermost ground, so that no suffering is suffered and nothing can touch the detached heart.

> “I find no virtue superior to complete detachment from all things; for all other virtues are in some way concerned with creatures, but detachment is free of all creatures.” (Meister Eckhart, [On Detachment, trans. Luke Dysinger](https://www.ldysinger.com/@texts2/1320_eckhart/04_tract-abgesch.htm))

The treatise's most quoted image gives the concept its emotional range. Detachment means standing unmoved before joy and sorrow, honor and disgrace, as a mountain of lead stands before a light breath of wind. In modern German the word has drifted toward seclusion and retirement from the world, a sense Eckhart's usage does not carry: his detachment lets a person remain among people and tasks while holding nothing ([Cambridge Dictionary](https://dictionary.cambridge.org/us/dictionary/german-english/abgeschiedenheit)). The Stanford Encyclopedia pairs it with gelâzenheit, releasement, describing both as negative virtues that strip away possessive grasping rather than adding new forms of behaviour, a reading that places Eckhart's ethics closer to Stoic therapy of the passions than to standard medieval virtue catalogues ([SEP, Meister Eckhart](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/meister-eckhart/)). Walshe's translation of the treatise's opening reaches the same ranking from a survey of authorities: only pure detachment surpasses all things ([Yale, Walshe trans.](https://german.yale.edu/sites/default/files/meister_eckhart_on_detachment.pdf)).

The concept anchors three Buddhist and Daoist parallels in this compendium: resting in the unborn mind, letting go as the final step of breath meditation, and sitting and forgetting.
