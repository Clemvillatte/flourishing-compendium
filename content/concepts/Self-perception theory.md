Self-perception theory is Daryl Bem's account of how people know their own attitudes and internal states: by observing their own behavior and the circumstances in which it occurs, much as an outside observer would. Bem proposed it in 1967 as an alternative interpretation of cognitive-dissonance findings.

> "the attitude statements which comprise the major dependent variables in dissonance experiments may be regarded as interpersonal judgments in which the observer and the observed happen to be the same individual" (Bem, 1967, [article PDF](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Motivation/Bem_1967_Self_perception.pdf)).

### The observer in the same skin

The theory rests on what Bem calls a single empirical generalization: "an individual's belief and attitude statements and the beliefs and attitudes that an outside observer would attribute to him are often functionally similar in that both sets of statements are partial 'inferences' from the same evidence: the public behaviors and accompanying stimulus cues upon which the socializing community has relied in training him to make such self-descriptive statements in the first place" (Bem, 1967). Where internal cues are not controlling, "an individual's attitude statements may be viewed as inferences from observations of his own overt behavior and its accompanying stimulus variables" (Bem, 1967). The dissonance findings then need no motivational drive to explain: "If one now places the hypothetical observer and the communicator into the same skin, the findings obtained by Festinger and Carlsmith are the result" (Bem, 1967).
