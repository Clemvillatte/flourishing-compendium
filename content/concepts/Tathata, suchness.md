Tathatā, "suchness", is a Mahayana term for how things are. The treatise The Awakening of Faith explains that things "appear under the forms of individuation" on account of "confused subjectivity", and names what suchness is:

> "What is meant by the soul as suchness (*bhûtatathatâ*), is the oneness of the totality of things (*dharmadhâtu*), the great all-including whole, the quintessence of the Doctrine." (The Awakening of Faith, trans. D. T. Suzuki, [Sacred Texts Archive](https://sacred-texts.com/bud/taf/taf21.htm))

### A word that admits its own limits

The treatise is explicit that language strains under the job: "But the term suchness is all that can be expressed in language, and through this term all other terms may be disposed of." Its account works by refusing what can be said of suchness: "In the essence of suchness, there is neither anything which has to be excluded, nor anything which has to be added."

### Both negation and affirmation

The treatise gives suchness two faces: "The first is trueness as negation (çûnyatâ), in the sense that it is completely set apart from the attributes of all things unreal, that it is the real reality. The second is trueness as affirmation (açûnyatâ), in the sense that it contains infinite merits, that it is self-existent." The refusals then go all the way down:

> "Thus we understand that suchness (*bhûtatathatâ*) is neither that which is existence, nor that which is non-existence, nor that which is at once existence and non-existence, nor that which is not at once existence and non-existence; that it is neither that which is unity, nor that which is plurality, nor that which is at once unity and plurality, nor that which is not at once unity and plurality." (The Awakening of Faith, trans. D. T. Suzuki, [Sacred Texts Archive](https://sacred-texts.com/bud/taf/taf21.htm))

### The Zen use

The Zen tradition keeps the term. The Stanford Encyclopedia's entry on Japanese Zen describes how, on its reading, "there is nothing in the bottomless background to determine or delimit how things appear", and notes that "Zen uses such terms as 'suchness' or 'thusness' to designate it". The seeing it speaks of "is 'not two,' i.e., it is non-dualistic in nature" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2022/entries/japanese-zen/)).
