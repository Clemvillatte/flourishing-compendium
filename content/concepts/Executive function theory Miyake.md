**Executive function theory Miyake** — Miyake, Friedman — executive function resolves into three separable functions: inhibition, shifting and updating.

Executive functions are the control processes that regulate thought and behavior in the service of goals. Before Akira Miyake and colleagues, the field mostly measured them with big clinical tasks like the Wisconsin Card Sorting Test and the Tower of Hanoi, which mix several abilities at once. Their 2000 study broke the lump apart with latent variable analysis: 137 college students performed simple tasks chosen to tap three candidate functions, and the analysis asked whether the functions were really distinct.

> "Confirmatory factor analysis indicated that the three target executive functions are moderately correlated with one another, but are clearly separable. Moreover, structural equation modeling suggested that the three functions contribute differentially to performance on complex executive tasks." (Miyake, Friedman, Emerson, Witzki, Howerter and Wager, [The unity and diversity of executive functions and their contributions to complex "Frontal Lobe" tasks: a latent variable analysis, Cognitive Psychology 41 (2000), pp. 49-100, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10945922/))

### The triad

The three functions are mental set shifting, information updating and monitoring, and inhibition of prepotent responses. The paper's own summary line is that both the unity and the diversity of executive functions need recognizing: the three correlate moderately, yet each contributes differently to complex tasks, with card sorting leaning on shifting, the Tower of Hanoi on inhibition, and operation span on updating. Later work by Friedman and Miyake carried the framework forward under the unity and diversity label.
