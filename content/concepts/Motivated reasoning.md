Ziva Kunda's 1990 article proposed that "motivation may affect reasoning through reliance on a biased set of cognitive processes—that is, strategies for accessing, constructing, and evaluating beliefs". The motivated reasoning phenomena she reviews "fall into two major categories: those in which the motive is to arrive at an accurate conclusion, whatever it may be, and those in which the motive is to arrive at a particular, directional conclusion" ([Kunda, The Case for Motivated Reasoning, Psychological Bulletin 108(3), 480-498, PDF](https://fbaum.unc.edu/teaching/articles/Psych-Bulletin-1990-Kunda.pdf)).

### The mechanism

Kunda's central claim is that "accuracy goals lead to the use of those beliefs and strategies that are considered most appropriate, whereas directional goals lead to the use of those that are considered most likely to yield the desired conclusion". So people do not simply believe what they like. They reach for the beliefs and reasoning rules that deliver the conclusion they want. A student summary adds that the bias is limited by one's ability to construct a justification, so beliefs extend only "to that what reason permits" ([Studysmart summary](https://www.studysmart.ai/en/summaries/the-case-for-motivated-reasoning-kunda-kunda/)), which is a secondary paraphrase, not Kunda's words.

### Accuracy goals are not a cure

Kunda notes that "one may even imagine that biases will sometimes be exacerbated and reasoning worsened in the presence of accuracy goals", if people believe faulty procedures are best. A passage the Synthese paper quotes says accuracy goals paired with directional goals may "enhance rather than reduce bias" because deeper processing "may facilitate the construction of justifications for desired conclusions" (see [Convincing ourselves: accuracy motives and rationalization, Synthese](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11229-025-05259-1?error=cookies_not_supported&code=4589f5c3-28fe-41b5-a75f-ad14d47d91ba)). The same paper adds that accuracy motives can also counteract distortions caused by directional or closing motives.

### Identity and the link to confirmation bias

A risk-governance chapter quotes Kahan (2016): under directional goals people "unconsciously conform assessment of factual information to some goal collateral to assessing its truth" ([Motivated Reasoning and Risk Governance, Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-24271-7_2?error=cookies_not_supported&code=6aebef60-0962-4ff2-9aea-40eaa27b7f2d)). A Springer entry lists the processes involved: seeking information that confirms a desired conclusion (confirmation bias), judging arguments by whether they support it (prior attitude effect), and generating counterarguments against contrary information (disconfirmation bias) ([Motivated Reasoning, Springer Nature](https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-3-032-06918-4_134-1?error=cookies_not_supported&code=308576ee-aeea-49bf-9b5b-c1b7aaad218b)). That entry notes that researchers "typically use the term 'motivated reasoning' to refer implicitly or explicitly to directional goals".

### Limits

Wikipedia flags that whether citizens are "more likely to be rational or rationalizing has not been determined" in political decisions ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motivated_reasoning)). I did not open Kahan (2013) or Nickerson (1998) directly; the seed cites them, so treat claims attributed to them here as unverified.
