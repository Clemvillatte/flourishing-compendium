Schachter described the finding in a later reflection: "precisely the same physiological state - the state of sympathetic arousal induced by an injection of adrenalin - could be labeled by the subject as any of a variety of emotional states or indeed as no emotional state at all, depending largely on cognitive and situational manipulations." He concluded that any physiologically based account of emotion "must specify the fashion in which physiological processes interact with stimulus, cognitive and situational factors" ([Schachter, Citation Classic commentary on Schachter and Singer 1962, Current Contents PDF](https://garfield.library.upenn.edu/classics1979/A1979HZ19200001.pdf)). The original paper is Schachter and Singer, Psychological Review 69(5), 379-399 (1962).

### The experiment

The original report says that subjects injected with epinephrine and "told precisely what they would feel and why" proved "relatively immune to any effects of the manipulated cognitions", while those without an adequate explanation of their bodily state were more open to the cues around them. It summarises: "Given a state of sympathetic activation, for which no immediately appropriate explanation is available, human subjects can be readily manipulated into states of euphoria, anger, and amusement" ([Schachter and Singer, 1962, PDF copy on gwern.net](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/1962-schachter.pdf)). The seed's "the same arousal can become different emotions" matches this.

The authors themselves flagged a weakness in the data: in the anger condition, patterns were "particularly troubling" because they made it "impossible to evaluate unequivocally the effects of the state of physiological arousal" (same PDF).

### Replication and criticism

A Penn State course page lists problems: confusing happy-to-angry ratios, no baseline measures of arousal, and a focus on slow, conscious appraisal, with quick automatic appraisal left out ([Schachter-Singer; Unexplained Arousal, Penn State](https://psu.pb.unizin.org/psych425/chapter/schachter-singer-unexplained-arousal/)). Wikipedia reports that Marshall and Zimbardo's replication found the euphoric confederate "had little impact" and that Maslach's hypnosis study found unexplained arousal produced negative emotion whatever the confederate did ([Two-factor theory of emotion, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-factor_theory_of_emotion)). A role-playing replication abstract concludes that "the role of arousal in emotion has been overstated" and that the data support at best an attenuated version, in which arousal can intensify emotion, mediated in part by attributions about its source ([A role-playing replication of Schachter and Singer (1962), Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/A-role-playing-replication-of-Schachter-and-(1962)-Manstead/2e8c936010679a6927ec7ade9c33878977dd0ddc)). An arXiv paper notes the study nonetheless inspired a large misattribution-of-arousal literature that was read as supporting the theory ([arXiv 2406.11086](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.11086)).

### Using it carefully

For essays, "cognitive labelling shapes how arousal is felt" is defensible. "Emotion is arousal plus a label" is a stronger claim that the replication record does not firmly support.
