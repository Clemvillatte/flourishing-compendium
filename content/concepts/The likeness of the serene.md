The likeness of the serene is the Stoic portrait of the wise person as Cato draws it in Cicero's De Finibus III: a character whose serenity is not mood but structure, following from the single judgment that moral worth is the only good. The peroration:

> "Then, how dignified, how lofty, how consistent is the character of the Wise Man as they depict it! Since reason has proved that moral worth is the sole good, it follows that he must always be happy... rightly also will he be styled beautiful, for the features of the soul are fairer than those of the body; rightly the one and only free man, as subject to no man's authority, and slave of no appetite; rightly unconquerable, for though his body be thrown into fetters, no bondage can enchain his soul." (Cicero, De Finibus III.75, tr. Rackham, [LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cicero/de_Finibus/3*.html))

### What the likeness carries

The grounding claim comes earlier in the same book: "inasmuch then as the final aim is to live in agreement and harmony with nature, it necessarily follows that all wise men at all times enjoy a happy, perfect and fortunate life, free from all hindrance, interference or want." The serenity is manufactured from two exclusions. First, the emotions: sorrow, fear, lust and delight "are not excited by any influence of nature; they are all of them mere fancies and frivolous opinions. Therefore the Wise Man will always be free from them." Second, externals: riches, kingship, freedom and beauty are redefined so that they belong to the person of good character whatever their circumstances, which is why the likeness reads as serene from any angle one views it. My principle Y116 carries the applied reading: be the portrait, since the likeness is meant as an image to resemble rather than a doctrine to admire. A compendium exercise drawn from the likeness is to take its list of the wise person's titles and ask which one is least true of oneself today, and what judgment that title rests on. What the likeness does not give: a claim that anyone has fully attained it; even Chrysippus confessed he had never known a Wise Man, so the portrait functions as direction rather than description.
