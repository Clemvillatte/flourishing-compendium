the finding that brutal honesty often serves the speaker's discharge, not the listener's benefit (Levine & Schweitzer, 2015).

Levine and Schweitzer's experiments pit honesty against benevolence and find that receivers, asked to judge, weight the benevolence. A lie told to benefit the listener can raise trust more than a truth told at the listener's expense, and intentions matter more than veracity.

> "Specifically, prosocial lies increase the willingness to pass money in the trust game, a behavioral measure of benevolence-based trust. In Studies 1a and 1b, we find that altruistic lies increase trust when deception is directly experienced and when it is merely observed. In Study 2, we demonstrate that mutually beneficial lies also increase trust. In Study 3, we disentangle the effects of intentions and deception; intentions are far more important than deception for building benevolence-based trust." (Levine and Schweitzer 2015, abstract, [Chicago Booth](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/research/rustandy/social-impact-research/research-papers/2015/prosocial-lies-when-deception-breeds-trust))
>
> "Prosocial lies increase benevolence-based trust, but harm integrity-based trust." (Levine and Schweitzer 2015, abstract, [Chicago Booth](https://www.chicagobooth.edu/research/rustandy/social-impact-research/research-papers/2015/prosocial-lies-when-deception-breeds-trust))

The finding cuts both ways for the speaker: the same sentence can build one kind of trust while eroding another, which is the study's own warning against reading it as a license.
