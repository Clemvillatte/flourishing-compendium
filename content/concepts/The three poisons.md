The three poisons are greed, hatred or aversion, and delusion. ([Princeton Dictionary of Buddhism, as quoted at the Buddha-Nature site](https://buddhanature.tsadra.org/index.php?title=Greed,_hatred,_and_ignorance)) The Mula Sutta identifies the corresponding three as roots of what is unskillful. ([Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an03/an03.069.than.html))

> "Monks, there are these three roots of what is unskillful. Which three? Greed is a root of what is unskillful, aversion is a root of what is unskillful, delusion is a root of what is unskillful." (Mula Sutta, AN 3.69, translated by [Thanissaro Bhikkhu, Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an03/an03.069.than.html))

The sutta then shows the poisons at work in the present, one by one. For greed it says:

> "Greed itself is unskillful. Whatever a greedy person fabricates by means of body, speech, or intellect, that too is unskillful." (Mula Sutta, AN 3.69, [Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an03/an03.069.than.html))

The same wording is then repeated for aversion and for delusion. In each case, the sutta says, the person's mind is "overcome" and "consumed", and the suffering that follows may be wrongly inflicted on another, so that "many evil, unskillful qualities" come into play. The practical point for a reader is how the sutta locates the problem. It is in the mind's own state, before any deed, since what the person "fabricates by means of body, speech, or intellect" inherits its quality from the root.

### What it is not

The "poison" image is a common rendering of the three roots. The sutta quoted here speaks of roots of what is unskillful, not of poisons, and the two terms are best treated as one teaching in two vocabularies. It is not a claim about personal worth: the sutta describes states of mind that can be recognised, and nothing in the passage quoted asks for self-condemnation.
