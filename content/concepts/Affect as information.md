Affect as information is the finding that people read their current feelings as data about whatever they are judging. In the original experiments, Schwarz and Clore (1983) found that people reported more happiness and life satisfaction when in a good mood than in a bad one, and that the impact of a bad mood disappeared when people attributed the feeling to a transient external source, such as a rainy day or an odd laboratory room. The feeling stayed; what changed was whether it counted as evidence about the topic of judgment.

> "It assumes that people attend to their feelings as a source of information, with different feelings providing different types of information." (Schwarz, [Feelings-as-Information Theory](https://dornsife.usc.edu/norbert-schwarz/wp-content/uploads/sites/231/2023/11/schwarz_feelings-as-information_7jan10.pdf), 2012)

### What the theory covers

Feelings-as-information theory extends the mood work to moods, emotions, metacognitive experiences and bodily sensations. Its later summary of the 1983 studies states that people attend to their momentary feelings in forming judgments, in effect asking themselves how they feel about something. The theory treats the feeling as one input among others, which is what the misattribution result shows: once a feeling is traced to a source irrelevant to the judgment, it stops informing the judgment.
