Affect as information is the account, in feelings-as-information theory, that people consult their current feelings when judging something. In Schwarz's description of the 1983 weather experiment, participants called on sunny days reported higher life-satisfaction ratings and a more positive mood than participants called on rainy days; asking about the weather before obtaining the ratings eliminated the negative-weather effect, and the same discounting effect was not found in sunny conditions. The theory treats a feeling's perceived relevance to the judgment, not just its valence, as central.

> "It assumes that people attend to their feelings as a source of information, with different feelings providing different types of information." (Schwarz, [Feelings-as-Information Theory](https://dornsife.usc.edu/norbert-schwarz/wp-content/uploads/sites/231/2023/11/schwarz_feelings-as-information_7jan10.pdf), author manuscript, opening paragraph; published as Schwarz 2012, Handbook of Theories of Social Psychology, pp. 289-308, Sage)

### What the theory covers

Feelings-as-information theory extends the mood work to moods, emotions, metacognitive experiences and bodily sensations. Its summary of the 1983 studies states that people attend to their momentary feelings in forming judgments, in effect asking themselves how they feel about something. Attributing feelings to an incidental source can reduce their perceived relevance; Schwarz also discusses overcorrection, a bias in the opposite direction, when people try to adjust without sufficient alternative information.
