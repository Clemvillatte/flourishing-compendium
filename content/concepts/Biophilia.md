“Biophilia” — Wilson, Biophilia.

- Capaldi et al., 2014
- Kahn, 1999

Edward O. Wilson's coinage names the human draw toward the living: the pull of a garden, a forest, a pet, even a window with a tree in it. His claim is that the affinity is part of evolved human nature, not a cultural taste.

> "According to E.O. Wilson, biophilia 'is the innately emotional affiliation of human beings to other living organisms. Innate means hereditary and hence part of ultimate human nature' (Wilson, 1991, p. 31)." — [Barbiero & Marconato, Visions for Sustainability 6 (2016), full text](https://ojs.unito.it/index.php/visions/article/download/1755/1642/0)

### What the definition commits to

The later formulation Wilson used is broader: biophilia as "the innate tendency to focus upon life and lifelike forms, and in certain circumstances to affiliate with them emotionally." A later review glosses the mechanism: "It is innate, but not instinctive, and it is based on a set of learning rules that appear to be genetically determined." Innate does not mean fixed, in other words: the tendency is prepared by evolution and filled in by each life. For the essays on flourishing, the concept explains why nature contact reliably shows up in the well-being research — and why its absence registers as a cost rather than a mere absence of pleasure.
