Yin and yang (陰陽) are the paired complementary principles that run through Chinese philosophy and medicine: yin names the shady, receptive, cooling, contracting side of a polarity, and yang names the sunny, active, warming, expanding side. The earliest Chinese characters for yin and yang are found in inscriptions made on oracle bones — skeletal remains used in ancient Chinese divination practices at least as early as the 14th century BCE — where they simply described natural phenomena such as weather conditions, especially the movement of the sun; in their earliest usages, yin and yang existed independently and were not connected ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)). The first written record of using the two characters together appears in a verse from the Shijing (Book of Songs): "Viewing the scenery at a hill, looking for yinyang" — indicating that yang is the sunny side and yin is the shady side of a hill ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)). The concept later became one of the dominant concepts shared by different schools throughout the history of Chinese philosophy ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)).

Three basic themes underlie nearly all deployments of the concept: (1) yinyang as the coherent fabric of nature and mind, exhibited in all existence; (2) yinyang as jiao (interaction) between the waxing and waning of the cosmic and human realms; and (3) yinyang as a process of harmonization ensuring a constant, dynamic balance of all things ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)). In none of these conceptions is there a valuational hierarchy, as if yin could be abstracted from yang (or vice versa), regarded as superior, or considered metaphysically separated and distinct — instead, yinyang is emblematic of valuational equality rooted in the unified, dynamic, and harmonized structure of the cosmos ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)).

## What it is NOT

Yin and yang are not a dualism of opposing forces in the Western moral sense — they are not good-versus-evil, spirit-versus-matter, or light-versus-dark in any hierarchy of value ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)). Each requires the other: yang is generated from yin and yin is generated from yang, and they mutually alternate ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)). Nor are yin and yang fixed essences of things; the most enduring interpretation relates them to qi as a dynamic and natural form of flowing energy, and the medical texts subdivide each organ further into yin and yang aspects — the activity or function of an organ is its yang aspect, while its substance is its yin aspect ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)). A situation can be yang relative to one thing and yin relative to another.

## Practical implications

The practical value of yin-yang thinking is diagnostic: it trains you to ask whether a problem is one of excess or deficiency, of over-activation or stagnation, and to seek dynamic balance rather than elimination of one side. The medical texts are explicit that yin should flow smoothly and yang should vivify steadily, and that they regulate themselves so as to maintain equilibrium ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/yinyang/)). For flourishing, this suggests that rest and activity, solitude and connection, and effort and ease are not opponents but complements, each meaningless without the other — and that health, whether of a body, a relationship, or a working life, is a matter of balance that must be continually re-tuned, not a state that, once achieved, stays.
