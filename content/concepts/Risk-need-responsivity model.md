Public Safety Canada states the three principles: "Risk principle: Match the level of service to the offender's risk to re-offend. Need principle: Assess criminogenic needs and target them in treatment. Responsivity principle: Maximize the offender's ability to learn from a rehabilitative intervention by providing cognitive behavioural treatment and tailoring the intervention to the learning style, motivation, abilities and strengths of the offender." The model was "first formalized in 1990 (Andrews, Bonta & Hoge)" and set within a general personality and cognitive social learning theory of criminal conduct ([Public Safety Canada, Risk-need-responsivity model for offender assessment and treatment](https://www.publicsafety.gc.ca/cnt/rsrcs/pblctns/rsk-nd-rspnsvty/index-en.aspx)).

### Correcting the seed's wording

The seed says the model matches "criminogenic needs with responsive service intensity". The sources split this into three separate matches. Intensity belongs to the risk principle, which says "the level of treatment services provided to the offender is proportional to the offender's risk to re-offend" (Public Safety Canada). Responsivity concerns the style and mode of delivery, in Andrews and Bonta's words, "in a style and mode that is responsive to the offender's learning style and ability" ([Andrews and Bonta, Rehabilitating Criminal Justice Policy and Practice, 2010, PDF](https://theacademy.sdsu.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/Andrews_Bonta_Criminal_Justice_Policy_2010.pdf)). That paper says the risk principle speaks to who should be treated and the need principle to what should be treated.

### Criminogenic needs

Criminogenic needs are "dynamic risk factors that are directly linked to criminal behaviour" (Public Safety Canada). A Sage article lists the most potent as antisocial personality pattern, antisocial attitudes and values, antisocial associates, and substance abuse; static factors such as criminal history cannot be changed by intervention ([Newsome and Cullen, The Risk-Need-Responsivity Model Revisited, 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/stoken/default+domain/WIs8M4SqaeRMpXmveEvm/full)). Non-criminogenic needs such as low self-esteem and personal distress are treated as tertiary, a point that matters for essays on suffering relief: the model targets recidivism, not well-being in general ([Risk Need Responsivity Model of Offender Rehabilitation, ERIC PDF](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/EJ1017929.pdf)).

### Evidence and scope

Andrews and Bonta report that programs adhering to the model "have been shown to reduce offender recidivism by up to 35%" (2010 PDF, written by the model's authors). A review of implementation says interventions adhering to all three principles are associated with significant reductions in recidivism, and those that did not have poorer outcomes, citing Andrews and Dowden (2005) ([Implementation of the Risk-Need-Responsivity Framework, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4376237/)). The ERIC article notes that RNR "is not a theory of intervention in itself", but a set of principles within which many interventions can be used.
