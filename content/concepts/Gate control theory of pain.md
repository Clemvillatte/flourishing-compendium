**Gate control theory of pain** — Melzack, Wall — spinal gates regulated by descending signals and touch determine how much pain reaches the brain.

Ronald Melzack and Patrick Wall published the theory as "Pain mechanisms: a new theory" in Science in 1965 ([PubMed 5320816](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/5320816/)). It remains historically central. The seed's summary is a fair outline of the idea, but the theory is not accepted as correct in its original form.

### What the theory proposed

The neuroscientist Lorne Mendell describes the original aim: to provide a mechanism for coding the nociceptive component of cutaneous sensory input, resolving a tension between few neurons that respond selectively to intense stimuli and the finding that small-fibre stimulation is needed for a stimulus to feel painful.

> "It incorporated recently discovered mechanisms of presynaptic control of synaptic transmission from large and small sensory afferents, which was suggested to 'gate' incoming information depending on the balance between these inputs." (Mendell 2014, Pain 155:210-216, [PubMed 24334188](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24334188/))

Other features he lists are the convergence of small and large sensory inputs on spinal neurons that pass information to the forebrain, and "the ability of descending control pathways to affect the biasing established by the gate". These correspond to the seed's "touch" (large-fibre input) and "descending signals".

### What has not held up

> "Although subsequent experiments and clinical findings have made clear that the model is not correct in detail, the general ideas put forth in the article and the experiments they prompted in both animals and patients have transformed our understanding of pain mechanisms." (Mendell 2014)

So the page should not say that a spinal gate has been shown to work as the 1965 diagram drew it. What survived is the broader idea that the nervous system modulates pain signals, from the periphery and from the brain.

### Clinical influence

A review of peripheral nerve stimulation says its "origin is based on the gate control theory", but that "the exact mechanism behind PNS' analgesic effect is largely unknown", and that evidence points to inflammatory pathways, the autonomic nervous system, endogenous pain inhibition pathways and cortical involvement as well. ([Lin et al. 2020, Pain Medicine, PubMed 32804230](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32804230/)) The history of pain theories before the gate is covered in [Moayedi & Davis 2013, Journal of Neurophysiology 109](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23034364/).

### What this page does not claim

The sources do not show that attention, mood or meditation close a spinal gate. The psychological reading in the compendium's principles is an extension of the modulation idea and not a finding of these papers.
