Reconstruction is John Dewey's name for the job he thought philosophy had on its hands in 1920: reworking inherited ideas and ways of thought that no longer fit the world science and industry had actually made. The book came out of lectures he gave at the Imperial University of Japan in Tokyo, and his prefatory note says the lectures were an "interpretation of the reconstruction of ideas and ways of thought now going on in philosophy."

Dewey's claim is that philosophy got tangled in inherited categories that block the expression of modern life. His diagnosis, verbatim from the first lecture:

> Philosophic reconstruction for the present is thus the endeavor to undo the entanglement and to permit the Baconian aspirations to come to a free and unhindered expression.

(John Dewey, Reconstruction in Philosophy, Lecture I, [Project Gutenberg #40089](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/40089))

The same passage describes what reconstructed philosophy esteems instead: intelligence not as the original shaper of the world but as "the purposeful energetic re-shaper of those phases of nature and life that obstruct social well-being," and the individual not as a self-sufficient ego but as the agent who re-creates the world through initiative, inventiveness and intelligently directed labor. That is the sense in which the compendium's [[P100 — Treat the past as resource, not prison]] invokes Dewey's term: the past's ideas are treated as material to be reworked, not as a prison to be endured.
