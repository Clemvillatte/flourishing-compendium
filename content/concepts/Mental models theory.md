Philip Johnson-Laird's mental models theory says people reason by imagining situations in which the premises are true. In his words: "we reason by imagining a situation in which the premises are true—that is, we construct a mental model of them. In the case of some premises, we may have to construct multiple models to capture multiple possibilities. A conclusion is necessary if it holds in all the models of the premises; it is probable if it holds in most of them; and it is possible if it holds in at least one of them" ([Profile of Philip N. Johnson-Laird, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1117174108)). The PNAS article states it as: "reasoning is a simulation of the world fleshed out with our knowledge, not a formal rearrangement of the logical skeletons of sentences" ([Mental models and human reasoning, PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1012933107)).

### Three assumptions

The article lists three. Each mental model "represents what is common to a distinct set of possibilities". Models are "iconic insofar as they can be". And "mental models of descriptions represent what is true at the expense of what is false", the principle of truth, which "reduces the load that models place on our working memory".

### Where errors come from

The seed note says errors come from considering too few models. The sources give two causes. First, load: "the greater the number of models humans have to construct, the greater the likelihood that we will draw an incorrect conclusion" (PNAS profile). Second, the principle of truth, which has "an unexpected consequence: it leads... to systematic errors in deduction".

The best-known case is the illusory inference. In a card problem where only one of three assertions is true, with an ace in two of them, "nearly everyone" says an ace is possible, yet it is not, because two assertions would then be true ([Mental Models, CUMINCAD PDF](https://papers.cumincad.org/data/works/att/e5c4.content.pdf)). Experiments on a set of such problems "yielded only 15% correct responses, whereas a contrasting set of control problems... yielded 91% correct responses", and the fallacies "tend to be compelling and to elicit judgments of high confidence", so they work as "cognitive illusions" (PNAS article). Illusions "can be reduced by making what is false more salient" (CUMINCAD PDF). Three experiments corroborated them and "eliminated a number of alternative explanations" ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10476604/)).

### Rationality and counterexamples

Johnson-Laird says "mental model theory implies that we are rational because we grasp that an inference is no good if we can think of a counterexample to it", adding "in practice, however, we often err, failing to find, search for, or heed a counterexample" (PNAS profile). Reasoners must "overcome the principle of truth and envisage fully explicit models" (PNAS article).

### Rival accounts

The theory competes with formal-rule theories. An eScholarship paper says illusions "are predicted by the model theory, but they refute current theories of reasoning based on formal rules of inference". Another paper offers a different account of one type of illusion, from a disjunction of conditionals ([PubMed 10856742](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10856742/)).
