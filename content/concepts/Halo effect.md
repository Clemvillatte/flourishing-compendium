The halo effect is the finding that a general evaluation of a person colours the judgments of their separate qualities ([Thorndike, 1920](https://people.ict.usc.edu/~gratch/CSCI534/Readings/Thordike1920.pdf)). Edward Thorndike named the error in 1920, from ratings of army officers by their superiors and of the employees of two large industrial corporations ([Thorndike, 1920](https://people.ict.usc.edu/~gratch/CSCI534/Readings/Thordike1920.pdf)):

> "Their ratings were apparently affected by a marked tendency to think of the person in general as rather good or rather inferior and to color the judgments of the qualities by this general feeling" (Thorndike, [A Constant Error in Psychological Ratings](https://people.ict.usc.edu/~gratch/CSCI534/Readings/Thordike1920.pdf), Journal of Applied Psychology 4(1), 1920, p. 25)

### Evidence that the alteration is unconscious

Nisbett and Wilson staged the same instructor on two videos, warm in one and cold in the other, then had students rate his appearance, mannerisms and accent ([Nisbett and Wilson, 1977](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/35_J_Personality_Social_Psychology_250_(Nisbett).pdf)). The global impression moved the attribute ratings:

> "These results indicate that global evaluations of a person can induce altered evaluations of the person's attributes, even when there is sufficient information to allow for independent assessments of them." (Nisbett and Wilson, [The Halo Effect: Evidence for Unconscious Alteration of Judgments](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/35_J_Personality_Social_Psychology_250_(Nisbett).pdf), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 35(4), 1977, abstract)

The same abstract reports that the subjects were unaware of the influence, and those who saw the cold instructor believed the direction of influence ran the other way, from disliked attributes to dislike of the man ([Nisbett and Wilson, 1977](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/35_J_Personality_Social_Psychology_250_(Nisbett).pdf)). Thorndike's own remedy was procedural: the observer should report the evidence, not a rating, and the rating for each quality should be given separately, without knowledge of the evidence concerning any other quality in the same individual ([Thorndike, 1920](https://people.ict.usc.edu/~gratch/CSCI534/Readings/Thordike1920.pdf), p. 29).
