[[T002 — Aristotle|Aristotle]]'s argument for rest is not that relaxation is pleasant, though he allows that it is. It is that rest is what work is for. The formula sits in Book X of the Nicomachean Ethics, and it is deliberately blunt:

> "Also, Happiness is thought to stand in perfect rest; for we toil that we may rest, and war that we may be at peace." — [Aristotle, NE X.7, Chase translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/8438/8438-h/8438-h.htm)

The same chapter carries the two-sided teaching. First, rest is not itself the end: "amusement is like rest, and men want rest because unable to labour continuously. Rest, therefore, is not an End, because it is adopted with a view to Working afterwards." Rest is a renewal, not a destination. Second, happiness is not amusement — "the very notion is absurd of the End being amusement, and of one's toiling and enduring hardness all one's life long with a view to amusement." The point is directional: everything in the practical life is chosen for something further, and rest is one of the few things that, properly placed, can be chosen for what it restores.

Aristotle ties this to contemplation. The contemplative life is the most self-sufficient and the most restful because it is "rested in for its own sake, since nothing results from it beyond the fact of having contemplated." Rest, for him, shades into the highest activity rather than standing against activity as its opposite. Pieper's later recovery of the Greek scholē runs with exactly this thread.

The modern attention sciences have their own version of the same point, stripped of the metaphysics. Real breaks restore attention; pseudo-breaks do not. Kang and Kurtzberg's 414-participant experiment found phone breaks impaired second-half anagram performance relative to paper or computer breaks, and Lim and Dinges's meta-analysis of 147 cognitive tests found short-term sleep deprivation produces its largest effect on simple attention (g = -0.776). Aristotle's claim, translated, survives the translation.
