"Verificationism"

Verificationism is the doctrine, central to the logical positivism of the Vienna and Berlin Circles of the 1920s and 1930s, that a statement carries cognitive meaning only if it is empirically verifiable or true by definition. Its blade was aimed at metaphysics, theology and ethics: a claim for which no possible observation could count for or against it was classed not as false but as meaningless. The movement's own slogan came from Moritz Schlick, in a formulation Britannica's Feigl calls "rather careless":

> "The meaning of a [declarative sentence] is the method of its verification" (Schlick, quoted in Feigl, "Positivism," Encyclopaedia Britannica)

The criterion was, in Britannica's words, "the most noteworthy, and also most controversial, contribution of the logical positivists." The controversy is structural: the criterion itself is neither an empirical observation nor a definition, so by its own test it struggles to mean anything, and later work replaced strict verifiability with weaker confirmability and falsifiability standards. For the compendium's purposes the interest runs the other way: verificationism is the hard edge of [[P022 — Verify in experience — test beliefs by living them]]. The principle asks a question verificationism inspired: what experience would show this belief true or false? A belief that cannot answer is not thereby worthless, but it is being held on something other than evidence.
