Truthfulness, in Aristotle's account in Nicomachean Ethics IV.7, is not keeping agreements (that belongs to justice) but the character of a person who is true in word and in life where nothing is at stake. The chapter says:

> "The boastful man, then, is thought to be apt to claim the things that bring glory, when he has not got them, or to claim more of them than he has, and the mock-modest man on the other hand to disclaim what he has or belittle it, while the man who observes the mean is one who calls a thing by its own name, being truthful both in life and in word, owning to what he has, and neither more nor less."
> — [Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.7, trans. W. D. Ross (MIT Classics)](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.4.iv.html)

The truthful person is the mean between the boaster and the mock-modest dissembler, and both extremes are culpable, "particularly the boastful man": "it is the boaster that seems to be opposed to the truthful man; for he is the worse character" ([NE IV.7, Ross](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.4.iv.html)). Aristotle adds a tonal detail: the truthful person "inclines rather to understate the truth; for this seems in better taste because exaggerations are wearisome" ([NE IV.7, Ross](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.4.iv.html)).
