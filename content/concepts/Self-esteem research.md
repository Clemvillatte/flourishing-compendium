Self-esteem research, in the line of work associated with Michael Kernis, learned to distinguish the form of a person's self-esteem from its level. Kernis's 2003 article distinguishes high self-esteem from optimal self-esteem and proposes secure and fragile forms.

> "High self-esteem can be fragile or secure depending upon the extent to which it is defensive or genuine, contingent or true, unstable or stable, and discrepant or congruent with implicit (nonconscious) feelings of self-worth" (Kernis, 2003, [abstract](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/TARGET-ARTICLE:-Toward-a-Conceptualization-of-Kernis/90e6fe5d604dee8d9662f988780b056c3989d13e)).

### Four markers of the difference

A review summarising this programme lists the four markers. Genuine high self-esteem contrasts with a defensive combination of interior negative self-feelings publicly presented positively; congruence between explicit self-report and implicit self-worth contrasts with a gap between them; true self-esteem contrasts with a contingent kind that depends on meeting standards and winning others' endorsement; and stability contrasts with short-term fluctuation in contextually based feelings of worth ([Cameranesi & Piotrowski, 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7163827/)).

### Why the distinction matters

The two forms behave differently. "Conversely, secure and well-anchored self-esteem does not require continual validation, is not highly vulnerable to threats, and reflects assertive feelings of self-worth associated to positive psychological adjustment and well-being" (Cameranesi & Piotrowski, 2017). Instability, by contrast, changes what self-esteem level predicts: "Unstable self-esteem was found to moderate the associations between self-esteem levels and global distress, depression, hopelessness, rejection sensitivity, negative affect, anxiety, hostility, interpersonal sensitivity, hostility, verbal and physical aggression, and psychological well-being" (Cameranesi & Piotrowski, 2017).
