Mill devotes the third chapter of On Liberty to individuality as an element of well-being, not a luxury. His objection to borrowed plans of life is blunt:

> "He who lets the world, or his own portion of it, choose his plan of life for him, has no need of any other faculty than the ape-like one of imitation." (Mill, On Liberty, ch. III, [Project Gutenberg #34901](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/34901))

His defence of experiments in living rests on the person's own judgment:

> "If a person possesses any tolerable amount of common-sense and experience, his own mode of laying out his existence is the best, not because it is the best in itself, but because it is his own mode." (Mill, On Liberty, ch. III, [Project Gutenberg #34901](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/34901))

And the positive picture is not selfishness but a richer common stock:

> "It is not by wearing down into uniformity all that is individual in themselves, but by cultivating it and calling it forth, within the limits imposed by the rights and interests of others, that human beings become a noble and beautiful object of contemplation…" (Mill, On Liberty, ch. III, [Project Gutenberg #34901](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/34901); the sentence continues)

The passage is explicit that the same things can be helps to one person and hindrances to another: "The same things which are helps to one person towards the cultivation of his higher nature, are hindrances to another," and "Customs are made for customary circumstances, and customary characters: and his circumstances or his character may be uncustomary" (ch. III, same text). The chapter's condition on all of it is the same as the book's: your experiments stop where the rights and interests of others begin, a limit Mill states inside the very sentence that praises cultivation of the individual.

**Plain words** A life you copied from others needs no faculty but imitation, and it is not even comfortable for the person living it. A life laid out by the person living it fits better, and the variety of such lives is what everyone else gets to look at, since Mill's sentence about the noble object of contemplation continues directly into human life becoming rich, diversified and animating by the same process.
