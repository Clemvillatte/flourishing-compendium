### Definition

Social investment theory holds that adult personality grows up because life asks it to. As people take on age-graded roles, work, marriage, parenthood, community, those roles demand and reward reliability, and the traits follow. In the fifty-year longitudinal review that tested the idea:

> The first type describes normative trait development, conceptually exemplified by social investment theory (Roberts, Wood & Smith., 2005; Roberts, Wood & Caspi, 2008), which posits that predictable patterns of personality development accompany aging as people engage in social institutions such as education, the labor market, and marriage/long-term relationships. ([Woods et al., PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8318356/))

### Origin

The theory is Brent Roberts and Avshalom Caspi's answer to the puzzle that personality is both stable and changeable: traits drift in normative directions across adulthood, and social investment explains the drift. The evidence base is Lodi-Smith and Roberts's 2007 meta-analysis of 94 studies, which found that "the extent of investment in social roles across these domains is positively related to agreeableness, conscientiousness, emotional stability, and low psychoticism", with the ties strongest when people are psychologically committed to the role rather than merely occupying it.

### How it explains growth

The mechanism is commitment, not mileage. Signing the mortgage does nothing; caring about the household does. The review distinguishes actual investment of time and effort from psychological investment, "investment and commitment in components of the work domain, relevant to self-narratives and identity", and reports that "both forms of investment predict trait development" ([Woods et al., PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8318356/)). Character, on this view, is the residue of promises kept.

### A real-life example

Two people take the same job. One clocks in; the other takes the role to heart, cares about the outcomes, and identifies with the work. Five years on, the second has become measurably more conscientious and steady. The role did it, but only because it was taken seriously.
