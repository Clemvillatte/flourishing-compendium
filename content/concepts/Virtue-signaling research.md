Virtue-signaling research is the empirical and philosophical study of conspicuous moral position-taking. The philosophical core was framed by Justin Tosi and Brandon Warmke as moral grandstanding, and the definition now common in the empirical literature: "According to philosophical accounts, Moral Grandstanding is the use of moral talk to seek social status" ([Grubbs, Warmke, Tosi, James & Campbell, 2019, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0223749)).

### What the studies found

The main empirical test of the construct ran six studies, over 7,000 participants across undergraduate, nationally matched and longitudinal samples, and reported: "Across studies, we found initial support for the validity of Moral Grandstanding as a construct. Specifically, moral grandstanding motivation was associated with status-seeking personality traits, as well as greater political and moral conflict in daily life" ([Grubbs et al., 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0223749)). The cynicism side of the concept is Tosi and Warmke's prediction, as summarized in a later philosophical treatment: "Virtue signalling, they claim, may induce moral cynicism, because those who engage it are not sincere in claiming to call attention to injustices", causing "a 'devaluation of the social currency of moral talk' (Tosi and Warmke 2016, p. 210)" ([Synthese, quoting Tosi & Warmke](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s11229-020-02653-9.pdf)). An audience that comes to expect performance in moral talk discounts the currency for everyone.
