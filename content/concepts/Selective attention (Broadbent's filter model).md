Selective attention is the capacity to process some information and not other information, and Broadbent's filter model (1958) was the first influential account of how that happens: a limited-capacity channel with a filter in front of it. Treisman (1964) revised it with attenuation, and Lavie (1995) later tied the early-or-late question to perceptual load. The summaries below come from later papers; the original 1958 and 1964 texts were not opened.

### Broadbent: an early filter

As later reviewers describe it, Broadbent's model puts the filter early. Information is selected for the limited-capacity channel on its physical characteristics of the signal (in hearing: frequency, intensity, timing), before its meaning is extracted, so unattended input is filtered out in an all-or-nothing way. The observation that people hear their own name in an unattended channel (Moray, 1959) strained that account. ([Marsh et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6994418/); [PMC8006147](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8006147/))

### Treisman: attenuation, not blocking

> "Attenuation theory was a response to findings that were beginning to challenge the early-filter model proposed by Broadbent (1958)." (Marsh et al. 2019, [PMC6994418](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6994418/))

In Treisman's version, unselected input is weakened, not removed. It can still reach a stage of processing where meaning is analysed if it is important enough, which is why a name can break through.

### Lavie: load decides

Lavie's load theory keeps the idea of a fixed capacity but makes the early-or-late question depend on the task. High perceptual load can eliminate distractor processing; low load lets distractors through.

> "Distractors' interference was found only under low-load conditions." (Lavie 1995, [PubMed 7790827](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7790827/))

Her 2005 review adds a practical point: "simply instructing people to ignore goal-irrelevant stimuli is not sufficient for preventing their processing." High load on frontal cognitive control, by contrast, increases distractor processing. ([PubMed 15668100](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15668100/))
