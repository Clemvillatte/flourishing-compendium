To kalon (τὸ καλόν) is the ancient Greek term Aristotle uses for what a virtuous act aims at: a word that "can mean 'beautiful', 'noble', or 'fine'", and that he invokes again and again in the Nicomachean Ethics. The Stanford Encyclopedia's account:

> "He often says, in the course of his discussion, that when the good person chooses to act virtuously, he does so for the sake of the 'kalon'—a word that can mean 'beautiful', 'noble', or 'fine' (see for example 1120a23–4)." ([Kraut, SEP, Aristotle's Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/))

### What the fine adds to the good

The term carries an aesthetic charge into ethics: "This term indicates that Aristotle sees in ethical activity an attraction that is comparable to the beauty of well-crafted artifacts, including such artifacts as poetry, music, and drama" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/)). A well-executed virtuous act, like a well-made craft product, "will not merely be advantageous but kalon as well—for the balance it strikes is part of what makes it advantageous" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/)). The training of character runs on this attraction: "The young person learning to acquire the virtues must develop a love of doing what is kalon and a strong aversion to its opposite—the aischron, the shameful and ugly" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/)). And it is not easy: "Determining what is kalon is difficult (1106b28–33, 1109a24–30)" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/)).

### In the compendium

- [[A041 — Know that virtue aims at the beautiful (to kalon)]] carries the term as its center: virtue's reward is not only usefulness but the beauty of the act itself, and the principle asks the reader to notice that beauty as a reason to act.
