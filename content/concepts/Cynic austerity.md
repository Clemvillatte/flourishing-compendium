Cynic austerity is the deliberate, trained simplicity of the Cynic philosophers, with Diogenes of Sinope as the template. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy explains that the Cynics advocate "askēsis, or practice, over theory as the means to free oneself from convention, promote self-sufficiency, and live in accord with nature," and that this practice "leads the Cynic to live in poverty, embrace hardship and toil" ([IEP: Cynics](https://iep.utm.edu/cynics/)).

### Training, not only deprivation

The term askēsis "is appropriated from athletic training." The IEP gives examples: "Antisthenes praised toil and hardship as goods; Diogenes of Sinope walked barefoot in the snow, hugged cold statues, and rolled about in the scalding summer sand in his pithos; Crates rid himself of his considerable wealth in order to become a Cynic" ([IEP: Cynics](https://iep.utm.edu/cynics/)).

### Why simple

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on ancient ethics says that for Diogenes "natural life is simpler." It cites Diogenes Laertius for a story in which he "saw a child drinking out of its hand" and "threw away his cup, saying that a child had bested him in frugality." The entry adds that Diogenes held the simple life to be a release from unnecessary concerns and also essential to virtue ([SEP: Ancient Ethical Theory](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-ancient/)). Diogenes Laertius also records Diogenes opposing "courage" to fortune, "nature" to convention and "reason" to passion, and the IEP says he held that he lived the good life despite being "a homeless and penniless exile" ([IEP: Diogenes of Sinope](https://iep.utm.edu/diogenes-of-sinope/)).

### Scope

The sources describe Diogenes mostly through Diogenes Laertius, who wrote centuries later, and the IEP's survey of ancient Greek philosophy warns this is "historically problematic" ([IEP: Ancient Greek Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/ancient-greek-philosophy/)). The Lives themselves were not read directly for this page. Cynic austerity was extreme, and the sources do not suggest it as a model for ordinary life. The compendium's principle uses the idea more gently, as an invitation to drop excess.
