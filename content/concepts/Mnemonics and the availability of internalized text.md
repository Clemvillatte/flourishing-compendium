This page covers, for the sense the compendium's principle M014 uses, the memory research on how material learned by heart stays available. The heading is the compendium's own phrase; the research it points to is the retrieval-practice literature. Roediger and Karpicke compared later recall of prose passages after repeated studying or free-recall tests without feedback ([Psychol Sci 17(3), 2006, PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/)):

> "Taking a memory test not only assesses what one knows, but also enhances later retention, a phenomenon known as the testing effect." ([Roediger & Karpicke, Psychol Sci 17(3), 2006, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/))

### What the study found

In two experiments, students studied prose passages and took one or three immediate free-recall tests, without feedback, or restudied the material the same number of times ([same record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/)). Students then took a final retention test 5 minutes, 2 days, or 1 week later. When the final test was given after 5 minutes, repeated studying improved recall relative to repeated testing ([same record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/)). The reversal came with time:

> "However, on the delayed tests, prior testing produced substantially greater retention than studying, even though repeated studying increased students' confidence in their ability to remember the material. Testing is a powerful means of improving learning, not just assessing it." ([same record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/))
