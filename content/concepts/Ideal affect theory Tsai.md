**Ideal affect theory Tsai** — cultures differ in the emotions they value and want to feel; people act to move toward their ideal affect.

Tsai's affect valuation theory (AVT) distinguishes how people want to feel (ideal affect) from how they actually feel (actual affect). Actual affect can be an immediate response to an event or an aggregate of responses over time. Ideal affect is a goal or desired state.

> The authors propose that how people want to feel ("ideal affect") differs from how they actually feel ("actual affect") and that cultural factors influence ideal more than actual affect. (Tsai, Knutson & Fung 2006, abstract, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16536652/); AVT premises restated in Tsai's "Dynamics of ideal affect" chapter, pp. 121-122, [Stanford Culture and Emotion Lab](https://culture-emotion-lab.stanford.edu/sites/culture_emotion_lab/files/media/file/tsai_dynamics_of_ideal_affect_2013.pdf))

### Excitement versus calm

Tsai's chapter reports that cultural values accounted for more variance in ideal than actual affect, while temperamental factors accounted for more variance in actual than ideal affect. In two studies controlling for actual affect, European American and Asian American participants valued high-arousal positive states such as excitement more than Hong Kong Chinese participants. Hong Kong Chinese and Asian American participants valued low-arousal positive states such as calm more than European American participants. For all groups in those studies, the discrepancy between ideal and actual affect correlated with depression. The chapter reports that people who valued excitement more preferred exciting over calm music and products, and rated physicians promoting energetic lifestyles more positively.
