**Ideal affect theory Tsai** — cultures differ in the emotions they value and want to feel; people act to move toward their ideal affect.

Ideal affect theory, later called affect valuation theory (AVT), is Jeanne Tsai's framework for the feelings people want to have. Its founding proposal separates two variables: how people want to feel, and how they actually feel. Actual affect is a response; ideal affect is a goal or desired state that people work to attain.

> "The authors propose that how people want to feel ('ideal affect') differs from how they actually feel ('actual affect') and that cultural factors influence ideal more than actual affect." (Tsai, Knutson & Fung 2006, [abstract, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16536652/))

### Excitement versus calm

The theory's second premise is that culture shapes ideal affect more than actual affect, while temperament does more of the work for actual affect. Its best-known contrast is between positive states of different arousal: in the 2006 studies, European American and Asian American individuals valued high-arousal positive affect such as excitement more than Hong Kong Chinese individuals, who valued low-arousal positive affect such as calm more. For all groups, the gap between ideal and actual affect correlated with depression. The theory's third premise is behavioral: the more people value excitement states, the more they prefer exciting music and products and rate physicians who promote energetic lifestyles.
