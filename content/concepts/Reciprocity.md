(Gouldner, 1960).

Gouldner's classic statement gives the norm its two demands: help those who have helped you, and do not injure those who have helped you.

> "I suggest that a norm of reciprocity, in its universal form, makes two interrelated, minimal demands: (1) people should help those who have helped them, and (2) people should not injure those who have helped them." (Gouldner, 1960, p. 171, [full text scan](http://kpolanyi.scoolaid.net:8080/xmlui/handle/10694/347))

### Three things called reciprocity

Gouldner opens by separating three things that get called reciprocity: a pattern of mutually contingent exchange of gratifications, an existential or folk belief in reciprocity, and a generalised moral norm ([p. 161](http://kpolanyi.scoolaid.net:8080/xmlui/handle/10694/347)). The norm is the third of these. On its reach he quotes Westermarck:

> "To requite a benefit, or to be grateful to him who bestows it, is probably everywhere, at least under certain circumstances, regarded as a duty." (Westermarck, quoted in Gouldner, 1960, p. 161, [full text](http://kpolanyi.scoolaid.net:8080/xmlui/handle/10694/347))

### Reciprocity and power

The norm is one support for exchange among others. Gouldner observes that if one party is considerably more powerful than the other, it may force benefits from the weaker party with little or no reciprocity; such arrangements are less stable than those in which reciprocity motivates the services, but they are sociologically real ([p. 164](http://kpolanyi.scoolaid.net:8080/xmlui/handle/10694/347)).

### Starting things

The norm also operates at the start of relationships. People are continually brought together in new combinations, and Gouldner suggests that the norm of reciprocity is one among the starting mechanisms by which ephemeral contacts settle into social systems ([pp. 176-177](http://kpolanyi.scoolaid.net:8080/xmlui/handle/10694/347)). A first small service creates a reason for a second. The compendium's SO039 records Axelrod's later answer to the question of how cooperation holds up over time: cooperation based on reciprocity can be self-policing ([[SO039|Axelrod's durability-of-relationship account]]).
