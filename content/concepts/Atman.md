Within Śaṅkara's Advaita Vedānta, the ātman is identified with [[Brahman]] and described as pure, non-intentional consciousness. Dalal distinguishes the empirical description of consciousness as a witnessing presence from the absolute account, which no longer assigns it that relational role ([Śaṅkara, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)).

From the empirical standpoint, consciousness is that by which mental cognition is revealed as known, and it is present while mental experiences change. The tradition traces the appearance of an individual person (jīva) to ignorance (avidyā), which confounds self and not-self; realization ([[Moksha]]) is the knowledge "I am Brahman" (ahaṃ brahmāsmi, BṛU 1.4.10), which negates the error of superimposition ([Śaṅkara, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/); [IEP Advaita Vedanta](https://iep.utm.edu/advaita-vedanta/)).

[[Maya (illusion)|māyā]] is the tradition's term for the appearing power involved. Related here: [[Anatta]], [[Ziran (naturalness)]].

### Knower and known

The IEP's summary of the tradition keeps the two sides of that distinction in one image:

> "The experiencing self (jīva) and the transcendental self of the Universe (ātman) are in reality identical (both are Brahman), though the individual self seems different as space within a container seems different from space as such." ([IEP, Advaita Vedanta](https://iep.utm.edu/advaita-vedanta/))

The container does the analogy's whole work: nothing is added to space by the jar, and nothing is lost when the jar breaks. A second line carries the epistemic side: "The self alone is the knower and the rest are knowable as objects of knowledge." What examines the mind is not one more item the mind can examine.

> "The fundamental thrust of Advaita Vedānta is that the ātman is pure non-intentional consciousness. It is one without a second, nondual, infinite existence, and numerically identical with brahman." (Dalal, [Śaṅkara](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/), Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, opening overview)
