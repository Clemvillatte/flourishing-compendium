Nomadology is Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari's name for their account of the nomad and the "war machine", set out in the plateau "1227: Treatise on Nomadology — The War Machine" in A Thousand Plateaus (translated by Brian Massumi). Its most counterintuitive claim is that the nomad is defined by not moving:

> "It is thus false to define the nomad by movement. Toynbee is profoundly right to suggest that the nomad is on the contrary he who does not move. Whereas the migrant leaves behind a milieu that has become amorphous or hostile, the nomad is one who does not depart, does not want to depart, who clings to the smooth space left by the receding forest, where the steppe or the desert advances, and who invents nomadism as a response to this challenge." (Deleuze & Guattari, [Nomadology: The War Machine](https://arl.human.cornell.edu/linked%20docs/Deleuze-Guattari_Nomadology.pdf))

The chapter's second axiom states that "the war machine is the invention of the nomads (insofar as it is exterior to the State apparatus and distinct from the military institution)", and that the machine's "sole and veritable positive object (nomos)" is smooth space, the open steppe, with war resulting only when the machine collides with States and cities.

Behind the paradox of the motionless nomad sits a distinction the text states plainly: "Movement is extensive; speed is intensive."

What the passage establishes about the distinction is less than the distinction's full theory. The text marks movement as extensive and speed as intensive, and the passage around the nomad's definition shows what the nomad holds onto: the smooth space left by the receding forest, and nomadism as the response invented to that challenge. The migrant leaves behind a milieu that has become amorphous or hostile; the nomad does not depart, does not want to depart. The two figures are therefore sorted not by how far they go but by what they do when their ground changes, and that is as far as the quoted material takes the contrast.
