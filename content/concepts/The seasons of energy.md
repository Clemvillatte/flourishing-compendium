The seasons of energy is the acceptance that a person's capacities are not constant. There are times of high output and times of low, and the low times are not defects in an otherwise steady machine. The seed for the entry is Ecclesiastes 3:1–8:

> "To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven: a time to be born, and a time to die; a time to plant, and a time to pluck up that which is planted; ... a time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance."
> — [Ecclesiastes 3:1–4, King James Version, Bible Hub](https://biblehub.com/kjv/ecclesiastes/3.htm)

The passage itself is about times appointed to human activities, not about energy. The application to a person's own fluctuating capacities is a reading of it, and a natural one: the poem's structure pairs each activity with its opposite, and its counsel is not to force one time's work into another time. Read that way, it is a warrant for planning around one's cycles rather than against them.

One caution travels with the idea. There is a difference between a season and a symptom. A period of unusually low energy, or a swing between extremes, can be the ordinary weather of a life or it can be a pattern with a clinical name, and the reading above does not decide which. The parallel entry on bipolar disorder makes the point: variability is not the same thing as the specific affective pattern that disorder describes.
