## In plain words

Allostasis theory is Peter Sterling and Joseph Eyer's account of how the body stays stable: not by holding every parameter at one fixed point, but by shifting them to match demands. Their founding statement reads: "To maintain stability an organism must vary all the parameters of its internal milieu and match them appropriately to environmental demands. We refer to this principle as allostasis, meaning 'stability through change'" ([Sterling & Eyer 1988, quoted at INHN](https://inhn.org/inhn-projects/ebooks/default-title-4)).

## Where it comes from

The term appeared in Sterling and Eyer's 1988 chapter in the Handbook of Life Stress, Cognition and Health, as a reworking of Walter Cannon's homeostasis. Bruce McEwen then built the health consequences on top of it.

## How it works

The contrast with homeostasis is a matter of what stays fixed. As a recent review puts it, "Homeostasis adapts to stress by maintaining parameters at a constant set point (B), while allostasis adapts to stress by adjusting parameters to a new set point (C)" ([Park et al. 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-025-08939-3)). McEwen's definition of the process: "the term 'allostasis' was introduced by Sterling and Eyer to refer to the active process by which the body responds to daily events and maintains homeostasis (allostasis literally means 'achieving stability through change')" ([McEwen 2006](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.31887/DCNS.2006.8.4/bmcewen)).

## What it explains

The theory explains why adaptation itself has a cost. When the shifting runs too often or never switches off, the bill comes due: "we introduced the term 'allostatic load or overload' to refer to the wear and tear that results from either too much stress or from inefficient management of allostasis, eg, not turning off the response when it is no longer needed" ([McEwen 2006](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.31887/DCNS.2006.8.4/bmcewen)). On this reading, "the difference between the original and new set points represents the cumulative cost of adaptation to stress" ([Park et al. 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s42003-025-08939-3)).

## Everyday life

A week of poor sleep and looming deadlines keeps the shifting machinery running; you feel wired at midnight and flat by morning. Allostasis theory describes this as load rather than malfunction, which reframes recovery: sleep, downtime and completed stress responses are how the system pays down its bill.
