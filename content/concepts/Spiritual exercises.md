Spiritual exercises is Pierre Hadot's name for the practice side of ancient philosophy ([IEP, "Pierre Hadot"](https://iep.utm.edu/hadot/)). On his reading the schools taught a way of life, and the exercises were its method: the daily, repeated work by which a student worked toward the "complete reversal of our usual ways of looking at things", in the IEP's rendering of Hadot's account ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/hadot/)). The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy's summary of the account:

> "These exercises encompassed all of those practices still associated with philosophical teaching and study: reading, listening, dialogue, inquiry, and research. However, they also included practices deliberately aimed at addressing the student's larger way of life, and demanding daily or continuous repetition: practices of attention (prosoche), meditations (meletai), memorizations of dogmata, self-mastery (enkrateia), the therapy of the passions, the remembrance of good things, the accomplishment of duties, and the cultivation of indifference towards indifferent things (PWL 84)." ([Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Pierre Hadot", section 5 "Spiritual Practices"](https://iep.utm.edu/hadot/))

The named exercises Hadot keeps returning to include the Stoic premeditation of evils, the examination of conscience guided by a master, meditation on mortality as philosophy's way of learning how to die, and the view from above, which is intended to challenge the importance assigned to fame, luxury and other personal concerns ([IEP, section 5 "Spiritual Practices"](https://iep.utm.edu/hadot/)). Hadot also uses "existential" as the adjective, and the practices call on rhetoric and imagination, not reason alone ([IEP, section 5](https://iep.utm.edu/hadot/)). The compendium's training principles sit on this terrain, among them daily consistency (D054), the day bookended by intention and review (B192), and truths treated as tasks rather than beliefs (B008).
