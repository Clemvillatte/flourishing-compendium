In Spinoza's Ethics, an affect is any change in the body's power of acting together with the idea of that change. The definition replaces the older vocabulary of passions with a term that covers both active and passive states.

> "By 'affect' I understand states of a body by which its power of acting is increased or lessened, helped or hindered, and also the ideas of these states. Thus, if we can be the adequate cause of any of these states, the affect in question is what I call an 'action'; otherwise it is a 'passion'." — Spinoza, Ethics Part III, Definition 3, trans. Bennett ([Early Modern Texts](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/spinoza1665part3.pdf))

The three primary affects are desire (the striving to persevere in being, or conatus), joy, and sadness. Joy is the passage to a greater perfection, sadness the passage to a lesser one. Love and hate are compound affects: joy or sadness accompanied by the idea of an external cause.

Because affects track increases and decreases in power of acting, they can be read as signals rather than mere disturbances. A joy indicates that something has strengthened one's power; a sadness that something has weakened it.
