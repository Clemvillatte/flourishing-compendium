Research on schadenfreude — pleasure at others' misfortune — showing it erodes trust and invites reciprocal hostility. That is the seed's legacy formulation. The opened study measures global impression and avoidance intentions, not trust erosion or reciprocal hostility. What the research shows is below.

## What the research shows

Schadenfreude is the malicious satisfaction and pleasure felt at someone else's misfortune. An Italian research team led by Stefano Pagliaro tested what happens when this pleasure is expressed publicly and other people witness it.

In two studies, each with 90 undergraduates, participants read a fictional scenario about a job interview. Study 1 compared a rival candidate who gloated over a third candidate's car accident with one who broke a stated rule and one who showed no reaction at all. Study 2 dropped the rule-breaker and compared the gloating rival under two conditions: one in which observers were not competing with the victim, and one in which they were. Participants then rated their global impression of the rival and their behavioral intentions toward him.

The finding, in the authors' summary:

> Two studies consistently showed that observing someone who expresses schadenfreude is detrimental for both the impression that we form about this person and our willingness to approach him.
> — Pagliaro, Brambilla, Di Prinzio & Teresi, "Observing schadenfreude," Psicologia Sociale, 2019, §4, General discussion ([author copy](https://www.mbrambilla.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/Pagliaro-et-al-2019-Schadenfreude.pdf))

The pattern behind that summary is worth stating precisely. In Study 1, observers rated the gloating candidate more negatively than the neutral candidate, and the rule-breaking candidate also rated worse than neutral. Between the gloater and the rule-breaker, the impression difference went in the predicted direction but did not reach statistical significance. On avoidance intentions the results were sharper. Participants showed a stronger intention to avoid the gloater than both the rule-breaker and the neutral candidate. In both studies, the authors' statistical mediation analysis found the effect on avoidance intentions ran through the impression measure, and they present that as the mechanism.

The second study asked whether observers would react differently if they themselves were competing with the victim. Avoidance intentions exceeded the control condition in both competition conditions, and no statistically significant difference was detected between those two conditions. The impression did shift somewhat, with somewhat less negative judgments when observers were in competition with the victim, though the authors note the scenarios left participants somewhat in competition with the gloater in any case, since all were applying for the same job.

The authors' closing line is modest and firm. Even just observing schadenfreude produces important consequences for social interactions. Those consequences, in these studies, are measured as impressions and intentions.

## In the compendium

The principle "If you must fight, grieve — never gloat" states its own counsel: gloating damages trust and invites reprisal. That is a claim about conduct, offered from the philosophy side of the compendium. This research gives it empirical company on the dimensions that were actually measured. A gloater loses standing in the eyes of onlookers, and onlookers intend to keep their distance.

The practice of veneration over contempt runs in the same direction. Its parallel entry lists schadenfreude's costs among the reasons to refuse contempt. The pleasure is real, and so is the bill that follows it.
