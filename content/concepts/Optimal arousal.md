“Optimal arousal” — Yerkes-Dodson law.

The law comes from 1908 experiments in which dancing mice learned a white-black discrimination habit under electric stimulation of varying strength. The relation between stimulus strength and speed of learning depended on how hard the task was: for easy discriminations, the stronger stimulus favored habit formation, while for difficult discriminations an intermediate strength was best — and the harder the task, the weaker the optimal stimulus.

> "A stimulus whose strength is nearer to the threshold than to the point of harmful stimulation is most favorable to the acquisition of a habit." (Yerkes & Dodson, 1908)

The inverted-U shape shows up in the difficult condition: learning speeds up as stimulation rises from the threshold, then past a point turns and falls.

> "When the boxes differ only slightly in brightness and discrimination is extremely difficult the rapidity of learning at first rapidly increases as the strength of the stimulus is increased from the threshold, but, beyond an intensity of stimulation which is soon reached, it begins to decrease." (Yerkes & Dodson, 1908)
