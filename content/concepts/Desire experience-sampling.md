desires occupy roughly half of waking hours and resisting them is effortful (Hofmann et al., 2012).

The first half of this line holds up. The second half needs care: the same study describes desires as "largely unproblematic", and resistance was needed for a minority of them. The findings come from 205 adults in the German city of Würzburg, who reported on their desires over a week; they are self-reports from one sample.

### What the study measured

> "A sample of 205 adults wore beepers for a week. They furnished 7,827 reports of desire episodes..." (Hofmann, Baumeister, Förster & Vohs 2012, [PubMed 22149456](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22149456/))

The authors tracked four things per episode: desire strength, conflict with other goals, resistance (use of self-control) and whether the desire was acted on.

### How often, and how problematic

> "The most conservative estimate from our data indicates that people feel some desire about half the time they are awake." ([full text, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 102(6):1318-1335](http://assets.csom.umn.edu/assets/lib/assets/AssetLibrary/2012/Hofmann_Baumeister_Forster_Vohs_2012_JPSP.pdf))

In the same paper, 53.2% of desires were rated as not conflicting at all, and 47% conflicted at least somewhat with the person's other goals. Desires were actively resisted on 42% of occasions and enacted on 48%. The abstract summarises it this way:

> "Results suggest that desires are frequent, variable in intensity, and largely unproblematic. Those urges that do conflict with other goals tend to elicit resistance, with uneven success." ([PubMed 22149456](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22149456/))

So "resisting them is effortful" is accurate only for the conflicting subset. Conflict triggered resistance, and resistance strongly reduced the likelihood of enactment, according to the full text.

### The hours-per-day figure

A companion paper extrapolates from the data: "the average adult spends approximately 8 hours per day feeling desires, 3 hours resisting them, and half an hour yielding to previously resisted ones." ([Hofmann, Vohs & Baumeister 2012, Psychological Science 23:582](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2019-04/hofmann_vohs_baumeister_2012_psych_science.pdf)) The authors present this as an extrapolation. It is not a direct measurement of hours.

The Psychological Science paper also found that desires for media use and work brought the most self-control failure, with 42% of media desires enacted despite an attempt to resist. It reports that earlier self-control predicted lower success at resisting later desires on the same day, which the authors read as support for a limited-resource model of self-control. That model is contested, so this page does not rely on it.

### Why it matters for the compendium

The data fit a milder reading of the desire-reduction principles: most desires are ordinary and need no fight, and the work is in the conflicting minority. The study does not test whether reducing desire improves wellbeing, and it does not address craving as the Buddhist texts define it.
