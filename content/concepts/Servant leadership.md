Servant leadership is Robert K. Greenleaf's coinage, first published in his 1970 essay "The Servant as Leader" and elaborated in the 1977 book. The definition inverts the usual order of the words: "The servant-leader is servant first… It begins with the natural feeling that one wants to serve, to serve first. Then conscious choice brings one to aspire to lead." ([Greenleaf Center](https://greenleaf.org/what-is-servant-leadership/)) The test of such leadership is written on the served, not the leader's career: "The best test, and difficult to administer, is: Do those served grow as persons? Do they, while being served, become healthier, wiser, freer, more autonomous, more likely themselves to become servants? And, what is the effect on the least privileged in society? Will they benefit or at least not be further deprived?"

### Servant first, leader second

Greenleaf's distinction is between servant-first and leader-first as "two extreme types": the one leads because serving requires it, the other serves, if at all, once leadership is established. The concept has since grown a research tradition (Liden and colleagues' 2008 model among them), with measures of the growth and autonomy of the served as outcomes. My use is the reversal it forces in self-audit: the question a leader asks of a role is usually what it does for them; Greenleaf's test moves the question to what happens to the people on the other side of it.
