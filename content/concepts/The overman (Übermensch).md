The overman (Übermensch) is Nietzsche's figure for what human beings might yet become, announced by Zarathustra in Thus Spoke Zarathustra (1883). The German word keeps its ambiguity: the Über- can mean over, above or beyond, and Nietzsche wants all of it. The figure is not a biological type and not a person who has arrived. It is the name for a direction of movement, the direction every living thing has been going.

### The announcement

Zarathustra opens with the teaching itself, then defines it by negation. To the ape, a human being is already an embarrassment, and the relation repeats one level up:

> "I teach you the Superman. Man is something that is to be surpassed. What have ye done to surpass man? All beings hitherto have created something beyond themselves: and ye want to be the ebb of that great tide, and would rather go back to the beast than surpass man? What is the ape to man? A laughing-stock, a thing of shame. And just the same shall man be to the Superman: a laughing-stock, a thing of shame."

([Nietzsche, Thus Spake Zarathustra, "Zarathustra's Prologue" §3, trans. Thomas Common, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1998/1998-0.txt).)

And the image the Prologue is famous for:

> "Man is a rope stretched between the animal and the Superman — a rope over an abyss. A dangerous crossing, a dangerous wayfaring, a dangerous looking-back, a dangerous trembling and halting."

([Zarathustra's Prologue §4, Common translation, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1998/1998-0.txt).)

The figure is defined by the crossing, not by the destination. That is why the text refuses to describe the overman in positive traits: to describe it would be to fix it, and the crossing is the point.

### How the compendium reads it

The concept pairs with [[E109 — Practise the overman (Übermensch)]], which turns the figure into a discipline: the overman is not a title you win but a movement you keep making, the leaving-behind of inherited settings you cannot defend. The concept also connects to [[Nihilism]] — Nietzsche's response to the death of God — and to the project of value creation, where the overman is the being for whom creating values replaces receiving them.
