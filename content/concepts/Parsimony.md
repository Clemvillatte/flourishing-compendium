Parsimony is the habit of not multiplying what a claim needs. The concept's canonical bearer is William of Ockham, and the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy's summary of his approach states its centrality plainly:

> "The principle of simplicity is the central theme of Ockham's approach, so much so that this principle has come to be known as 'Ockham's Razor.' Ockham uses the razor to eliminate unnecessary hypotheses." ([IEP, William of Ockham](https://www.iep.utm.edu/ockham/))

The same entry situates the razor inside Ockham's larger economy: in metaphysics he admits "only individual substances and qualities", holding that universal essences such as humanity or whiteness "are nothing more than concepts in the mind". Parsimony for Ockham is not an aesthetic preference for tidiness; it is a discipline of ontology — what cannot be shown to be needed is not shown to be.
