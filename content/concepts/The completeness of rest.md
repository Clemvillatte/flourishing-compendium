[[T002 — Aristotle|Aristotle]]'s treatment of rest in Book X of the Nicomachean Ethics belongs to his argument that happiness does not consist in amusement. Rest answers a limit he states plainly: no human being can work continuously. Those who live for amusements treat rest as the end, and he counts that a mistake; relaxation is taken for the sake of activity.

> "But to amuse oneself in order that one may exert oneself, as Anacharsis puts it, seems right; for amusement is a sort of relaxation, and we need relaxation because we cannot work continuously. Relaxation, then, is not an end; for it is taken for the sake of activity." (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book X, chapter 6, Ross translation, [MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.10.x.html))

### Why rest is needed at all

The need for rest comes from the nature of activity itself. Earlier in the same book Aristotle observes that "all human beings are incapable of continuous activity", and that pleasure is not continuous for the same reason: it accompanies activity, and activity tires. Rest restores the capacity that work spends, which is why he denies that a life of amusement could be the happy life; the happy life requires exertion.

The book also describes how good activity carries its own completion. Pleasure, Aristotle writes, completes the activity not as a permanent state does, "but as an end which supervenes as the bloom of youth does on those in the flower of their age". Rest in this account is the counterpart of completion on the recovery side: activity spends, rest restores, and the two belong to one rhythm rather than competing for a life.
