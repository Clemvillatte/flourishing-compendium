The social self is George Herbert Mead's answer to the question of what a self is made of. His claim, stated flatly in Mind, Self and Society: "The self is something which has a development; it is not initially there, at birth, but arises in the process of social experience and activity, that is, develops in the given individual as a result of his relations to that process as a whole and to other individuals within that process" ([Mead, Mind, Self and Society 1934](https://ia601500.us.archive.org/5/items/mead-1934/mead%201934.pdf)). A self is not an organ that matures; it is an achievement that depends on other people.

### How one becomes an object to oneself

The mechanism is strange on first hearing: you get a self by treating yourself as another person treats you. Mead's formulation: "The individual experiences himself as such, not directly, but only indirectly, from the particular standpoints of other individual members of the same social group, or from the generalized standpoint of the social group as a whole to which he belongs." A person "becomes an object to himself only by taking the attitudes of other individuals toward himself within a social environment or context of experience and behavior in which both he and they are involved" ([Mead 1934](https://ia601500.us.archive.org/5/items/mead-1934/mead%201934.pdf)). The route runs through others, and only through them: "he enters his own experience as a self... not by becoming a subject to himself, but only in so far as he first becomes an object to himself just as other individuals are objects to him or in his experience" ([Mead 1934](https://ia601500.us.archive.org/5/items/mead-1934/mead%201934.pdf)).

The details of that route are on the companion page: children rehearse others in play, organize all the attitudes at once in the game, and crystallize them into the generalized other. The self that results has two phases, the "me" of taken attitudes and the "I" that responds; and because the response is never settled in advance, the social self is a process rather than a possession.

### What follows

If the self is social through and through, two consequences matter for practice. Different relationships really do house different selves, which is the observation behind recognizing that you have different selves in different relationships. And self-cultivation that ignores other people is working on a fiction, since the material of the self is made of taken attitudes; even solitude is populated. The compendium's relational principles take the first consequence, and xiushen, self-cultivation, answers the second by treating the internalized community as the thing one cultivates.
