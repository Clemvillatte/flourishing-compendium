The phrase is the hinge of the Great Learning's famous sequence, in Legge's translation:

> "Such extension of knowledge lay in the investigation of things." (Confucius, The Great Learning, trans. James Legge, [Legge text reproduction](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~dkjordan/chin/Koong/GreatLearning.html))

> "Things being investigated, knowledge became complete. Their knowledge being complete, their thoughts were sincere. Their thoughts being sincere, their hearts were then rectified." (Confucius, The Great Learning, trans. Legge, [Legge text reproduction](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~dkjordan/chin/Koong/GreatLearning.html))

The Chinese term at the centre is gewu, and Legge's "investigation of things" is his reading of it, one that the text itself does not define in the passages the compendium anchors to. The chain of the ladder is stated in full, with gewu as its entry step: "Wishing to cultivate their persons, they first rectified their hearts. Wishing to rectify their hearts, they first sought to be sincere in their thoughts. Wishing to be sincere in their thoughts, they first extended to the utmost their knowledge. Such extension of knowledge lay in the investigation of things" ([Legge's Great Learning, UCSD reproduction](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~dkjordan/chin/Koong/GreatLearninge.html)). What the text does supply is the order: the chapter's own preface sentence, "Things have their root and their branches. Affairs have their end and their beginning. To know what is first and what is last will lead near to what is taught in the Great Learning," puts sequencing knowledge before character work, and makes the study of things the entry step of the whole ladder from person to kingdom.

**Plain words** Before you can straighten your thinking, the old sequence says, you have to know things as they actually are — root and branch, first and last. Looking into things is not preparation for the moral work; it is the first rung of it.
