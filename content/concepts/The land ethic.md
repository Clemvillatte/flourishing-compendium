The land ethic is Aldo Leopold's extension of the moral community, set out in the essay of that name in A Sand County Almanac (1949): the community to which ethics applies is enlarged to include soils, waters, plants and animals, "or collectively: the land." Its summary maxim:

> "A thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability, and beauty of the biotic community. It is wrong when it tends otherwise." (Leopold, A Sand County Almanac, 1949, pp. 224–225, quoted in [Wardrope, 2020](https://jme.bmj.com/content/46/12/791))

### What the ethic carries

The Leopold Foundation, keeper of the essay's legacy, states the core plainly: a land ethic expands the definition of community beyond humans to include the other parts of the Earth, "soils, waters, plants, and animals – 'the land'", and in it "care for people cannot be separated from care for the land." Leopold's own line for the shift in stance: "When we see land as a community to which we belong, we may begin to use it with love and respect" ([Aldo Leopold Foundation](https://www.aldoleopold.org/about/the-land-ethic)). The change he asks for is a change of role: from conqueror of the land-community to plain member and citizen of it. My principle R117 carries it as practice: extend the audit of a decision beyond its effects on people, to the community it touches. A compendium exercise drawn from the maxim is to take one ordinary choice, a purchase or a route, and read it in the maxim's terms, what it tends to preserve or degrade. What the ethic does not give: a rule for conflicts between human welfare and biotic integrity; the maxim names the criterion, and the debate over its hard cases, including the eco-fascism worry, is carried on by the literature around it.
