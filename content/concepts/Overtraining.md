Overtraining, in my physiological register, is the point at which a training or effort load stops building capacity and starts wearing the system down. The scientific frame is Bruce McEwen's concept of allostatic load, from "Protective and damaging effects of stress mediators" (New England Journal of Medicine, 1998). The key lines:

> Allostasis — the ability to achieve stability through change — is critical to survival. Through allostasis, the autonomic nervous system, the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, and the cardiovascular, metabolic, and immune systems protect the body by responding to internal and external stress. The price of this accommodation to stress can be allostatic load, which is the wear and tear that results from chronic overactivity or underactivity of allostatic systems.

(McEwen, NEJM 338(3), 1998, [PDF](https://www.psychiatry.wisc.edu/courses/Nitschke/seminar/mcewen_njm_1998.pdf))

The paper names the four failure modes: "repeated 'hits' from multiple stressors; lack of adaptation; prolonged response due to delayed shutdown; and inadequate response that leads to compensatory hyperactivity of other mediators." Overtraining is the athletic and work-side instance of the same pattern: the stress response that was supposed to turn off stays on, and the adaptation that was supposed to happen stops happening.

My [[B091 — Tune effort without strain (relaxed diligence)]] is the practice this research grounds: hard work is not the problem; unrecovered hard work is.
