“Worldmaking” — Goodman, Ways of Worldmaking.

Nelson Goodman's thesis in Ways of Worldmaking (1978) is that the world we know is not received ready-made. The book's most-quoted sentence, as cited in a standard companion volume on his theory:

> "Worldmaking as we know it always starts from worlds already on hand; the making is a remaking." (Goodman, Ways of Worldmaking, Hackett 1978, p. 6, as quoted in Nünning & Nünning, Ways of Worldmaking in Literature and Other Media)

Goodman's irrealism holds there is no single given world beneath our descriptions, only world-versions. The editors of that companion volume put the point this way: "According to Goodman, there is no such thing as a given world - the only thing we can ever have access to are culturally shaped world-models or world-versions." Each version is built from earlier ones by identifiable operations. The five Goodman lists are summarized there as "composition and decomposition, weighting (i.e. emphasis or ratings of relevance), ordering (and reordering), deletion and supplementation, and deformation" (cf. Goodman 1992, pp. 7-17): taking apart and putting together, deciding what matters, arranging, weeding out and filling in, and reshaping. A history of economics and a history of ideas can agree on the facts and still be different worlds, because emphasis and omission are themselves world-making moves.

The stakes for the compendium are practical rather than merely metaphysical. If knowing is remaking, then attention, framing and vocabulary are not neutral reporting tools; they are the workshop in which the world one inhabits is built. This is why the concept anchors principles on accepting many versions of the world, noticing that language shapes what one can see, and owning that one enacts one's world.
