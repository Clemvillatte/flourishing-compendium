I use "Moral emotions" for the emotions that link moral standards to moral behavior, as reviewed by June Price Tangney and colleagues:

> "Moral emotions represent a key element of our human moral apparatus, influencing the link between moral standards and moral behavior." (Tangney, Stuewig and Mashek 2007, abstract, [Annual Review of Psychology 58:345-372](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16953797/))

### The family

The review's map of the field: "We first focus on a triad of negatively valenced “self-conscious” emotions-shame, guilt, and embarrassment" (Tangney et al. 2007, [PubMed 16953797](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16953797/)). "In recent years, the concept of moral emotions has been expanded to include several positive emotions-elevation, gratitude, and the sometimes morally relevant experience of pride" (Tangney et al. 2007, [PubMed 16953797](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16953797/)).

### What the review covers

The abstract says the review's center of gravity is the difference between the two self-conscious emotions: "As in previous decades, much research remains focused on shame and guilt. We review current thinking on the distinction between shame and guilt, and the relative advantages and disadvantages of these two moral emotions." (Tangney et al. 2007, abstract, [PubMed 16953797](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16953797/)). The newer research it maps runs wide: "Several new areas of research are highlighted: research on the domain-specific phenomenon of body shame, styles of coping with shame, psychobiological aspects of shame, the link between childhood abuse and later proneness to shame, and the phenomena of vicarious or "collective" experiences of shame and guilt" (Tangney et al. 2007, abstract, [PubMed 16953797](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16953797/)). The abstract's last named area is not an emotion but a process: "a morally relevant emotional process-other-oriented empathy" (Tangney et al. 2007, abstract, [PubMed 16953797](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16953797/)).
