Continuing bonds is a term from bereavement research, introduced by Dennis Klass, Phyllis Silverman and Steven Nickman in their 1996 edited book Continuing Bonds: New Understandings of Grief. The book argued against the then-dominant view that healthy mourning means detaching from the dead. The APA Dictionary of Psychology defines a continuing bond as "the emotional attachment that a bereaved person continues to maintain with the deceased long after the death", and notes the approach contrasts with the view, prevalent after Freud's 1917 essay "Mourning and Melancholia", that successful mourning requires complete emotional detachment ([APA Dictionary](https://dictionary.apa.org/continuing-bond)).

Later researchers put it plainly: "continuing bonds has been defined as the presence of an ongoing inner relationship with the deceased person" ([Stroebe et al. 2012, Utrecht repository PDF](https://research-portal.uu.nl/ws/files/67801213/STROEBE_et_al_2012_Personal_Relationships.pdf)). The relationship can take many forms: keeping and using possessions, talking to the dead, sensing their presence, incorporating their characteristics into one's own identity.

### What the evidence supports, and what it does not
The 1996 book rested on qualitative studies and case material, not quantitative tests of adjustment. A later longitudinal study by Stroebe and colleagues, which followed 162 recently bereaved people over two years, found no support for the claim that continuing bonds is generally adaptive, and concluded the blanket formulation is too simple ([Stroebe et al. 2012](https://research-portal.uu.nl/ws/files/67801213/STROEBE_et_al_2012_Personal_Relationships.pdf)). Nigel Field's attachment-theory work distinguishes expressions that reflect a symbolic, integrated tie from those that reflect failure to accept the death ([Field 2006, PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16972368/)). The honest summary: keeping a bond is normal and common; whether it helps or harms depends on the form the bond takes, the relationship it grew from, and the culture around the mourner.
