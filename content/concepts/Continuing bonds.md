Continuing bonds is a term from bereavement research, introduced by Dennis Klass, Phyllis Silverman and Steven Nickman in their 1996 edited book Continuing Bonds: New Understandings of Grief. The book argued against the then-dominant view that healthy mourning means detaching from the dead. The APA Dictionary of Psychology defines a continuing bond as "the emotional attachment that a bereaved person continues to maintain with the deceased long after the death", and notes the approach contrasts with the view, prevalent after Freud's 1917 essay "Mourning and Melancholia", that successful mourning requires complete emotional detachment ([APA Dictionary](https://dictionary.apa.org/continuing-bond)).

Later researchers put it plainly: "Continuing bonds has been defined as the presence of an ongoing inner relationship with the deceased person (Shuchter & Zisook, 1993)" ([Stroebe et al. 2012, Utrecht repository PDF](https://research-portal.uu.nl/ws/files/67801213/STROEBE_et_al_2012_Personal_Relationships.pdf)). In the research literature the relationship takes many forms: maintaining the deceased's possessions, sensing their presence, drawing comfort from memories, following their wishes or using them as an ongoing example.

### What the evidence supports, and what it does not
The 2012 longitudinal study by Stroebe, Abakoumkin, Stroebe and Schut followed 60 bereaved spouses at three points across the first two years of bereavement. People whose loss was unexpected and who retained strong bonds were the least well adapted and, in the study's words, "fail to recover over time"; those with expected losses and strong ties suffered initially but improved; those with weaker ties showed fewer signs of maladaptation regardless of how expected the death was. Field's overview puts the balance plainly: "It is increasingly recognized that it is a normative part of adjustment to the loss of a loved one and that it can be adaptive. It may be the case however that a continuing bond to the deceased may also be maladaptive under certain conditions" ([Field 2006, PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16972368/)). A 2023 systematic review of 79 studies found the literature "unable to confirm whether retaining, rather than relinquishing, bonds is helpful" ([systematic review](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/pdf/10.1080/07481187.2023.2223593)). Whether a bond helps or harms depends on the conditions around it, including how sudden the loss was.
