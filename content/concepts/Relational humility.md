“Relational humility” — Davis et al., 2013.

Relational humility is Don Davis and colleagues' model of humility as a judgment other people make about someone, not a trait a person simply reports about themselves. The 2013 paper the seed cites tested whether it strengthens social bonds over time; the model and its scale come from a 2011 paper by the same team.

### The definition

In a 2013 article for the APS Observer, Davis and Joshua Hook name two characteristics.

> "On the intrapersonal level, humility involves an accurate view of the self. On the interpersonal level, humility involves a stance that is other-oriented rather than self-focused." (Davis & Hook 2013, [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/observer/measuring-humility-and-its-positive-effects))

The team chose an other-report method because of a measurement paradox: "Someone who claims to be 'very humble' on a self-report measure might be displaying arrogance and lack of awareness." They recommend triangulating self-report, other-report and behaviour relevant to humility.

### The scale

The 2011 paper (Davis, Hook, Worthington and colleagues) reports five studies that built a 16-item Relational Humility Scale with three subscales: Global Humility, Superiority and Accurate View of Self. Study 1 had 300 participants and Study 2, an independent sample of 196, confirmed the factor structure. Studies 3 to 5 gave evidence of construct validity, including links to empathy and forgiveness and a comparison with the Honesty-Humility scale of the HEXACO inventory. ([PubMed 21516581](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21516581/))

### The social bonds test

The 2013 paper (Davis, Worthington, Hook and others, Self and Identity 12:58-77) reports two longitudinal studies. In Study 1, 123 college students in romantic relationships, each recently hurt or offended by their partner, filled in measures over six weeks, and relational humility predicted unforgiving motives one time-point later. In Study 2, 84 students did group tasks meant to challenge humility, and "trait humility was associated with greater group status and acceptance". ([abstract via SciSpace](https://scispace.com/papers/humility-and-the-development-and-repair-of-social-bonds-two-35p028rahk))

### What to be careful about

In the Observer piece the authors describe their support for the social bonds idea as "initial evidence". The samples in the abstracts above are college students, and the studies run for weeks, not years.
