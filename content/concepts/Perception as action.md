"Perception as action" is the first of two points in Varela, Thompson and Rosch's account of enaction in The Embodied Mind (1991). They state it at the point where they give "a preliminary formulation of what we mean by enaction" ([Varela, Thompson & Rosch, The Embodied Mind, 1991, p. 173, PDF copy](https://monoskop.org/images/2/21/Varela_Thompson_Rosch_The_Embodied_Mind_Cognitive_Science_and_Human_Experience_1991.pdf)):

> "In a nutshell, the enactive approach consists of two points: (1) perception consists in perceptually guided action and (2) cognitive structures emerge from the recurrent sensorimotor patterns that enable action to be perceptually guided."

### What they mean by it

For the authors, the starting question changes. Representationists treat perception as "the information-processing problem of recovering pregiven properties of the world," whereas "the point of departure for the enactive approach is the study of how the perceiver can guide his actions in his local situation." Because those situations keep changing as the perceiver acts, "the reference point for understanding perception is no longer a pregiven, perceiver-independent world but rather the sensorimotor structure of the perceiver." The overall concern is "to determine the common principles or lawful linkages between sensory and motor systems that explain how action can be perceptually guided in a perceiver-dependent world" (same page).

They are also explicit about the word "action": "sensory and motor processes, perception and action, are fundamentally inseparable in lived cognition." And they place their view against Gibson's: "Whereas Gibson claims that perception is direct detection, we claim that it is sensorimotor enactment" (same book).

### Scope

This is a claim in cognitive science and philosophy of mind about how perception is structured, put forward by the authors as a preliminary formulation (they say the two points "will perhaps appear somewhat opaque"). The compendium's practice-oriented principles (E014, D056, P099, K306) use the idea in a looser, applied sense that the book passage does not discuss.
