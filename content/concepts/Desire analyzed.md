Desire analyzed is Harry Frankfurt's way of sorting desires by level, set out in his 1971 paper "Freedom of the Will and the Concept of a Person". The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on compatibilism explains that Frankfurt "distinguishes between first-order and second-order desires": first-order desires have actions as their objects, while "the latter are desires about desires" ([McKenna and Coates, SEP: Compatibilism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/compatibilism/)).

### Levels of desire

The entry defines a person's will as her effective first-order desire, the one moving her "all the way to action." Some second-order desires are only wishes to have a certain desire, such as a therapist who wants to know what craving feels like without wanting to act on it. Others are wishes that a desire be effective, and Frankfurt calls these "second-order volitions" ([SEP: Compatibilism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/compatibilism/)).

### The two addicts

The entry's illustration contrasts two people with conflicting first-order desires about a drug. The unwilling addict has a second-order volition that her desire to take the drug not be her will, and yet it is. The willing addict embraces the desire. The entry sums up Frankfurt's theory: "One acts of her own free will if and only if her action issues from the will she wants." On this view the unwilling addict does not act freely, because her will conflicts at a higher level with what she wishes it to be ([SEP: Compatibilism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/compatibilism/)).

### Objections

The theory "has faced intense scrutiny". The entry names two difficulties, one about its hierarchical structure and one about relying on a mesh between parts of the psychology alone ([SEP: Compatibilism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/compatibilism/)).

### Scope

The entry treats Frankfurt's work as a theory of free will and moral responsibility, and it does not offer a technique for managing urges. Using the levels as a tool for examining what one wants is an adaptation. Frankfurt's original paper was not read in full for this page.
