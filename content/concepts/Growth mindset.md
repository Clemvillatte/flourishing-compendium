Growth mindset is Carol Dweck's term for the belief that a person's abilities and intelligence can be developed. The technical background is older: in a 1988 paper with Ellen Leggett she called these beliefs implicit theories, distinguishing an entity theory (ability is fixed) from an incremental theory (ability is malleable), with "fixed mindset" and "growth mindset" as the later, more user-friendly labels for the same contrast. The belief does not travel alone: it sits in what Dweck calls a meaning system of goals, effort beliefs and attributions for failure.

> "A growth mindset embodies the thought that one's personal attributes, such as intelligence, skill, and ability can be developed and improved through effort." (Ku and Stager 2022, [Frontiers in Psychology systematic review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9284032/))

### From theories to mindsets

The research lineage runs through learned helplessness and attribution theory: 1970s studies found that children's attributions for failure predict whether they respond with helplessness or with mastery-oriented persistence. Mindsets are not two types of person. Growth and fixed are the two ends of a continuum, and individuals hold varying degrees of it depending on circumstances (Haimovitz and Dweck 2017).

### Evidence and limits

The empirical status is contested. Sisk and colleagues' 2018 twin meta-analyses found a weak overall correlation between mindset and achievement (r = .10 across 273 effect sizes, N = 365,915) and a small intervention effect (d = 0.08 across 43 effect sizes, N = 57,155): "Overall effects were weak for both meta-analyses." The same analyses found some support for one tenet of the theory, namely that students of low socioeconomic status or who are academically at risk might benefit from mindset interventions. Reviewers also describe mindset effects as meaningfully heterogeneous across persons and situations, and note that few studies test whether intervention gains endure or transfer to other contexts.
