The craft-specificity of skill is the Dreyfus brothers' claim that expertise belongs to a particular craft: it is built from a vast repertoire of situations met inside one practice, and it does not travel as general ability. Stuart Dreyfus developed the model with Hubert Dreyfus and set it out in Mind Over Machine (1986); his 2004 summary of it appeared in Bulletin of Science, Technology & Society.

> "The expert not only sees what needs to be achieved; thanks to his or her vast repertoire of situational discriminations, he or she also sees immediately how to achieve this goal." (Stuart E. Dreyfus, "The Five-Stage Model of Adult Skill Acquisition", Bulletin of Science, Technology & Society 24 (2004), p. 180, [PDF](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/facdev-medicine/files/2012/03/Dreyfus-skill-level.pdf))

### Why skill stays inside the craft

The expertise is the repertoire. The same summary describes how it is built: "with enough experience in a variety of situations, all seen from the same perspective but requiring different tactical decisions, the brain of the expert gradually decomposes this class of situations into subclasses, each of which requires a specific response." The training also starts inside the craft: the instructor begins by "decomposing the task environment into context-free features" the beginner can recognize. Skill grows by concrete experience rather than abstract principles, so the expert's intuition is indexed to the craft that trained it.
