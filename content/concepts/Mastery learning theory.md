**Mastery learning theory** — Bloom — nearly all students reach high standards when given the time and feedback suited to them.

Mastery learning is an instructional strategy Benjamin Bloom outlined in 1968 as "learning for mastery" and later shortened to "mastery learning" (Guskey, [Mastery Learning (2015), pp. 752-759](https://tguskey.com/wp-content/uploads/Mastery-Learning-1-Mastery-Learning.pdf)). Its root idea comes from John B. Carroll's 1963 model of school learning: aptitude is better read as an indicator of how fast a child can learn than of a ceiling on what the child can learn, so degree of learning depends on the time actually spent relative to the time needed (Guskey, 2015, pp. 752-753). Bloom's strategy turns that premise into a classroom cycle: teach a unit, quiz it, and use the quiz to fix what did not land before moving on.

> "instead of using assessments only as evaluation devices that mark the end of each unit, Bloom recommended using them as part of the instructional process to diagnose individual learning difficulties (feedback) and to prescribe remediation procedures (correctives)." (Guskey, [Mastery Learning, International Encyclopedia of the Social & Behavioral Sciences, 2nd ed. (2015), p. 753](https://tguskey.com/wp-content/uploads/Mastery-Learning-1-Mastery-Learning.pdf))

### The 2 sigma benchmark

Bloom's doctoral students Anania and Burke compared three conditions: conventional classes of about 30, mastery learning classes of the same size, and one-to-one tutoring paired with the same feedback cycle. On final achievement, the average mastery learning student landed about one standard deviation above the conventional class average, above 84% of its students, while the average tutored student landed about two standard deviations above it, above 98% (Bloom, 1984, p. 4, as quoted in Guskey, 2015, p. 758). Bloom called the gap the "2 Sigma" problem: tutoring works, but is "too costly for most societies to bear on a large scale", so the research task is to devise group methods that reach tutoring levels (Bloom, 1984, pp. 4-5, as quoted in Guskey, 2015, p. 758).
