Interoceptive exposure is the part of exposure therapy where the thing approached is a bodily sensation: a racing heart, dizziness, breathlessness. The person brings the sensation on deliberately, in a safe setting, and stays with it, so that they can learn that it does not lead to the disaster they expect. Panic disorder is its best-known setting in this compendium's sources.

### Where it appears in the evidence

Barlow, Gorman, Shear and Woods tested the cognitive-behavioral therapy developed at the Boston site against imipramine, the two combined, and placebo in 312 patients with panic disorder. The JAMA article states that this therapy "combines interoceptive exposure, cognitive restructuring, and breathing retraining" ([Barlow et al., 2000, JAMA](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/fullarticle/192707)). Both imipramine and the therapy beat placebo, and "CBT appeared durable in follow-up" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10815116/)). That trial tested a three-part package, so it cannot show how much of the benefit comes from interoceptive exposure alone. The seed line's wording, "inducing feared bodily sensations reduces panic", is a fair summary of the technique, but it goes beyond what this trial can isolate.

### A worked example from the literature

Craske and colleagues describe a client who feared that dizziness, shortness of breath and a racing heart meant an imminent stroke or heart attack. He had stopped exercising, avoided effortful games with his children, and his wife came with him to work sites. In therapy he did exposures to each sensation, including drinking more caffeine than he expected would trigger a heart attack. Two sessions went to each of three symptoms, aimed at violating the expectation that a stroke or heart attack would follow. Safety signals were then removed step by step: he attended without his wife waiting outside the room, and he was asked to stop bringing his benzodiazepines to sessions ([Craske et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4114726/)). Later sessions combined feared sensations that had already been faced separately, which the paper calls deepened extinction.

### One research note

Craske's paper reports that Deacon and colleagues (2013) found interoceptive exposure continued until the patient's expectancy of an aversive outcome fell below 5% was better than standard interoceptive exposure ([Craske et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4114726/)). I saw this only as Craske reports it, not in the original study.
