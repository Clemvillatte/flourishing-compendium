In the well-being literature, "acts of kindness" names the activities used in kindness interventions: deliberately performing kind acts for others, as in the "random acts of kindness" exercises of positive psychology. The question this concept tracks is what those acts do to the well-being of the person who performs them.

### The evidence

The experimental record was gathered in a 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis ([Curry, Rowland, Van Lissa, Zlotowitz, McAlaney and Whitehouse, 2018](https://research-portal.uu.nl/ws/files/247379826/1-s2.0-S0022103117303451-main.pdf)). The review screened 489 articles and kept 24 (27 studies) meeting its inclusion criteria, with a total of 4045 participants and 52 effect sizes. Its result:

> "Multi-level modeling revealed that the overall effect of kindness on the well-being of the actor is small-to-medium (δ = 0.28). The effect was not moderated by sex, age, type of participant, intervention, control condition or outcome measure. There was no indication of publication bias." (Curry et al., 2018, [open-access PDF](https://research-portal.uu.nl/ws/files/247379826/1-s2.0-S0022103117303451-main.pdf), abstract)

The authors read the result as consistent with the claim that people derive satisfaction from helping others. The effect is small to medium, not a transformation.

### Limits

The review's own discussion lists restrictions. Most of the reviewed studies were under-powered: the average sample size per condition was 79, giving power of 0.42 to detect the typical effect. Most used non-clinical student samples from Western, Educated, Industrial, Rich, Democratic societies, so generalisation to clinical samples and non-WEIRD societies is unclear. And the studies varied whether actions benefiting others were performed, but did not manipulate the motive behind them.
