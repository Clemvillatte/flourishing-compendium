Emplotment is Paul Ricoeur's name for the act of turning scattered events into a story that makes sense. The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy places it at the middle of his three stages of narrative, mimesis2, where "narrative emplotment brings the diverse elements of a situation into an imaginative order, in just the same way as does the plot of a story" ([IEP, "Paul Ricoeur", section 5](https://iep.utm.edu/ricoeur/)).

### What emplotment does

The same article says emplotment "configures events, agents and objects and renders those individual elements meaningful as part of a larger whole in which each takes a place in the network that constitutes the narrative's response to why, how, who, where, when, etc." By bringing together unlike elements, it "creates a 'concordant discordance,' a tensive unity which functions as a redescription of a situation" ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/ricoeur/)).

It also changes how events relate: "emplotment forges a causal continuity from a temporal succession, and so creates the intelligibility and credibility of the narrative." The shift is "from the structure of one thing after another" to "the conceptual relation of one thing because of another". That conversion, the article adds, "so well 'imitates' the continuity demanded by a life, and makes it the ideal model for personal identity and self-understanding" ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/ricoeur/)).

The Stanford Encyclopedia describes the same work without the word: through their plots, narratives are "a synthesis of heterogeneous concepts into a kind of discordant concordance" ([SEP, "Paul Ricoeur", section 4](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ricoeur/)).

### A story that can be retold

Emplotment is the middle of three stages. Mimesis1 is the competence we already bring to understanding action, and mimesis3 is the return of the story into lived life, where our life stories "must be 'read,' and when they are read they are taken as one's own and integrated into one's identity and self-understanding" ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/ricoeur/)).

Because the process is cyclical, the story can change. "We can redescribe our past experiences, bringing to light unrealized connections", and "the possibility of redescription of the past offers us the possibility of re-imagining and reconstructing a future inspired by hope." The article is also plain about the limits: "narrative need not have a happy ending. The concern of narrative is coherence and structure, not the creation of a particular kind of experience" ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/ricoeur/)).

The Stanford Encyclopedia notes a second limit: because narratives have endings, "they never fully exhaust time or the possible long-term meaning of action". It links the idea to identity in Ricoeur's later question, "what story does a person tell about his or her life, or what story do others tell about it?" ([SEP, sections 4 and 5](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ricoeur/)).

### Scope

Both quotations come from encyclopedia accounts of Ricoeur, and the volumes of Time and Narrative were not read. The IEP traces the concept to Aristotle's Poetics. Whether retelling a life story helps well-being in practice is an empirical question that these sources do not address.
