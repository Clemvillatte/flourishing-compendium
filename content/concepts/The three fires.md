The three fires — greed, aversion and delusion — is the Buddhist analysis of what keeps a person suffering and what keeps them acting in ways that produce more of it. In Pāli the three are usually named as the three unwholesome roots; the imagery of fire belongs to the same picture read differently: as things that burn.

> "Monks, there are these three roots of what is unskillful. Which three? Greed is a root of what is unskillful, aversion is a root of what is unskillful, delusion is a root of what is unskillful."
> — [Mūla Sutta (AN 3.69), trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an03/an03.069.than.html)

The sutta then runs the same pattern three times: whatever a greedy, aversive or deluded person fabricates by body, speech or intellect is itself unskillful, and so are the acts they wrongly inflict on another person while in that state. The root is not merely a bad outcome. It is a way of seeing that manufactures bad outcomes.

### Why fire

> "There is no fire like lust; there is no grip like hatred; there is no net like delusion; there is no river like craving."
> — [Dhammapada 251, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita, Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/dhp/dhp.18.budd.html)

The fire image carries two things at once. A fire consumes what it burns, and it spreads. Greed reaches for the next object and is not satisfied; aversion pushes away and hardens; delusion mistakes a passing thing for something permanent or self-owned. Each one, fed, grows. Nibbāna, the term for liberation, means literally going out — the fires ceasing to burn.
