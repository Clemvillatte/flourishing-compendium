Objective self-awareness theory is a social-psychological account of what happens when attention turns toward the self. Shelley Duval and Robert Wicklund formulated it in their 1972 book A Theory of Objective Self-Awareness.

> "When attention is directed inward and the individual's consciousness is focused on himself, he is the object of his own consciousness--hence 'objective' self awareness" (Duval & Wicklund, 1972, p. 2, quoted in [Silvia & Duval, 2001](https://scispace.com/pdf/objective-self-awareness-theory-recent-progress-and-enduring-1yszdadcu3.pdf)).

### Two directions of attention

The theory starts from a dichotomy. In "subjective self-awareness," attention is directed away from the self and the person "experiences himself as the source of perception and action" (Duval & Wicklund, 1972, p. 3, quoted in Silvia & Duval, 2001). Turn attention inward and the self becomes an object of one's own consciousness instead. Experiments induce the inward state with simple devices; one classic manipulation was "the presence or absence of a mirror" (Silvia & Duval, 2001).

### What self-focus sets in motion

Focusing attention on the self "initiated an automatic comparison of the self against standards" (Silvia & Duval, 2001). A standard was "defined as a mental representation of correct behavior, attitudes, and traits"; taken together, the standards of correctness "define what a 'correct' person is" (Duval & Wicklund, 1972, pp. 3-4, quoted in Silvia & Duval, 2001). "If a discrepancy was found between self and standards, negative affect was said to arise. This aversive state then motivated the restoration of consistency" (Silvia & Duval, 2001).

### Discrepancy and its two exits

In the original formulation the comparison could end two ways: "People could either actively change their actions, attitudes, or traits to be more congruent with the representations of the standard or could avoid the self-focusing stimuli and circumstances. Avoidance effectively terminates the comparison process and hence all self-evaluation" (Silvia & Duval, 2001). Later work collected in the 2001 review refines the conditions: people reduce a discrepancy when they believe their rate of progress is sufficient relative to its size, and avoid when it is not; and "If self is seen as causal, people will change self; if standards are seen as causal, people will change standards" (Silvia & Duval, 2001).

*For example:* Imagined case. Rehearsing a presentation in front of a mirror, someone notices the gap between their slouched rehearsal and their own standard of a composed speaker. They can adjust the posture — or leave the room and stop rehearsing. The theory predicts which choice appears as the comparison feels more or less winnable.
