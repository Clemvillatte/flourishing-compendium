"Sensation seeking" is Marvin Zuckerman's name for a personality trait, defined in his 1994 book as "a trait defined by the seeking of varied, novel, complex, and intense sensations and experiences, and the willingness to take physical, social, legal, and financial risks for the sake of such experience" (Zuckerman, 1994, p. 27, quoted in [Roberti, "A review of behavioral and biological correlates of sensation seeking"](https://people.wku.edu/richard.miller/520_Sensation-seeking%20physiology%20correlates.pdf)). The definition has two halves that travel together: an appetite for intensity and a willingness to pay in risk for it.

### Four factors

The trait was found to split into four factors in the scale development work, described by Zuckerman, Eysenck and Eysenck (1978): "The first factor was called Thrill and Adventure Seeking (TAS), and it contained items expressing a desire to engage in sports or other activities involving speed or danger. The second factor was called Experience Seeking (ES), and it represented the seeking of experience through the mind and senses, travel, and a nonconforming life-style. The third factor was labeled Disinhibition (Dis), which seemed to represent the desire for social and sexual disinhibition as expressed in social drinking, partying, and variety in sexual partners. The fourth factor, called Boredom Susceptibility (BS), represented an aversion to repetition, routine, and dull people, and restlessness when things are unchanging." (Zuckerman, S. Eysenck & H. J. Eysenck, 1978, [PDF](https://hanseysenck.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/1978_zuckerman_eysenck_et_al_-_sensation_seeking_in_england_and_america_cross-cultural_age.pdf))

The four are distinct scales, so the model allows, for instance, a person drawn to travel and art without the partying — an illustration of the factor distinctions, not an observed result. My principle on avoiding the adventurer concerns the risk side of the trait; the curiosity pages concern its novelty side.

## Facets

- **mortality research** — the trait's definition includes willingness to take physical, social, legal, and financial risks for the sake of experience; the risk side of the trait is what connects it to mortality research. Specific mortality findings are not treated on this page.
