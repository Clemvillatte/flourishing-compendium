"The via negativa of appetite" is the companion's heading for the tradition's subtraction method, taken from Pseudo-Dionysius and applied to desire. In *The Mystic Theology*, the way up is by taking away. The seeker prays "to enter within the super-bright gloom, and through not seeing and not knowing, to see and to know that the not to see nor to know is itself the above sight and knowledge," and the method is given a sculptor's image: "just as those who make a lifelike statue, by extracting all the encumbrances which have been placed upon the clear view of the concealed, and by bringing to light, by the mere cutting away, the genuine beauty concealed in it" (Pseudo-Dionysius, *The Mystic Theology*, Caput II, trans. Parker; [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Works_of_Dionysius_the_Areopagite/Mystic_Theology)). The statue was in the block all along; the work is removal. The chapters then perform the removal in litany form, Caput V ascending through denials: the Cause "is neither soul, nor mind, nor has imagination, or opinion, or reason, or conception... neither is It darkness, nor light; nor error, nor truth; neither is there any definition at all of It, nor any abstraction." The method is transferable because it is formal: where the positive way adds predicates and accumulates, the negative way subtracts what occludes, on the wager that the essential was never missing, only covered. Applied to appetite, the wager reads: the pursuit is rarely starved of addition — more options, more intensity, more occasions — and what it lacks is subtraction. The flesh's limits, satiety, the point past which more becomes less, are not obstacles to the good of appetite but the cutting away that reveals it, the encumbrance-extraction of the statue. The practice is not to renounce appetite but to let each limit end the pursuit where the limit ends it, and to treat the remainder, what still stands when nothing more is added, as the figure.
