The complexity of the soul is Aristotle's own framing of the problem in De Anima: the soul is one thing with distinct capacities, and the difficulty is saying how. His opening caution: "Grasping anything trustworthy concerning the soul is completely and in every way among the most difficult of affairs" (*De Anima* i 1, 402a10-11; [SEP, Aristotle's Psychology](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-psychology/)). And the parts question is left open: "It's clear that the soul is not separable from the body — or that certain parts of it, if it naturally has parts, are not separable from the body" (*De Anima* ii 1, 413a3-5; [same](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-psychology/)).

### The faculties

The structure the inquiry settles on is a list of capacities rather than a single substance: "the soul's individual capacities or faculties, which he first lists as nutrition, perception, and mind," with desire added later ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-psychology/)). Mind is described functionally as "the part of the soul by which it knows and understands" (*De Anima* iii 4, 429a9-10; [same](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-psychology/)), and desire as "the sort of faculty in the soul which initiates movement" (*De Anima* iii 10, 433a31-b1; [same](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-psychology/)). Britannica's summary of the structure: "The parts of the soul, moreover, are faculties, which are distinguished from each other by their operations and their objects" ([Britannica, Aristotle, Philosophy of mind](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Aristotle/Philosophy-of-mind)).

### The open question

The scholarly reading of DA II.3 keeps the complexity honest: the parts "can be defined separately from the other parts," and they "jointly constitute the explanatory essence of living things," which is why the treatise "explicitly recommends defining the soul as the explanatory principle of the science of living things by going through the series of the definitions of the parts in turn" ([PhilArchive, The Unity of the Human Soul](https://philarchive.org/archive/LABTUO)). The De Anima text itself asks the structural question directly: "The soul must either be homogeneous, or such that" its parts differ, and if it holds the body together as one, "is it one or multipartite?" ([De Anima I, J.A. Smith translation, psychclassics.yorku.ca](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Aristotle/De-anima/de-anima1.htm)). Aristotle leaves the answer as the layered account: one soul, many capacities, the wax and its shape.
