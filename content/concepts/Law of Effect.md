In his 1911 formulation of the Law of Effect, Thorndike describes how satisfaction and discomfort strengthen or weaken a response's connection with the situation ([Thorndike, 1911, chapter V, p. 244](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Thorndike/Animal/chap5.htm)).

> "Of several responses made to the same situation, those which are accompanied or closely followed by satisfaction to the animal will, other things being equal, be more firmly connected with the situation, so that, when it recurs, they will be more likely to recur; those which are accompanied or closely followed by discomfort to the animal will, other things being equal, have their connections with that situation weakened, so that, when it recurs, they will be less likely to occur. The greater the satisfaction or discomfort, the greater the strengthening or weakening of the bond." (Thorndike, [Animal Intelligence, chapter V](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Thorndike/Animal/chap5.htm), 1911, p. 244)

### What the law claims

Thorndike qualifies the principle with "other things being equal" and states that greater satisfaction or discomfort produces greater strengthening or weakening of the connection ([Thorndike, 1911, p. 244](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Thorndike/Animal/chap5.htm)). He illustrates with a cat escaping a box: a cat forms the habit of prompt escape most rapidly when the door opens and the food follows the response most closely ([Thorndike, 1911, p. 249](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Thorndike/Animal/chap5.htm)).
