The Law of Effect is Edward L. Thorndike's principle of learning, set out in Animal Intelligence (1911). It states that responses followed by satisfaction become more firmly connected to the situation that produced them, while responses followed by discomfort have that connection weakened.

> "Of several responses made to the same situation, those which are accompanied or closely followed by satisfaction to the animal will, other things being equal, be more firmly connected with the situation, so that, when it recurs, they will be more likely to recur; those which are accompanied or closely followed by discomfort to the animal will, other things being equal, have their connections with that situation weakened, so that, when it recurs, they will be less likely to occur. The greater the satisfaction or discomfort, the greater the strengthening or weakening of the bond." (Thorndike, [Animal Intelligence, chapter V](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Thorndike/Animal/chap5.htm), 1911, p. 244)

### What the law claims

The law makes consequence, not insight, the mechanism of habit: the animal does not need to understand why a response worked for the response to be strengthened. Note the qualifier "other things being equal" and the graded final sentence: the effect is proportional, not all-or-nothing. Thorndike's cats in puzzle boxes, the book's experimental subject, were his evidence: responses that led to food and escape recurred sooner on later trials ([Thorndike, 1911](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Thorndike/Animal/chap5.htm)).
