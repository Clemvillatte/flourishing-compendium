Deliberate simplicity is Henry David Thoreau's practice, set out in Walden (1854), of choosing what to live with and what to cut away. His stated reason for moving to the woods:

> "I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately, to front only the essential facts of life, and see if I could not learn what it had to teach, and not, when I came to die, discover that I had not lived." (Thoreau, Walden, [Gutenberg #205](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/205/pg205.txt))

### What simplicity is for

The diagnosis behind the practice is that detail multiplies until nothing is left for living: "Our life is frittered away by detail" (Thoreau, Walden, [Gutenberg #205](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/205/pg205.txt)). The instruction that follows is arithmetic, not asceticism:

> "Simplify, simplify. Instead of three meals a day, if it be necessary eat but one; instead of a hundred dishes, five; and reduce other things in proportion." (Thoreau, Walden, [Gutenberg #205](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/205/pg205.txt))

The same chapter keeps the ledger small by hand: "I say, let your affairs be as two or three, and not a hundred or a thousand; instead of a million count half a dozen, and keep your accounts on your thumb nail" (Thoreau, Walden, [Gutenberg #205](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/205/pg205.txt)). Simplicity serves the deliberate life; the point of cutting away detail is to front the essential facts.
