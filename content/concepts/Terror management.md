Terror management theory is the social-psychological account of how humans live with the knowledge that they will die. Its premise is a conflict between two facts: the instinct for self-preservation, and the realization that death is inevitable and to some extent unpredictable. The conflict produces anxiety, which "is often managed through escapism and cultural beliefs that counter biological reality with more significant and enduring forms of meaning and value," countering the personal insignificance represented by death with the significance provided by symbolic culture ([Wikipedia, Terror management theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terror_management_theory)).

The theory descends from Ernest Becker's 1973 book The Denial of Death. Becker's summary of the underlying claim: "The idea of death, the fear of it, haunts the human animal like nothing else; it is a mainspring of human activity designed largely to avoid the fatality of death, to overcome it by denying in some way that it is the final destiny for man" (Becker 1973, quoted in [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terror_management_theory)). Greenberg, Solomon and Pyszczynski turned the claim into experimental psychology in 1986.

### The two buffers

The theory's cultural values come in two strengths. The obvious ones "purport to offer literal immortality (e.g. belief in the afterlife through religion)"; the subtler ones "offer symbolic immortality" through "values of national identity, cultural perspectives on gender, and human superiority over animals" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terror_management_theory)). Self-esteem is the individual-side buffer: TMT "describes self-esteem as being the personal, subjective measure of how well an individual is living up to their cultural values" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terror_management_theory)). On this reading, self-esteem is not a mood but an anxiety-management device, which is why threats to the worldview land so hard.

### The criticisms

The theory's standing troubles are recorded plainly in the article: the experimental results are difficult to replicate, suppression of fear is implausible from an evolutionary point of view, and the observed responses may be better explained by coalitional psychology, fear of uncertainty, or the search for meaning ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terror_management_theory)). Proponents answer that the coalitional alternative cannot account for the symbolic, culture-wide character of the defenses ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terror_management_theory)). The compendium's mortality principles use the theory for its insight, the anxiety-buffering role of meaning and self-esteem, without betting on any one experiment.
