The Great Death — in the Rinzai Zen of the Japanese master Hakuin Ekaku, an experience of the death of the self, called daishi, that the practitioner passes through in the course of kōan training.

The phrase does not refer to physical dying. In the account of Hakuin's curriculum used here, the great death belongs to a staged training: it follows the arising of faith and insight, and it is not the end of the path.

### Origin

The great death belongs to the Zen curriculum of the Japanese master Hakuin Ekaku: the opened chapter describes daishi as what Hakuin calls the experience. The fetched passage does not state Hakuin's dates or the chapter's author, so none are asserted here.

### Core claims

The account describes where the great death sits in the curriculum:

> "With this conviction the practitioner must then, of course, see her own nature and undergo what Hakuin calls a 'great death' (J. daishi 大死). However, to die the great death, the practitioner had to have great doubt, and to have great doubt, she could not settle with her initial satori or kenshō. She had to cultivate this doubt with the help of a rigorous regimen of 'difficult-to-pass' (J. nantō 難透) kōan barriers, which requires great determination." ([Chapter on Hakuin's curriculum, Terebess Zen](https://terebess.hu/zen/Hakuin.pdf))

And what must follow it:

> "Lastly, having successfully generated and resolved great doubt, she must not, Hakuin adds, rest content with this achievement or, worse still, become attached to it and become sick—more on this later—but rather come back to 'life' from this 'death' and teach Zen to others. This, too, requires great determination." ([Chapter on Hakuin's curriculum, Terebess Zen](https://terebess.hu/zen/Hakuin.pdf))

Three claims are explicit in these passages. The great death is an experience of seeing one's own nature, not a doctrine to be believed. It requires great doubt, which means the practitioner must not settle with an initial awakening. And it is incomplete without a return: the practitioner comes back to life from this death, and resting content with the achievement, or becoming attached to it, is described as a way of becoming sick.

### How it explains transformation

The curriculum explains transformation as a sequence of conviction, doubt and return. Faith in one's capacity for insight begins it; doubt deepens it; the great death marks the decisive turn; and the return to life, with the determination to teach, completes it. The passage's warning against attachment to attainment is the hinge: the point of the death is the life that follows it, not the state itself.

### In everyday life

An illustration, not a documented case: a practitioner in training who, after an initial experience of insight, is told by the tradition not to settle there, is taken through further doubt and difficulty, and only later returns to ordinary activities and to helping others. The pattern the concept names is that the low point and the disorientation are treated as passages in a curriculum, with a return expected on the far side.
