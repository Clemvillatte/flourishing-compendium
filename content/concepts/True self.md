“True self” — Schlegel et al., 2009.

## The concept

In this research, the true self is the concept people hold of who they really are, distinct from how they sometimes act in daily life. Rebecca Schlegel and Joshua Hicks, reviewing the field, define it as follows.

> “Your true self is composed of the characteristics, roles or attributes that define who you really are – even if those characteristics are different than how you sometimes act in your daily life (originally adapted from Bargh, McKenna, & Fitzsimmons, 2002).”
>
> — Schlegel and Hicks, [The True Self and Psychological Health](http://existentialpsych.sites.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/152/2016/08/SchlegelHicks2011Compass.pdf), 2011

## The findings

In the 2009 studies, participants listed words describing their true self and their actual self. Reaction times on a Me/Not Me task measured how accessible each self-concept was, and priming techniques manipulated it. Accessibility of the true self-concept, but not the actual self-concept, predicted judgments of meaning in life: “the more accessible one's true self-concept, the greater one's perception that his or her life was meaningful.” The authors report the effects held after controlling for social desirability, liking of the self-concepts, or self-esteem, and in one study even when exclusively negative aspects of the true self were primed.

Related work concerns expression rather than knowledge. A daily diary study by Heppner and colleagues found that daily reports of feeling in touch with the true self predicted increased self-esteem and positive affect.

The researchers are careful about ontology: what is studied is the avowed true self-concept, not a verified essence. As they put it, “Although we take no stance on whether there really is such a thing as a true self, it is clear that people value the traits and roles that most accurately represent their true self concept.”
