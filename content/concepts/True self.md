“True self” — Schlegel et al., 2009.

## The concept

In this research, the true self is the concept people hold of who they really are, distinct from how they sometimes act in daily life ([Schlegel and Hicks, 2011](http://existentialpsych.sites.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/152/2016/08/SchlegelHicks2011Compass.pdf)). Rebecca Schlegel and Joshua Hicks, reviewing the field, define it as follows ([Schlegel and Hicks, 2011](http://existentialpsych.sites.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/152/2016/08/SchlegelHicks2011Compass.pdf)).

> “Your true self is composed of the characteristics, roles or attributes that define who you really are – even if those characteristics are different than how you sometimes act in your daily life (originally adapted from Bargh, McKenna, & Fitzsimmons, 2002).”
>
> — Schlegel and Hicks, [The True Self and Psychological Health](http://existentialpsych.sites.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/152/2016/08/SchlegelHicks2011Compass.pdf), Social and Personality Psychology Compass (2011), p. 990

## The findings

In the 2009 studies, participants listed words describing their true self and their actual self ([Schlegel and Hicks, 2011](http://existentialpsych.sites.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/152/2016/08/SchlegelHicks2011Compass.pdf)). Reaction times on a Me/Not Me task measured how accessible each self-concept was, and priming techniques manipulated it ([Schlegel and Hicks, 2011](http://existentialpsych.sites.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/152/2016/08/SchlegelHicks2011Compass.pdf)). Accessibility of the true self-concept, but not the actual self-concept, predicted judgments of meaning in life: “the more accessible one's true self-concept, the greater one's perception that his or her life was meaningful” ([Schlegel, Hicks, Arndt and King, 2009, quoted in Schlegel and Hicks, 2011, p. 994](http://existentialpsych.sites.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/152/2016/08/SchlegelHicks2011Compass.pdf)). The authors report the effects held after controlling for social desirability, liking of the self-concepts, or self-esteem, and in one study even when exclusively negative aspects of the true self were primed ([Schlegel and Hicks, 2011](http://existentialpsych.sites.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/152/2016/08/SchlegelHicks2011Compass.pdf)).

Related work concerns expression rather than knowledge: a daily diary study by [Heppner and colleagues](http://existentialpsych.sites.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/152/2016/08/SchlegelHicks2011Compass.pdf) found that daily reports of feeling in touch with the true self predicted increased self-esteem and positive affect.

The researchers are careful about ontology: what is studied is the avowed true self-concept, not a verified essence ([Schlegel, Hicks, King and Arndt, 2011](https://existentialpsych.sites.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/152/2016/08/SchlegelHicksPSPB2011online.pdf)). As [Schlegel, Hicks, King and Arndt (2011)](https://existentialpsych.sites.tamu.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/152/2016/08/SchlegelHicksPSPB2011online.pdf) write: “Although we take no stance on whether there really is such a thing as a true self, it is clear that people value the traits and roles that most accurately represent their true self concept.”
