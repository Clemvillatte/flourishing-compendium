Knowing-how is Gilbert Ryle's term for the kind of knowledge that shows in ability: knowing how to do something rather than knowing that something is the case. Ryle introduced the contrast in The Concept of Mind (1949), arguing that intelligence epithets like "shrewd" or "prudent" impute "not the knowledge or ignorance of this or that truth, but the ability, or inability, to do certain sorts of things" ([Ryle 1949: 27, quoted in SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/knowledge-how/)).

### Ability, not stored propositions

For Ryle, learning how differs in kind from learning facts: "Learning how or improving in ability is not like learning that or acquiring information. Truths can be imparted, procedures can only be inculcated" ([Ryle 1949: 46, quoted in SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/knowledge-how/)). He analyzed skill as "a complex of dispositions" ([Ryle 1949: 33, quoted in SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/knowledge-how/)). This is the "anti-intellectualist" position in the debate the SEP entry frames; intellectualists reply that knowing-how is itself a kind of propositional knowledge ([SEP, Knowledge How](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/knowledge-how/)).
