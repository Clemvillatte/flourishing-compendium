## In plain words

The Big Two is the finding that social judgment runs on two great axes. "Agency arises from striving to individuate and expand the self and involves qualities such as efficiency, competence, and assertiveness," while "communion arises from striving to integrate the self in a larger social unit through caring for others and involves qualities such as benevolence, cooperativeness, and empathy" ([Chen, Li & Wei 2019, quoting Abele & Wojciszke](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02867/full)). In short: getting ahead and getting along.

## Where it comes from

A large body of research, reviewed in the article opened here, has converged on the pair: "Communion and agency are the two fundamental dimensions of social perception," with lines of work cited including Judd et al. (2005), Abele and Wojciszke (2007, 2014) and Fiske et al. (2007) ([Chen, Li & Wei 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02867/full)).

## How it works

The dual perspective model adds the twist: "The dual perspective model (DPM) predicts that communion is more desirable and important in the other perspective, whereas agency is more desirable and important in the self-perspective" ([Chen, Li & Wei 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02867/full)). We want our colleagues competent and warm, but in ourselves we prize the assertive striving.

## What it explains

The model explains what any description of a person is doing: locating them on two axes. The article links the pair to the stereotype content tradition's findings on competence and warmth, and the model structures how self and other are described.

## Everyday life

The model gives a check for one-sided lives: a season of pure agency (deadlines, training, self-improvement) leaves the communion axis untended, and the reverse. Asking "what did I do for others, and what did I get done" tracks both.
