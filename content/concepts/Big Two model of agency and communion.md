## In plain words

The Big Two is the finding, traced to David Bakan and developed by Andrea Abele and Bogdan Wojciszke, that social judgment runs on two great axes. "Agency arises from striving to individuate and expand the self and involves qualities such as efficiency, competence, and assertiveness," while "communion arises from striving to integrate the self in a larger social unit through caring for others and involves qualities such as benevolence, cooperativeness, and empathy" ([Chen, Li & Wei 2019, quoting Abele & Wojciszke](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02867/full)). In short: getting ahead and getting along.

## Where it comes from

Bakan (1966) named the two modalities of human existence; trait research (Wiggins, Paulhus) and social-cognition research (Judd, Fiske) converged on the same pair from different directions, and Abele and Wojciszke's 2007 and 2014 syntheses consolidated them as the Big Two of social cognition ([Chen, Li & Wei 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02867/full)).

## How it works

The dual perspective model adds the twist: "communion is more desirable and important in the other perspective; conversely, agency is more desirable and important in the self-perspective" ([Chen, Li & Wei 2019](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.02867/full)). We want our colleagues competent and warm, but in ourselves we prize the assertive striving. Recent work adds that each dimension splits into facets: assertiveness and competence on the agency side, warmth and morality on the communion side.

## What it explains

The model explains what any description of a person is doing: locating them on two axes. It underwrites the stereotype content model's warmth-and-competence findings, the structure of self-description, and the observation that moral information about others weighs more than competence information.

## Everyday life

The model gives a check for one-sided lives: a season of pure agency (deadlines, training, self-improvement) quietly starves the communion axis, and the reverse. Asking "what did I do for others, and what did I get done" tracks both.
