withdrawing effort and commitment from a goal that cannot be reached; the research links this capacity to well-being and health (Wrosch et al., 2003; Wrosch et al., 2007).

Goal disengagement is one half of what Carsten Wrosch and colleagues call goal adjustment. When a goal is out of reach, disengagement means withdrawing effort and commitment from it; the other half, goal reengagement, means identifying, committing to and pursuing alternative goals. The theory's claim is that quitting an unattainable goal prevents repeated failure and frees resources for other pursuits.

> "To disengage successfully, a person needs to withdraw effort and commitment from pursuing the unattainable goal. The primary function of goal disengagement is to enable the person to quit trying to attain something that is unattainable, thereby preventing repeated goal failure and its associated psychological distress." (Wrosch, Miller, Scheier & Brun de Pontet 2007, [Giving up on unattainable goals: Benefits for health?, author-hosted PDF](https://www.concordia.ca/content/dam/artsci/wrosch-lab/documents/PSPB_UG_2007.pdf))

### What the studies found

The 2003 studies (undergraduates, young and older adults, parents of children with cancer) found goal disengagement and reengagement associated with ratings of high subjective well-being, with interactive effects between the two. The 2007 paper ran three further studies: people better able to disengage reported fewer health problems and showed more normative patterns of diurnal cortisol secretion, while participants with difficulty disengaging secreted more cortisol during the day and evening hours. The authors note the studies do not settle the causal direction of these processes.
