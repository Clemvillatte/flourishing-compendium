The obstacle's origin analysis is Epictetus' practice, drawn from the Discourses and distilled in the Enchiridion, of tracing a disturbance back to the judgement that produced it before responding. The doctrine behind it:

> "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things. Death, for instance, is not terrible, else it would have appeared so to Socrates. But the terror consists in our notion of death that it is terrible. When therefore we are hindered, or disturbed, or grieved, let us never attribute it to others, but to ourselves; that is, to our own principles." (Epictetus, Enchiridion 5, tr. Carter, [Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html))

### What the analysis carries

The method applied to a first impression is a two-step interrogation: "Work, therefore to be able to say to every harsh appearance, 'You are but an appearance, and not absolutely the thing you appear to be.' And then examine it by those rules which you have, and first, and chiefly, by this: whether it concerns the things which are in our own control, or those which are not; and, if it concerns anything not in our control, be prepared to say that it is nothing to you." The passage also marks the moral arc of the practice: an uninstructed person blames others, a beginner blames himself, and the one perfectly instructed "will place blame neither on others nor on himself" — the point is diagnosis, not self-accusation. The compendium principle K037 carries the applied form: learn the three sources of harm before responding, since the origin of the obstacle is usually an assented-to appearance rather than the event itself. A compendium exercise drawn from the analysis is to take one recurring grievance and write, first, what happened, and second, the notion that made it unbearable. What the analysis does not give: a denial of real events; it relocates the harm, not the happening.
