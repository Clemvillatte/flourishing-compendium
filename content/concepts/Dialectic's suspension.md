Dialectic's suspension names the point in a Platonic dialogue where the inquiry stops without an answer and the speakers are left puzzled. The Greek word for it is aporia. A clear instance is the Meno, in Benjamin Jowett's translation, where Meno, after failing to define virtue, tells Socrates: "at this moment I cannot even say what virtue is" ([Plato, Meno, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1643)).

### The torpedo fish

Meno compares Socrates to "the flat torpedo fish", which numbs whoever touches it. Socrates accepts the comparison only in part: "I perplex others, not because I am clear, but because I am utterly perplexed myself." He then invites Meno to continue the inquiry together ([Plato, Meno](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1643)).

### Perplexity as progress

Later, Socrates questions Meno's slave boy about doubling a square. The boy first answers confidently, then fails. Socrates comments: "He did not know at first, and he does not know now ... but then he thought that he knew, and answered confidently as if he knew ... now he has a difficulty, and neither knows nor fancies that he knows." He asks, "Is he not better off in knowing his ignorance?" and Meno agrees. Socrates concludes that the boy "was the better for the torpedo's touch" ([Plato, Meno](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1643)). In the dialogue, the stalled state is what makes the boy want to learn: Socrates says he would not have inquired "until he had fallen into perplexity under the idea that he did not know, and had desired to know."

### Scope

My phrase refers to the dialogues' dead ends in general. This page draws only on the Meno, in one nineteenth-century translation, and does not survey other dialogues or the scholarship on aporia. The passage is about inquiry. It does not say the suspended state is calm or pleasant, and Meno experiences it as numbness.
