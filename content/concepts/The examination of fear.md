“The examination of fear” — Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus.

### What it is

The compendium uses this title for the method of Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus: take a fear, hold it up to reasoning, and watch it dissolve or shrink to its real size. His famous case is death: "Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not. It is nothing, then, either to the living or to the dead, for with the living it is not and the dead exist no longer" (Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, [epicurus.net](http://www.epicurus.net/en/menoeceus.html)). The letter's diagnosis of dread is precise: "Whatever causes no annoyance when it is present, causes only a groundless pain in the expectation" (Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, [epicurus.net](http://www.epicurus.net/en/menoeceus.html)).

### What it carries

The same examination applies to the gods and to imagined punishments: "there are gods, and the knowledge of them is manifest; but they are not such as the multitude believe" (Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, [epicurus.net](http://www.epicurus.net/en/menoeceus.html)), and the letter's summary of the practice is methodical: "It is sober reasoning, searching out the grounds of every choice and avoidance, and banishing those beliefs through which the greatest tumults take possession of the soul" (Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, [epicurus.net](http://www.epicurus.net/en/menoeceus.html)).

The B109 principle turns this into a habit: name the fear, write down what it assumes, and check each assumption. Most dreads, like Epicurus's death, are pain in the expectation only, and the examination is how the expectation gets audited.
