Context-dependent behaviour is the finding at the centre of Wendy Wood and David Neal's [2007 model of habits](https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/wood.neal_.2007psychrev_a_new_look_at_habits_and_the_interface_between_habits_and_goals.pdf). Their definition: "Habits are learned dispositions to repeat past responses. They are triggered by features of the context that have covaried frequently with past performance, including performance locations, preceding actions in a sequence, and particular people."

[Gardner's later review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25207647/) reframes the same idea at the process level: habit is "a process by which a stimulus generates an impulse to act as a result of a learned stimulus-response association" (Gardner, 2015). The impulse, Gardner adds, "need not generate behaviour": it can compete or combine with impulses and inhibitions from other sources, including conscious decision-making.

### How the cuing works

The context is broad: physical locations, other people, and preceding actions in a sequence can all serve as cues. The associations accrue slowly, in procedural memory, and "do not shift appreciably with current goal states or infrequent counterhabitual responses" ([Wood and Neal, 2007](https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/wood.neal_.2007psychrev_a_new_look_at_habits_and_the_interface_between_habits_and_goals.pdf)). Habits are typically "the residue of past goal pursuit": acquired while pursuing goals, then performed without a mediating goal.

That persistence is the practical sting. Habits resist persuasion: "interventions to maximize habit change provide people with concrete tools for controlling habit cuing" rather than appeals to motivation ([Wood and Neal, 2007](https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/wood.neal_.2007psychrev_a_new_look_at_habits_and_the_interface_between_habits_and_goals.pdf)).

The model offers two control routes. Effortful inhibition fires after the cue and is described in the model as drawing on a limited regulatory resource; the authors judge strategies built on inhibition alone unlikely to achieve long-term change. Altering cue exposure works upstream: disabling a notification, or exploiting naturally occurring context changes such as moving house or changing job, which Verplanken and Wood proposed as windows for habit change interventions.

### In the compendium

BE003, "Know that behaviour is triggered by context — environment drives action," states the model directly, and MO036, "Learn to use context-dependent repetition — same context, same behaviour," is its formation recipe. PP072, "Design the environment to support the practice," and Y066 on preparing the practice environment take the cue-control route, as does AT045, "Learn to protect attention through environmental design — the environment is stronger than willpower," which carries the model's jab at willpower.

A108, "Practise habituation through repeated action," is Aristotle's version of the repetition mechanism, and A042's hexis is the character-level analogue. SP094, "Keep the free person's calendar," makes a nice comparison: the Stoic routine and the habit model agree that stable contexts automate action and free control for what matters. That reading is a comparison, not a documented influence.
