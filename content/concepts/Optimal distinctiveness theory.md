Marilynn Brewer's 1991 abstract proposes "a model of optimal distinctiveness in which social identity is viewed as a reconciliation of opposing needs for assimilation and differentiation from others". Individuals "avoid self-construals that are either too personalized or too inclusive and instead define themselves in terms of distinctive category memberships", and "social identity and group loyalty are hypothesized to be strongest for those self-categorizations that simultaneously provide for a sense of belonging and a sense of distinctiveness" ([Brewer, The Social Self: On Being the Same and Different at the Same Time, Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 17(5), 475-482, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167291175001)). The abstract also reports one initial laboratory experiment in which depersonalization and group size interacted to determine the strength of identification.

### The tension

Brewer writes that social identities derive "from a fundamental tension between human needs for validation and similarity to others (on the one hand) and a countervailing need for uniqueness and individuation (on the other)" (Brewer, 1991, p. 477, as quoted in [Brewer, Sydney Symposium chapter, PDF](https://sydneysymposium.unsw.edu.au/2007/Chapters/BrewerSSSP07.pdf)). Her later account states that the two needs are independent and "work in opposition". As a person is immersed in larger units, the need for inclusion eases while the need for differentiation rises, and the reverse holds for smaller units, so counterpressures lead toward "an equilibrium point".

### Mid-level groups

The seed's "mid-level inclusiveness" matches the later review chapter: needs for inclusion and differentiation "can be met by membership in moderately inclusive (optimally distinct) groups", and the evidence "strongly supports" a curvilinear relation between group inclusiveness and group identification ([Leonardelli, Pickett and Brewer chapter abstract, Academia.edu listing](https://www.academia.edu/484840/Optimal_Distinctiveness_Theory_A_Framework_for_Social_Identity_Social_Cognition_and_Intergroup_Relations)). Wikipedia lists the theory's tenets from page 478 of the 1991 paper, including that optimal distinctiveness "is independent of the evaluative implications of group membership, although, other things being equal, individuals will prefer positive group identities" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimal_distinctiveness_theory)).

### Scope

The reviews I opened were written largely by Brewer and colleagues, so the "strongly supports" verdict is from the theory's own research programme. A separate management review shows the idea has moved to organisations, where it concerns firms being similar to and different from peers ([Zhao and Glynn, Optimal Distinctiveness: On Being the Same and Different, Oxford ORA PDF](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:7f26b1ea-f5ef-4c8e-aedc-f787dacf76b9/files/sh989r5005)).
