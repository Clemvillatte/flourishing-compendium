In Heidegger's Being and Time, the concept of authentic existence appears as Eigentlichkeit ([Varga & Guignon, SEP, §3.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/authenticity/)). The entry explains the term (§3.1):

> "The word we translate as 'authenticity' is actually a neologism invented by Heidegger, the word Eigentlichkeit, which comes from an ordinary term, eigentlich, meaning 'really' or 'truly', but is built on the stem eigen, meaning 'own' or 'proper'. So the word might be more literally translated as 'ownedness', or 'being owned', or even 'being one's own', implying the idea of owning up to and owning what one is and does."
> — [Varga & Guignon, "Authenticity", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/authenticity/)

The entry's gloss on Heidegger (§3.1): in merely "doing what one does", "we fail to own up to who we are. We do not take over our own choices as our own and, as a result, we are not really the authors of our own lives". Kierkegaard supplies the relational self — "The self is a relation that relates itself to itself…" — and the suggestion that each of us is to "become what one is" (§3.1). Sartre frames the obstacle as bad faith, which "is a denial of transcendence or freedom" (§3.2). Sartre's positive formula, from Anti-Semite and Jew: "Authenticity, it is almost needless to say, consists in having a true and lucid consciousness of the situation, in assuming the responsibilities and risks it involves, in accepting it … sometimes in horror and hate" (§3.2) ([SEP, Authenticity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/authenticity/)). The Kierkegaard and Sartre quotations are verified through this entry, not directly against the seed books.
