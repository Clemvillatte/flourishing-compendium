Autonomy is self-governance: acting from one's own endorsement rather than from external pressure. The word joins two traditions, one philosophical and one psychological, and the compendium draws on both. In Kant it is the courage to think for oneself; in self-determination theory it is a basic need.

### Kant's maturity

In the 1784 essay on enlightenment, autonomy appears as the exit from a comfortable dependence:

> "Enlightenment is man's emergence from his self-imposed immaturity. Immaturity is the inability to use one's understanding without guidance from another. This immaturity is self-imposed when its cause lies not in lack of understanding, but in lack of resolve and courage to use it without guidance from another. Sapere Aude! [dare to know] 'Have courage to use your own understanding!' — that is the motto of the enlightenment." (Kant, "What Is Enlightenment?", 1784, [reading text](https://users.manchester.edu/facstaff/ssnaragon/kant/lp/Readings/Kant,%20Enlightenment.html))

The guardians in his example make staying dependent easy: a book to serve as understanding, a pastor as conscience, a physician for the diet. Paying others to do the thinking is the comfort he diagnoses, and the courage to stop paying for it is the whole of his enlightenment.

### A basic need

In self-determination theory autonomy is one of three basic psychological needs, alongside competence and relatedness. The theory's encyclopedia entry states the contrast directly:

> "To be autonomous involves acting with a full sense of volition, endorsement, and choice, whereas, to be controlled involves feeling externally pressured or compelled to behave whether by the promise of a contingent reward, fear of punishment, ego-involvement, or other external factors." (Ryan & Deci, [SDT encyclopedia entry](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/2022_RyanDeci_SDT_Encyclopedia.pdf))

The same entry formalizes the needs' claim: three needs "which are universal and essential for the psychological well-being and optimal functioning of all people", with satisfaction and frustration affecting wellness invariantly. A World Values Survey analysis of 66 countries and nearly 100,000 people bears the cross-cultural part out: "autonomy was positively related to well-being across the world", with the link stronger in wealthier and more individualistic societies ([Martela, Joshanloo & Krys](https://aaltodoc.aalto.fi/bitstreams/d01330cc-c9cd-4620-9c0e-0284646f1da1/download)).
