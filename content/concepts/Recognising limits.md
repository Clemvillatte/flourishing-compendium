Recognising limits, in the Stoic account, is the first distinction the Enchiridion asks you to make: what is up to you and what is not. Epictetus opens the handbook with the line:

> "There are things which are within our power, and there are things which are beyond our power. Within our power are opinion, aim, desire, aversion, and, in one word, whatever affairs are our own. Beyond our power are body, property, reputation, office, and, in one word, whatever are not properly our own affairs."
> — [Epictetus, Enchiridion I, trans. Thomas W. Higginson (Gutenberg)](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45109/45109-h/45109-h.htm)

The Enchiridion puts opinion, aim, desire and aversion within our power, and body, property, reputation and office beyond it ([Epictetus, Enchiridion I, trans. Thomas W. Higginson (Gutenberg)](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45109/45109-h/45109-h.htm)). The handbook draws the consequence immediately: the things within our power are "by nature free, unrestricted, unhindered", while those beyond it are "weak, dependent, restricted, alien" ([Epictetus, Enchiridion I, trans. Thomas W. Higginson (Gutenberg)](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45109/45109-h/45109-h.htm)). Epictetus then makes the promise that carries the whole training: "if you take for your own only that which is your own and view what belongs to others just as it really is, then no one will ever compel you, no one will restrict you" ([Epictetus, Enchiridion I, trans. Thomas W. Higginson (Gutenberg)](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/45109/45109-h/45109-h.htm)). The compendium's own list of what has limits — body, illness, medication, clinical boundaries — is the compendium's framing, not Epictetus's; it sits alongside his category without being contained by it.
