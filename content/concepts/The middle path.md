The middle path (*majjhimāpaṭipadā*) is the Buddha's first teaching-point: the way of practice that avoids the two extremes of sensual indulgence and self-mortification, delivered in the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, the first sermon ([Wikipedia, “Middle Way”](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Way); [Springer, “Middle Way (Buddhism)”](https://link.springer.com/rwe/10.1007/978-94-024-0852-2_280)). The biographical frame is the Buddha's own: years of palace pleasure failed to answer suffering, years of ascetic starvation failed too — the insight of the middle path was that the mind clears not through indulgence or punishment but through tuned effort, the image of the lute string ([[B009 — Walk the Middle Way]]; [[Right effort]]).

The teaching is double: an ethical middle (moderation in pleasure, but not the denial of pleasure — [[A085 — Practise moderation in pleasure]]; [[S069 — Do not let pleasure enslave you]]) and a cognitive middle (the view of reality that avoids both eternalism and nihilism — things arise dependently, neither fixed nor meaningless). The middle path is therefore not lukewarm averaging: it is the exact tuning of effort, emotion, and view that the situation requires, which is why it rhymes with Aristotle's mean ([[The doctrine of the mean]]) and with the Daoist refusal to force ([[Wu wei]]; [[Y037 — Choose deliberate difficulty in the right dose]]).

The distinction to hold: the middle path is not the average of two errors. Standing midway between “only pleasure matters” and “nothing matters” is still an error; the path is a third thing — a practice with its own logic ([[MO059 — Learn to balance striving and contentment — ambition without suffering]]; [[A012 — Practice temperance (sophrosyne)]] is its Greek cousin).

An example at its smallest: on a low-energy day, practice ten minutes instead of forty — not the indulgent zero, not the punishing forty ([[B091 — Tune effort without strain (relaxed diligence)]], cited from this concept's tradition).

The Confucian cousin is [[Zhongyong]]; the Aristotelian one is [[The doctrine of the mean]].
