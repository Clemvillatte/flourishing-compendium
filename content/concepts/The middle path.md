The middle path (*majjhimā paṭipadā*) is the Buddha's name for a way of practice that keeps clear of two extremes. It comes in his first sermon, the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (SN 56.11), where he says:

> "There are these two extremes that are not to be indulged in by one who has gone forth. Which two? That which is devoted to sensual pleasure with reference to sensual objects: base, vulgar, common, ignoble, unprofitable; and that which is devoted to self-affliction: painful, ignoble, unprofitable." (SN 56.11, Thanissaro translation, [Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn56/sn56.011.than.html))

### A path, not a midpoint

The sutta goes on to say what the middle way is: "Precisely this Noble Eightfold Path". That is a full programme of eight factors, from right view to right concentration, not a compromise struck halfway between two vices. A common modern reading takes the middle path as moderation or averaging. The sutta's own definition does not say that: it names a practice with its own logic, and the extremes are what it avoids. For the Greek take on balance, see [[The doctrine of the mean]].

### Tuned effort

The same idea shows up as a matter of effort in AN 6.55. The monk Sona was thinking of giving up the training. The Buddha reminded him that, as a former house-dweller, he had played the vina, the Indian stringed instrument, and asked whether it played when the strings were too taut or too loose. Then:

> "In the same way, Sona, over-aroused persistence leads to restlessness, overly slack persistence leads to laziness. Thus you should determine the right pitch for your persistence" (AN 6.55, Thanissaro translation, [Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an06/an06.055.than.html))

The tuning is a skill and the right pitch changes with the day. On a low-energy morning, ten minutes of practice instead of either zero or forty is the middle path at its smallest. (Illustrative scenario, not a reported case.)
