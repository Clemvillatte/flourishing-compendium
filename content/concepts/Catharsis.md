Catharsis is Aristotle's word for what tragedy does with pity and fear. His definition of tragedy, in Butcher's translation:

> "Tragedy, then, is an imitation of an action that is serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude; in language embellished with each kind of artistic ornament... in the form of action, not of narrative; through pity and fear effecting the proper purgation of these emotions." (Aristotle, Poetics VI, tr. Butcher, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1974/1974-h/1974-h.htm))

### What the concept carries

The word's later history turned it into a folk theory of venting: get the anger out and it is discharged. The research record has been unkind to that reading, which is why the original entry's companion page is anger regulation research, where the venting theory this concept inspired was disconfirmed; expressing an emotion at full intensity tends to rehearse it rather than drain it. Aristotle's own claim is narrower and better aged: the emotions are worked through, given form and completion, by an imitation — the shaped experience, not the raw discharge. That is the sense in which music and the arts still earn their place in moral education (A110): the shaping is the medicine. A compendium exercise drawn from the concept is to take an emotion one wants rid of and give it a form — a written scene, a piece of music, a drawn image — rather than an outburst. What the concept does not give: a license to ventilate on other people; the purgation Aristotle describes happens in the audience, not at the expense of one.
