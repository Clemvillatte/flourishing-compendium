Symbolic interaction theory holds that people act toward things, other people included, on the basis of the meanings those things carry for them, and that those meanings are made and revised in interaction with others. The tradition runs from George Herbert Mead's pragmatist social psychology to Herbert Blumer, who coined the term and condensed the approach into three premises.

> "Humans act toward things on the basis of the meanings they ascribe to those things." (Blumer's first premise, [Wikipedia, "Symbolic interactionism"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_interactionism), citing Blumer 1969)

### Origin

The tradition runs from George Herbert Mead's pragmatist social psychology to Herbert Blumer, who, per the opened carrier, coined the term and condensed the approach into the three premises quoted here.

### The three premises

> "The meaning of such things is derived from, or arises out of, the social interaction that one has with others and the society." (Blumer's second premise, [Wikipedia, "Symbolic interactionism"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_interactionism), citing Blumer 1969)

> "The Meanings are handled in, and modified through, an interpretative process used by the person in dealing with the things he/she [sic] encounters." (Blumer's third premise, [Wikipedia, "Symbolic interactionism"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_interactionism), citing Blumer 1969)

Together the premises make meaning a social product rather than a property of objects: a tree is lumber, shade, a home or a sacred being depending on the community of use, and each member's reading stays open to revision.

### Thinking as inner conversation

The approach treats thought itself as interaction turned inward. Symbolic interactionists describe thinking as an inner conversation; Mead called this inner dialogue minding, a delay in one's thought process. On this view the self is not a fixed thing one brings to interactions but something built and rebuilt through them.

### In everyday life

The theory reads small scenes: the same request lands differently as an order, a plea or a joke, and the difference is made in the exchange, not in the words alone. For a writer on flourishing, it relocates change: to alter what people do, one often has to alter what things mean to them, and meanings are negotiated, not decreed.
