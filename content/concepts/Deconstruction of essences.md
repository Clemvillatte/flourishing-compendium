The deconstruction of essences is the Madhyamaka (Middle Way) practice of examining things that seem to stand on their own and finding that they do not. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on Nāgārjuna says the thing he calls empty is svabhāva, a Sanskrit word for which "inherent existence" and "intrinsic nature" are the more popular translations, while "substance" and "essence" "have also been proposed" ([SEP: Nāgārjuna](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)).

### What is actually targeted

The entry separates two senses, and the difference matters for the word "essence". Svabhāva understood as essence is a property an object cannot lose without ceasing to be itself, as heat is for fire. The entry states that "this concept of svabhāva ... is not the target of the Madhyamaka critique." The main target is svabhāva as substance, meaning something that exists as a "primary existent" and is independent of anything else. In the entry's words, "It is the understanding of svabhāva as a primary existent or substance that constitutes the main target of Nāgārjuna's philosophical criticism" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)). Nāgārjuna argues that there is no such end-point of dependence, so nothing is an ultimate foundation.

### Not nihilism

The same entry says the Mādhyamikas "themselves are very clear that their position avoids both of the extreme views": belief in svabhāva and its nihilistic opposite. It also describes emptiness as a corrective, not a hidden reality: "Emptiness is not some kind of primordial reality ante rem but a corrective to a mistaken view of how the world exists" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)).

### Why it matters for suffering

The entry links the analysis to practice: "Realization of the non-existence of svabhāva is supposed to have important soteriological consequences as part of Buddhist practice; ultimately it is understood to be the way to the liberation from existential suffering" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)). For a reader working on flourishing, the claim is philosophical first. Seeing that a fixed self, enemy or failure is held in place by conditions and by how we describe it is meant to loosen grip on it. The entry does not offer this as an empirical result.

### Scope

The compendium's earlier note credits Garfield's Engaging Buddhism. That book was not consulted for this page, so the attribution is unchecked, and the account here rests on the Stanford entry by Westerhoff. The compendium's phrase "nothing has a fixed essence" is looser than that entry: the target is substance and independent existence, and essence in the narrow sense is not the main one.
