Ego development is Jane Loevinger's name for the lifelong maturation of the frame through which a person interprets self and world. Loevinger, a developmental psychologist (1918-2008), drew on Erikson's psychosocial model and Harry Stack Sullivan's interpersonal theory, in which "the ego was theorized to mature and evolve through stages across the lifespan as a result of a dynamic interaction between the inner self and the outer environment" ([Wikipedia, Loevinger's stages of ego development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loevinger%27s_stages_of_ego_development)). The Wikipedia article summarizes her view of the ego with a sentence that turns the noun into a process:

> "Loevinger describes the ego as a process, rather than a thing; it is the frame of reference (or lens) one uses to construct and interpret one's world." ([Wikipedia, Loevinger's stages of ego development, as the article's summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loevinger%27s_stages_of_ego_development))

The theory "goes beyond the fragmentation of trait psychology and looks at personality as a meaningful whole" ([ibid.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loevinger%27s_stages_of_ego_development)), and it is empirical rather than purely philosophical: Loevinger built the Washington University Sentence Completion Test as the primary method of scoring the stages ([ibid.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loevinger%27s_stages_of_ego_development)). The model proposes the stages as sequential and cannot be skipped, per the article's summary:

> "Since each new ego stage or frame of reference builds on the previous one and integrates it, no one can skip a stage ... One has not yet acquired the interpersonal logic." ([Wikipedia, Loevinger's stages of ego development, as the article's summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loevinger%27s_stages_of_ego_development))

The nine stages move from dependence to integration. The Pre-Social ego "really has no ego to speak of until it begins to differentiate itself from its caregivers and the demands of the outer environment" ([ibid.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loevinger%27s_stages_of_ego_development)). The Impulsive stage views the world egocentrically, oriented almost exclusively to the present. Self-Protective is "the first step towards self-control of impulses," with morality reduced to anticipating rewards and punishments. The Conformist sees "a right way and a wrong way and it is the same for everyone." The Self-Aware stage, a "model for adults in our society," shows "rudimentary self-awareness and self-criticism"; Loevinger thought few passed it before age twenty-five. Conscientious internalizes rules and recognizes exceptions, judging people by motives as well as actions. Individualistic begins to replace moralism with awareness of inner conflict. Autonomous tolerates ambiguity, accepts emotional interdependence, and makes self-fulfillment a frequent goal. Integrated, "rarely attained," exhibits "wisdom, broad empathy, awareness of or tolerance for inner conflicts, and the ability to make peace with them" ([all stage descriptions from](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loevinger%27s_stages_of_ego_development)).

The route Loevinger took to this theory was through a research anomaly. She built a Family Problems Scale to test mothers' attitudes, found the expected results did not appear, and noticed that women at the extreme ends of an authoritarian scale tended to be the most immature ([ibid.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loevinger%27s_stages_of_ego_development)). She observed that a liberal, non-authoritarian personality was not the simple opposite of a highly authoritarian one; anomie, "a disorganized and detached social style," occupied the opposite pole ([ibid.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loevinger%27s_stages_of_ego_development)). Theorizing that her scale measured a broader underlying concept, and combining that with Sullivan and Grant's interpersonal-maturity continuum, she created the concept of ego development ([ibid.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loevinger%27s_stages_of_ego_development)).

The theory matters to this compendium because it names the growth the essays assume a person can undergo: a widening of the lens through which suffering, other people, and one's own conflicts are interpreted. It sits alongside other developmental frames in the compendium — [[Ego integrity (Erikson)]] and the Buddhist [[Ego dissolution]] tradition — offering a measurable, sequential, testable shape for that widening.
