The leap of faith is Kierkegaard's image for a commitment that reason cannot settle: "the act of believing in or accepting something not on the basis of reason," a phrase "commonly associated with Danish philosopher Søren Kierkegaard" ([Wikipedia, Leap of faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leap_of_faith)). Kierkegaard's own word is a spring, not a stumble. In The Concept of Anxiety his pseudonym holds that "a morally free action always comes from a 'leap' and cannot be seen as necessary" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Søren Kierkegaard](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)). What the leap lands on is not a proof but a commitment.

### The double movement

In Fear and Trembling the leap is the second of two movements. The first one anyone with willpower can make:

> "Infinite resignation—the first move in the 'double movement' of faith—involves the renunciation of important goods or commitments in favor of those considered to be 'higher'" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Søren Kierkegaard](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/))

The second is different:

> "whereas infinite resignation is something that I can achieve through my own will, the knight of faith both recognizes his dependence upon a divine power beyond himself and trusts in God's promises—even in the most extraordinary 'trial' of the akedah—in a way that appears to an outsider like Johannes as 'absurd'" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Søren Kierkegaard](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/))

The full figure is drawn on the companion page [[The commitment of the knight of faith]]. Its shape matters here: the knight does not get the finite back by managing the odds. He receives it back:

> "the true measure of Abraham's faith is less his willingness to sacrifice Isaac than his trust and hope that he will 'receive him back'—in this life, not just in eternity" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Søren Kierkegaard](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/))

And the joy is real: "the knight of faith is able to take a genuine joy in the finite world, receiving it back (in the form of Isaac or the princess) despite having given it up in 'resignation'" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Søren Kierkegaard](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)).

### What the leap costs

The leap is not a technique for hard decisions. It is individualizing and costly: "the leap individualizes us; it cuts us off from the comforting truths of the public and its blanket conceptions of right and wrong" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Existentialism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/existentialism/)). Faith itself is a passion under contest, not a calm conclusion: "Faith and offense are opposite and rival passions, and neither can be fully justified by reason" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Søren Kierkegaard](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kierkegaard/)).

Contemporary philosophy of faith keeps the caution. Faith "seems essentially to involve some kind of active venture in commitment and trust," and venture models vindicate faith as a virtue only if they provide "robust entitlement conditions, to ensure that not just any 'leap of faith' is permissible" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Faith](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/faith/)). The James-Clifford divide frames the same caution: Clifford held it "wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence," while James allowed that for questions that are "living, forced and momentous," "faith based on desire is certainly a lawful, and possibly an indispensable thing" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Pragmatism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/)).

### Why it matters for flourishing

My principles [[E052 — Practise the leap of faith]] and [[E042 — Practise commitment without guarantees]] treat the leap as the limit case of committing. Most commitments are not leaps; they are made inside probabilities. But the structure of deciding when the facts run out is trainable, and it is what [[Intolerance of uncertainty]] measures from the clinical side: the ability to act without a guarantee is a capacity, and it can grow or shrink. That extension to everyday life is my reading, stated as such; Kierkegaard's leap is a narrower, stranger thing.
