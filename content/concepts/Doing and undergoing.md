"Doing and undergoing" is John Dewey's name for the two-sided rhythm inside every experience: the part where a person acts on the world, and the part where the world answers back and is felt. Dewey set the pair out in Art as Experience (1934), in the chapter "Having an Experience". His point is that the two phases are not separate events that happen to alternate. An activity only becomes meaningful experience when the doer registers what came back from the doing, and lets it shape the next move.

> "An experience has pattern and structure, because it is not just doing and undergoing in alternation, but consists of them in relationship." (Dewey, [Having an Experience, Art as Experience (1934)](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/philosophy/works/us/an-experience.htm))

### Why the connection matters

Dewey presses the point into a definition of thinking itself. The tie between act and consequence has to be grasped, and grasping it is where meaning comes from:

> "The action and its consequence must be joined in perception. This relationship is what gives meaning; to grasp it is the objective of all intelligence." (Dewey, [Having an Experience](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/philosophy/works/us/an-experience.htm))

A cook who tastes the sauce and adjusts the salt is in the rhythm; a cook who pours and never tastes is not, whatever the amount of activity. Dewey extends the same structure to art: works of art, in his phrasing, "unite the very same relation of doing and undergoing, outgoing and incoming energy, that makes an experience to be an experience."
