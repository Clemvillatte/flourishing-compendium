Self-forgiveness is the forgiveness a person extends to the self after committing an offense. Julie Hall and Frank Fincham defined it in 2005, in terms that a 2023 article renders as follows.

> "a set of motivational changes whereby one becomes decreasingly motivated to avoid stimuli associated with the offense, decreasingly motivated to retaliate against the self [.], and increasingly motivated to act benevolently toward the self" (Hall & Fincham, 2005, p. 622, as quoted in Hampton, Carruth, Lurquin & Miyake, [Personality correlates of dispositional forgiveness](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10646512/), Frontiers in Psychology, 2023)

The definition adapts Michael McCullough's three motivations for interpersonal forgiveness, avoidance, revenge and benevolence, and turns them on the self. The same article records that Hall and Fincham once called self-forgiveness the "stepchild of the forgiveness literature" (p. 621), and observes that less is known about it than about forgiveness of others.
