Self-forgiveness is the forgiveness a person extends to the self after committing an offense. Julie Hall and Frank Fincham brought it into the research literature in 2005, and their definition is the one later studies keep returning to.

> "Based on McCullough et al. (1998) framework, Hall and Fincham (2005) defined self-forgiveness as "a set of motivational changes whereby one becomes decreasingly motivated to avoid stimuli associated with the offense, decreasingly motivated to retaliate against the self [.], and increasingly motivated to act benevolently toward the self" (p. 622)." (Hampton, Carruth, Lurquin & Miyake, [Personality correlates of dispositional forgiveness](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10646512/), Frontiers in Psychology, 2023, quoting Hall & Fincham 2005)

The definition adapts Michael McCullough's three motivations for interpersonal forgiveness, avoidance, revenge and benevolence, and turns them on the self. Hall and Fincham also noted how little attention the topic had received: a 2023 review records that they "once called self-forgiveness the "stepchild of the forgiveness literature" (p. 621)". Research has since grown, though the same review observes that less is known about self-forgiveness than about forgiveness of others.
