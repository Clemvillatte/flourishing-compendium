The narrative fallacy is Nassim Taleb's name for the mind's inability to leave a sequence of facts alone: given events in order, we weave an explanation into them, and the story then feels like understanding. Taleb's definition, from The Black Swan:

> "The narrative fallacy addresses our limited ability to look at sequences of facts without weaving an explanation into them, or, equivalently, forcing a logical link, an arrow of relationship upon them. Explanations bind facts together. They make them all the more easily remembered; they help them make more sense." (Taleb, 2007, [The Black Swan](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5045839/), pp. 63–64)

### What the definition carries

The passage continues: "Where this propensity can go wrong is when it increases our impression of understanding." The fallacy is not storytelling itself but the confidence the story smuggles in. A later study of expert judgment, quoting the definition, adds the practical warning: "we sometimes make up things to fill in missing information or neglect information that does not fit in with our story. Consequently, we sometimes base our conclusions on a wrong story" ([Kunst & Van de Wiel, 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5045839/)). The compendium pairs this with hindsight's double move, CO024: after the fact, the story makes the outcome look inevitable, and "I knew it all along" feels true. A compendium exercise drawn from the concept is to take a recent outcome and write the bare facts twice, once as a story with a cause and once as an unexplained sequence, and notice the confidence difference. What the concept does not give: a laboratory measure of the bias; Taleb's formulation is an essayist's diagnosis, later taken up by experimental work.
