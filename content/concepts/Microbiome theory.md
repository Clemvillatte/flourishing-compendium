O'Hara and Shanahan describe intestinal microflora as having collective metabolic activity comparable to "a virtual organ within an organ" ([EMBO Reports, 2006, PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16819463/)). Their abstract states that the microflora is "a positive health asset" that influences the normal structural and functional development of the mucosal immune system, and later states:

> "The flora has a collective metabolic activity equal to a virtual organ within an organ, and the mechanisms underlying the conditioning influence of the bacteria on mucosal homeostasis and immune responses are beginning to be unravelled." ([O'Hara & Shanahan, EMBO Reports, 2006, PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16819463/))

The same abstract notes that in genetically susceptible individuals, some components of the flora can become a liability and contribute to intestinal disorders including inflammatory bowel diseases, and that manipulating the flora to enhance its beneficial components is a promising therapeutic strategy ([same record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16819463/)).

### The behaviour half: microbiota-gut-brain axis

Cryan and Dinan's review in Nature Reviews Neuroscience in 2012 cites studies in germ-free animals and animals exposed to pathogenic bacterial infections, probiotic bacteria or antibiotic drugs as suggesting a role for the gut microbiota in the regulation of anxiety, mood, cognition and pain ([abstract, PubMed PMID 22968153](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22968153/)). The abstract states that accumulating data indicate "that the gut microbiota also communicates with the CNS--possibly through neural, endocrine and immune pathways--and thereby influences brain function and behaviour" ([abstract, PubMed PMID 22968153](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22968153/)). The abstract ends by describing the microbiota-gut-brain axis as an emerging concept whose modulation may be a tractable strategy for developing novel therapeutics for complex CNS disorders ([same record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22968153/)).
