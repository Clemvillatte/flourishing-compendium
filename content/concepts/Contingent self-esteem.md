Self-esteem is contingent when a person treats meeting particular criteria as a condition of being worthwhile. Crocker discusses these criteria as contingencies of self-worth in "The Costs of Seeking Self-Esteem":

> Connie Wolfe and I have called these contingencies of self-worth, because they reflect the domains in which people believe they must achieve or succeed to be worthwhile.
>
> (Crocker, "The Costs of Seeking Self-Esteem", Journal of Social Issues 58(3), 2002, [full text](https://docslib.org/doc/4349695/the-costs-of-seeking-self-esteem-jennifer-crocker-university-of-michigan))

The article's hypothesis concerns the external contingencies in particular: "external contingencies of self-worth require validation from others and are unreliable as a basis of self-esteem, and hence are associated with greater costs" (Crocker 2002). Crocker reports that data from a longitudinal study of an ethnically diverse sample of 642 college freshmen support the view that contingencies of self-worth shape how students spend their time, and support the prediction that external contingencies, especially appearance, have high costs for stress, aggression, drug and alcohol use, and symptoms of disordered eating.
