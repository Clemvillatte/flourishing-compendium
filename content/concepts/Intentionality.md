The modern concept starts with Brentano's 1874 revival of the Scholastic term, quoted here as the Stanford Encyclopedia gives it:

> "Every mental phenomenon is characterized by what the Scholastics of the Middle Ages called the intentional (or mental) inexistence of an object, and what we might call, though not wholly unambiguously, reference to a content, direction toward an object (which is not to be understood here as meaning a thing), or immanent objectivity." (Brentano, Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint, as quoted in the [SEP entry on intentionality](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/intentionality/))

The same passage makes the mark of the mental the having-of-an-object:

> "In presentation, something is presented, in judgment something is affirmed or denied, in love loved, in hate hated, in desire desired and so on." (Brentano, as quoted in the [SEP entry on intentionality](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/intentionality/))

And Brentano's test: "No physical phenomenon exhibits anything like it." The SEP's own one-liner for the derived concept: "In philosophy, intentionality is the power of minds and mental states to be about, to represent, or to stand for, things, properties and states of affairs."

Husserl's treatment of the concept in the fifth of the Logical Investigations carried it into phenomenology, and the tradition is downstream of the move: begin from acts as being-of-something, then ask how the something is meant. Brentano's third sentence in the entry's quotation gives the doctrine its edge — the distinction between mental and physical phenomena is to be drawn by this criterion alone, since no physical phenomenon exhibits anything like intentional containment of an object.

**Plain words** Thinking is always thinking of something; there is no such thing as a thought with no object, the way there can be a wave with nothing to wave in. Every fear is a fear of something, every plan a plan of something.
