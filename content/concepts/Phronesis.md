Phronesis (φρόνησις) is the Aristotelian virtue of practical wisdom: the intellectual excellence that enables a person to deliberate well about what is good and advantageous for themselves and for living well in general. Aristotle discusses it at length in Nicomachean Ethics Book VI, where he distinguishes it from the other intellectual virtues ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phronesis)). Epistēmē is scientific knowledge of necessary truths; sophia is theoretical wisdom, the combination of nous (intuitive grasp of first principles) and epistēmē. Phronesis alone is directed at action in particular situations: it is the capacity to perceive what the situation requires and to choose the means that actually realise good ends. Aristotle is explicit that phronesis concerns not just selecting means to given ends but also determining which ends are worth pursuing.

A crucial feature is that phronesis cannot be reduced to rules. Aristotle observes that the young can be expert geometers but cannot be practically wise, because phronesis requires knowledge of particulars that only comes with experience (NE VI.8 1142a). It is therefore an acquired perceptual capacity, trained by habituation, not a body of propositional knowledge. Aristotle also holds that phronesis is inseparable from moral virtue: without the moral virtues, phronesis degenerates into an inability to take practical action with regard to genuine goods for human beings ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phronesis)).

Aristotle also makes phronesis the bridge between childhood habituation and mature virtue. All free persons are born with the potential to become ethically virtuous and practically wise, but they must go through two stages: childhood habituation forms the dispositions, and once reason is fully developed, practical wisdom refines them ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/)). Ethical virtue is fully developed only when combined with practical wisdom (1144b14–17).

## What it is NOT


Phronesis is not the mere application of rules. Aristotle is explicit that the circumstances of action vary so much that no set of rules, however complicated, can solve every practical problem. Without the moral virtues, phronesis degenerates into an inability to take practical action with regard to genuine goods ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phronesis)). Aristotle's claim is that phronesis and ethical virtue require each other. Modern attempts to formalise phronesis (such as the Aristotelian Phronesis Model) are reconstructions, not Aristotle's own theory. In the Rhetoric, phronesis also appears as one of the three character appeals (ethos), which is a related but distinct use.

## Practical implications


For habit and flourishing, phronesis names the skill the entire self-improvement literature presupposes but rarely discusses: the judgment about what to do, when, and to what degree, in a particular situation that no rule fully specifies. It is the corrective to one-size-fits-all advice ("wake at 5am", "always say yes") and to the opposite failure, endless rule-searching. Aristotle's own prescription is habituation plus reflection: act, notice the result, and let the perception improve. This is analogous to modern deliberate practice and to the research on coping flexibility, though the connection is interpretive rather than a demonstrated equivalence.
