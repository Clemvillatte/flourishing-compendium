"Anger examined early" names a piece of Seneca's advice in On Anger, the three-book essay he wrote to his brother Novatus. The examination happens at the beginning: before the belief that one has been injured settles, and before the opinion that licenses revenge is formed. Seneca's Stoic position is that anger cannot begin without the mind's agreement — it "can venture upon nothing by itself, without the approval of mind" ([Seneca, Of Anger, Book II, chapter I, translated by Aubrey Stewart](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Of_Anger/Book_II)) — so the place to intervene is the judgment, not the eruption.

> "We ought to fight against the first causes of evil: the cause of anger is the belief that we are injured; this belief, therefore, should not be lightly entertained. We ought not to fly into a rage even when the injury appears to be open and distinct: for some false things bear the semblance of truth. We should always allow some time to elapse, for time discloses the truth. Let not our ears be easily lent to calumnious talk: let us know and be on our guard against this fault of human nature, that we are willing to believe what we are unwilling to listen to, and that we become angry before we have formed our opinion." ([Seneca, Of Anger, Book II, chapter XXII](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Of_Anger/Book_II))

### The practical method

The examination Seneca prescribes is delay. "The greatest remedy for anger is delay: beg anger to grant you this at the first, not in order that it may pardon the offence, but that it may form a right judgment about it: if it delays, it will come to an end." He adds not to attempt to quell anger all at once, "for its first impulses are fierce; by plucking away its parts we shall remove the whole." Time is what lets the appearance of injury be checked against what actually happened.
