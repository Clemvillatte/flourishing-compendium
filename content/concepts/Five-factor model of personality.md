The five-factor model (FFM), often called the Big Five, sorts personality differences into five broad dimensions. An Oxford Academic reference article describes it as "a set of five broad trait dimensions or domains": Extraversion, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, Neuroticism (sometimes named by its opposite, Emotional Stability) and Openness to Experience (sometimes named Intellect) ([Oxford Academic, Five-Factor Model of Personality](https://academic.oup.com/reference/62403/reference-article/571608672)).

### What the five labels mean

The same article gives the poles: extraverted people are "assertive and sociable, rather than quiet and reserved"; agreeable people are "cooperative and polite, rather than antagonistic and rude"; conscientious people are "task focused and orderly, rather than distractible and disorganized"; and neurotic people are "prone to experiencing negative emotions, such as anxiety, depression, and irritation, rather than being emotionally resilient".

### Why researchers trust it

Britannica names three lines of support. First, "the five factors have consistently emerged from factor analyses conducted on numerous data sets composed of descriptive trait terms from a number of languages, including English, Chinese, and German". Second, twin and adoption studies show "a substantial genetic component". Third, people's standings on the factors "have also been shown to be fairly stable across much of the adult life span" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/science/five-factor-model-of-personality)). Paul Costa's 1992 abstract in the Journal of Personality Disorders says the model is "a dimensional representation of personality structure" and reviews its consensual validity, comprehensiveness, universality, heritability and longitudinal stability ([Costa, 1992](https://guilfordjournals.com/doi/10.1521/pedi.1992.6.4.343)).

### Stable, but not fixed

The Oxford Academic article cites a meta-analysis finding that "about 40 percent of individual differences in the Big Five can be attributed to genes, and about 60 percent attributed to environmental factors". Another meta-analysis it lists finds that "levels of Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, and the social-dominance facet of Extraversion (assertiveness and talkativeness) increase with age across adulthood" while Neuroticism decreases. A third finds that rank-order consistency "is modest throughout the lifespan for each of the Big Five traits, indicating that both stability and change can coexist".

Trull and Widiger's review also notes that personality "does change over time, typically for the better (ie, increased conscientiousness and agreeableness, along with decreased neuroticism) as one matures through adulthood" ([Trull and Widiger, 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3811085/)).
