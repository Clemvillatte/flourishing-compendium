This entry discusses CBT coping cards. Crane and Watters recommend using them as cues for practising interventions learned in treatment ([Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Strategies, VA CBT-D clinician guide, p. 100](https://www.mirecc.va.gov/visn5/EBT/CBT-D/Cognitive_Behavioral_Therapy_Strategies.pdf)):

> "Coping cards are a great way to practice the interventions you have learned in treatment. Identify common situations that are difficult for you. Include any relevant information including internal experiences. Review and revise these cards, use them as cues, until they become your dominant responses." ([Crane & Watters, VA CBT-D clinician guide, p. 100](https://www.mirecc.va.gov/visn5/EBT/CBT-D/Cognitive_Behavioral_Therapy_Strategies.pdf))

### The guide's example

The guide's worked example shows a situation ("A cashier is rude to me.") followed by coping skills: "Think: Their behavior isn’t my problem. Maybe they are having a bad day. Everyone can have a bad day." and a "Do:" list of actions ([same document, p. 100](https://www.mirecc.va.gov/visn5/EBT/CBT-D/Cognitive_Behavioral_Therapy_Strategies.pdf)). The instruction to review and revise the cards until they become dominant responses is the guide's own emphasis.
