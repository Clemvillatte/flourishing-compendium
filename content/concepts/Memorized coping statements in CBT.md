Coping cards are the CBT version of rehearsed responses to recurrent fears: short written statements a person prepares for the situations that reliably trigger them, then reads when the situation arrives. The compendium treats them as the modern cousin of the older practice of learning a cure by heart, which principle X007 carries for Epicurus's fourfold cure. A clinical guide for CBT for depression, prepared for the US Department of Veterans Affairs, describes the tool:

> "Coping cards are a great way to practice the interventions you have learned in treatment. Identify common situations that are difficult for you. Include any relevant information including internal experiences. Review and revise these cards, use them as cues, until they become your dominant responses." ([Crane & Watters, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy Strategies, VA CBT-D clinician guide, p. 100](https://www.mirecc.va.gov/visn5/EBT/CBT-D/Cognitive_Behavioral_Therapy_Strategies.pdf))

### What the writing does

The work happens twice. The card is composed in a calm state, where the balanced response can be reasoned out, and it is read in the difficult moment, when composing it fresh would be harder. The guide's example shows the format: a situation on one side ("A cashier is rude to me"), thoughts and actions on the other ("Think: Their behavior isn't my problem. Maybe they are having a bad day. Everyone can have a bad day."). The instruction to review and revise the cards until they become dominant responses is the memorization half of the technique; the card itself is a bridge while the response is still being internalized.
