“Intergenerational responsibility” — Gardiner, A Perfect Moral Storm; Jonas, The Imperative of Responsibility.

Intergenerational responsibility names the duties owed to people who do not yet exist and cannot return them. Hans Jonas built the concept for the technological age, on the ground that modern action now reaches across generations while traditional ethics looked only to the immediate setting of an act.

> "An imperative responding to the new type of human action and addressed to the new type of agency that operates it might run thus 'Act so that the effects of your action are compatible with the permanence of genuine human life'; or expressed negatively: 'Act so that the effects of your action are not destructive of the future possibility of such life'; or simply: 'Do not compromise the conditions for an indefinite continuation of humanity on earth'; or, again turned positive: 'In your present choices, include the future wholeness of Man among the objects of your will'" (Jonas 1984, p. 11). — [quoted in Mangone, SN Social Sciences 1(1) (2021), full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7797197/)

### Why the future is owed anything

The one-directional structure is the point: future generations are exposed to present choices and can give nothing back, which for Jonas makes the responsibility unconditional rather than contractual. Gardiner's Perfect Moral Storm, the other original citation, sharpens the political problem — the worst effects fall on those with no vote and no voice, while the benefits of inaction accrue now. For the essays on flourishing, the concept does two jobs: it extends care beyond the horizon of one life, and it reframes present comfort as a decision made on someone else's behalf.
