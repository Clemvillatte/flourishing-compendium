Bandura's social cognitive theory of morality explains moral conduct through the interaction of personal standards, self-regulation and social circumstances ([Bandura, 2002, pp. 101–102](https://centrodocumentacion.psicosocial.net/wp-content/uploads/2003/01/bandura-selective-moral-disengagement-in-the-exercise-of-moral-agency.pdf)). In this account, people monitor their actions, judge them against their standards, and respond to themselves with satisfaction or self-condemnation ([p. 102](https://centrodocumentacion.psicosocial.net/wp-content/uploads/2003/01/bandura-selective-moral-disengagement-in-the-exercise-of-moral-agency.pdf)).

> Moral agency is manifested in both the power to refrain from behaving inhumanely and the proactive power to behave humanely.
>
> Albert Bandura, “Moral Disengagement in the Perpetration of Inhumanities,” [abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1207/s15327957pspr0303_3).

### When moral control is disengaged

For Bandura, moral standards do not automatically govern behavior; the self-regulatory processes must be activated ([2002, p. 102](https://centrodocumentacion.psicosocial.net/wp-content/uploads/2003/01/bandura-selective-moral-disengagement-in-the-exercise-of-moral-agency.pdf)). He describes moral disengagement through justifying harmful conduct, obscuring responsibility, minimizing harm, and dehumanizing or blaming victims ([p. 102](https://centrodocumentacion.psicosocial.net/wp-content/uploads/2003/01/bandura-selective-moral-disengagement-in-the-exercise-of-moral-agency.pdf)). He argues for safeguards in social systems alongside humane personal standards ([1999, abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1207/s15327957pspr0303_3)).
