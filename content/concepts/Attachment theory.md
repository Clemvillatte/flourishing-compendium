Attachment theory is John Bowlby's account of love bonds. Its core claim is that the bond between child and caregiver is organized by an inborn behavioral system whose business is protection, and that this system keeps running, in adult relationships as much as in childhood. The account below follows Shaver and Mikulincer's 2005 review of the theory and its research program.

> "According to Bowlby (1982/1969), human beings are born with an innate psycho-biological system (the attachment behavioral system) that motivates them to seek proximity to significant others (attachment figures) in times of need. The set-goal of the system is the attainment of actual or perceived protection and security; hence, the system is automatically activated when a potential or actual threat to one's sense of security is appraised." ([Shaver and Mikulincer, 2005](https://adultattachment.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/66/2015/09/Shaver_2005_Attachment-theory-and-research.pdf))

### Security

What counts as success for the system is a felt sense of safety. Interactions with attachment figures who are available and responsive in times of need "promote a core sense of attachment security — a sense that the world is generally a safe place, that attachment figures are generally helpful when called upon, and that it is possible to explore the environment curiously and to engage effectively with other people" ([Shaver and Mikulincer, 2005](https://adultattachment.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/66/2015/09/Shaver_2005_Attachment-theory-and-research.pdf)). That last clause is the theory's bridge to exploration: security is what frees a person to venture out.

When figures are not reliably available, "a sense of security is not attained, serious doubts about one's self-efficacy and others' intentions are formed, and secondary strategies of affect regulation other than appropriate proximity seeking are adopted." The review describes "two major kinds: hyperactivation and deactivation of the attachment system" — intense, compulsive efforts to attain proximity and attention on one side; on the other, "the inhibition of proximity-seeking inclinations and actions, and the suppression or discounting of any threat that might activate the attachment system."

### Working models

The theory's account of how early bonds shape later relationships runs through mental representations. "Bowlby (1973) proposed that such interactions can have enduring, long-term effects on personality development which are mediated by mental representations he called 'attachment working models.'" These are expectations about whether others will respond and whether one is worth responding to, carried forward into new relationships.
