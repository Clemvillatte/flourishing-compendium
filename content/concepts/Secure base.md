"Secure base" is attachment theory's name for what an attachment figure provides: the person whose reliable availability makes exploration possible. The concept belongs to Bowlby and Ainsworth jointly, and its theoretical weight is stated plainly in the modern literature: "The secure base concept is central to the logic and coherence of attachment theory and to its status as an organizational construct." (Everett Waters and E. Mark Cummings, "A Secure Base From Which To Explore Close Relationships," 2000, [PDF](https://psychology.psy.sunysb.edu/attachment/online/waters_cummings.pdf))

### The excursions

Ainsworth's original description, from Infancy in Uganda (1967), gives the picture in the infant's own terms: "Once an infant is able to crawl, it does not always stay close to the mother but rather make little excursions away from her, exploring other objects and interacting with other people, but returning to the mother from time to time. The mother seems to provide a secure base from which these excursions may be made without anxiety." (quoted in [Waters, "Measuring Attachment"](https://safersociety.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/12_EWATERS-Measuring_Attachment_Selected-sections-from-SSP-chapter.pdf)) The base is not a place the child stays; it is what makes leaving possible. Waters and Cummings state the general form: for both Bowlby and Ainsworth, to be attached is to use someone preferentially as a secure base for exploration.

Bowlby's late formulation widens the scope to the whole of life: all of us, from the cradle to the grave, are happiest when life is organised as a series of excursions, long or short, from the secure base provided by our attachment figures (Bowlby, 1988, quoted in the attachment literature). Adult versions of the base need less physical proximity and more psychological availability: the felt confidence that the figure is there and responsive.
