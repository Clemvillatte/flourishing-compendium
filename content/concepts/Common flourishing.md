Common flourishing names the good that Aristotle's Politics places as the purpose of the city. The chain of associations runs from household to village to city, and the city completes it: it holds, in his words, "the end and perfection of government", founded for the sake of living and continued for the sake of living happily.

> "And when many villages so entirely join themselves together as in every respect to form but one society, that society is a city, and contains in itself, if I may so speak, the end and perfection of government: first founded that we might live, but continued that we may live happily." (Aristotle, Politics, Book I Chapter II, William Ellis translation, [A Treatise on Government, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/6762/6762-h/6762-h.htm))

### The end of the city

Book III states the point as a definition of what a city is: "It is not therefore founded for the purpose of men's merely living together, but for their living as men ought" ([Politics III.IX](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/6762/6762-h/6762-h.htm)). The same chapter gives the positive formulation: the end for which a city is established is that its inhabitants may live happy, and the city is "a community of families and villages for the sake of a perfect independent life; that is, as we have already said, for the sake of living well and happily" ([Politics III.IX](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/6762/6762-h/6762-h.htm)). Common flourishing is this shared end: the city exists so that the good life, on Aristotle's account, is something its members hold together.
