Divergent thinking generates many varied ideas from the same starting point; convergent thinking narrows to the one already known answer. The standard definitions of the two constructs, as Jaarsveld and Lachmann summarize them:

> "Convergent thinking is defined as the ability to apply conventional and logical search, recognition, and decision-making strategies to stored information in order to produce an already known answer (Cropley, 2006). Divergent thinking, by contrast, is defined as the ability to produce new approaches and original ideas by forming unexpected combinations from available information and by applying such abilities as semantic flexibility, and fluency of association, ideation, and transformation (Guilford, 1959, as cited in Cropley, 2006, p. 1)." (Jaarsveld and Lachmann, [Intelligence and Creativity in Problem Solving](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5292426/), Frontiers in Psychology, 2017)

### Where the terms come from

The same article grounds the history: "Guilford (1967) introduced convergent and divergent thinking as part of a set of five operations that apply in his Structure of Intellect model (SOI model) on six products and four kinds of content, to produce 120 different factors of cognitive abilities." The earlier 1950 presidential address named the divergent side's abilities before the later terms existed: "There is very likely a fluency factor, or there are a number of fluency factors, in creative talent" (Guilford, [Creativity](https://creativityinmusiced.weebly.com/uploads/2/3/8/5/23859476/guilford-1950-creativity.pdf), American Psychologist 5(9): 444-454, 1950, the passage on factors in creative talent).
