Divergent and convergent thinking are the two directions J. P. Guilford's research programme gave to creative cognition: generating many varied ideas from the same starting point, and narrowing to the one best answer. The compendium's seed line, creativity involving both generation and selection, captures the pairing. The 1950 presidential address that launched the programme names the divergent side's abilities:

> "There is very likely a fluency factor, or there are a number of fluency factors, in creative talent. Not that all creators must work under pressure of time and must produce rapidly or not at all. It is rather that the person who is capable of producing a large number of ideas per unit of time, other things being equal, has a greater chance of having significant ideas." (Guilford, [Creativity](https://creativityinmusiced.weebly.com/uploads/2/3/8/5/23859476/guilford-1950-creativity.pdf), APA presidential address, American Psychologist 5(9), 1950)

### What the address names

The same address names the other abilities of the divergent side: "The creative person has novel ideas", and flexibility of mind, "the ease with which he changes set". The address predates the terms divergent and convergent production, which Guilford's Structure of Intellect model later used for the two directions; the 1950 text works with fluency, novelty and flexibility instead.
