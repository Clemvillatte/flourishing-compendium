The illusion of explanatory depth is Leonid Rozenblit and Frank Keil's name for the gap between how well people think they understand causal mechanisms and how well they actually do. Ask someone to rate their understanding of how a zip fastener or a cylinder lock works, then ask them to write a step-by-step explanation, and the confidence collapses.

> "People feel they understand complex phenomena with far greater precision, coherence, and depth than they really do; they are subject to an illusion—an illusion of explanatory depth. The illusion is far stronger for explanatory knowledge than many other kinds of knowledge, such as that for facts, procedures or narratives." — [Rozenblit & Keil, Cognitive Science 26(5) (2002), 521–562, full text](https://cogdevlab.yale.edu/sites/default/files/files/rozenblit%20&%20keil%20%202002.pdf)

### Why the drop matters

In the experiments, participants rated their understanding of 48 items, wrote detailed causal explanations for four test phenomena, and then re-rated their understanding. As the paper puts it, "Attempts to explain the various phenomena led to a decrease in self-rated understanding." The illusion is specific to explanation: ratings for facts, procedures and narratives barely move. The practical use is the one the compendium's principle draws, and the act of writing the explanation is itself the cure, since the exercise exposes the gaps it reveals.
