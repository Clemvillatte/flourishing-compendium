Taking the role of the other is George Herbert Mead's term for the mechanism he thought builds minds and selves: responding to one's own gesture as another person would. The root of it is in communication: "What is essential to communication is that the symbol should arouse in one's self what it arouses in the other individual" ([Mead, Mind, Self and Society 1934](https://ia601500.us.archive.org/5/items/mead-1934/mead%201934.pdf)). In meaningful conduct, "there must be symbols, vocal gestures generally, which arouse in the individual himself the response which he is calling out in the other," and this involves "a taking of the rôle of the other, a tendency to act as the other person acts" ([Mead 1934](https://ia601500.us.archive.org/5/items/mead-1934/mead%201934.pdf)).

### Play, game, and the self

Mead traces the mechanism through childhood. In play, the child "addresses himself as a parent, as a teacher; he arrests himself as a policeman," and the passage closes: "He takes this group of responses and organizes them into a certain whole. Such is the simplest form of being another to one's self" ([Mead 1934](https://ia601500.us.archive.org/5/items/mead-1934/mead%201934.pdf)). The organized game raises the demand: "the child who plays in a game must be ready to take the attitude of everyone else involved in that game, and that these different rôles must have a definite relationship to each other." In a ball game "He must know what everyone else is going to do in order to carry out his own play. He has to take all of these rôles" ([Mead 1934](https://ia601500.us.archive.org/5/items/mead-1934/mead%201934.pdf)).

The internalized audience of the game gets its name later in the book: "the given individual taking the attitudes of others toward himself, and of his finally crystallizing all these particular attitudes into a single attitude or standpoint which may be called that of the 'generalized other'" ([Mead 1934](https://ia601500.us.archive.org/5/items/mead-1934/mead%201934.pdf)). The self built this way has two phases: "The attitudes of others which one assumes as affecting his own conduct constitute the 'me,' and that is something that is there, but the response to it is as yet not given" ([Mead 1934](https://ia601500.us.archive.org/5/items/mead-1934/mead%201934.pdf)). The response to the organized attitudes is the "I"; the self, on this account, is something social through and through, and something still in motion.
