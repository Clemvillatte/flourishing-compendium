False memory implantation is the experimental demonstration that suggestion can plant memories of events that never happened. The founding study is Elizabeth Loftus and Jacqueline Pickrell's 1995 "Lost in the Mall" experiment, in which participants were given short descriptions of three real childhood events plus one fabricated event, being lost in a shopping mall at around age six, constructed with help from an older relative.

> "people can be led to believe that entire events happened to them after suggestions to that effect" — [Loftus & Pickrell, Psychiatric Annals 25(12) (1995), 720–725, p. 723, as quoted in Murphy et al. 2023](https://cora.ucc.ie/server/api/core/bitstreams/075c236a-356f-4694-94c5-9500b6c148d3/content)

### What the study found

About one participant in four, 25 percent in the original report, came to remember the fabricated mall experience as the interviews went on, describing details of an event that never occurred. The finding matters for more than memory: a remembered experience carries confidence and texture, and the study showed both can be manufactured. A preregistered replication in 2023 with 123 participants coded 35 percent as reporting a false memory, most of them partial rather than full memories, while only 14 percent of participants themselves said they remembered the fake event and a further 52 percent said they believed it had happened. So the effect is real, and so is the gap between what coders judge and what people themselves report remembering.

### Why confidence is not accuracy

My principle on implanted memories draws the practical lesson: vividness and confidence tell a person how strongly a memory is held, not whether it happened. The same caution runs through the reconstructive-memory principle next to it, since both rest on the finding that remembering is rebuilding, not replaying.
