Intuitive expertise is the idea that an expert's fast, unreasoned judgments can be genuine skill rather than guesswork, provided two conditions hold: the environment is regular enough to contain learnable cues, and the person has practised long enough with clear feedback to learn them. The term's anchor is the 2009 paper by Daniel Kahneman and Gary Klein, an adversarial collaboration between the heuristics-and-biases and naturalistic decision-making traditions that ended in what the title calls a failure to disagree.

> "Two conditions must be satisfied for skilled intuition to develop: an environment of sufficiently high validity and adequate opportunity to practice the skill." (Kahneman & Klein, [Conditions for intuitive expertise: a failure to disagree](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/kahneman-klein-2009.pdf), American Psychologist 64(6), 2009, pp. 515-526)

### Recognition, not magic

Both authors endorse Herbert Simon's definition of skilled intuition: "The situation has provided a cue: This cue has given the expert access to information stored in memory, and the information provides the answer. Intuition is nothing more and nothing less than recognition" (Simon, 1992, p. 155, quoted in Kahneman & Klein, 2009). On this reading the expert fireground commander who senses a collapse before any alarm sounds is doing what a chess master does with a familiar position: matching cues to a library of stored situations. The paper's warning runs the other way: without opportunities to learn, "a valid intuition can only be due to a lucky accident or to magic—and we do not believe in magic" ([Kahneman & Klein, 2009](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/kahneman-klein-2009.pdf)). Subjective confidence is not a reliable indicator of judgment accuracy; the regularity of the environment is the thing to check.
