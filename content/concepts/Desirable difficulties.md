Desirable difficulties are practice conditions that slow learning down on the surface and speed it up underneath. The term is Robert Bjork's (1994), and the framework was laid out by Elizabeth Ligon Bjork and Robert Bjork in a 2011 chapter whose title carries the whole idea: Making Things Hard on Yourself, But in a Good Way. The finding it names is uncomfortable for anyone who studies: the conditions that feel best while studying often teach least.

> "Conditions of learning that make performance improve rapidly often fail to support long-term retention and transfer, whereas conditions that create challenges and slow the rate of apparent learning often optimize long-term retention and transfer." (Bjork & Bjork, [2011 chapter](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf), p. 57)

### Two strengths

The machinery behind it is a distinction between two strengths of a memory. Storage strength is "how entrenched or interassociated a memory representation is with related knowledge and skills," while retrieval strength is "the current activation or accessibility of that representation and is heavily influenced by factors such as situational cues and recency of study or exposure" ([Bjork & Bjork 2011](https://bjorklab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/13/2016/04/EBjork_RBjork_2011.pdf)). Current performance runs on retrieval strength alone; storage strength is what forgets slowly. The named difficulties follow: "varying the conditions of learning, rather than keeping them constant and predictable; interleaving instruction on separate topics, rather than grouping instruction by topic (called blocking); spacing, rather than massing, study sessions on a given topic; and using tests, rather than presentations, as study events" (same text).

The catch is metacognitive. Learners "interpret current retrieval strength as storage strength" and so "become susceptible to preferring poorer conditions of learning to better conditions of learning" (same text). Fluency masquerades as mastery, which is why rereading feels better than self-testing and teaches less.
