The unreliability of memory is the recognition that remembering is not playback. What comes back when a person remembers has been selected, reorganized and sometimes quietly rewritten, and the rewriter has interests. Nietzsche compressed the whole problem into four sentences:

> "'I did that,' says my memory. 'I could not have done that,' says my pride, and remains inexorable. Eventually—the memory yields."
> — [Nietzsche, Beyond Good and Evil, §68, trans. Helen Zimmern, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/4363/4363-h/4363-h.htm)

The aphorism is about motivation, not storage. The memory does not malfunction; it yields. Something in the person needs the past to be other than it was, and over time the need wins. Nietzsche's point generalizes beyond pride: any strong motive can do this work — guilt, love, self-image, the wish to have been consistent.

Modern psychology reaches the same place by a different route. Bartlett, in the experiments that established the point, put it this way:

> "Remembering … is an imaginative reconstruction, or construction, built out of the relation of our attitude towards a whole mass of organized past reactions or experiences."
> — [F. C. Bartlett, Remembering (1932), as quoted in the Learning and Memory dictionary, Encyclopedia.com](https://www.encyclopedia.com/psychology/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/reconstructive-memory)

What is retrieved is rebuilt from fragments plus expectation, and the confidence felt in a memory is a property of the reconstruction, not a certificate of its accuracy. This is why confidence and correctness come apart: a witness can be certain and wrong, and an implanted scene can feel exactly like a lived one.

The practical consequence is a change in stance toward one's own past. Not disbelief, since most memories are broadly serviceable, but a standing discount on vividness and certainty, especially where the memory flatters or protects.
