Information Manipulation Theory (IMT) was proposed by Steven McCornack in Communication Monographs 59(1), 1-16, in 1992. The abstract explains: "Information Manipulation Theory suggests that deceptive messages function deceptively because they covertly violate the principles that govern conversational exchanges." Because listeners assume how much information should be given and of what kind, "it is possible for speakers to exploit any or all of these assumptions by manipulating the information that they possess so as to mislead listeners" ([McCornack, 1992, Taylor & Francis abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/03637759209376245)).

### The four dimensions

IMT builds on Paul Grice's conversational maxims: quantity, quality, relation and manner. A paper that applies IMT to workplace messages summarises McCornack's view: deception is "a phenomenon in which speaker exploits listener's expectations for disclosure, by covertly altering the information that is disclosed in terms of 'quantity' (via omission), 'quality' (via falsification), 'relevance' (via evasion) and/or 'manner' (via equivocation)" ([paper on perceived deception in upward organizational communication, Indian Journal of Extension Education, PDF](https://epubs.icar.org.in/index.php/IJEE/article/download/111751/43812/465519)). Wikipedia's summary adds that IMT argues "the vast majority of everyday deceptive discourse involves complicated combinations" that sit between truth and lie, with omission the most common form ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_manipulation_theory)). I treat Wikipedia as a pointer, not as proof of McCornack's wording.

A Northwestern computer-science paper gives the roots: McCornack followed earlier suggestions that deviant messages mislead by violating Gricean maxims, relating earlier categories of distortion, concealment and diversion to Grice's maxims ([Telling it like it isn't, PDF](https://users.cs.northwestern.edu/~ortony/Andrew_Ortony_files/2013%20-%20Telling%20it%20like%20it%20isn't.pdf)). It quotes McCornack's "dimensional" approach: deceptive messages as "a potentially infinite class of specific message forms resulting from the manipulation of information in particular, characteristic ways".

### Empirical support and criticism

The companion study by McCornack, Levine and colleagues tested whether altered information is viewed as deception (Communication Monographs 59, 17-29, as cited in the Osaka paper below). A Hong Kong working paper reports that violations of each maxim are rated more deceptive than a baseline message, and that "violations of quality and relevance are typically seen as more deceptive than violations of quantity and manner" ([Lingnan University working paper, PDF](https://commons.ln.edu.hk/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1069&context=hkibswp)).

The theory has a direct challenger. Jacobs, Dawson and Brashers argue that IMT "ignores the role of implicatures in deception" and proposes "a dubious editing model of deception". In their replication "none of the results are consistent with IMT predictions"; instead "the results uniformly support a model of deceptive message design based on conversational implicature" ([Jacobs, Dawson and Brashers, 1996, University of Illinois record](https://experts.illinois.edu/en/publications/information-manipulation-theory-a-replication-and-assessment/)).

### Reading the seed claim

The seed note says deception works through subtle violations "rather than inventing wholesale lies". That fits the Wikipedia gloss and the omission and equivocation categories, but the 1992 abstract itself says only that the violations are covert. Use "covert violation of conversational expectations" as the core claim.
