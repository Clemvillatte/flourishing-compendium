The they (das Man) is Heidegger's name, in Being and Time, for the anonymous public self a person drifts into when they live by what "one" does, says and thinks. It is not a crowd or a class. It is the structure of everydayness itself: the way most people, most of the time, take over their opinions, their dress and their choices from no one in particular.

> "Distantiality, averageness, and levelling down, as ways of Being for the \"they\", constitute what we know as 'publicness' [\"die Offentlichkeit\"]."
> — [Heidegger, Being and Time, Macquarrie-Robinson translation, §27](https://www.beyng.com/pages/en/BeingandTimeMR/BeingandTimeMR.165.html)

The three ways of being named there are the they's working parts. Distantiality is the constant measuring of oneself against others: whether one has fallen behind or pulled ahead. Averageness is the settling of what counts as valid, what is granted success and what is denied it. Levelling down is the pressure this puts on anything exceptional.

### How the they holds

> "In this averageness with which it prescribes what can and may be ventured, it keeps watch over everything exceptional that thrusts itself to the fore."
> — [Heidegger, Being and Time, §27](https://www.beyng.com/pages/en/BeingandTimeMR/BeingandTimeMR.165.html)

Heidegger's point is not that conformity is a moral failing. The they is where a person starts, and for the most part stays, because it is what makes everyday life workable without each decision having to be made from scratch. The retrieval of oneself from it is what he calls authenticity, and his account of conscience treats the call as addressed to the they-self.
