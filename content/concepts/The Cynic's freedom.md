The Cynic's freedom is Diogenes of Sinope's redefinition of *eleutheria* as self-sufficiency rather than status. The source is the anecdote tradition in Diogenes Laertius, Book VI of the Lives. Hicks's translation of the asked-origin line: "Asked where he came from, he said, 'I am a citizen of the world'" ([Diogenes Laertius, Lives 6.63, Hicks translation, Perseus; LacusCurtius text](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Diogenes_Laertius/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/6/Diogenes*.html)).

### What the freedom consists in

The IEP summary of the Cynic triad: "The Cynics clearly privilege freedom, but not merely in a personal sense as a kind of negative liberty. Instead, freedom is advocated in three related forms: *eleutheria*, freedom or liberty, *autarkeia*, self-sufficiency, and *parrhēsia*, freedom of speech or frankness" ([IEP, Cynics](https://iep.utm.edu/cynics/)). The most-quoted anecdote is the Alexander exchange, in Philoscopia's rendering:

> "Ask of me any boon you like."
> "Stand out of my sunlight."

([Diogenes Laertius, Lives, VI, 38, quoted at Philoscopia](https://www.philoscopia.com/en/quotes/diogenes/).) The IEP gives the exchange's political reading: "whereas Plato views paying court as freeing one from poverty, the Cynic sees poverty as freeing one from having to pay court to a ruler" ([same](https://iep.utm.edu/cynics/)). And the most beautiful thing: "Being asked what was the most beautiful thing in the world, he replied, 'Freedom of speech'" ([Lives 6.69, LacusCurtius](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Diogenes_Laertius/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/6/Diogenes*.html)).

### The mechanism

The freedom is negative-dependency: "Whoever desires nothing external depends on no one: self-sufficiency makes the most destitute of men freer than the mightiest of kings" ([Philoscopia's summary](https://www.philoscopia.com/en/philosophers/diogenes/)). The training is *askēsis*: "The Cynics advocate *askēsis*, or practice, over theory as the means to free oneself from convention, promote self-sufficiency, and live in accord with nature" ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/cynics/)). My principle on preferring humble freedom to gilded captivity runs on this account, and the honest caveat the source tradition itself supplies is that the anecdotes are reported, not transcripts: "all our knowledge of him passes through the anecdotes (chreiai) reported three centuries later by Diogenes Laertius in Book VI of his Lives" ([Philoscopia](https://www.philoscopia.com/en/philosophers/diogenes/)).
