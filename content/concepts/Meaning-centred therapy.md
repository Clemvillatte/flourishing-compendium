Meaning-centred therapy (also called meaning-centered psychotherapy, MCP) is a short-term treatment for people with advanced cancer. William Breitbart and colleagues at Memorial Sloan-Kettering Cancer Center created it, rooted in the writings of [[T019 — Frankl, Viktor|Viktor Frankl]], the psychiatrist and Holocaust survivor who wrote Man's Search for Meaning (Montross Thomas, Meier and Irwin). The developers' stated objective, from the first randomized controlled trial:

> "In response to the need for short-term interventions to address spiritual well-being, we developed Meaning Centered Group Psychotherapy (MCGP) to help patients with advanced cancer sustain or enhance a sense of meaning, peace and purpose in their lives, even as they approach the end of life."
>
> (Breitbart, Rosenfeld, Gibson, Pessin, Poppito, Nelson, Tomarken, Kosinski Timm, Berg, Jacobson, Sorger, Abbey and Olden, "Meaning-centered group psychotherapy for patients with advanced cancer: a pilot randomized controlled trial", Psycho-Oncology 19(1), 2010, pp. 21-28. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3648880/))

### Origin
The therapy grew out of Frankl's existential account, in which the creation of meaning is a primary force of human motivation, created through chosen attitude in dire situations, connection with life, engaged work, and one's legacy (Montross Thomas et al.). A later review by Breitbart's group describes the lineage plainly: "Inspired primarily by the works of Viktor Frankl [...] and his emphasis on the importance of meaning in human existence, Meaning Centered Group Psychotherapy (MCGP) was initially conceived of as a group-based intervention for individuals with advanced cancer" (Saracino, Rosenfeld, Breitbart and Chochinov).

### Core claims
The program's working proposition is the Franklian one the review states: it "draws heavily from Frankl's concepts, by identifying the sources of meaning as a resource to help patients develop or sustain a sense of meaning and purpose, even while in the midst of suffering" (Saracino, Rosenfeld, Breitbart and Chochinov). The group program runs eight sessions, mixing teaching and experiential exercises on the sources of meaning. The trial evidence: four randomized trials by Breitbart and colleagues (2010, 2012, 2015, 2018) have tested the group and individual formats, the later ones in samples of 253 and 325 patients (Saracino et al.). In the first trial, 90 patients with stage III or IV solid tumor cancers were randomized to eight weeks of the meaning-centered group or to supportive group psychotherapy, with follow-up two months later. The published abstract reports:

> "MCGP resulted in significantly greater improvements in spiritual well-being and a sense of meaning. Treatment gains were even more substantial (based on effect size estimates) at the second follow-up assessment. Improvements in anxiety and desire for death were also significant (and increased over time). There was no significant improvement on any of these variables for patients participating in SGP."
>
> (Breitbart et al. 2010. [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3648880/))

### How the therapy works
In a 2017 analysis pooling two trials (124 meaning-centered vs 94 supportive-group patients), the improvement was statistically mediated by the patients' enhanced sense of meaning: "improvement due to MCGP is mediated by advanced cancer patients' enhanced sense of meaning" (Rosenfeld, Cham, Pessin and Breitbart). Mediation is evidence for the construct, not proof of a mechanism. The program's working material is the sources of meaning: creative work, connection with life, engaged activity, chosen attitude in dire situations, and legacy (Montross Thomas et al.). Honest limits: the first trial's authors called for larger samples; the review notes attrition was problematic (only 35% attended all eight sessions, most often because they became too sick) and samples were mostly Caucasian women around 60 (Montross Thomas et al.).

### In everyday life
(illustrative scenario) A woman with advanced cancer who feels her life is over is offered, in the group, the Franklian inventory: what she has created, whom she has loved, what she can still choose her attitude toward. In the first trial's own terms, improvements in desire for death were significant in the meaning-centered group and absent in the supportive-group arm (Breitbart et al. 2010).
