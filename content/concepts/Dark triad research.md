The Dark Triad is a grouping of three traits that tend to travel together: narcissism, Machiavellianism and psychopathy. Jones and Paulhus (2014) describe them as "three socially aversive traits" studied "as an overlapping constellation", and developed a 27-item questionnaire, the Short Dark Triad, to measure them. Across four studies with 1,063 people, they report that the scale "provides efficient, reliable, and valid measures of the Dark Triad of personalities" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24322012/)).

### A review with some doubts

Muris and colleagues (2017) reviewed the literature by meta-analysis. They found that "dark triad traits are substantially intercorrelated, somewhat more prevalent among men than women, predominantly related to the Big Five personality factor of agreeableness and the HEXACO factor of honesty-humility, and generally associated with various types of negative psychosocial outcomes."

They also raise two cautions. They "question whether dark triad traits are sufficiently distinct" and argue that "the way they are currently measured is too simple to capture the malevolent sides of personality." Because "most research in this domain is cross-sectional and based on self-reports", they recommend "a cross-informant approach and prospective, longitudinal research designs" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28346115/)).

### Scope

Both abstracts concern what the traits are and how they are measured and how they correlate. They do not describe a link between the Dark Triad and intelligence, and the 2002 article that first named the grouping (Paulhus and Williams) is not summarised here because its abstract was not available for checking.

### In the compendium

- [[A037 — Distinguish cleverness (deinotes) from practical wisdom]] uses Aristotle's cleverness, the skill of finding means to any goal, whether the goal is good or bad. The Dark Triad literature does not test that distinction, so the two are linked by theme: cleverness serving a bad aim is, in Aristotle's words, "mere smartness".
