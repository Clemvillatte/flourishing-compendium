The seed's definition is wrong. The fundamental attribution error (FAE) is not a bias toward over-crediting the self. Ross (1977) named it as a bias about explaining other people's behavior, and the seed's own second line states it correctly. Over-crediting the self belongs to a different idea, self-serving attribution, which the compendium keeps as a separate concept. The page below separates the two.

### What Ross described

> "The first identified (Heider, 1958) and most frequently cited bias or error, one which we shall term the fundamental attribution error, is the tendency for attributers to underestimate the impact of situational factors and to overestimate the role of dispositional factors in controlling behavior." (Ross 1977, The intuitive psychologist and his shortcomings, Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 10:173-220, [text on the Internet Archive](https://ia800601.us.archive.org/21/items/15341_Readings/15341_Readings/Social_Cognition/Ross_Intuitive_Psychologist_in_Adv_Experiment_Soc_Psych_vol10_p173_text.pdf))

Ross cites Jones and Harris (1967) as the evidence most often used for this bias.

### The Jones and Harris experiments

In three experiments, participants estimated the "true" attitude of a person after reading or hearing a speech on a controversial topic, with the writer either choosing or being assigned the position. Among their findings was "a tendency to attribute attitude in line with behavior, even in no-choice conditions". The authors concluded that perceivers do take account of prior probabilities and situational constraints, "but perhaps do not weight these factors as heavily as would be expected by a rational analysis". ([Jones & Harris 1967, abstract](https://sciencedatabase.strategian.com/?p=6053)) That is a milder claim than "people ignore the situation".

### The related actor-observer claim

Jones and Nisbett's actor-observer hypothesis says people explain their own behavior with situation causes and other people's with person causes. A meta-analysis of 173 studies found average effect sizes from d = -0.016 to d = 0.095. The asymmetry appeared only in some conditions, such as when the actor was highly idiosyncratic or when hypothetical events were explained. For negative events it held, and for positive events a reverse asymmetry held, which "may indicate a self-serving pattern in attribution, but across valence, no actor-observer asymmetry exists". ([Malle 2006, Psychological Bulletin 132:895-919, PubMed 17073526](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17073526/)) The compendium's "others to disposition, self to situation" line is a version of that hypothesis and is not supported as a general rule.

### What was not checked

The seed's claim about a reversal of self-serving attribution in depression research was not checked for this page. The meta-analysis above covers valence only, and no depression source was opened.
