Temptation bundling is Katherine Milkman's method of pairing an instantly gratifying "want" with a valuable but effortful "should." The field experiment's own abstract:

> "We introduce and evaluate the effectiveness of temptation bundling—a method for simultaneously tackling two types of self-control problems by harnessing consumption complementarities. [...] Initially, full and intermediate treatment participants visited the gym 51% and 29% more frequently, respectively, than control participants, but treatment effects declined over time (particularly following Thanksgiving). After the study, 61% of participants opted to pay to have gym-only access to iPods containing tempting audiobooks, suggesting demand for this commitment device."

([Milkman, Minson, and Volpp 2014, Management Science 60(2), abstract at EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/inmormnsc/v_3a60_3ay_3a2014_3ai_3a2_3ap_3a283-299.htm).)

### The design and the mechanism

The study gave 226 gym members an iPod with four "addictive" audiobooks, with the full-treatment group's access restricted to the gym. Milkman's own description: "The idea is to tie your engagement into activities so that they're inextricably intertwined—where one of those activities is instantly gratifying but could be wasteful if you do it too much, and the other is really good for you in the long run" ([Penn Today](https://penntoday.upenn.edu/2014-01-16/research/guilty-pleasures-and-obtaining-goals)). The later four-week extension found more modest results: "Giving participants audiobooks and encouraging temptation bundling boosted their likelihood of a weekly workout by 10-14% and average weekly workouts by 10-12% during and up to seventeen weeks post-intervention" ([Wharton teaching paper PDF](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/TemptationBundling.pdf)).

The theory in one line from the same authors: "Valuable healthy behaviors could be increased, while guilt and wasted time from indulgent behaviors decreased, through the use of temptation bundling" ([Wharton Knowledge article](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/researchers-used-hunger-games-encourage-healthier-choices/)). The 61% willing to pay for the restriction is the finding that marks the method as a self-binding device rather than an instruction.
