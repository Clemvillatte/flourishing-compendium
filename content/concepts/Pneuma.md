"Pneuma" — Aristotle, On the Soul; Stoic tradition.

Pneuma is breath, and then more than breath: the bodily spirit that lets soul act on body. In Aristotle's account of animal movement, desire moves the animal through a connatural spirit seated around the heart, a pusher and puller that expands and contracts. The Stoics generalized the term into physics itself, making pneuma the active principle that pervends and structures all matter, giving each thing its qualities and its unity.

> "Now experience shows us that animals do both possess connatural spirit and derive power from this. (How this connatural spirit is maintained in the body is explained in other passages of our works.) And this spirit appears to stand to the soul-centre or original in a relation analogous to that between the point in a joint which moves being moved and the unmoved." (Aristotle, On the Motion of Animals X, trans. Farquharson, [Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/motion_animals.html))
>
> "Pneuma, by its nature, has a simultaneous movement inward and outward which constitutes its inherent 'tensility'. Pneuma passes through all (other) bodies; in its outward motion it gives them the qualities that they have, and in its inward motion makes them unified objects (Nemesius, 47J; cf. Helle 2021)." ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Stoicism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/))

The two carriers agree on the shape: a warm breath-stuff, extended through the body, whose inner tension is what holds a living thing together and lets it act.
