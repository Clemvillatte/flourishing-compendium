Progressive muscle relaxation is "a method of deep muscle relaxation that does not involve any medications, meaning it is a non-pharmacological intervention" ([Wikipedia, Progressive muscle relaxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_muscle_relaxation)). Edmund Jacobson, an American physician and physiologist, developed it from research that began at Harvard in 1908 and published the full procedure in Progressive Relaxation (1929; the 1938 edition is the seed's citation). The practice itself is a two-step drill: tense a muscle group, release it, and attend to the difference. In the article's words, "When the muscle tension is released, attention is directed towards the differences felt during tension and relaxation so that the patient learns to recognize the contrast between the states" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_muscle_relaxation)).

### Where it went next

Jacobson's original protocol ran long: weekly instruction and daily practice, muscle group by muscle group. Joseph Wolpe, building his systematic desensitisation, "shortened Jacobson's technique to a 20 minute exercise" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Progressive_muscle_relaxation)) and used the trained relaxation as the anxiety-inhibiting half of his exposure pairing. Öst's applied relaxation (1988) carried the same logic into cognitive-behavioural therapy, teaching relaxation as a coping skill deployed in the anxiety situation itself.

### Evidence status

The quantitative case is decent. A meta-analysis of 27 studies (1997-2007) of relaxation training for anxiety found that "Cohen's d was .57 (95% CI: .52 to .68) in the within analysis and .51 (95% CI: .46 to .634) in the between group analysis", a medium effect, and reported that "While all relaxation trainings reduced anxiety, applied relaxation, progressive relaxation and meditation showed greater effect sizes than other techniques" ([Manzoni, Pagnini, Castelnuovo and Molinari, BMC Psychiatry 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2427027/)). The authors' own reading of the pooled evidence: "The results show consistent and significant efficacy of relaxation training in reducing anxiety" ([Manzoni et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2427027/)). Effects were larger for longer treatments, and the technique family needs practice to work, which is why the compendium carries it as a learnable skill rather than a one-off trick.
