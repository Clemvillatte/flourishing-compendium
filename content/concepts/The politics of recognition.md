The politics of recognition is Charles Taylor's name, in his essay "The Politics of Recognition" (1992, reprinted 1994), for the strand of contemporary politics that turns on being seen for who one is. The IEP notes that the essay is often treated as the classic expression of a theory of recognition, though it is more accurate to say it awoke a general interest in the idea.

> "can inflict a grievous wound, saddling its victims with a crippling self-hatred"
> — Taylor on misrecognition, "The Politics of Recognition", p. 26, as quoted in the [IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/recog_sp/), which also records his calling recognition "a vital human need" at the same page

The specific importance of recognition lies in its relationship to identity. The IEP records Taylor's definition of identity as "a person's understanding of who they are, of their fundamental characteristics as a human being", and his claim that identity is "partly shaped by recognition or its absence".

### Two ways of being recognised

The IEP summarises Taylor's historical narrative: the collapse of social hierarchies, which had provided the basis for bestowing honour on certain individuals, led to the modern notion of dignity, which rests on universalist and egalitarian principles. Alongside dignity there emerged a new understanding of individualised identity, which Taylor calls the ideal of authenticity, "being true to myself and my own particular way of being".

Taylor constructs from this a politics of equal recognition, and identifies two ways the idea has been understood. The first is the politics of equal dignity, or universalism, which aims at the equalisation of all rights and entitlements. The second is the politics of difference, in which the uniqueness of each individual or group is recognised. The IEP sums them up: the politics of equal dignity is difference-blind, whereas the politics of difference is difference-friendly. Both belong to the account: recognition is not only being treated differently.
