The politics of recognition is Charles Taylor's name, in his 1992 essay "The Politics of Recognition", for the strand of contemporary politics that turns on being seen for who one is, rather than being treated identically to everyone. The essay, reprinted in Multiculturalism: Examining the Politics of Recognition (1994), is now the standard starting point for the debate.

> "can inflict a grievous wound, saddling its victims with a crippling self-hatred"
> — Taylor on misrecognition, "The Politics of Recognition", p. 26, as quoted in the [IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/recog_sp/), which also records his calling recognition "a vital human need" at the same page

The background is a historical shift Taylor traces: from honour-based societies, where recognition was scarce and tied to rank, to modern societies organised around a universal dignity, where it is owed to everyone equally. What multiculturalism adds is a second demand inside that dignity, what Taylor calls the politics of difference. Equal dignity hands out the same basket of rights. Difference asks that the particular identity, the group or the person, be acknowledged as having its own shape.

### The two liberalisms

Taylor's argument runs against two models of the liberal state. The first, procedural liberalism, guarantees rights and leaves cultures to compete; the second treats the survival of a culture as a legitimate goal alongside those rights. His own judgement is cautious. He ends the essay saying that what the presumption of equal value requires is not "peremptory and inauthentic judgments of equal value", but a willingness to study other cultures seriously enough to be changed by the encounter. The [IEP summary](https://iep.utm.edu/recog_sp/) records the key tension: recognition can either be for what is universally present, or for what is specific, and the two pull against each other inside the same demand.
