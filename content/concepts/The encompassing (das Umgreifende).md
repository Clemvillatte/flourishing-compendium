“The encompassing (das Umgreifende)” — Jaspers, Way to Wisdom.

### What it is

Das Umgreifende, rendered "the encompassing" and in Manheim's translation "the Comprehensive", is Karl Jaspers's name for being as a whole, which thought can never turn into an object. His starting point is the structure of consciousness: "The thing that we think, of which we speak, is always something other than ourselves, it is the object toward which we as subject are oriented" (Jaspers, Way to Wisdom, ch. 3, trans. Manheim, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/stream/waytowisdomintro00jasp/waytowisdomintro00jasp_djvu.txt)). The subject-object split therefore points beyond both terms: "being as a whole is neither subject nor object but must be the Comprehensive, which is manifested in this dichotomy" (Jaspers, Way to Wisdom, ch. 3, trans. Manheim, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/stream/waytowisdomintro00jasp/waytowisdomintro00jasp_djvu.txt)).

### What it carries

The Comprehensive cannot be inspected, only indicated: "The Comprehensive does not itself become an object but is manifested in the dichotomy of I and object. It remains itself a background, it boundlessly illumines the phenomenon" (Jaspers, Way to Wisdom, ch. 3, trans. Manheim, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/stream/waytowisdomintro00jasp/waytowisdomintro00jasp_djvu.txt)). Hence "in our thinking we gain only an intimation of the Comprehensive. It is not manifested to us, but everything else is manifested in it" (Jaspers, Way to Wisdom, ch. 3, trans. Manheim, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/stream/waytowisdomintro00jasp/waytowisdomintro00jasp_djvu.txt)).

Jaspers gives the Comprehensive two directions: "the Comprehensive, conceived as being itself, is called transcendence (God) and the world, while as that which we ourselves are it is called being-there, consciousness, mind, and existence" (Jaspers, Way to Wisdom, ch. 3, trans. Manheim, [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/stream/waytowisdomintro00jasp/waytowisdomintro00jasp_djvu.txt)). The compendium's E094 principle draws the practical lesson: every self-description leaves the describer out, so self-knowledge stays useful and incomplete, a map rather than a verdict.
