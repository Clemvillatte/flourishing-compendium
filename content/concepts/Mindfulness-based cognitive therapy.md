Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT) is a group program for people who have recovered from depression and want to stay well. It combines systematic mindfulness training with elements from cognitive therapy. The 2016 meta-analysis paper of the program describes it this way:

> "MBCT teaches people with a history of depression psychological skills that target cognitive mechanisms implicated in depressive relapse (4) by combining systematic mindfulness training with elements from cognitive therapy."
>
> (Kuyken, Warren, Taylor et al., "Efficacy of Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy in Prevention of Depressive Relapse", JAMA Psychiatry 73(6), 2016, pp. 565-574, Introduction. The paper's reference (4) is Segal, Williams and Teasdale's 2002 book Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy for Depression. [Open repository copy](https://pearl.plymouth.ac.uk/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1060&context=psy-research))

### Origin
MBCT grew out of Jon Kabat-Zinn's mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR), the eight-week program started in 1979 at the University of Massachusetts Medical Center. As the MBCT program's Common Questions page puts it:

> "Zindel Segal, Mark Williams and John Teasdale adapted the MBSR program so it could be used especially for people who had suffered repeated bouts of depression in their lives."
>
> ([MBCT — Common Questions](https://mbct.co.uk/about-mbct/))

The three set out the approach in the 2002 Guilford book Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy for Depression: A New Approach to Preventing Relapse. The 2016 paper adds the clinical placement: "MBCT was developed for patients in remission, but at high risk for depressive relapse/recurrence."

### Core claims
The program's claim about how relapse works, and what interrupts it, in co-developer Mark Williams' account for the Royal College of Psychiatrists:

> "It helps break the link between negative mood and the negative thinking that it would normally have triggered."
>
> "Participants reported being able to develop a different ('decentred') relationship to their experience, so that their depression-inducing thoughts could be viewed from a wider perspective as they were occurring."
>
> (Mark Williams, mindfulness-based cognitive therapy talk, Royal College of Psychiatrists Spirituality Special Interest Group [PDF](https://www.rcpsych.ac.uk/docs/default-source/members/sigs/spirituality-spsig/resources/spirituality-special-interest-group-publications-mark-williams-mindfulness-based-cognitive-therapy.pdf))

The Common Questions page gives the same shift in plainer words:

> "It teaches us to shift mental gears, from the mode of mind dominated by critical thinking (likely to provoke and accelerate downward mood spirals) to another mode of mind in which we experience the world directly, non-conceptually, and non-judgementally."
>
> ([MBCT — Common Questions](https://mbct.co.uk/about-mbct/))

### How the theory explains relapse
The rationale is a proposal about mechanism, tested by its results. The proposal is that low mood can reactivate depressive thinking, and that learning to notice and disengage from those patterns may interrupt the downward spiral. The Common Questions page describes the vulnerable state as a kind of tunnel vision, and what the practice trains:

> "Mindfulness practice helps us to see more clearly these patterns of mind; and to learn how recognise when our mood is beginning to go down. This means we can 'nip it in the bud' much earlier than before."
>
> ([MBCT — Common Questions](https://mbct.co.uk/about-mbct/))

The outcome evidence: in a 2016 individual-patient-data meta-analysis of nine randomized trials (1258 participants with data, 60-week follow-up), patients receiving MBCT had a reduced risk of depressive relapse compared with those who did not (hazard ratio 0.69), and a reduced risk against active treatments including antidepressants (hazard ratio 0.79); 229 of 596 who received MBCT relapsed, against 327 of 662 who did not (Kuyken et al. 2016). Williams' account records the trial finding for the group the program was built for: "it substantially reduced the risk of relapse in those who had three or more previous episodes of depression (from 66 per cent to 37 per cent)". These results concern relapse prevention in recurrent depression during full or partial remission; they do not establish efficacy during an acute depressive episode.

### In everyday life (illustrative scenario)
A person who has been depressed before notices the first dip on a grey Monday: a flatness, a flicker of the old thought that nothing is worth doing. The trained move is to register the dip as the start of a known spiral, feel the breath, and attend to what is actually present, so the spiral is met while it is still small. Williams describes the skill participants develop: the capacity "to allow distressing mood, thoughts and sensations to come and go, without having to battle with them". The Common Questions page adds the everyday payoff:

> "Mindfulness teaches us a way in which we can get back in touch with the experience of being alive – learning to savour again the simple pleasures that may have been available to us all along but which we took for granted."
>
> ([MBCT — Common Questions](https://mbct.co.uk/about-mbct/))
