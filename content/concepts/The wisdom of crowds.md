The wisdom of crowds is the finding that a group's aggregated judgment can be more accurate than most or all of its individual members. James Surowiecki's 2004 book of that name made the term current, resting on a counterintuitive claim:

> "Under the right circumstances, groups are remarkably intelligent, and are often smarter than the smartest people in them."
> — [Surowiecki, The Wisdom of Crowds, quoted in Philosophy Etc.](https://www.philosophyetc.net/2004/07/collective-intelligence-rousseau.html)

The "right circumstances" are the load-bearing part. Surowiecki named four conditions, and the crowd that fails them is not wise but merely a crowd:

- **Diversity of opinion** — each person has some private information, even if only an eccentric reading of the known facts
- **Independence** — people's opinions are not determined by the opinions of those around them
- **Decentralization** — people specialize and draw on local knowledge
- **Aggregation** — some mechanism turns private judgments into a collective decision

The founding example is Francis Galton's 1906 report on a country-fair weight-judging contest, where the central estimate of some 800 fairgoers landed within about one percent of the ox's actual weight. Galton, skeptical of democratic judgment, published the result in Nature anyway, remarking it "more creditable to the trustworthiness of a democratic judgment" than he expected.

The mechanism is error cancellation: each guess mixes information with error, and if the errors are independent they tend to cancel in the aggregate, leaving the information. Break independence and the errors correlate — the crowd amplifies its shared bias instead of washing it out. This is why the idea is a conditional claim about good aggregations, not a slogan for consensus.
