Fallibilism is the epistemological principle that any belief, however well supported, may turn out to be mistaken. The standard account of fallibilistic knowledge gives the thesis its precise shape:

> "the subject has fallibilistic knowledge that *p* when she knows that *p* on the basis of some justification *j*, and yet the subject's belief could have been false while still held on the basis of *j*" ([SEP, "Certainty"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/certainty/))

### Peirce's doctrine

The term's philosophical career is largely Charles Sanders Peirce's, and his own definition:

> "Fallibilism is the doctrine that our knowledge is never absolute but always swims, as it were, in a continuum of uncertainty and indeterminacy" (Peirce, CP 1.171, quoted in ["Peirce's Fallibilism"](https://philarchive.org/archive/OKWPFA))

And his conclusion after years of study of the logic of science:

> "we can never be absolutely sure of anything, nor can we with any probability ascertain the exact value of any measure or general ratio" (Peirce, CP 1.147, quoted in ["Peirce's Fallibilism"](https://philarchive.org/archive/OKWPFA))

For Peirce this is a feature of science's self-correcting method rather than a scandal: errors are how investigation makes progress.

### Fallibilism is not skepticism

The distinction that organises my use: fallibilism is not skepticism. The skeptic withholds assent; the fallibilist assents, to the best-tested conclusion available, while holding the assent revisable. It is the epistemic version of the middle path between dogmatism and relativism, and it is what makes [[The examined life]] a permanent practice rather than a completed audit.

### The practical reading

Treat error as information rather than insult, keep the self-concept as revisable as the beliefs, and let intellectual humility do the work that certainty cannot.

### In everyday life

In any argument, naming in advance the observation that would change your mind, and noticing whether one is available at all. (Illustrative scenario, not a reported participant or study result.)

Its Peircean twin is [[The pragmatic maxim]], meaning and inquiry where certainty is refused.
