Correspondent inference theory is Edward Jones and Keith Davis's 1965 account of how an observer reads a person's dispositions from their actions, published as "From acts to dispositions" in Advances in Experimental Social Psychology. An inference is "correspondent" when the act is taken to reflect a disposition directly.

> "A theory is proposed to account for an observer's attribution of personal dispositions upon the perception of an act. Informative ("correspondent") dispositions will be inferred to the extent that there are few distinctive reasons for the act and these reasons have low "prior probability."" (Jones & Davis 1965, chapter abstract, [Garfield Citation Classics, University of Pennsylvania](https://garfield.library.upenn.edu/classics1979/A1979HZ31600001.pdf))

Jones later described the animating notion in plain terms: "strong and confident dispositional inferences are drawn about a person when we see him or her act under conditions of high choice". Standard textbook summaries give three conditions for a confident inference: the behaviour yields non-common effects, is freely chosen, and is low in social desirability, so that the act cannot be explained away as conformity or coercion.
