The dawn and the lamp in the windless place pairs two images of inner steadiness: the lamp the Bhagavad Gita uses for a mind shut away from disturbance, and the dawn Thoreau uses for a mind fully awake. The pairing is my own; neither source makes it.

### The lamp

Arnold's rendering of the Gita gives the image at the end of its chapter on self-restraint:

> "Steadfast a lamp burns sheltered from the wind; Such is the likeness of the Yogi's mind Shut from sense-storms and burning bright to Heaven." (Bhagavad Gita 6.19, trans. Sir Edwin Arnold, The Song Celestial, [Project Gutenberg #2388](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2388/2388-h/2388-h.htm))

The passage goes on to name the state the likeness points to: when the mind, knowing that joy, "cannot be stirred or shook / By any gravest grief, call that state 'peace,' / That happy severance Yoga; call that man / The perfect Yogin!" (ch. 6, same translation). The lamp is not the achievement itself; it is what the settled mind looks like from outside.

### The dawn

Thoreau's dawn is not the hour on the clock but the state of attention:

> "To him whose elastic and vigorous thought keeps pace with the sun, the day is a perpetual morning. It matters not what the clocks say or the attitudes and labors of men. Morning is when I am awake and there is a dawn in me." (Thoreau, Walden, "Where I Lived, and What I Lived For", [Project Gutenberg #205](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-h/205-h.htm))

The two images meet in my use: the lamp describes a mind that disturbance cannot reach, the dawn a mind that sleep has left.
