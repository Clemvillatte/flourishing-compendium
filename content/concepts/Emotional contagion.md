(Hatfield, Cacioppo and Rapson, 1994); social network contagion of behavior and mood (Christakis and Fowler, 2007).

Two different bodies of work sit under this heading. Hatfield and colleagues describe a moment-to-moment process between people in contact. Christakis and Fowler describe patterns across a social network over years. The two should not be merged into one mechanism. One attribution also needs fixing: Christakis and Fowler's 2007 paper concerns obesity, and the study on the spread of happiness is a 2008 paper by Fowler and Christakis.

### Primitive emotional contagion

The 2014 review restates the original 1994 definition.

> "The tendency to automatically mimic and synchronize facial expressions, vocalizations, postures, and movements with those of another person's and, consequently, to converge emotionally." (Hatfield, Bensman, Thornton & Rapson 2014, [Interpersona](https://ojs.interpersonajournal.com/index.php/ojs/article/download/419/3419.pdf), quoting Hatfield, Cacioppo & Rapson 1994)

The theory runs in three stages: mimicry, then feedback from that mimicry, then contagion. The same review gives a hedged account of the mechanism: "Our best guess at the moment, however, is that facial, vocal, and postural mimicry generally produce emotional feedback that sparks contagion." It describes the process as "relatively automatic, unintentional, uncontrollable, and largely inaccessible to conversant awareness".

A point of dispute is whether mimicry and contagion are the same thing. Hess and Fischer propose using "emotional contagion" only for the matching of a subjective emotional experience and "mimicry" for the matching of nonverbal displays. Hatfield and colleagues, by contrast, include mimicry within their definition. ([Hess & Fischer 2014, Emotional mimicry](https://www.ursulakhess.com/resources/HF14.pdf))

### Contagion across a network

> "Clusters of happy and unhappy people are visible in the network, and the relationship between people's happiness extends up to three degrees of separation (for example, to the friends of one's friends' friends)." (Fowler & Christakis 2008, BMJ, [PubMed 19056788](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19056788/))

That study followed 4,739 people in the Framingham Heart Study from 1983 to 2003. Its authors report that "longitudinal statistical models suggest that clusters of happiness result from the spread of happiness and not just a tendency for people to associate with similar individuals". A friend within a mile who becomes happy raised the probability of being happy by 25% (95% confidence interval 1% to 57%), and the effect decays with time and with geographical separation. The wide interval is worth noticing.

The 2007 paper cited in the seed reports that in 12,067 people, a person's chance of becoming obese rose by 57% (95% CI 6 to 123) if a friend became obese in a given interval. ([Christakis & Fowler 2007, New England Journal of Medicine 357:370-379](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17652652/)) This is about body weight, so it supports the seed's word "behavior" at most indirectly and says nothing about mood.

### What the sources do not show

Network clustering in a single cohort does not show the mimicry-and-feedback process Hatfield describes. The 2008 study measured happiness with a four-item scale, not the face-to-face exchange of expressions. The two literatures share the word contagion, and they should be kept apart.
