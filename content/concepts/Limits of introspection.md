“Limits of introspection” — Nisbett and Wilson, 1977.

In their review, Nisbett and Wilson gathered evidence that people may have little or no direct introspective access to higher order cognitive processes. Their subjects were sometimes unaware of the stimulus that influenced a response, unaware of the response itself, and unaware that the stimulus had affected the response. When asked how a stimulus produced a response, people did not consult the process; their reports came from a priori causal theories about what plausibly causes what.

> "Evidence is reviewed which suggests that there may be little or no direct introspective access to higher order cognitive processes. Subjects are sometimes (a) unaware of the existence of a stimulus that importantly influenced a response, (b) unaware of the existence of the response, and (c) unaware that the stimulus has affected the response." (Nisbett & Wilson, 1977, Psychological Review 84(3), 231–259, [abstract](https://home.csulb.edu/~cwallis/382/readings/482/nisbett%20saying%20more.pdf))

The claim is scoped to processes, not to all self-knowledge: the review's own closing suggests people "will sometimes be able to report accurately" when the influential stimuli are salient and plausible causes of the responses they produce. The everyday cost is confabulated reasons — explanations of one's own choices that sound like reports from inside the machine but work like an observer's guess.
