A mediator, in the research sense process-based therapy leans on, is the variable a treatment actually moves that then produces the outcome: the mechanism through which therapy works. The PBT manual quotes the classic definition from Baron and Kenny's 1986 paper:

> "the generative mechanism through which the focal independent variable is able to influence the dependent variable of interest" (Baron & Kenny, 1986, p. 1173, quoted in Hofmann, Hayes & Lorscheid, 2021, Learning Process-Based Therapy, Chapter 3)

PBT's research program is organised around a mediational question: not "did the package beat the control condition?" but "which process did the intervention change, and did changing that process carry the outcome?" The book's kernel standard asks that a change method be linked to evidence-based processes of change — a mediation path is what such linkage looks like when the evidence supports it.

The 1986 paper's own framing is worth having in view: mediation is "the mediator function of a third variable, which represents the generative mechanism through which the focal independent variable is able to influence the dependent variable of interest", a variable standing between cause and effect that carries the influence. What PBT adds is a way of naming the candidates: the EEMM's dimensions and levels say where a mediator can live (affect, cognition, attention, self, motivation, overt behavior, at biophysiological or sociocultural levels), so a mediational hypothesis can be stated precisely enough to be tested and to fail.

The clinical reading follows from the research one. A mediator is a claim about where the work happens; the therapy hour and the homework are only deliveries. When a client improves, the mediational question is which process moved — and when a treatment fails, the same question localizes the failure to a process rather than to a whole package.
