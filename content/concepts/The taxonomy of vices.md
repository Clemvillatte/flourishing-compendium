The taxonomy of vices is Aristotle's scheme in Nicomachean Ethics Book II: every moral virtue sits at a mean between two vices, one of excess and one of deficiency, and the vices are defined by their relation to the virtue rather than by their own content. The scheme is the source of the table the compendium uses throughout: rashness and cowardice flanking courage, self-indulgence and insensibility flanking temperance, prodigality and illiberality flanking liberality.

### The doctrine of the mean

The definition, in Ross's translation:

> "Virtue, then, is a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e. the mean relative to us, this being determined by a rational principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it. Now it is a mean between two vices, that which depends on excess and that which depends on defect."

([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.6, 1106b36-1107a3, Ross translation, Boston University full text](https://people.bu.edu/wwildman/courses/wphil/readings/wphil_rdg09_nichomacheanethics_entire.htm).)

The examples follow immediately in Book II, chapter 7 and 8:

> "For the brave man appears rash relatively to the coward, and cowardly relatively to the rash man; and similarly the temperate man appears self-indulgent relatively to the insensible man, insensible relatively to the self-indulgent, and the liberal man prodigal relatively to the mean man, mean relatively to the prodigal."

([Nicomachean Ethics II.8, Ross translation, Boston University full text](https://people.bu.edu/wwildman/courses/wphil/readings/wphil_rdg09_nichomacheanethics_entire.htm).)

Aristotle is explicit that the extremes are not symmetric: "it is not rashness, which is an excess, but cowardice, which is a deficiency, that is more opposed to courage, and not insensibility, which is a deficiency, but self-indulgence, which is an excess, that is more opposed to temperance" ([II.8, Ross translation](https://people.bu.edu/wwildman/courses/wphil/readings/wphil_rdg09_nichomacheanethics_entire.htm)).

### Why the taxonomy matters

The scheme treats vice as a positional error rather than an alien substance, which is why it is useful for practice: you do not fight cowardice by becoming rash, and you do not fix stinginess by becoming a spendthrift. The Buddhist three poisons (Know the three root poisons, B016) do the same work with a different structure, naming greed, hatred and delusion rather than excess and deficiency.
