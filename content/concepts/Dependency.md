In care ethics, dependency is treated as an ordinary feature of human life that moral and political thought must account for, not as a lapse to be hidden. The concept is associated with Eva Feder Kittay, whose 1999 book is titled Love's Labor: Essays on Women, Equality, and Dependency. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry on feminist ethics describes her "attention to caregivers as 'secondarily' or 'derivatively dependent'", meaning that people who care for the dependent take on dependency of their own ([Norlock and Pascoe, SEP: Feminist Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/feminism-ethics/)).

### Interdependence as a starting point

The same entry says scholars have emphasised caring as a normative framework "distinguished by a recognition that we are necessarily interdependent beings with responsibilities to one another that follow from the fact of our vulnerability", citing Kittay 2011 among others ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/feminism-ethics/)). It also reports that this concern has been developed in work on vulnerability, need and precarity.

### A challenge to contract theories

According to the entry, social contract theory has traditionally "not attended to the inclusion of the needs of children, disabled community members, or their caregivers" (naming Kittay 1999 among its sources). Feminist critics argue against "describing gender, embodiment, or dependency as a mere secondary characteristic irrelevant to what a body in need of care requires to flourish" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/feminism-ethics/)). The point for flourishing is that a theory built on independent adults leaves out most of the people who need help and those who give it.

### Scope

My earlier note cites Kittay's Love's Labor. That book was not read for this page, and the account here rests on the Stanford entry's summary of it. The entry does not say how a reader should treat their own dependence, so any practical advice is an inference.
