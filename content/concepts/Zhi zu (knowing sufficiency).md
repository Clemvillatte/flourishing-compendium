“Zhi zu (knowing sufficiency)” — Daoism, Daodejing 46.

Zhi zu (知足) is the Daoist practice of knowing what is enough: sizing desire to what life already supplies instead of chasing increase. The Daodejing grounds it in observation. Wanting more is the engine of conflict, and contentment is counted there as a kind of wealth. Laozi states it directly in chapter 33 ("He who is satisfied with his lot is rich") and warns of its opposite in chapters 44 and 46.

> "There is no guilt greater than to sanction ambition; no calamity greater than to be discontented with one's lot; no fault greater than the wish to be getting. Therefore the sufficiency of contentment is an enduring and unchanging sufficiency." ([Daodejing 46, James Legge translation, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

### What the chapters do with it

Chapter 46 contrasts two states of the world: when the way prevails, horses are sent back to the dung-carts; when the way is disregarded, war-horses breed in the border lands. The wants of rulers are the difference. Chapter 44 puts the same arithmetic to the reader as a question: fame or life, wealth or life? Its answer: "Who is content / Needs fear no shame. / Who knows to stop / Incurs no blame." Knowing sufficiency works as a decision rule for where to stop, a rule the reader can run on career, possessions and appetite alike.

The compendium's principle CO044 applies it to satisficing: good enough is often optimal.
