“Tragic optimism” — Frankl, 1946; Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning; Lebowitz et al., 2022.

Tragic optimism is Viktor Frankl's name for the stance of saying yes to life in spite of what he called the tragic triad. The concept's anchor text is the 1984 postscript to Man's Search for Meaning, a lecture Frankl gave at the Third World Congress of Logotherapy in 1983:

> "In brief it means that one is, and remains, optimistic in spite of the 'tragic triad,' as it is called in logotherapy, a triad which consists of those aspects of human existence which may be circumscribed by: (1) pain; (2) guilt; and (3) death. This chapter, in fact, raises the question, How is it possible to say yes to life in spite of all that?" (Frankl, [The Case for a Tragic Optimism, postscript to Man's Search for Meaning](http://fabian.ca/sites/default/files/Frankl%20-%20Search%20for%20Meaning%20%28excerpt%29.pdf), p. 161)

The word pairs "tragic" with a Latin pun on the best:

> "'The best,' however, is that which in Latin is called optimum - hence the reason I speak of a tragic optimism, that is, an optimism in the face of tragedy and in view of the human potential which at its best always allows for: (1) turning suffering into a human achievement and accomplishment; (2) deriving from guilt the opportunity to change oneself for the better; and (3) deriving from life's transitoriness an incentive to take responsible action." (same text, pp. 161-162)

The chapter is careful about what the stance does not allow. Optimism is a capacity to draw on, and Frankl explicitly bars commanding it, in oneself or others:

> "It must be kept in mind, however, that optimism is not anything to be commanded or ordered. One cannot even force oneself to be optimistic indiscriminately, against all odds, against all hope." (same text, p. 162)

His examples run to the defiant power of the human spirit, as logotherapy calls it: the quadriplegic student who writes that he views his life as abundant with meaning, and the Vietnam prisoners of war who described captivity, in Frankl's summary of the Yale research, as a growth experience.
