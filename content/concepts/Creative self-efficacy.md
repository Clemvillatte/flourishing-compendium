Creative self-efficacy extends Albert Bandura's self-efficacy construct to creative work. Tierney and Farmer's 2002 study, published in the Academy of Management Journal 45(6), introduced it: in their definition, quoted by later researchers, it is "the belief one has the ability to produce creative outcomes" (Tierney and Farmer 2002, p. 1138, as quoted in [Haase et al. 2018](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10400419.2018.1411436)). Their study, using data from two different firms, "tested a new construct, creative self-efficacy, tapping employees' belief that they can be creative in their work roles"; job tenure, job self-efficacy, supervisor behavior and job complexity fed these beliefs, and "creative self-efficacy also predicted creative performance beyond the predictive effects of job self-efficacy" ([Tierney and Farmer 2002, Academy of Management Journal 45(6), 1137-1148; opened OpenAlex record](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.2307/3069429)).

Later work checked how strong the link is. A meta-analysis that "synthesized 60 effect sizes from 41 papers (overall N = 17226)" found that "taken as a whole, the relation between CSE and creativity measures was of medium size (r = .39)" ([Haase et al. 2018](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10400419.2018.1411436)). The relation depends on how creativity is measured: "Studies that used self-assessment measures of creativity correlated significantly higher with (creative) self-efficacy measures (r = .53) than those using other assessment methods, such as performance tasks and expert-ratings (r = .25)", and "studies that used DT measures of creativity correlated significantly lower with (creative) self-efficacy measures (r = .23) than those studies using other methods than DT (r = .41)" ([Haase et al. 2018](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10400419.2018.1411436)). Confidence and creative output travel together, and they are not the same thing.
