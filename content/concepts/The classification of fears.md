Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus does not set out a formal list of fears. It does address the two fears he treats as the main sources of disturbance, fear of the gods and fear of death, and it sorts desires into kinds so that those which cause fear and pain can be dropped. The "classification of fears" is the compendium's label for that teaching. The text below is Robert Drew Hicks's translation on the [Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Epicurus/menoec.html).

### Fear of the gods

Epicurus affirms that the gods are living, immortal and happy beings. He writes: "Not the person who denies the gods worshipped by the multitude, but he who affirms of the gods what the multitude believes about them is truly impious" ([Letter to Menoeceus](https://classics.mit.edu/Epicurus/menoec.html)).

### Fear of death

"Accustom yourself to believe that death is nothing to us, for good and evil imply awareness, and death is the privation of all awareness." He continues: "Foolish, therefore, is the person who says that he fears death, not because it will pain when it comes, but because it pains in the prospect. Whatever causes no annoyance when it is present, causes only a groundless pain in the expectation." And: "when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not" ([Letter](https://classics.mit.edu/Epicurus/menoec.html)).

### Sorting desires

"Of desires some are natural, others are groundless; and that of the natural some are necessary as well as natural, and some natural only." The one who understands this "will direct every preference and aversion toward securing health of body and tranquillity of mind". Epicurus says pleasure means "the absence of pain in the body and of trouble in the soul", and that "sober reasoning" removes "those beliefs through which the greatest disturbances take possession of the soul" ([Letter](https://classics.mit.edu/Epicurus/menoec.html)).

### The future

"We must remember that the future is neither wholly ours nor wholly not ours, so that neither must we count upon it as quite certain to come nor despair of it as quite certain not to come" ([Letter](https://classics.mit.edu/Epicurus/menoec.html)).

### Scope

The letter is a primary text in one translation. It is an ancient philosophical argument and not clinical evidence that its reasoning reduces anxiety. The letter does not supply a ranked taxonomy of fears, so the page's title is an editorial summary.
