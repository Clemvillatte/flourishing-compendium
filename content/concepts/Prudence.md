Prudence here refers to Aristotle's practical wisdom, phronêsis, a capacity for good deliberation connected with ethical virtue ([Richard Kraut, Aristotle's Ethics, sections 4 and 5.3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/)). Its concern is living well as a whole, rather than success in one specialised activity ([Nicomachean Ethics VI.5](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.6.vi.html)).

> "Practical wisdom, then, must be a reasoned and true state of capacity to act with regard to human goods." (NE VI.5, [W. D. Ross translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.6.vi.html)).

Aristotle distinguishes practical wisdom from cleverness, which can efficiently serve a bad purpose; practical wisdom requires a good character ([NE VI.12–13](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.6.vi.html)). It is therefore wider than simple caution about danger, the context in which several principles below use it as a comparison ([NE VI.5, VI.12](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.6.vi.html)).

## Sources

- Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI
- [Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.5 and VI.12–13, translated by W. D. Ross, MIT Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.6.vi.html): exact definition and the distinction from cleverness.
- [Richard Kraut, Aristotle's Ethics, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, sections 4 and 5.3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-ethics/): practical wisdom's terminology and relation to ethical virtue.
