Aristotle's claim in Nicomachean Ethics IX is that the company we keep shapes the people we become. It comes at the end of his long treatment of friendship, in a passage that sets the friendship of bad men beside the friendship of good ones:

> Thus the friendship of bad men turns out an evil thing (for because of their instability they unite in bad pursuits, and besides they become evil by becoming like each other), while the friendship of good men is good, being augmented by their companionship; and they are thought to become better too by their activities and by improving each other; for from each other they take the mould of the characteristics they approve-whence the saying 'noble deeds from noble men'.

([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX, trans. W. D. Ross, Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.9.ix.html), ch. 12.)

### What the passage claims

Two points stand out. First, the effect runs both ways: bad men "become evil by becoming like each other", and good men "are thought to become better". Second, the mechanism is shared activity and imitation: friends take "the mould of the characteristics they approve". Note the hedge in Ross's English: good friends "are thought to become" better. Aristotle reports this as a received view, not a measured result.

The same chapter describes the reason friends spend their days together: "since they wish to live with their friends, they do and share in those things which give them the sense of living together." Friends drink, dice, exercise, hunt or study philosophy together, "each class spending their days together in whatever they love most in life."

### A related thread in the same book

Earlier in Book IX Aristotle says the good man's friend "is another self". The sentence is "he is related to his friend as to himself (for his friend is another self)" ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX, trans. W. D. Ross, Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.9.ix.html), ch. 4). It helps explain why a friend's character matters to our own: the friend is treated as an extension of the self that we look after.

### What the text does not claim

It does not give a method for choosing friends, and it does not claim that friends of bad character cannot change. It also does not appeal to the modern idea of social contagion; links to that research belong to the compendium's own psychological parallels, not to Aristotle.
