The impact bias is the systematic error people make when predicting their own future feelings. People "base many decisions on affective forecasts, predictions about their emotional reactions to future events", and "they often display an impact bias, overestimating the intensity and duration of their emotional reactions to such events" ([Wilson and Gilbert 2005](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.0963-7214.2005.00355.x)).

### Where the error comes from

The abstract names two mechanisms. The first is attention: "one cause of the impact bias is focalism, the tendency to underestimate the extent to which other events will influence our thoughts and feelings" (ibid.). The second is recovery: "another is people's failure to anticipate how quickly they will make sense of things that happen to them in a way that speeds emotional recovery" (ibid.). Both run in the same direction — the forecast treats the future event as bigger and more permanent than living through it turns out to be.

### Why it matters here

The bias is strongest for negative events: "people fail to anticipate how quickly they will cope psychologically with such events in ways that speed their recovery from them" (ibid.). That asymmetry is the empirical backing for the compendium's advice to distrust both the dread forecast and the euphoria forecast before acting on either.
