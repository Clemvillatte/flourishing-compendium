In "The Will to Believe," William James surveys the tests that philosophers have proposed for truth and reports that none has won agreement. Each candidate criterion has had its defenders, and each has been found wanting in turn. ([James, "The Will to Believe," 1896](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/26659/pg26659.txt))

> "No concrete test of what is really true has ever been agreed upon. Some make the criterion external to the moment of perception, putting it either in revelation, the consensus gentium, the instincts of the heart, or the systematized experience of the race. Others make the perceptive moment its own test,--Descartes, for instance, with his clear and distinct ideas guaranteed by the veracity of God; Reid with his 'common-sense;' and Kant with his forms of synthetic judgment a priori." (James, "The Will to Believe," section III, [Project Gutenberg #26659](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/26659/pg26659.txt))

James's own test is experiential rather than external. The essay adds that objective evidence, so often appealed to, "is never triumphantly there" but remains a distant ideal of thinking life, and that belief is measured by action: what a person holds true shows itself in what they do, and the essay's own examples are ones in which acting on a belief helps bring about the fact believed. ([James, "The Will to Believe," 1896](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/26659/pg26659.txt))
