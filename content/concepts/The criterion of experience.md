In "The Will to Believe," William James reports that no concrete test of what is really true has ever been agreed upon, and lists the criteria philosophers have proposed, from revelation and the consensus gentium to the systematized experience of the race. ([James, "The Will to Believe," 1896](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/26659/pg26659.txt))

The quotation's "his system" is the scholastic's: James is contrasting a way of thinking that rests on its starting principles with one that looks to what experience confirms in the end. ([James, "The Will to Believe," 1896](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/26659/pg26659.txt))

> "The strength of his system lies in the principles, the origin, the terminus a quo of his thought; for us the strength is in the outcome, the upshot, the terminus ad quem. Not where it comes from but what it leads to is to decide. It matters not to an empiricist from what quarter an hypothesis may come to him: he may have acquired it by fair means or by foul; passion may have whispered or accident suggested it; but if the total drift of thinking continues to confirm it, that is what he means by its being true." (James, "The Will to Believe," section VI, [Project Gutenberg #26659](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/26659/pg26659.txt))

The criterion runs forward rather than backward. The essay's own examples include cases in which acting on a belief helps bring about the fact believed, as when trust in another person's liking helps the liking come. ([James, "The Will to Believe," 1896](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/26659/pg26659.txt))
