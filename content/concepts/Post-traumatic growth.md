“Post-traumatic growth” — Bonanno, 2004; Tedeschi & Calhoun; Tedeschi & Calhoun, 1996.

Two findings sit side by side in the research on how people come through disaster. The first, from George Bonanno's review of the evidence, is that resilience is more common than often believed:

> The author challenges these assumptions by reviewing evidence that resilience represents a distinct trajectory from the process of recovery, that resilience in the face of loss or potential trauma is more common than is often believed, and that there are multiple and sometimes unexpected pathways to resilience.

The second, from Richard Tedeschi and Lawrence Calhoun, is that some people also report that the struggle changed them for the better:

> Posttraumatic growth is the experience of positive change that occurs as a result of the struggle with highly challenging life crises.

The growth is not vague. Their 21-item inventory factors it into named domains: greater appreciation of life and changed priorities, warmer relationships, a greater sense of personal strength, recognition of new possibilities, and spiritual development. And the finding carries a guard that matters clinically: growth can coexist with distress. As the authors put it, "virtually everyone reporting posttraumatic growth also acknowledges at least some distress".

### The narrative route

Robert Neimeyer's constructivist work adds the mechanism the facets below record. Grieving, on his account, is an attempt "to reaffirm or reconstruct a world of meaning that has been challenged by loss". Part of that reconstruction is sense making, and part is benefit finding, where mourners encounter "unsought growth through grief, valued life lessons in the experience, or reordered life priorities". The two are not the same thing, and his studies suggest benefit finding buffers complicated grief chiefly when sense making itself fails.
