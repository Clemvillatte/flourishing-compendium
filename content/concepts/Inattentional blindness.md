Inattentional blindness is the failure to perceive a fully visible but unexpected object when attention is engaged elsewhere. Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris gave the effect its most famous demonstration in 1999: viewers counting basketball passes between players in white shirts often failed to see a person in a gorilla suit walk through the middle of the game.

> "Furthermore, without attention, we may not even perceive objects ('inattentional blindness'). Taken together, these findings suggest that we perceive and remember only those objects and details that receive focused attention." — [Simons & Chabris, Perception 28(9) (1999), 1059–1074, abstract via the author's publications page](https://www.chabris.com/Publications.html)

### What the gorilla study found

> "Our results suggest that the likelihood of noticing an unexpected object depends on the similarity of that object to other objects in the display and on how difficult the primary monitoring task is." — [Simons & Chabris 1999](https://www.chabris.com/Publications.html)

Two details matter beyond the spectacle. Similarity drove detection: observers counting passes made by the black-clothed team noticed the dark gorilla more often than those counting the white team's passes. And spatial proximity did not help: an unexpected object right next to where attention is aimed still goes unseen, which suggests perception attaches to objects and events rather than to patches of the visual field.
