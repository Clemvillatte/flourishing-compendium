The paradigm's founding study is Reber's 1967 report, which defined the process under study in its first sentence:

> "Two experiments were carried out to investigate the process by which Ss respond to the statistical nature of the stimulus array, a process defined as 'implicit learning'." (Reber 1967, Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior 6(6): 855-863, [abstract](https://oamonitor.ireland.openaire.eu/national/search/publication?pid=10.1016/s0022-5371(67)80149-x))

Participants memorised letter strings that had been generated by a finite-state grammar they were never told about. The finding, in the abstract's words, was that they had become sensitive to the grammar's structure: "Experiment II showed that information gathered about the grammar in a memorization task could be extended to a recognition task with new stimuli." They could classify new strings as fitting or not fitting the pattern, without being able to state the rules that produced the classification. Reber argued that the learning was a response to "the general grammatical nature of the stimuli, rather than learning to respond according to specific coding systems imposed upon the stimuli."

The design feature the studies share is exposure instead of instruction: performance is measured on materials the learner was never asked to verbalise a rule for. The abstract record goes on to name the family the process belongs to: "It was argued that this 'implicit' learning is similar in nature to the 'differentiation' process of perceptual learning espoused by Gibson and Gibson (1955)." (same Reber 1967 abstract record)

**Plain words** You can pick up a pattern by living in it without ever being able to say what the pattern is. The grammar lives in your thumbs before it lives in your words.
