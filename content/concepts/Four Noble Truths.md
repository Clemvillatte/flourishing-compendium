The Four Noble Truths (Pali cattāri ariya saccāni) are the Buddha's first and framing teaching, delivered in the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (SN 56.11). They are not four propositions to believe but four tasks to perform. The sutta assigns each truth its verb:

> "This noble truth of stress is to be comprehended... This noble truth of the origination of stress is to be abandoned... This noble truth of the cessation of stress is to be realized... This noble truth of the way of practice leading to the cessation of stress is to be developed." (SN 56.11, [Thanissaro translation, dhammatalks.org](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN56_11.html))

Verbs of practice, not articles of faith.

### The four tasks

The first truth names dukkha, the fact that experience, grasped, is unsatisfactory. The second names its origin: tanha, craving, which produces renewal of becoming. The third names its cessation: the remainderless fading of that very craving. The fourth names the path: the Noble Eightfold Path. The compendium's reading (principle B008) is the practical one: treat the truths as tasks, not beliefs, a diagnosis-and-prescription for the mind, closer to medicine than metaphysics.

### The family of diagnostics

The framework is the Buddhist member of a family of diagnostic traditions: the [[The discipline of assent|Stoic discipline of assent]] to what is, the Aristotelian mean between excess and deficiency, and the medical model of symptom, cause, cure, regimen. What distinguishes the Buddhist version is its insistence that the sickness is craving and that the cure is a trained mind, here and now.

### In everyday life

A bad week run through the four questions: what is the friction, what am I reaching for that feeds it, what would the reaching's end feel like, and what is one step of the path. (Illustrative scenario, not a reported participant or study result.)
