The commonwealth of the good is the ideal of a community held together by the highest good its members hold in common, rather than by territory, ethnicity, or fear. The phrase is the compendium's own heading; the seed names Augustine's City of God, and the Spinozism principles that link here carry the same idea in their own words.

### Augustine's two cities

The governing image is the contrast of two communities. As the [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy's article on Augustine's political and social philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/augustine-political-and-social-philosophy/) summarizes, the distinction between those elected for salvation and those elected for damnation "gives rise to two classes of persons, to whom Augustine refers collectively and allegorically as cities—the City of God and the earthly city," one ordered by the love of God, the other by the love of self. The standard that tells them apart is justice, since for Augustine "where there is no justice there is no commonwealth." The distinction is spiritual rather than institutional: "No political state, nor even the institutional church, can be equated with the City of God," and its citizens are "pilgrims and foreigners" who live within political societies without owing them their final loyalty. The commonwealth of the good is therefore never identical with any existing state, and it does not wait for one; it exists wherever people order their loves around the highest good.

### Spinoza's social order

The Spinozism principles that link here carry the idea in the Treatise on the Improvement of the Understanding. Spinoza names the chief good as shared by nature: "The chief good is that he should arrive, together with other individuals if possible, at the possession of the aforesaid character," the knowledge of the union existing between the mind and the whole of nature. From this he derives a political task: it is necessary "to form a social order such as is most conducive to the attainment of this character by the greatest number with the least difficulty and danger." The motive is not sacrifice but happiness, for "it is part of my happiness to lend a helping hand, that many others may understand even as I do, so that their understanding and desire may entirely agree with my own." A good person's understanding becomes more secure as it spreads, so building the commonwealth and pursuing one's own good are the same labor.
