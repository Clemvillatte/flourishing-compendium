### Definition

Outcome forecasting is the mind's attempt to preview what future events will feel like and which of their consequences will matter. The research program that maps it is affective forecasting, built by Timothy Wilson and Daniel Gilbert. Their key finding: we guess the direction of our feelings fairly well and their force badly. As their 2003 review states:

> Affective forecasts can be broken down into four components: predictions about the valence of one's future feelings, the specific emotions that will be experienced, the intensity of the emotions, and their duration. ([Wilson & Gilbert 2003](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Wilson-Affective_Forecasting.pdf))

### Origin

Wilson and Gilbert's 2003 chapter, "Affective Forecasting" (Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, vol. 35), organised a decade of studies into a single picture. Its central error has a name:

> We will refer to mispredictions of this sort as an impact bias, defined as the tendency to overestimate the enduring impact that future events will have on our emotional reactions (Gilbert, Driver-Linn, & Wilson, 2002). ([Wilson & Gilbert 2003](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Wilson-Affective_Forecasting.pdf))

One cause is focalism: the event in question fills the whole screen of the imagination, crowding out the rest of life that will also be happening. Another is our failure to expect how fast the mind makes sense of setbacks, a repair mechanism we rarely credit in advance.

### A real-life example

The candidate rehearses the disaster of rejection for weeks: ruined plans, a dented self, months of gloom. The rejection arrives, stings hard for two days, and by the weekend she is irritated mostly that she spent so long dreading it. Her forecast got the valence right and the duration badly wrong.
