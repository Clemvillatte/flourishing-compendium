Moral dilemmas are situations in which moral considerations conflict, so that honoring one seems to require violating another. The seed line anchors the page to Jonathan Haidt's work; the research programme his model comes from is the psychology of how people actually judge such cases. His 2001 statement of the model, in Psychological Review:

> "Research on moral judgment has been dominated by rationalist models, in which moral judgment is thought to be caused by moral reasoning. The author gives 4 reasons for considering the hypothesis that moral reasoning does not cause moral judgment; rather, moral reasoning is usually a post hoc construction, generated after a judgment has been reached." ([Haidt, Psychol Rev 108(4), 2001, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11699120/))

### Why the intuitions matter for dilemmas

The social intuitionist model Haidt presents states that "moral judgment is generally the result of quick, automatic evaluations (intuitions)", and emphasizes social and cultural influences over private reasoning ([same record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11699120/)). For dilemmas, the practical reading is that the conflict is often felt first and reasoned about after: the quick evaluations arrive on both sides, and the deliberation that follows is frequently a construction that defends a verdict already reached. The model claims greater consistency than rationalist models with recent findings in social, cultural, evolutionary and biological psychology, anthropology and primatology ([same record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11699120/)).
