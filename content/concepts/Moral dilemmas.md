A moral dilemma, as the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy defines it:

> "The crucial features of a moral dilemma are these: the agent is required to do each of two (or more) actions; the agent can do each of the actions; but the agent cannot do both (or all) of the actions. The agent thus seems condemned to moral failure; no matter what she does, she will do something wrong (or fail to do something that she ought to do)." ([McConnell, "Moral Dilemmas", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-dilemmas/))

The entry distinguishes genuine dilemmas from mere conflicts: when one requirement overrides the other, there is a conflict but not a genuine dilemma, and genuine dilemmas, if there are any, are ontological rather than merely epistemic ([same entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-dilemmas/)).

### A model of moral judgment

The seed line anchors this page to Jonathan Haidt's work. His 2001 model in Psychological Review addresses the judgment behind such cases rather than the dilemma structure itself:

> "Research on moral judgment has been dominated by rationalist models, in which moral judgment is thought to be caused by moral reasoning. The author gives 4 reasons for considering the hypothesis that moral reasoning does not cause moral judgment; rather, moral reasoning is usually a post hoc construction, generated after a judgment has been reached." ([Haidt, Psychol Rev 108(4), 2001, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11699120/))

The social intuitionist model he presents states that "moral judgment is generally the result of quick, automatic evaluations (intuitions)", and emphasizes social and cultural influences over private reasoning; it claims greater consistency than rationalist models with recent findings in social, cultural, evolutionary and biological psychology, anthropology and primatology ([same record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11699120/)).
