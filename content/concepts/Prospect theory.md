Prospect theory is Kahneman and Tversky's 1979 descriptive model of choice under risk, the founding paper of behavioral economics. Its own summary of what it does:

> "This paper presents a critique of expected utility theory as a descriptive model of decision making under risk, and develops an alternative model, called prospect theory." ([Kahneman & Tversky, 1979, Econometrica 47(2), abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecm/emetrp/v47y1979i2p263-91.html))

The theory's shape, in the abstract's terms: value is assigned to gains and losses rather than to final assets, probabilities are replaced by decision weights, and "the value function is normally concave for gains, commonly convex for losses, and is generally steeper for losses than for gains" ([1979 abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecm/emetrp/v47y1979i2p263-91.html)). Losses loom larger than gains; the same curve that shows diminishing pleasure in gains shows diminishing pain in losses, but the loss side is steeper. The seed's line — people are risk-averse in gains and risk-seeking in losses — is the abstract's certainty effect sentence: "This tendency, called the certainty effect, contributes to risk aversion in choices involving sure gains and to risk seeking in choices involving sure losses" ([1979 abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/ecm/emetrp/v47y1979i2p263-91.html)).

The compendium pairs the theory with its neighbors: the Anchoring effect, Framing effects, Behavioral economics theory and Regret Theory are its related concepts, and CO019 and CO031 list it among their psychology parallels.
