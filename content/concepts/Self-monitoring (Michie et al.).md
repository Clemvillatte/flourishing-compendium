Self-monitoring, as a behaviour-change technique, means setting up a way to record one's own behaviour or its results, such as a food diary or weight log. Michie and colleagues' 2009 meta-regression of 122 evaluations (N = 44,747) of physical-activity and healthy-eating interventions found that "the technique, 'self-monitoring,' explained the greatest amount of among-study heterogeneity (13%)" ([Michie et al., 2009, Health Psychology 28(6), 690-701, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19916637/)).

> "Interventions that combined self-monitoring with at least one other technique derived from control theory were significantly more effective than the other interventions (0.42 vs. 0.26)." (Michie et al., 2009, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19916637/))

### How firm is the finding

The overall pooled effect of the interventions was 0.31 (95% CI 0.26 to 0.36, I-squared = 69%) (same abstract). The independent summary from the UK Centre for Reviews and Dissemination concludes that the analyses "provided clear support for inclusion of self-monitoring of behaviour in addition to other techniques" in these interventions, but also that, given a lack of details on study quality and other methodological concerns, "the authors' conclusions may be not reliable," and that "funnel plots suggested evidence of publication bias" ([CRD summary](https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/CRDWeb/ShowRecord.asp?ID=12010000208&ID=12010000208)). A later review of reviews found mixed results: for physical activity, "eight reviews, examining effects on 10 outcomes, found no benefit of self-monitoring on physical activity, whereas four reviews, examining five outcomes, found that adding self-monitoring significantly improved physical activity" ([Self-regulatory behavior change techniques in interventions, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7429262/)). The compendium's "highly effective" wording is therefore stronger than the evidence opened here supports, and "often helpful, especially combined with goals and feedback" is closer.
