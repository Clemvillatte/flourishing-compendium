Habit stacking is the anchoring of a new behavior to an existing routine: "After I [existing habit], I will [tiny new behavior]" — the recipe format BJ Fogg gives in Tiny Habits, completed by immediate celebration to wire the new behavior in ([Fogg, Tiny Habits recipe cards](https://tinyhabits.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/tinyhabits-recipe-cards.pdf)). The name is the popularization of the same idea in James Clear's Atomic Habits; the research lineage is the habit-formation work of Phillippa Lally and colleagues, who found that automaticity develops along an asymptotic curve — steeply at first, then plateauing — with the time to reach 95% of the asymptote of automaticity ranging from 18 to 254 days, and a consistent context speeding the curve ([Lally et al., "How are habits formed" (2010)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ejsp.674)).

The mechanism: an existing habit is a stable context cue. Since habits form faster in consistent contexts, deliberately reusing an established routine as the trigger gives the new behavior the old habit's consistency for free. The new behavior must be tiny — a two-minute sit, not an hour — because the anchor is a cue, not a schedule, and celebration is the reinforcement step that marks the behavior as done ([[K176 — Habit stacking]]; [[MO037 — Learn to stack habits — link new behaviours to existing ones]]).

The distinction to hold: habit stacking is not productivity-chaining. The goal is automaticity of a valued practice, not throughput; the new behavior serves the practice, and the celebration is what teaches the brain the behavior counts.

An example at its smallest: after the morning coffee, sit for two minutes ([[B058 — Sit daily — consistency over intensity]]).

Its kin are [[Implementation intentions]] (the if-then cousin in goal-setting research) and the contemplative sequence-first logic of [[Gradual training]].
