Social determinants of well-being is the idea that how people rate their own lives depends heavily on the social conditions around them, and not only on their own traits or income. It borrows its shape from the public-health work on social determinants of health. The clearest statement in the sources opened here comes from John Helliwell:

> "Whether across nations or among individuals within a society, subjective well-being is determined by the circumstances of life, and especially by the social context in which people live." (Helliwell, 2019, Annals of Nutrition and Metabolism 74(suppl 2):8-14, [PDF](https://blogs.ubc.ca/helliwell/files/2020/10/Annals_2019.pdf))

### What the estimates say

Helliwell reports that the World Happiness Report explains about three-quarters of the variation in national average life evaluations with six variables: GDP per capita, healthy life expectancy, and four social ones (someone to count on in times of trouble, freedom to make life decisions, trust measured as perceived absence of corruption, and generosity). He gives one calculation: moving all of those variables from the world's lowest levels to the world average "would raise average life evaluations by almost 2 points on the 0-10 scale, with almost 1.2 points due to having more people with someone to count on in times of trouble" ([Helliwell, 2019](https://blogs.ubc.ca/helliwell/files/2020/10/Annals_2019.pdf)).

The 2020 World Happiness Report chapter by Helliwell, Huang, Wang and Norton reports that "for every one of the identified risks to well-being, a stronger social environment provides significant buffering against loss of well-being, ranging from 30% to over 70% for the separate risks, and averaging 40%." It uses individual-level Gallup World Poll data from about a million respondents in 143 countries ([World Happiness Report 2020, ch. 2](https://files.worldhappiness.report/WHR20_Ch02.pdf)).

### Limits the authors state

Both sources are explicit that these are correlational. Helliwell writes that "these estimates of course rely on correlations, and there are many complex feedbacks in play, so that the empirical results are best seen as illustrative rather than definitive" ([Helliwell, 2019](https://blogs.ubc.ca/helliwell/files/2020/10/Annals_2019.pdf)). The 2020 chapter says the variables "may be taking credit properly due to other variables, or to unmeasured factors," and that "there are also likely to be vicious or virtuous circles, with two-way linkages among the variables" ([World Happiness Report 2020, ch. 2](https://files.worldhappiness.report/WHR20_Ch02.pdf)). The figures describe life evaluations measured on a 0-10 scale, not flourishing in any wider sense.

### In the compendium

The principles listed (for example A006, SO001, SO009, P138 and R107) treat the social setting as part of what flourishing needs. The sources above support a narrower claim: life evaluations vary systematically with trust, support and the other social variables, and a stronger social environment is associated with smaller losses under adversity. The compendium's further step, that a good life requires particular social arrangements, is its own argument. The seed citation to Marmot (2004) was not opened for this revision.
