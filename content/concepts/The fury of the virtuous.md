The fury of the virtuous is Nietzsche's diagnosis in the Genealogy of Morals, Essay 1: the moral vocabulary of the weak can be a weapon, and the anger can wear the clothes of goodness. His engine is *ressentiment*:

> "The slave revolt in morality begins when ressentiment itself becomes creative and gives birth to values: the ressentiment of natures that are denied the true reaction, that of deeds, and compensate themselves with an imaginary revenge."

(GM I, section 10; [Kaufmann translation at Wikiquote](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/On_the_Genealogy_of_Morality); [Johnston translation](https://web.viu.ca/johnstoi/nietzsche/genealogy1.htm).)

### The lambs and the birds

The essay's own image for the fury: the lambs, unable to fight the birds of prey, answer with a valuation — "these birds of prey are evil; and whoever is least like a bird of prey, but rather its opposite, a lamb — would he not be good?" (GM I, section 13; [Berkeley lecture text](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~ngkolodny//F10Phil104L23(Nietzsche-I).pdf)). Nietzsche's point is not that the lambs are wrong to hurt, but that their fury smuggles in a metaphysical error: the belief "that the strong has the option of being weak, and the bird of prey of being a lamb," by which "they win for themselves the right of attributing to the birds of prey the responsibility for being birds of prey" (GM I, section 13; [Wikiquote, Kennedy translation](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/On_the_Genealogy_of_Morality)). The doer, he says, is merely a fiction added to the deed ([SparkNotes summary](https://www.sparknotes.com/philosophy/genealogyofmorals/section4/)).

### The honest use

The concept survives its polemics. Moral anger is real anger, and the more certain it feels, the more it deserves an audit: is this a defense of a value, or an imaginary revenge wearing one? My research-side treatment of indignation, that it should be handled carefully because it escalates, is the same caution without the genealogy.
