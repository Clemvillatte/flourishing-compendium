Arousal reinterpretation is an "alternative strategy: reappraising anxiety as excitement" for tasks that make people anxious beforehand, such as public speaking, karaoke singing or math performance. Most people believe trying to calm down is the best way to cope with such anxiety; across several studies, Alison Wood Brooks found that relabelling the arousal as excitement worked better ([Brooks, 2014](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=45869)).

> "Compared to those who attempt to calm down, individuals who reappraise their anxious arousal as excitement feel more excited and perform better." — Brooks, abstract, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2014 ([HBS](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=45869))

Brooks reports that brief self-talk such as saying "I am excited" helped participants adopt an opportunity mindset and improve their performance ([Brooks, 2014](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=45869)). She interprets the findings as suggesting the importance of arousal congruency during the emotional reappraisal process ([Brooks, 2014](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/item.aspx?num=45869)).

The mechanism the paper names is arousal congruency, and the report frames the finding in terms of mindset rather than of suppression. The comparison is between reappraising anxious arousal as excitement and attempting to calm down, and the paper's result is that the reappraisal group felt more excited and performed better. The concept puts the leverage on the label rather than on the feeling, and the label is the part of the state a person can change without changing the state itself. What the study does not establish is that the two states are physiologically identical, so the finding should be read as about the effect of the reinterpretation rather than as a claim about the underlying arousal.
