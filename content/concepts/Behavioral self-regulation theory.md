Behavioral self-regulation theory is Carver and Scheier's cybernetic account of how actions are steered: goals act as reference values, perception supplies the input, a comparator measures the gap, and behaviour changes to close it. Their summary of the core claim:

> "We have long held that movement toward a goal reflects the functioning of a feedback loop: perception of present conditions being compared to a desired or intended condition, and any discrepancies noted being countered by subsequent action." ([Carver & Scheier, "Action, Affect, Multi-tasking, and Layers of Control"](https://sydneysymposium.unsw.edu.au/2008/Chapters/CarverSSSP08.pdf))

### What the theory carries

The loop's elements are an input function (perception of the present state), a reference value (the goal or standard), a comparator, and an output function (behaviour), with the whole arrangement working to negate sensed deviations from the standard. The theory's most distinctive extension concerns feeling: a second loop runs in parallel to the action loop, and "the input for this second loop thus is the rate of discrepancy reduction in the action system over time." Affect is the error signal of that meta-loop: "A rate of progress below the criterion yields negative affect. A rate high enough to exceed the criterion yields positive affect. If the rate is not distinguishable from the criterion, there is no valence." On this view, feeling bad is information about pace rather than proof about the goal. My principle B065 carries the contemplative parallel: guard the mind at the source, since the standard one holds determines what the loop counts as error. A compendium exercise drawn from the theory is to take one recurring frustration and ask whether the target is genuinely valued or merely the reference value currently loaded. What the theory does not give: an account of where goals come from; the loop regulates toward whatever standard is installed, wise or not.
