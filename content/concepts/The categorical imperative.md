The categorical imperative is Immanuel Kant's name, in the Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785), for the single supreme principle of morality. It is imperative because it commands, and categorical because it binds regardless of what a person wants: in the Stanford Encyclopedia's summary, it is "categorical in virtue of applying to us unconditionally, or simply because we possess rational wills, without reference to any ends that we might or might not have", whereas a hypothetical imperative "requires us to exercise our wills in a certain way given we have antecedently willed an end" ([Johnson and Cureton, Kant's moral philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-moral/), SEP).

> "act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law" (Kant, Groundwork, G 4:421, quoted in [Johnson and Cureton, Kant's moral philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-moral/), SEP)

### The two famous formulas

The Groundwork offers several expressions of the one principle. The Formula of Universal Law is the passage quoted above. The Formula of Humanity, as Johnson and Cureton summarize it, holds that "we should never act in such a way that we treat humanity, whether in ourselves or in others, as a means only but always as an end in itself". The first turns a proposed action into a test question, whether its maxim could hold for everyone; the second puts a limit on using people, which is where the compendium's facet of forbidding manipulation comes from.

### How the test works

The Encyclopedia lays out the four steps of the universalizability test: formulate your maxim, "recast that maxim as a universal law of nature governing all rational agents", then ask whether the maxim "is even conceivable in a world governed by this new law of nature", and finally "whether you would, or could, rationally will to act on your maxim in such a world". Failure at the third step yields a perfect duty admitting "of no exception in favor of inclination" (G 4:421); failure at the fourth yields an imperfect duty. The test's hinge is consistency: a maxim whose universalized form contradicts itself, or could not be willed without undermining the agent's ends, is not moral law material.
