The moral circle names the boundary between entities people consider worthy of moral concern and those they do not. The research program around it asks how far that boundary extends and how it varies between people. Its anchor observation, as the key empirical paper puts it, is that philosophers, historians and social scientists have noted that people's moral boundaries have expanded over the last few centuries; the paper credits Peter Singer's 1981 account of the expanding circle, summarizing Singer's moral circle as "a boundary distinguishing those entities deemed worthy of moral consideration from those that are not" (Crimston, Bain, Hornsey and Bastian, [Moral expansiveness: Examining variability in the extension of the moral world, JPSP 111(4), 2016](https://purehost.bath.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/187913488/Crimston_Bain_Hornsey_Bastian_2016_Moral_Expansiveness_JPSP.PDF), abstract and "What is Moral Expansiveness?" section).

> "Moral expansiveness refers to the breadth of entities deemed worthy of moral concern and treatment. A less morally expansive person restricts concern to those entities that are considered 'close' (e.g., their family). A more morally expansive person extends moral care and consideration beyond these boundaries to more 'distant' entities (e.g., animals or plants)." (Crimston et al., [2016, author copy](https://purehost.bath.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/187913488/Crimston_Bain_Hornsey_Bastian_2016_Moral_Expansiveness_JPSP.PDF), "What is Moral Expansiveness?" section)

### The Moral Expansiveness Scale

The paper's own contribution is a measure of individual variation rather than of history: the Moral Expansiveness Scale (MES). Participants place 30 entities spanning 10 categories (family and friends, ingroup, outgroup, revered people, stigmatized people, villains, high sentience animals, low sentience animals, plants, environment) on four concentric circles of concern, scored inner circle = 3, outer circle = 2, fringes = 1, outside = 0, for an aggregate range of 0 to 90; in the paper's first study the 30 items formed a scale with strong internal consistency (α = .92) (Crimston et al., 2016, "Measuring Moral Expansiveness" section). Across six studies, the MES uniquely predicted willingness to prioritize humanitarian and environmental concerns over personal and national self-interest, willingness to sacrifice one's life to save others ranging from human outgroups to animals and plants, and volunteering behavior, independently of established constructs such as moral foundations and moral identity (Crimston et al., 2016, abstract).
