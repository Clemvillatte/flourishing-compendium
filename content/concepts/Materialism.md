In psychology, materialism means an orientation toward money and possessions, not a view about matter. A marketing-journal paper gives the working definition: "an orientation towards money and the acquisition of purchases that convey status as a way to attain personal achievement and individual well-being" ([Materialistic cues make us miserable, Psychology & Marketing](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/mar.21387)). The question for flourishing is how that orientation relates to how people fare.

### The main meta-analysis

Dittmar, Bond, Hurst and Kasser examined "753 effect sizes from 259 independent samples". Materialism "was associated with significantly lower well-being" on the most widely used measures: for materialist values and beliefs, r = -.19, and for the relative importance of materialist goals, r = -.16. The link was weaker for life satisfaction and negative affect (rs = -.13 to -.15) and stronger for "risky health and consumer behaviors and for negative self-appraisals" (rs = -.28 to -.44). The authors suggest "the negative link may be explained by poor psychological need satisfaction", and call for "more experimental, longitudinal, and developmental research" ([Dittmar et al., 2014, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25347131/)).

A Handbook of Well-Being chapter reads the result as "small but consistently negative", robust "even if it is not large". It reports that the dissatisfaction of needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness "each partially mediated" the link ([Materialism and Living Well, Handbook of Well-Being](https://www.handbookofwellbeing.com/chapters/56-materialism-and-living-well.html)).

### Experimental evidence

Correlations leave causation open. A later meta-analysis pooled 27 priming studies (N = 3,649). It found effects of materialistic cues on individual well-being (δ = -0.39) and societal well-being (δ = -0.41), which the authors read as "suggesting that materialistic cues cause lower well-being" (Psychology & Marketing paper above).

Dittmar et al. observed "a stronger relationship between materialism and individual well-being in older populations"; a Cardiff-hosted meta-analysis of bidirectional effects says its own moderation effects "contrast with" that finding ([Bidirectional effects of materialism, Cardiff ORCA PDF](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/172978/1/cb.2409.pdf)). The age pattern is therefore not settled.

### Caution

The seed note cites Kasser (2002) and says the pursuit of possessions predicts lower life satisfaction. I did not open Kasser's book. The meta-analytic figures show a modest average association that is weakest for life satisfaction. Essays should say "tends to go with lower well-being", and avoid wording that treats materialism as a sure cause of unhappiness.
