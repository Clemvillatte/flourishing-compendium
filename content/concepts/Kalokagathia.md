The Greek phrase kalos kagathos joins two adjectives, beautiful and good, into one word for a person; the Wikipedia article on it records the phrase as "used by classical Greek writers to describe an ideal of gentlemanly personal conduct, especially in a military context," attested since Herodotus ([Wikipedia, Kalokagathia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalokagathia)). Aristotle gives the concept its sharpest definition in the Eudemian Ethics, distinguishing the merely good man from the kalos kagathos:

> "But one is kalos kagathos because goods that are fine are his on their own account and because he is the sort who does fine actions also for their own sake. And the goods that are fine are virtues and virtuous actions." (Aristotle, Eudemian Ethics VIII.3, 1248b26-37, trans. Inwood and Woolf, [as quoted in a scholarly study of EE VIII.3](https://repository.bilkent.edu.tr/server/api/core/bitstreams/d1900c6e-0b55-436d-9887-923d16e4ed0c/content))

The difference is motivational. Aristotle's example is the Spartan civic disposition: possessing virtue "but only for the sake of natural goods" — hence, in the same passage, "they are good men (since the natural goods are good for them), but they do not have kalokagathia" (EE VIII.3, 1248b37-1249a2, trans. Inwood and Woolf, as quoted in the same study). The good man's goods work for him; the kalos kagathos's goods are chosen for their own sake because they are fine.

Plato, the Wikipedia article notes, coined agathos kai sophos, good and wise, to avoid the association of external beauty with virtue.

**Plain words** Two grades of good person. One is good because good things work out for him. The other is good because he does fine things for their own sake, and that is the whole difference: the same acts, done from a different centre.
