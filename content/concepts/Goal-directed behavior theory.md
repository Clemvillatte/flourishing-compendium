Goal-directed behaviour theory, developed by Anthony Dickinson and Bernard Balleine, says an action counts as goal-directed only when it meets two tests. A review of habits and addiction by Ostlund and Balleine puts it this way: "in order to be classified as goal-directed, the performance of an action must be shown to depend on two things: (i) the causal relationship between that action and its outcome (i.e., its action-outcome contingency) and (ii) the current motivational value of that outcome" ([Ostlund and Balleine, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2891067/)).

### Belief and desire

De Wit and Dickinson call the two tests the belief criterion and the desire criterion. The belief criterion holds that "if a behaviour is goal-directed it must be based upon knowledge of the relationship between the action and its consequences". Under the desire criterion, "goal-directed actions are controlled by the affective or motivational value of the outcome at the time that the action is performed", so that "the action is only executed when the expected consequences are currently desired and therefore have the status of a goal" ([de Wit and Dickinson, Psychological Research, 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2694930/)).

### How the tests work

In the standard outcome-devaluation test, rats learn to press a lever for food, and the food is then made unwanted away from the lever. If the press was goal-directed, the animal withholds it. Ostlund and Balleine describe rats that "withhold their performance of the response that, in training, had earned the now devalued outcome, while continuing to perform the response that had earned the other, non-devalued outcome". The second test, contingency degradation, checks the first criterion: "If the outcome subsequently becomes equally available with and without a lever press, goal-directed control leads to a decrease in pressing" (["Goals and Habits in the Brain", review](https://pure.mpg.de/rest/items/item_2615036_3/component/file_2617849/content)).

### The contrast with habit

Habits are the other class. A habit is "relatively insensitive" to these manipulations, which "suggest[s] that such training encourages stimulus-response, or habit, learning" (Ostlund and Balleine). The "Goals and Habits in the Brain" review adds that behaviour "is initially goal directed but then becomes habitual over the course of experience", with extended training on interval schedules a key promoter. Another review says that when rewards come on a timed schedule, so that pressing barely changes reward rate, "actions tend to become habitual", whereas when reward is proportional to pressing, "actions tend to remain goal-directed" ([Balleine, Daw and O'Doherty, Princeton PDF](http://www.princeton.edu/~ndaw/bdo08.pdf)).

### Limits to note

A recent paper in the Journal of the Experimental Analysis of Behavior writes that while "goal-directed control can be confirmed via tests of outcome devaluation and contingency-degradation sensitivity, a comparable criterion for positively detecting habits has not been established" ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/jeab.898)). The evidence base is mainly animal learning, and de Wit and Dickinson's title argues for "animal-human translational models" to carry it over to people.
