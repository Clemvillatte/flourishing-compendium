Moral intuition research asks whether moral judgments come from reasoning or from fast evaluations that reasoning later explains. Haidt's 2001 abstract gives "4 reasons for considering the hypothesis that moral reasoning does not cause moral judgment; rather, moral reasoning is usually a post hoc construction, generated after a judgment has been reached". It adds that the model "states that moral judgment is generally the result of quick, automatic evaluations (intuitions)" ([Haidt, 2001, Psychological Review 108(4), 814-834, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11699120/)).

### The model and its terms

Haidt calls the account social because it "deemphasizes the private reasoning done by individuals and emphasizes instead the importance of social and cultural influences" (PubMed abstract). A review of the article's reception quotes his definitions. Intuition is "the sudden appearance in consciousness of a moral judgment, including an affective valence (good-bad, like-dislike), without any conscious awareness of having gone through steps of searching, weighing evidence, or inferring a conclusion". Moral judgments are "evaluations (good vs. bad) of the actions or character of a person that are made with respect to a set of virtues held to be obligatory by a culture or subculture" ([The Emotional Dog Was a Glauconian Canine, Revistas UV PDF](https://revistas.uv.cl/index.php/RHV/article/download/2947/version/2974/3161/12830)).

The Cambridge chapter version opens with a story of two siblings: people "immediately say that it was wrong", search for reasons, and eventually say "I don't know, I can't explain it, I just know it's wrong" ([Cambridge, Reasoning, chapter 53](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/reasoning/emotional-dog-and-its-rational-tail-a-social-intuitionist-approach-to-moral-judgment/CAE7368993B5B2BF8175BF6ED9786899)).

### Pushback

Two commentaries challenge how little role the model gives to reasoning. One presents evidence that fast moral intuitions "are actually shaped and informed by prior reasoning", and concludes the findings "limit the strong claims of the social intuitionist model concerning the irrelevance of conscious deliberation" ([The intelligence of the moral intuitions, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12529062/)). The other argues that "moral reasoning may sometimes disrupt the automatic process of judgment formation" and that automatic judgments "may reflect the 'automatization' of judgment goals based on prior moral reasoning" ([Is the emotional dog wagging its rational tail, or chasing it?, Taylor and Francis](http://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/13869790500492680)).

### Reading the seed claim

The seed says moral judgments "often precede reasoning". The abstract hedges with "usually" and "generally", so it does not claim reasoning never matters. Keep the intuitionist claim attributed to Haidt rather than stated as settled.
