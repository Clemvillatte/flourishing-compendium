“Hickman et al” — 2021.

The 2021 reference is Caroline Hickman and colleagues' global survey "Climate anxiety in children and young people and their beliefs about government responses to climate change" (The Lancet Planetary Health 5(12):e863-e873). It is the first large-scale international survey of its kind, and it links young people's climate distress to how they judge their governments' response.

### What was asked and found

The team surveyed 10,000 people aged 16 to 25 in ten countries (Australia, Brazil, Finland, France, India, Nigeria, the Philippines, Portugal, the UK and the USA), 1,000 per country, through an online polling platform in May and June 2021. ([The Lancet Planetary Health](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lanplh/article/PIIS2542-5196(21)00278-3/fulltext))

> "Respondents across all countries were worried about climate change (59% were very or extremely worried and 84% were at least moderately worried)." (Hickman et al. 2021)

More than half reported each of sadness, anxiety, anger, powerlessness, helplessness and guilt. More than 45% said their feelings about climate change negatively affected their daily life and functioning; 75% said the future is frightening. Respondents rated government responses negatively and reported more feelings of betrayal than of reassurance, and climate anxiety and distress were correlated with perceived inadequate government response.

### How the paper frames the feeling

The authors say climate anxiety is often based on "constructive or practical anxiety", and that "although painful and distressing, climate anxiety is rational and does not imply mental illness". Later, in the discussion, they write that it "might not constitute a mental illness", but that the realities of climate change alongside governmental failures to act are chronic stressors likely to raise the risk of mental health problems, particularly in vulnerable people.

### Limits the authors state

The study used non-standardised measures because none existed for its purposes, and it used a yes, no or prefer-not-to-say scale. In the authors' words, "without measures of mental health, these results cannot assess how or whether climate anxiety is affecting mental health outcomes in these populations". The country samples were equal-sized and not population-weighted, and the online polling meant samples were "not fully representative of the countries' populations". The Lancet lists the funder as Avaaz.
