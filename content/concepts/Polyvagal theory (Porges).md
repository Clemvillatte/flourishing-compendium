proposes distinct dorsal/ventral vagal branches mapping to emotional states (Porges, 1995, 2011).

Stephen Porges introduced the theory in 1994 ([Wikipedia, polyvagal theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyvagal_theory)); its initial formulation appeared the following year in Psychophysiology ([Porges, 1995](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1469-8986.1995.tb01213.x)). As Porges summarizes it, "Polyvagal Theory proposes that autonomic state functions as an intervening variable ‒ a neurophysiological platform that shapes how organisms detect, evaluate, and respond to cues of safety, danger, and life threat" ([Porges, 2026, Clinical Neuropsychiatry](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12937496/)). In his account, the mammalian nervous system pairs a social-communication system built on the myelinated vagus with older mobilization and immobilization systems ([Porges, 2009, Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3108032/)).

The most distinctive claim concerns how those shifts happen. Porges coined neuroception: "Neuroception refers to the nervous system's capacity to detect cues of safety, danger, or life threat and to adjust autonomic state accordingly" ([Porges, 2026, Clinical Neuropsychiatry](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12937496/)). Detection is reflexive rather than deliberative: "Polyvagal Theory proposes that the neural evaluation of risk and safety reflexively triggers shifts in autonomic state without requiring conscious awareness" ([Porges, 2022, Frontiers in Integrative Neuroscience](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9131189/)). On Porges's account, "when the environment is appraised as being safe, the defensive limbic structures are inhibited, enabling social engagement and calm visceral states to emerge" ([Porges, 2009, Cleveland Clinic Journal of Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3108032/)).

### Why it matters

The theory matters because it treats feelings of safety as emerging from internal physiological states regulated by the autonomic nervous system, and it makes sociality the lever: "Polyvagal Theory emphasizes sociality as the core process in mitigating threat reactions and supporting mental and physical health" ([Porges, 2021, Comprehensive Psychoneuroendocrinology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9216697/)).

> "All co-authors agree that major tenets of the PVT are not supported by past or current knowledge and, in several instances, are inconsistent with the broader evidence base." ([Grossman et al., 2026, Clinical Neuropsychiatry](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12937499/))

That consensus statement, signed by 39 experts on the vagus nerve and vertebrate social behavior, is the theory's central problem. Their objection is functional, not merely anatomical: the "functional distinctions between brainstem dorsal and ventral groups of vagal preganglionic neurons in mammals postulated by PVT are clearly wrong," so the "ladder of autonomic responses" is contradicted by existing evidence ([Grossman et al., 2026, Clinical Neuropsychiatry](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12937499/)). A companion critique argues that treating respiratory sinus arrhythmia as cardiac vagal tone is "conceptually a category mistake (Ryle, 1949)" ([Grossman, 2023, Biological Psychology](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37230290/)). Porges replies that the charge of untenability "does not apply to Polyvagal Theory as formulated, but instead reflects a critique that fails to engage the theory on its own terms" ([Porges, 2026, Clinical Neuropsychiatry](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12937496/)).

For a flourishing-focused reader, the practical reading is that evidence for particular breathing exercises can be assessed separately from polyvagal theory. A systematic review finds that "slow breathing techniques seem to promote a predominance of the parasympathetic autonomic system with respect to the sympathetic one, mediated by the vagal activity" ([Zaccaro et al., 2018, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6137615/)). A randomized study found that "breathwork, especially the exhale-focused cyclic sighing, produces greater improvement in mood (p < 0.05) and reduction in respiratory rate (p < 0.05) compared with mindfulness meditation" ([Balban et al., 2023, Cell Reports Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9873947/)). The findings stand on their own.

### Related concepts

- [[Heart rate variability theory]] — Porges treats respiratory sinus arrhythmia as an index of ventral vagal tone and social engagement, while critics call equating RSA with cardiac vagal tone a category mistake.
- [[Autonomic nervous system (sympathetic vs parasympathetic)]] — the theory adds a proposed mammalian social-communication circuit on top of the classic sympathetic-parasympathetic division.
- [[Parasympathetic activation]] — the theory's ventral vagal component is described by Porges as inhibiting sympathetic influences to the heart and dampening the HPA axis.
- [[Slow-paced breathing]] — the parasympathetic shift from slow breathing is often framed in polyvagal terms, though the effect stands on its own evidence.
- [[Heart-rate-variability biofeedback]] — draws on vagal-tone interpretations of RSA, with baroreflex strengthening as its best-supported mechanism.
- [[Interoception]] — neuroception is neural detection of safety and danger without awareness, distinct from the felt sense of bodily signals.
- [[Micro-moments of connection]] — the theory's sociality claim parallels the finding that vagal tone reciprocally predicts positive emotions and social connectedness (Kok & Fredrickson, 2010).
