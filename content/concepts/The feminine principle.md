The feminine principle is Luce Irigaray's name for the half of sexual difference that Western language has organised itself around excluding. Her starting diagnosis, as the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy summarises it: "She believes that language typically excludes women from an active subject position," and inclusion on the current terms is no repair: "Irigaray's goal is for there to be more than one subject position in language." ([IEP, "Luce Irigaray"](https://iep.utm.edu/irigaray/))

### What the principle carries

In Between East and West (2002), Irigaray looks for resources outside the Western canon: the book "draws on Eastern philosophy and meditative techniques such as yoga to suggest new approaches to the question of sexual difference" (IEP bibliography note), and her judgment of the West is blunt — "At the end of her An Ethics of Sexual Difference, it is clear that Irigaray does not believe that Western culture is ethical, and that the primary reason is its treatment of women and nature." The principle is therefore not a claim about what women are; it is a claim about what a culture loses when it admits only one subject position, the same loss visited, in her account, on nature. Change, she insists, starts small and relationally: "While she believes that women's social and political situation has to be addressed on a global level, she also thinks that change begins in individual relationships between women," above all the mother/daughter bond, "a constant theme in Irigaray's work." The compendium principle D051 carries the Eastern parallel that drew her: know the masculine, keep to the feminine — an attunement the Daodejing treats as strength rather than surrender. A compendium exercise drawn from the principle is to ask what any practice of ours assumes as its default subject, and whom that default leaves doing the supporting. What the principle does not give: an essence of woman; Irigaray's feminine is a position language has refused, not a fixed nature.
