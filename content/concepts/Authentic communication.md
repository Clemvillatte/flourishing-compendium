The term is grounded here in Buber's I and Thou. For Buber, a person's world is twofold depending on the primary word they speak. One primary word is I-Thou, spoken with the whole being; the other is I-It, through which the other person becomes an object of experience and use. Authentic communication belongs to the first: the direct address in which the other is met as a Thou rather than observed and described as an It. Buber writes:

> "The relation to the Thou is direct. No system of ideas, no foreknowledge, and no fancy intervene between I and Thou. The memory itself is transformed, as it plunges out of its isolation into the unity of the whole. No aim, no lust, and no anticipation intervene between I and Thou." (Martin Buber, I and Thou, Part One, translated by Ronald Gregor Smith, 1937, [full text via the Internet Archive](https://archive.org/stream/IAndThou_572/BuberMartin-i-and-thou_djvu.txt))

Two things follow for practice. First, the words may be ordinary; what makes the communication authentic is that nothing is interposed between speaker and other, no theory about them, no agenda for them, no rehearsal of what they will say next. Second, the meeting is mutual: Buber's formula for this is that all real living is meeting, and the relation gives and receives in both directions. Communication aimed at managing the other, by contrast, keeps the relation on the I-It side even when it is warm.
