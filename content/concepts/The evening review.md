The evening review is the nightly examination of actions and words Seneca describes in On Anger 3.36 ([Aubrey Stewart's translation](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Of_Anger/Book_III)). He first reports the questions Sextius asked himself on retiring to rest, then describes his own habit of reviewing the day after the lamp was removed and his wife had fallen silent ([3.36](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Of_Anger/Book_III)).

> "What bad habit of yours have you cured to-day? what vice have you checked? in what respect are you better?" (Questions attributed to Sextius by Seneca, On Anger 3.36, [translated by Aubrey Stewart](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Of_Anger/Book_III).)

### Correction and pardon

Seneca recalls speaking too contentiously in a dispute and reprimanding someone so freely that he caused offence instead of improvement ([3.36](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Of_Anger/Book_III)). His examination includes telling himself he is pardoned this time, while requiring a change in future conduct ([3.36](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Of_Anger/Book_III)). The criticism thus concerns particular deeds and words that can be reconsidered ([3.36](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Of_Anger/Book_III)).

### What the account leaves open

The passage describes recollection and self-address, without prescribing a written-journal format or a set duration ([3.36](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Of_Anger/Book_III)). Seneca praises the sleep following this practice and claims that anticipated examination softens anger; these are his philosophical observations, not results of a controlled treatment study ([3.36](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Of_Anger/Book_III)).
