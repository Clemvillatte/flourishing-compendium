A reinforcement schedule is the rule by which a consequence follows behaviour in the operant research tradition of C. B. Ferster and B. F. Skinner, charted in their 1957 book Schedules of Reinforcement. The basic schedules cross two distinctions: whether reinforcement depends on a number of responses or on a response after a period of time, and whether that requirement is fixed or variable ([Baum, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12162439/)).

> "The former is called a ratio schedule and the latter is called an interval schedule. If each earned inducer requires a fixed amount of activity, the schedule is a fixed-ratio (FR) schedule. If inducers require a variable, unpredictable, amount of activity, the schedule is a variable-ratio (VR) schedule. Likewise, if each inducer is available only after a fixed time interval, the schedule is a fixed-interval (FI) schedule, and if inducers are available only after variable, unpredictable, intervals, the schedule is a variable-interval (VI) schedule (Ferster & Skinner, 1957)." (Baum, [Explaining Performance on Interval and Ratio Schedules, introduction](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12162439/))

### Continuous and intermittent reinforcement

The schedule matters for both acquisition and extinction. When every response is reinforced (continuous reinforcement, CRF), behaviour is built quickly, and when a response has been reinforced every time it occurs, it usually extinguishes more rapidly than behaviour reinforced intermittently, a result known as the partial reinforcement extinction effect ([Nevin, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3335979/)). The two schedule families also differ in steady state: ratio schedules maintain extremely high activity rates, and when the ratio requirement climbs too high, activity becomes erratic and can drop to zero, a state called ratio strain ([Baum, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12162439/)).
