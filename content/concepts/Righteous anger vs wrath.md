Righteous anger versus wrath is the contrast between anger that is fitting to its object and anger that is excessive, prolonged or vengeful. The compendium's seed cites Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV. The phrase "righteous anger" is not Aristotle's. He treats the question under good temper, as a mean with respect to anger, and he names the faulty states.

### Aristotle: anger with the right measure

> "The man who is angry at the right things and with the right people, and, further, as he ought, when he ought, and as long as he ought, is praised." (Aristotle, NE IV.5, Ross translation, [Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.4.iv.html))

Aristotle says good temper "is a mean with respect to anger," with an excess that "might [be] called a sort of 'irascibility'" and a deficiency that is blamed: "those who are not angry at the things they should be angry at are thought to be fools," and to "put up with insult to one's friends is slavish." The excesses have several forms. Hot-tempered people "get angry quickly and with the wrong persons and at the wrong things and more than is right." Sulky people "are hard to appease, and retain their anger long." He calls "bad-tempered" those who are "angry at the wrong things, more than is right, and longer, and cannot be appeased until they inflict vengeance or punishment" ([Aristotle, NE IV.5](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.4.iv.html)). The last description is the nearest Aristotelian counterpart to what the compendium calls wrath.

He also says the line is hard to draw: "it is not easy to define how, with whom, at what, and how long one should be angry," and "the decision depends on the particular facts and on perception." Only the middle state is clearly praiseworthy ([Aristotle, NE IV.5](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.4.iv.html)).

### A scriptural pairing

The word wrath comes more from scripture than from Aristotle. Two verses in the English Standard Version set the poles: "Be angry and do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger" (Ephesians 4:26), and "let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger; for the anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God" (James 1:19-20) ([Bible Gateway, ESV](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Ephesians+4%3A26-27%3B+James+1%3A19-20&version=ESV)). They are not in the seed, which cites Aristotle alone. I include them because the page title uses that vocabulary. The two verses are not a worked theory of anger, and the sources do not say how they should be reconciled.
