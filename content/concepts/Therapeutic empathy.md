“Therapeutic empathy” — Elliott et al., 2011.

Therapeutic empathy is the therapist's perception and communication of understanding of the client's inner world, and the question for research is whether it is merely a pleasant feature of the room or an active ingredient of change. The meta-analysis by Elliott, Bohart, Watson and Greenberg aggregated 59 independent samples and 3,599 clients:

> "Results indicated that empathy is a moderately strong predictor of therapy outcome: mean weighted r = .31 (p < .001; 95% confidence interval: .28–.34), for 59 independent samples and 3599 clients." (Elliott et al. 2011, Psychotherapy 48(1), p. 43)

Two refinements from the same review carry the concept's practical weight. The empathy–outcome relation "held equally for different theoretical orientations", which marks empathy as a common factor rather than the property of any school. And the perception that matters is the client's: "Client and observer perceptions of therapist empathy predicted outcomes better than therapist perceptions of empathic accuracy measures, and the relation was strongest for less experienced therapists." Feeling understood, as the client experiences it, is the active quantity, not the therapist's private estimate of accuracy. That is why the compendium's [[R092 — Practise listening as a moral act]] treats receiving another person's account as an act in its own right, not a preface to advice.
