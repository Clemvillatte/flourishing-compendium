John Holland's theory of vocational personalities and work environments holds that, in the words of a Wiley book chapter, "by late adolescence most people can be characterized in terms of how closely they resemble each of six basic personality types: realistic, investigative, artistic, social, enterprising, and conventional". The chapter adds that "because it provides a parallel way for conceptualizing people and environments, Holland's theory is optimally suited for assisting people who are seeking to make initial or subsequent educational or career choices" ([Holland's Theory of Vocational Choice and Adjustment, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781394258994.ch3)).

### The core assumptions

A 2026 Frontiers review lists four. Individuals can be categorised by similarity to the six types. Environments, including occupations, can be classified into the same six. Individuals "naturally tend to seek out environments that are congruent with their personality type". And outcomes depend on "the degree of person-environment fit, with congruence posited to yield positive outcomes such as job satisfaction and performance" ([Frontiers in Organizational Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/organizational-psychology/articles/10.3389/forgp.2026.1792707/abstract)).

The types are arranged on a hexagon. The meta-analysis by Nye and colleagues explains that adjacent types "are most related, alternate types (e.g., realistic and artistic) have an intermediate relationship, and opposite types (e.g., realistic and social) are least related" ([Nye et al., 2012, PDF](https://www.allesoverassessments.nl/pdf/Nye_etal_2012.pdf)).

### Secondary constructs

Beyond the type code, Holland described consistency (how close a person's top types sit on the hexagon), differentiation (how peaked the profile is), and congruence (the match between person and environment). Sources differ on how well they hold up. A methods paper on Holland-based software states that "evidence regarding the effects of consistency and differentiation is somewhat equivocal", while congruence is "widely studied" ([Psych 2021, TUM repository PDF](https://mediatum.ub.tum.de/doc/1689523/document.pdf)).

### What the evidence shows

- Satisfaction and stability: an open-access review quotes Nauta (2010) that "there is strong evidence that congruence predicts individuals' choice of... and persistence or stability in... college majors and occupations", and that meta-analyses support a link between congruence and job satisfaction ([Personality and interests for selection, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10013216/)).
- Performance: Nye and colleagues' meta-analysis of 60 studies and about 568 correlations found interests related to "performance and persistence in work and academic contexts", with congruence indices stronger than interest scores alone ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26168474/)). The PMC review reports a later meta-analysis (Nye et al., 2017) with r = .32 for congruence and r = .16 for interest scores alone.
- Limits of use: the 2026 Frontiers review reports that evidence for self-assessment tools as career interventions is "mixed" and "inconsistent and context dependent".

### Reading the claim

The congruence hypothesis has moderate support. For the essays, the safe wording is that fit between interests and work environment is associated with satisfaction, persistence and performance. It is not proof that matching interests produces flourishing.
