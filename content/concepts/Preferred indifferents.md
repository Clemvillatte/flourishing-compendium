Preferred indifferents are the things the Stoics rank as neither good nor bad for happiness, yet worth choosing over their opposites. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy gives the mainstream view: "some indifferents - such as health, wealth, and reputation - are of a promoted or preferred type (proêgmena / praeposita), have value (axia / aestimatio), and are according to nature (kata phusin / secundum naturam), such that [it] is appropriate or dutiful or one's proper function (kathêkon / officium) to select them over their opposites in normal circumstances" ([SEP, Stoicism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/)).

> "The virtuous agent is therefore not lackadaisical in her attitude toward indifferents, according to the orthodox Stoic account, but rather energetic and conscientious in her pursuit of those which are preferred, valuable, and according to nature: her general policy will be to select these and reject their opposites." ([SEP, Stoicism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/))

### The standing objection

The same entry records an ancient objection that the doctrine is incoherent: "it is indeed difficult to see how something could be both indifferent to our happiness and nevertheless rational to care about and pursue." In the critics' dilemma, either the preferred items are really good, which contradicts the claim that only virtue is good, or happiness is not the ultimate goal, which breaks the eudaimonist thesis (same entry). Anyone using the concept in an essay should know that its coherence has been disputed since antiquity.

### Where Epictetus comes in

The compendium's seed citation is the Enchiridion. The Enchiridion does call outcomes indifferent. In section 32, on divination, Epictetus says one should "acquire a distinct knowledge that every event is indifferent and nothing to you, of whatever sort it may be, for it will be in your power to make a right use of it" ([Epictetus, Enchiridion, trans. Elizabeth Carter, MIT Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html)). I did not find the phrase "preferred indifferents" in the Enchiridion text. The SEP sets out the technical category as the mainstream Stoic view, citing Cicero and Stobaeus, so the label belongs to the school's technical vocabulary and not to the Enchiridion's wording.

### In the compendium

S015 (Distinguish preferred from dispreferred indifferents) and S017 (Refuse to see externals as good or bad) are the closest principles. The SEP also notes that, for the Stoics, being alive is itself a preferred indifferent, and that a just agent may in some cases select a dispreferred one for herself to give others more.
