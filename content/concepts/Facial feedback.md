The facial feedback hypothesis holds that the face acts as an input to emotion as well as an output of it: "people's affective responses can be influenced by their own facial expression (e.g., smiling, pouting), even when their expression did not result from their emotional experiences" ([Wagenmakers et al. 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1745691616674458)).

### The classic study, and its collapse

The famous test was the pen study. Strack, Martin and Stepper had participants hold a pen in the mouth in ways that "either inhibited or facilitated the muscles typically associated with smiling without requiring subjects to pose in a smiling face", and "subjects reported more intense humor responses when cartoons were presented under facilitating conditions than under inhibiting conditions" ([Strack, Martin and Stepper 1988](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3379579/)). A 17-lab direct replication changed the picture: "the original Strack et al. (1988) study reported a rating difference of 0.82 units on a 10-point Likert scale", but "our meta-analysis revealed a rating difference of 0.03 units with a 95% confidence interval ranging from −0.11 to 0.16" ([Wagenmakers et al. 2016](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/1745691616674458)). The textbook effect, as measured by the pen, did not survive.

### Where that leaves the hypothesis

The later Many Smiles meta-analysis kept the question open rather than closing it: "evidence of facial feedback effects was less conclusive when facial feedback was manipulated unobtrusively via a pen-in-mouth task" ([Coles et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1038/s41562-022-01458-9)). So the honest formulation is the seed's: expressions may influence affect, the evidence is mixed, effects are small, and phrase with care.
