“Hospitality” — Derrida, Of Hospitality; Levinas, Totality and Infinity.

Hospitality is the welcome owed to the stranger who arrives. In the tradition running from Kant through Levinas to Derrida it is treated as a question of right and ethics rather than mere etiquette: the stranger at the door is not an intruder but a test of what a closed life amounts to.

> "We are speaking here, as in the previous articles, not of philanthropy, but of right; and in this sphere hospitality signifies the claim of a stranger entering foreign territory to be treated by its owner without hostility. The latter may send him away again, if this can be done without causing his death; but, so long as he conducts himself peaceably, he must not be treated as an enemy." — [Kant, Perpetual Peace (1795), third definitive article](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/50922/50922-h/50922-h.htm)

### What the stranger is owed

Kant's formulation is deliberately narrow. The stranger has "a right of visitation," not a right to be treated as a guest; the claim does not exceed what the host can give without ceasing to be host. Derrida's later reading pushes on precisely this limit: any hospitality that sets conditions — a threshold, a language, a question of who owns the house — is no longer pure hospitality, and yet an unconditional welcome with no conditions cannot be lived by anyone. The concept is useful to flourishing because it names a virtue that only exists in the act of accepting some cost to one's own boundary. Levinas sharpens it further: the face of the other makes a demand before any negotiation has begun.
