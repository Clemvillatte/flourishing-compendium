Emergence names the idea that wholes can have properties and behaviours that depend on their parts and are still not just the parts' properties over again. O'Connor's Stanford Encyclopedia entry puts the starting point with a tornado. A tornado "depends for its existence on dust and debris, and ultimately on whatever micro-entities compose it", yet "its features and behaviors appear to differ in kind from those of its most basic constituents, as is reflected in the fact that one can have a rather good understanding of how tornadoes work while being entirely ignorant of particle physics" ([SEP, "Emergent Properties", §1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/properties-emergent/)).

### Two features: dependence and autonomy

The entry says "[t]he general notion of emergence is meant to conjoin these twin characteristics of dependence and autonomy." It "mediates between extreme forms of dualism, which reject the micro-dependence of some entities, and reductionism, which rejects macro-autonomy" ([SEP, §1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/properties-emergent/)).

### Weak and strong emergence

Weak emergence "affirms the reality of entities and features posited in the special sciences, while also affirming physicalism". On this view there are "molecules, cells, organisms, and minded creatures, and they do not reduce to ... complex combinations of basic physical entities or features" ([SEP, §3](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/properties-emergent/)). Strong emergentists "maintain that at least some higher-level phenomena exhibit a weaker dependence/stronger autonomy than weak emergence permits," and the most commonly cited candidate is the conscious mind in relation to its neural substrate ([SEP, §4](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/properties-emergent/)). The entry also lists commonly pressed objections to each view, and strong emergence is the more controversial of the two.

### History

The entry credits the nineteenth-century British Emergentists with rejecting both idealism and reductionist materialism, with Lewes (1875) "first using the term 'emergence' for the philosophical position". It follows the line through to C. D. Broad (1925), for whom the whole's characteristic properties "cannot, even in theory, be deduced from the most complete knowledge of the properties of" its parts in isolation ([SEP, §1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/properties-emergent/)). C. Lloyd Morgan's Emergent Evolution, the older source attached to this page, is not covered by the entry, so no claim about his version is made here.
