Kounios and Beeman's review defines the phenomenon in its first sentences:

> "Insight occurs when a person suddenly reinterprets a stimulus, situation, or event to produce a nonobvious, nondominant interpretation. This can take the form of a solution to a problem (an 'aha moment'), comprehension of a joke or metaphor, or recognition of an ambiguous percept." (Kounios and Beeman 2014, Annual Review of Psychology 65: 71-93, [abstract, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24405359/))

The research program summarized there contrasts insight solving with deliberate, analytic solving as two modes rather than one good and one bad method. The review also notes that insight research began a century ago, but that neuroimaging and electrophysiological techniques have been applied to its study only during the decade before the review, and that recent studies have even begun to apply direct brain stimulation with the aim of facilitating insight in the laboratory. Its own one-line conclusion is that the cognitive neuroscience of insight is an exciting new area of research with connections to fundamental neurocognitive processes. The review reports that insight-related work has found coarse semantic coding in the right hemisphere and internally focused attention preceding and during problem solving, and that people who tend to solve insightfully show different resting-state brain activity than people who tend to solve analytically:

> "Individual differences in the tendency to solve problems insightfully rather than in a deliberate, analytic fashion are associated with different patterns of resting-state brain activity." (Kounios and Beeman 2014, [abstract, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24405359/))

**Plain words** Some answers arrive by working the steps; some arrive all at once, sideways, with a click. The research says the click is not a myth: it has measurable stages before it, including a period of turning attention inward away from the problem.
