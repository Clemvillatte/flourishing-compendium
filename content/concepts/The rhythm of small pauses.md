The rhythm of small pauses is the compendium's name for kinhin, the formal walking meditation practiced in Zen between periods of seated zazen. The form is deliberate about rhythm: it alternates stillness and motion on a schedule, roughly fifty minutes of sitting followed by ten minutes of walking, and the walking itself is paced to the breath. In the Soto tradition founded by Dogen, the instruction attributed to his teacher Ju-ching is one half-step per full breath ([Sotoshu Shumucho instructions, quoted by Terebess](https://terebess.hu/zen/szoto/etikett.html)).

### The form and the point

The practice is not a leg-stretch. As one Soto teacher puts it, kinhin "gives you a way to refresh the mind and body, without interrupting the stillness of sitting practice. We sit for fifty minutes, then do Kinhin for ten minutes — sitting, walking, sitting" ([Sotoshu Shumucho, quoted by Terebess](https://terebess.hu/zen/szoto/etikett.html)). The same instructions preserve the lineage: "According to the Soto tradition, we do Kinhin practice between sitting sessions as taught by Zen master Dogen, as he learned from his Chinese teacher Ju-Ching" ([same](https://terebess.hu/zen/szoto/etikett.html)).

The rhythm is the teaching. Attention is trained in a cycle rather than held continuously in one posture, which is why kinhin is described as "a bridge between sitting still, and moving in daily activity" ([Terebess, Sotoshu instructions](https://terebess.hu/zen/szoto/etikett.html)). The story of [[ST015 — Paterson Writes Poems on His Lunch Break|the bus driver who writes poems]] is the same rhythm at secular scale: a small, regular pause that is not a break from the work but the thing that lets the work continue.

### What the research says

The evidence for micro-breaks is modest and honest about its limits. A meta-analysis of 22 study samples (N = 2,335) found small effects of breaks of up to ten minutes on vigor (d = .36) and fatigue (d = .35), a non-significant effect on performance (d = .16), and the authors note that "recovering from highly depleting tasks may need more than 10-minute breaks" ([Albulescu et al. 2022, PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0272460)). That fits kinhin's design: the pause is short, rhythmic and embodied, not a recovery from exhaustion.
