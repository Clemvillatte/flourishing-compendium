The culturally embedded self is the account of personhood Taylor argues for in Sources of the Self: identity is not held privately but defined by the commitments and identifications a person draws on, and those come from the frames a culture supplies. In the chapter "The Self in Moral Space" he writes:

> "identity is defined by the commitments and identifications which provide the frame or horizon within which" we "can try to determine from case to case what is good, or valuable, or what ought to be done..." (Taylor, 1989, p. 27, as quoted verbatim in [MacIntyre's review](https://eclass.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/PRIMEDU143/A.%20MacIntyre,%20Sources%20of%20the%20Self.pdf))

The commitments in Taylor's definition are cultural through and through: Catholic or anarchist, Armenian or Quebecois, in his examples, are not private tastes but horizons inherited from communities. To ask "who am I" in abstraction from self-interpretations, Taylor argues elsewhere in the same work, is to ask a question to which there could not in principle be an answer.
