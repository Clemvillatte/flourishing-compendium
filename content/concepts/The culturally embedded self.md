Taylor connects identity with commitments and identifications that provide a framework for judging what is good, valuable, or ought to be done. MacIntyre reproduces part of this account from Sources of the Self, p. 27. ([MacIntyre's review](https://eclass.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/PRIMEDU143/A.%20MacIntyre,%20Sources%20of%20the%20Self.pdf)) MacIntyre quotes Taylor as follows:

> "identity is defined by the commitments and identifications which provide the frame or horizon within which" we "can try to determine from case to case what is good, or valuable, or what ought to be done..." (Taylor, 1989, p. 27, as quoted verbatim in [MacIntyre's review](https://eclass.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/PRIMEDU143/A.%20MacIntyre,%20Sources%20of%20the%20Self.pdf))

### A psychological parallel

Cross-cultural psychology makes a related claim from the empirical side. Markus and Kitayama open their 1991 paper: "People in different cultures have strikingly different construals of the self, of others, and of the interdependence of the [two]. These construals can influence, and in many cases determine, the very nature of individual experience, including cognition, emotion, and motivation." They contrast an independent self with one that "is viewed as interdependent with the surrounding context", so that "it is the 'other' or the 'self-in-relation-to-other' that is focal in individual experience" (Markus & Kitayama, 1991, Psychological Review 98(2), [PDF hosted at MIT](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Influence_Compliance/Markus_Kitayama_1991_Culture_and_the_self.pdf)).

The two accounts differ in kind. Taylor argues that a self is constituted by its commitments within a horizon of what is good, and Markus and Kitayama describe how construals of the self vary by culture and shape cognition and emotion. Taken together they suggest that advice about flourishing addressed to a bare individual leaves out the setting in which a self takes shape.

### What it is not

The culturally embedded self is not cultural determinism. Markus and Kitayama say construals "can influence, and in many cases determine" experience, which is a claim of degree, and this page does not claim that either account excludes individual variation.
