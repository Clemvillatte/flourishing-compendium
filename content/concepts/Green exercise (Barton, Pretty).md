Green exercise is physical activity done in the presence of nature. Jo Barton and Jules Pretty, who developed the research program, define it and its promise in one line: "Green exercise is activity in the presence of nature. Evidence shows it leads to positive short and long-term health outcomes" ([Barton and Pretty, 2010, abstract, PubMed 20337470](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20337470/)).

### The dose findings

Their multi-study meta-analysis of ten UK studies (1,252 participants) asked what dose of green exercise best improves self-esteem and mood. The overall effect sizes were moderate: "The overall effect size for improved self-esteem was d = 0.46 (CI 0.34-0.59, p < 0.00001) and for mood d = 0.54 (CI 0.38-0.69, p < 0.00001)" ([Barton and Pretty, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20337470/)). The shape of the finding matters more than the size:

> "Dose responses for both intensity and duration showed large benefits from short engagements in green exercise, and then diminishing but still positive returns." ([Barton and Pretty, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20337470/))

The benefit front-loads: the first minutes of moving in nature carry most of the measured change. "Every green environment improved both self-esteem and mood; the presence of water generated greater effects" ([Barton and Pretty, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20337470/)). These are self-esteem and mood indicators, not clinical outcomes.
