"The tyranny of the ideal" is the companion's heading for a teaching Nietzsche works out in the second essay of the *On the Genealogy of Morals*: the ideal becomes an instrument of inner tyranny. What begins as a life of external rules of conduct, enforced by habit, slowly and gracefully, is displaced by the instinct for freedom turned back on itself. The force that once arranged a single life as if it were a structure, choosing a profession, a spouse, an abode, "looks out on the wide and dangerous world, and feels itself growing into the perpetual necessity of defending itself," and the reflex of that instinct, when it can no longer discharge itself outward, "makes itself a bad science in the 'labyrinth of the breast,' to use Goethe's phrase, and which builds negative ideals" (Nietzsche, *On the Genealogy of Morals*, II.17–18, trans. Samuel; Project Gutenberg, [eBook 52319](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/52319/52319-h/52319-h.htm)). The ideal is "negative" because its material is not the other man but the self. The same force that raises states and arts, once it is forced back into the self, works as "this secret self-tyranny, this cruelty of the artist, this delight in giving a form to one's self as a piece of difficult, refractory, and suffering material, in burning in a will, a critique, a contradiction, a contempt, a negation; this sinister and ghastly labour of love on the part of a soul, whose will is cloven in two within itself, which makes itself suffer from delight in the infliction of suffering." The ideal in this use is a critique made flesh, a standing form against which the person is measured and found wanting, and its energy is the old will to power working on the only material left to it. The passage explains why harsh self-talk feels like discipline: it has the genuine structure of domination, with the self divided into ruler and ruled, and it presents itself as labour of love precisely because the enforcing instinct is real. Nietzsche's verdict is double-edged; the bad conscience is the "true fountainhead as it is of idealism and imagination," and has "produced an abundance of novel and amazing beauty and affirmation, and perhaps has really been the first to give birth to beauty at all." The tyranny is not refuted by observing that it wounds; it is a productive force whose cost is that the person bears it in their own body. The companion heading is the reader's own name for this teaching; Nietzsche's own words are the "negative ideals" and the "secret self-tyranny" of the instinct of freedom turned on itself.
