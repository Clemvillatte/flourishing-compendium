An experiential purchase is money spent mainly to gain a life experience, such as a trip, a concert or a meal out. A material purchase is money spent mainly to gain a possession. Van Boven and Gilovich (2003) asked whether the first kind makes people happier than the second.

Their abstract reports that "in two surveys, respondents from various demographic groups indicated that experiential purchases ... made them happier than material purchases." In a laboratory follow-up, "participants experienced more positive feelings after pondering an experiential purchase than after pondering a material purchase." A third experiment found that people were more likely to predict that experiences would make them happier "after adopting a temporally distant, versus a temporally proximate, perspective" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14674824/)).

### Why the authors think it happens

The paper's discussion points to evidence that experiences make people happier "because they are more open to positive reinterpretations, are a more meaningful part of one's identity, and contribute more to successful social relationships" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14674824/)).

### Limits

These findings rest on self-reported happiness in surveys and short laboratory tasks. The abstract does not say the pattern holds for every person or every income level, and it should not be read as a rule against owning things.
