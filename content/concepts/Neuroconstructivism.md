Westermann, Mareschal, Johnson, Sirois, Spratling and Thomas (2007) define the approach in their Developmental Science abstract: "Neuroconstructivism is a theoretical framework focusing on the construction of representations in the developing brain. Cognitive development is explained as emerging from the experience-dependent development of neural structures supporting mental representations. Neural development occurs in the context of multiple interacting constraints acting on different levels, from the individual cell to the external environment of the developing child" ([ERIC record of Neuroconstructivism, Developmental Science 10(1), 75-83](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ849108)).

### Development as a trajectory

The abstract continues: "Cognitive development can thus be understood as a trajectory originating from the constraints on the underlying neural structures". It adds that the view "offers an integrated view of normal and abnormal development as well as of development and adult processing" and "stands apart from traditional cognitive approaches in taking seriously the constraints on cognition inherent to the substrate that delivers it" (same record; the [Wiley page](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1467-7687.2007.00567.x) carries the same abstract).

### Use in developmental disorders

A Birkbeck chapter by Westermann, Thomas and Karmiloff-Smith says the approach "places the developmental process at the heart of explanations of developmental deficits" and encourages researchers "to focus on trajectories of development, rather than static snapshots of behaviour at different ages" ([Westermann, Thomas and Karmiloff-Smith, Neuroconstructivism, Birkbeck PDF](https://psyc.bbk.ac.uk/dnl/wp-content/uploads/sites/10/2025/07/Westermann_Thomas_Karmiloff-Smith.pdf)). The fuller statement appears in the book by Mareschal and colleagues, Neuroconstructivism: How the Brain Constructs Cognition (Oxford University Press, 2007), which I saw cited but did not open.

### Fit with the seed

The seed says "brain, body and environment". The abstract names neural structures, constraints at many levels and the child's external environment; it does not mention the body as a separate term. Use the abstract's wording when quoting. "Dynamic interaction over time" is supported by the trajectory framing.
