The affective dynamics framework studies emotion as a time series rather than a score. Its founding claim, from the meta-analysis that consolidated the field:

> "Not only how good or bad people feel on average, but also how their feelings fluctuate across time is crucial for psychological health."
> — [Houben, Van Den Noortgate & Kuppens, Psychological Bulletin 141 (2015), PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25822133/)

The framework's three core measures describe different shapes of fluctuation. Variability is how widely feeling ranges (within-person standard deviation). Instability is how sharply it lurches between consecutive measurements. Inertia is how strongly each moment's feeling predicts the next (autocorrelation) — the degree to which a mood, once arrived, persists.

The meta-analytic result pairs all three with low well-being: across 793 effect sizes from 79 studies, low psychological well-being co-occurred with more variable, more unstable and more inert emotions. The finding cuts against a simple stability ideal. A healthy pattern is neither rigid (high inertia, mood stuck) nor chaotic (high instability, mood lurching), and the authors' conclusion frames flourishing as a specific temporal shape:

> "The findings demonstrate that psychological flourishing is characterized by specific patterns of emotional fluctuations across time, and provide insight into what constitutes optimal and suboptimal emotional functioning."
> — [Houben et al., Psychological Bulletin 141 (2015)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25822133/)
