**Focus theory of attention** — the attended stimulus dominates awareness and processing; focus determines what becomes of experience.

The theory's laboratory form is the zoom lens model of Charles Eriksen and James St. James. Before it, the standard picture was a spotlight: attention illuminates a fixed patch of the visual field. Their 1986 paper proposed a variable-power lens instead, one that can widen or narrow ([Eriksen and St. James, 1986, abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03211502)):

> "The operation of attention in the visual field has often been compared to a spotlight. We propose that a more apt analogy is that of a zoom or variable-power lens." (Eriksen and St. James, [Visual attention within and around the field of focal attention: A zoom lens model, Perception & Psychophysics 40 (1986), pp. 225-240, abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03211502))

### What the lens means

The model's working idea is a trade-off: as the attended area grows, the processing efficiency within it drops, and the boundary shows a gradual dropoff rather than a sharp edge ([Eriksen and St. James, 1986, abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03211502)). Narrow the focus and detail resolves; widen it and the field takes in more at lower resolution. The experiments tested this with letter displays and precues, and the results were in good agreement with the zoom lens model ([Eriksen and St. James, 1986, abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/BF03211502)).
