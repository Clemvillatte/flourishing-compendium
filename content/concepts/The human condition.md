Hannah Arendt's The Human Condition (1958) is a study of the active life, the labor, work and action of the vita activa, under the conditions given to human life. The title term names the conditions under which life is given, which Arendt distinguishes sharply from a human nature.

> "To avoid misunderstanding: the human condition is not the same as human nature, and the sum total of human activities and capabilities which correspond to the human condition does not constitute anything like human nature." (Arendt, The Human Condition, Prologue, [Internet Archive text of the Doubleday edition](https://archive.org/details/the-human-condition-hannah-arendt))

### What the condition consists of

Arendt lists the conditions: "the conditions of human existence—life itself, natality and mortality, worldliness, plurality, and the earth—can never 'explain' what we are or answer the question of who we are for the simple reason that they never condition us absolutely". People are, in her phrase, conditioned beings: "Men are conditioned beings because everything they come in contact with turns immediately into a condition of their existence". The book's warning is against reading these conditions as an essence; even the most radical imaginable change in them would leave us human.

### How Camus and Beauvoir use the term

The Arendt coinage is the philosophical origin, but the two French existentialists in this wiki use "the human condition" in a closely related way. For Camus the condition is the absurd itself: the fact that we keep asking a question the world will not answer, and that the answer is not coming. For Beauvoir it is the ambiguous fact that we are both subjects and objects to each other, and that freedom is disclosed in and by a situation rather than exercised in a vacuum. In all three writers the phrase does the same job: it names where we start, not what we are.
