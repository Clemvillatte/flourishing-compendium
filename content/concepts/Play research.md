Play research, in Stuart Brown's hands, is the study of what play does to the adults who keep doing it. Brown's book, Play: How It Shapes the Brain, Opens the Imagination, and Invigorates the Soul (Avery, 2009), is built on more than 6,000 "play histories" collected from humans ranging from serial murderers to Nobel laureates. The book's opening description:

> Most obviously, it is intensely pleasurable. It energizes us and enlivens us. It eases our burdens. It renews our natural sense of optimism and opens us up to new possibilities. Those are all wonderful, admirable, valuable qualities. But that is just the beginning of the story. Neuroscientists, developmental biologists, psychologists, social scientists, and researchers from every point of the scientific compass now know that play is a profound biological process.

(Brown & Vaughan, [excerpt](https://campusstore.miamioh.edu/play-how-shapes-brain-opens-imagination/bk/9781583333785))

The chapter "The Opposite of Play Is Not Work" carries the clinical claim in its title. The book's most quoted line is compressed from it: "The opposite of play is not work — the opposite of play is depression." The seven properties of play Brown lists are: apparently purposeless (done for its own sake), voluntary, inherent attraction, freedom from time, diminished consciousness of self, improvisational potential, and continuation desire.

My [[D014 — Preserve your childlike vitality]] and [[A020 — Practice wit (eutrapelia)]] are the practices this research backs: play is not a break from the good life but one of its load-bearing parts.
