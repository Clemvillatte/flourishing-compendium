The priestly piety critique is Nietzsche's argument, running through the Genealogy of Morals, that piety can be the costume weakness wears to rule. His portrait of the ascetic priest is admiring and damning at once: "The ascetic priest must be accepted by us as the predestined saviour, herdsman, and champion of the sick herd: thereby do we first understand his awful historic mission." ([Nietzsche, Genealogy of Morals, tr. Samuel](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/52319/pg52319-images.html))

### What the critique carries

The mechanism is guilt. Nietzsche has the priest answer the sufferer: "'I suffer: it must be somebody's fault'—so thinks every sick sheep. But his herdsman, the ascetic priest, says to him, 'Quite so, my sheep, it must be the fault of some one; but thou thyself art that some one, it is all the fault of thyself alone'": a move he calls "bold enough, false enough," with one thing attained: "the course of resentment is—diverted." The healing itself is double-edged: "he brings with him, doubtless, salve and balsam; but before he can play the physician he must first wound; so, while he soothes the pain which the wound makes, he at the same time poisons the wound." The First Essay states the root: "the priests are, as is notorious, the worst enemies—why? Because they are the weakest. Their weakness causes their hate to expand into a monstrous and sinister shape." The compendium principle D184 carries the practical reading: do not over-cultivate the appearance of virtue, the displayed piety held for its effect on the audience. What the critique does not give: a claim that all care for the suffering is disguised cruelty; Nietzsche's target is the power the posture wins, not compassion itself.
