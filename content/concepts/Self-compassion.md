Self-compassion is Kristin Neff's construct for relating to one's own suffering the way one would to a friend's, with three components: self-kindness (versus self-judgment), common humanity (versus isolation), and mindfulness (versus over-identification) ([Neff, the three components of self-compassion](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Neff_Handouts_(1).pdf)). Where self-esteem is a global self-evaluation that must be defended, self-compassion is a stance toward suffering that can be taken in any state, which is why it is more robust than contingent self-worth.

The mechanism: the three components are the antidotes to the three amplifiers of pain. Self-judgment amplifies ("I am worthless"); self-kindness meets the failure with warmth. Isolation amplifies ("this only happens to me"); common humanity names suffering as shared. Over-identification amplifies (drowning in the feeling); mindfulness holds the pain neither suppressed nor dramatized, which is what keeps kindness from becoming self-pity and common humanity from becoming fatalism.

The distinction to hold: self-compassion is not self-pity (which isolates), not self-indulgence (which avoids), and, against the strongest folk objection, not soft on motivation: self-compassionate people are *more* motivated to improve after failure, not less. The folk belief that self-criticism is necessary for discipline is what the kin pages' evidence contradicts.

An example at its smallest: hand on chest, "this is a moment of suffering; suffering is part of life; may I be kind to myself in this moment".

Its contemplative cognates are [[Metta (loving-kindness)]] turned inward and [[Compassion]] (karuna), the Buddhist insight that the self-other boundary in kindness is itself soft.

self-kindness versus harsh self-judgment predicts resilience (Neff, 2003; MacBeth and Gumley, 2012).
