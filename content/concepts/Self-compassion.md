Self-compassion is Kristin Neff's construct for relating to one's own suffering the way one would to a friend's, with three components: self-kindness (versus self-judgment), common humanity (versus isolation), and mindfulness (versus over-identification) ([Neff, the three components of self-compassion](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Neff_Handouts_(1).pdf)). Where self-esteem is a global self-evaluation that must be defended, self-compassion is a stance toward suffering that can be taken in any state — which is why it is more robust than contingent self-worth ([[SE004 — Understand that self-esteem is not the same as self-worth]]).

The mechanism: the three components are the antidotes to the three amplifiers of pain. Self-judgment amplifies ("I am worthless"); self-kindness meets the failure with warmth. Isolation amplifies ("this only happens to me"); common humanity names suffering as shared. Over-identification amplifies (drowning in the feeling); mindfulness holds the pain neither suppressed nor dramatized — which is what keeps kindness from becoming self-pity and common humanity from becoming fatalism ([[SE025 — Cultivate self-compassion — treat yourself as you would a good friend]]; [[SE027 — Practise self-kindness over self-judgment — kindness is more motivating than cruelty]]; [[SE028 — Learn mindful self-awareness — hold your experience without over-identifying]]).

The distinction to hold: self-compassion is not self-pity (which isolates), not self-indulgence (which avoids), and — against the strongest folk objection — not soft on motivation: self-compassionate people are *more* motivated to improve after failure, not less ([[SE017 — Recognise and manage the inner critic — harsh self-talk is not motivation]]; [[MO050 — Learn to practise self-compassion when procrastinating — guilt backfires]]). The folk belief that self-criticism is necessary for discipline is what the kin pages' evidence contradicts.

An example at its smallest: hand on chest — "this is a moment of suffering; suffering is part of life; may I be kind to myself in this moment" ([[K162 — Self-compassion practice]]).

Its contemplative cognates are [[Metta (loving-kindness)]] turned inward and [[Compassion]] (karuna) — the Buddhist insight that the self-other boundary in kindness is itself soft.

self-kindness versus harsh self-judgment predicts resilience (Neff, 2003; MacBeth and Gumley, 2012).
