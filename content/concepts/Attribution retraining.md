This entry covers attribution retraining in peer-aggression interventions. Hudley and Graham's program trained aggressive elementary school boys to judge peers' intentions more accurately, to interpret ambiguous intent as benign, and to connect those interpretations with nonaggressive responses. Dodge's review of this line of work:

> "Hudley and Graham (1993) focused specifically on changing hostile attributional biases in their sample of African American elementary school-aged aggressive boys. They developed and implemented a 12-session cognitive intervention designed to help boys more accurately identify others' intentions, to presume benign intent when the portrayed intent is ambiguous, and to link nonaggressive behavioral responses to benign intent attributions." (Kenneth A. Dodge, [Translational science in action: Hostile attributional style and the development of aggressive behavior problems](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2745254/), Development and Psychopathology 18(3), 2006, "Interventions to Alter Attributional Style" section, pp. 791-814)

A random-assignment experiment contrasted this intervention with a control group that received training in nonsocial problem solving skills. Following intervention, relative to controls, boys assigned to intervention displayed lower hostile attributional bias scores in response to hypothetical provocations as well as to an actual peer provocation engineered during a laboratory task, and they received lower teacher ratings for reactive aggressive behavior. No changes were evident in school office referrals for discipline in that experiment, and the authors acknowledged the intervention included both attribution retraining and behavioral training elements. Dodge also notes a later experiment by Sukhodolsky et al. (2005) in which only the attribution retraining condition lowered hostile attributional bias scores, which is why specific focus on attribution retraining is treated as the active ingredient question in this literature.
