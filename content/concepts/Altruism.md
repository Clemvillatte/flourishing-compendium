Altruism is motivation that aims at another person's good, for that person's sake and not as a means to one's own. The Stanford Encyclopedia's working definition: "Behavior is normally described as altruistic when it is motivated by a desire to benefit someone other than oneself for that person's sake" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Altruism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/altruism/)). The word is young for so old a thing: "Like the word 'sociology', the word 'altruism' was coined by Comte" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Auguste Comte](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/comte/)), from French altruisme, "as an antonym of egoism" ([Wikipedia, Altruism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altruism)).

### The egoism dispute

The interesting question is whether altruism exists at all. Psychological egoism denies it: "Psychological egoism claims that each person has but one ultimate aim: her own welfare" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Egoism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/egoism/)). On that view, "we do not care about others for their sake. Altruism, in other words, does not exist" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Altruism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/altruism/)). Batson's empathy-altruism hypothesis is the strongest reply: "if someone feels empathy towards another person, they will help them, regardless of what they can gain from it" ([Wikipedia, Empathy-altruism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empathy-altruism)).

The experiments behind it are built to separate caring from self-relief. In the escape paradigm, participants heard a recorded interview with a student who had broken her legs in a car accident and might drop out of college. Low-empathy participants helped mostly when escape from further exposure was difficult; "participants in the high-empathy condition helped at consistently high rates regardless of escape difficulty" ([Wikipedia, Empathy-altruism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empathy-altruism)). Egoistic accounts predict the helping should collapse when escape is easy. It did not.

### Mixed motives

Real giving is usually messier than either pure camp allows. Economics has a term for the mix: people "may be 'impurely altruistic', meaning they simultaneously maintain both altruistic and egoistic (selfish) motivations for giving," including the joy of giving itself ([Wikipedia, Warm-glow giving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warm-glow_giving)). That joy is not a disqualification; it is a design feature worth knowing about. Evolutionary biology, for its part, uses a different sense entirely: "an organism is said to behave altruistically when its behaviour benefits other organisms, at a cost to itself," measured in reproductive fitness, not intentions ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Biological Altruism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/altruism-biological/)).

### Why it matters for flourishing

Whether pure altruism is possible matters less than what the empathy route shows: attending to another's situation for that person's sake is a trainable motivational pathway, and I treat it as such. Related practices include [[Empathic concern]] and the Buddhist [[Karuna]], whose content is "a wish for others to be free from suffering" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Ethics of Indian Buddhism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-indian-buddhism/)). Whiting's line about love sets the standard the whole family of concepts is aiming at: "I ought to be concerned with my beloved for her sake, and not because I somehow get something out of it" ([Stanford Encyclopedia, Love](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/love/)).

## Related concepts

- [[Prosocial behaviour]] — the wider category of acts that benefit others, whatever the motive
- [[Empathic concern]] — the other-oriented feeling Batson's experiments trigger
- [[Compassion]] — the emotional response to suffering that aims to relieve it
- [[Karuna]] — the Buddhist cultivation of the same aim
- [[Compassionate love]] — altruistic motivation sustained in close relationships
- [[Acts of kindness (Lyubomirsky)]] — the practical, trainable unit of other-benefiting behavior
- [[Moral obligation]] — the debate over whether aid is owed or supererogatory
- [[Prosocial spending]] — spending money on others as a flourishing intervention
- [[Reciprocity]] — the self-interested shadow that often accompanies helping
