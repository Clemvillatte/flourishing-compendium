**Social capital theory Putnam** — Putnam, Bourdieu — networks, norms and trust enable cooperation; bonding capital joins similar people, bridging capital spans groups.

## Field

Sociocultural · Psychological science
## What the theory says

Robert Putnam drew the term from the analogy with physical and human capital. Tools and training make an individual more productive. Networks, norms and trust make a community more productive. His definition, from the 1995 essay that made the idea famous:

> By analogy with notions of physical capital and human capital—tools and training that enhance individual productivity—"social capital" refers to features of social organization such as networks, norms, and social trust that facilitate coordination and cooperation for mutual benefit.
> — Robert D. Putnam, "Bowling Alone: America's Declining Social Capital," Journal of Democracy 6(1), 1995

The theory also carries a practical edge. Life is easier for communities blessed with a substantial stock of social capital. Networks of civic engagement foster sturdy norms of generalized reciprocity and encourage the emergence of social trust. They facilitate coordination and communication, amplify reputations, and allow dilemmas of collective action to be resolved. Dense networks of interaction probably broaden the participants' sense of self, developing the "I" into the "we".

The essay's famous emblem is bowling. More Americans were bowling than ever before, but between 1980 and 1993 league bowling fell by 40 percent while the total number of bowlers rose by 10 percent. The broader social significance lay in the social interaction, and occasionally civic conversation, that league members had over beer and pizza. Putnam's conclusion was stark. American social capital in the form of civic associations had significantly eroded over the last generation.

Putnam later split the concept in two. Bonding social capital joins people who are alike, and it bolsters the narrower selves. Bridging social capital spans groups, and it can generate broader identities and reciprocity. His own image for the pair: "Bonding social capital constitutes a kind of sociological superglue, whereas bridging social capital provides a sociological WD-40." Both have their uses. Bonding helps people get by; bridging helps them get ahead.

## In the compendium

The principle on treating trust as social capital already runs on this theory. Trust is infrastructure, the confidence that others will cooperate and institutions will function, and it is built in small repeated keepings-of-word. The principle on social capital as a community resource adds the compendium's practical reading. The resource is built with boring things: showing up, joining, reciprocating. The compound interest is real, and Putnam's erosion finding is the warning that it can also run in reverse.
