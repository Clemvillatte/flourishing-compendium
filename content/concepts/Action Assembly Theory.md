Action assembly theory (AAT) is John Greene's account of how thought becomes behaviour. Greene stated its aim directly: "Action assembly theory (AAT) seeks to explain message behavior (both verbal and nonverbal) by describing the system of mental structures and processes that give rise to those behaviors" ([Greene's encyclopedia entry](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/9781405186407.wbieca008.pub2)). In plain words, it is the theory of how you can say something you have never said before: stored mental building blocks are selected, combined, and issued as fresh, recognisably yours output.

## In plain words

The theory addresses the production side of communication: what happens between having something to say and the saying of it. Its premise is that behaviour is assembled moment to moment from memory rather than retrieved whole.

## Where it comes from

Greene published the theory in 1984 as "A cognitive approach to human communication: An action assembly theory," *Communication Monographs* 51, 289–306. The abstract opens with the puzzle the theory answers: "An essential characteristic of human communicative behavior is that it is at once novel and creative yet patterned and repetitive" ([Taylor & Francis, article record](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/03637758409390203)). He extended it through the 1990s in work on message production and social skill.

## How it works

The core unit is the procedural record. "Procedural records are mental structures that incorporate knowledge about behavioral features (what to do and say) along with information about interactional features (how it affects others) and contextual features (when it applies)" ([Bylund, Peterson & Cameron 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3297682/)). A record joins the assembly when its activation crosses a threshold, and activation turns on two factors: how often the record has been used before, and how recently. The assembled records form an output representation that guides the behaviour; the reviewing authors offer a lego analogy: enough properly connected records produce a recognizable structure.

## What it explains

AAT explains novelty-with-pattern: behaviour that is fresh each time yet consistent across situations. Its limit is stated in Greene's own summary: the theory "does not yield specific, testable predictions about behavior in the abstract"; predictions require specifying structure and process for a particular case ([Greene's encyclopedia entry](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/9781405186407.wbieca008.pub2)).

## Everyday life

Comforting a grieving friend, you deliver a sentence you have never spoken before, assembled from earlier attempts at comfort and what they did to others. The practical upshot for flourishing: skill grows by strengthening records through rehearsal and use, not by intention alone, which is why role-play practice works.
