Interpersonal psychoanalysis is a school of psychoanalytic theory and treatment based on the work of the American psychiatrist Harry Stack Sullivan (1892-1949). Its premise, set against Freudian drive theory, is that personality is not a private inner structure but a pattern that exists only in relationships with others. Sullivan's definition of personality is quoted by later researchers as the tradition's anchor:

> "It follows from Harry Stack Sullivan's (1953) definition of personality as 'the relatively enduring pattern of recurrent interpersonal situations which characterize a human life' (p. 110-111) that the 'interpersonal situation' is the fundamental unit of analysis in interpersonal theory. Interpersonal situations are events involving a self and other and associated with an affective experience." (Hopwood, Wright, Ansell & Pincus, [The interpersonal core of personality pathology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3675800/), Journal of Personality Disorders, 2013)

### What the analyst listens for

Sullivan held that the details of a patient's interpersonal interactions can provide insight into the causes and cures of mental disorder. In therapy the analyst is a participant observer, and the work focuses on personifications, a person's internalised views of self and others, gathered through detailed questioning about moment-to-moment interactions, including those with the analyst. Sullivan proposed that patients keep threatening aspects of relationships out of awareness through selective inattention, a defence that blocks notice of the threat before repression sets in ([Wikipedia, Interpersonal psychoanalysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpersonal_psychoanalysis)).
