Overconfidence effects are the family of biases in which people rate themselves, their performance or their knowledge too highly. The original entry's source is Don Moore and Paul Healy's reconciliation of the field, whose abstract sorts the phenomenon into three:

> "The authors present a reconciliation of 3 distinct ways in which the research literature has defined overconfidence: (a) overestimation of one's actual performance, (b) overplacement of one's performance relative to others, and (c) excessive precision in one's beliefs." (Moore & Healy 2008, [Psychol Rev, PMID 18426301](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18426301/))

### The pattern

The three effects do not move together. The abstract's summary of the task-dependent pattern: "On difficult tasks, people overestimate their actual performances but also mistakenly believe that they are worse than others; on easy tasks, people underestimate their actual performances but mistakenly believe they are better than others." Overprecision, the third form, behaves differently: it "appears to be more persistent than either of the other 2 types of overconfidence, but its presence reduces the magnitude of both overestimation and overplacement".

The practical lesson is diagnostic: before calling someone overconfident, say which of the three it is, because the word names different errors with different causes.
