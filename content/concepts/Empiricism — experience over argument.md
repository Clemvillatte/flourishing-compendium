Empiricism is the view that knowledge starts in experience: whatever the mind knows, it first received through outward or inward impression. Hume's An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748) gives the priority its most quoted test, aimed at books whose reasoning never touches experience:

> "If we take in our hand any volume; of divinity or school metaphysics, for instance; let us ask, Does it contain any abstract reasoning concerning quantity or number? No. Does it contain any experimental reasoning concerning matter of fact and existence? No. Commit it then to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion." (Hume, Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, Section XII, Part III, [Project Gutenberg ebook 9662](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/9662/9662-h/9662-h.htm))

### The constructive side

The destructive test rests on Section II's copy principle: "all our ideas are nothing but copies of our impressions". Reasoning can combine and refine the materials that impressions supply, but on Hume's account it has no material of its own. Read together, the two passages give this concept its shape: experience is the material of thought, and argument that cannot show its experiential credentials does not count as knowledge.
