The paradox of hedonism is Henry Sidgwick's finding that pleasure, pursued as a direct target, tends to defeat itself; it arrives instead as the by-product of being absorbed in something else. Sidgwick named it in The Methods of Ethics:

> "Here comes into view what we may call the fundamental paradox of Hedonism, that the impulse towards pleasure, if too predominant, defeats its own aim." (Sidgwick, The Methods of Ethics, 1907 seventh edition, [Book I, Ch. IV, §2](https://www.laits.utexas.edu/poltheory/sidgwick/me/me.b01.c04.s02.html))

### What Sidgwick observed

The observation is about attention, not about pleasure being bad. The person who keeps "his main conscious aim perpetually fixed on his own pleasure," Sidgwick writes, "does not catch the full spirit of the chase; his eagerness never gets just the sharpness of edge which imparts to the pleasure its highest zest." The rule holds for active enjoyments of every kind: "we cannot attain them, at least in their highest degree, so long as we keep our main conscious aim concentrated upon them." In a later chapter Sidgwick adds the practical consequence: the principle of egoistic hedonism "is practically self-limiting; i.e. that a rational method of attaining the end at which it aims requires that we should to some extent put it out of sight and not directly aim at it" ([Book II, Ch. III, §2](https://www.laits.utexas.edu/poltheory/sidgwick/me/me.b02.c03.s02.html)).

### What it carries for practice

The paradox is the reason the compendium pairs happiness with objects outside it: work, people, practices, things worth doing for their own sake. A compendium exercise drawn from the observation is to notice when an activity is being monitored for its yield of enjoyment, and to put the monitoring out of sight for a stretch, as Sidgwick's rational method suggests. What the paradox does not show: that pleasure-seeking is always futile. Sidgwick himself denies the strong reading; his claim is that the direct aim weakens the result, not that hedonism is incoherent as a theory of good.
