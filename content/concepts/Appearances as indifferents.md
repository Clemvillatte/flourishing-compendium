The Stoics held that externals are neither good nor evil, and that appearances (phantasiai) put that doctrine to the test daily. Epictetus trains his students to meet each impression with a judgment: is it in our power, or not? The training is the practice behind this page, and it is why I treat the discipline of appearances as part of wisdom development rather than mood management.

> "As we exercise ourselves against sophistical questions, so we ought to exercise ourselves daily against appearances; for these appearances also propose questions to us. 'A certain person son is dead.' Answer: the thing is not within the power of the will: it is not an evil. 'A father has disinherited a certain son. What do you think of it?' It is a thing beyond the power of the will, not an evil." (Epictetus, Discourses III.3, trans. George Long, [Internet Classics Archive](http://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/discourses.3.three.html))

### What the indifference is for

The point is not numbness. In the same chapter, Epictetus writes that "with respect to that which is neither good nor bad" the soul "feels indifferent", while the good and evil move desire and aversion. The indifference of externals clears the field so that the one thing in our power, the use of appearances, can be exercised well: "The business of the wise and good man is to use appearances conformably to nature" (same page). The training is therefore a daily practice rather than a doctrine to hold, since appearances arrive whether or not they were invited, and each arrival is a fresh occasion to make the judgment again.
