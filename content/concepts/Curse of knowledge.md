The curse of knowledge is the difficulty of setting aside what you know when you try to judge how things look to someone who does not know it. The term comes from economics. Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber (1989) describe "a systematic violation" of the assumption that better-informed people can reproduce the judgments of less-informed ones: "Better-informed agents are unable to ignore private information even when it is in their interest to do so; more information is not always better." In their market experiments, market forces "reduce the curse by approximately 50 percent but do not eliminate it" ([Journal of Political Economy abstract](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/261651)).

A note on wording: the paper is about better-informed and less-informed agents in laboratory markets. The common shorthand that experts overestimate what novices know is a natural extension, but it is not what that abstract tests.

### Adults and children

Keysar, Lin and Barr (2003) found that adults who knew perfectly well that a "director" could not see an object hidden in a bag "often took it as the referent of the director's description, sometimes even attempting to comply with the instruction by actually moving the bag itself". They conclude there is "a stark dissociation between an ability to reflectively distinguish one's own beliefs from others', and the routine deployment of this ability in interpreting the actions of others" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12893123/)).

Birch and Bloom (2003) tested 3- to 5-year-olds and found the bias "to a greater extent" in younger children. The misattributions were limited to "cases in which the children were more knowledgeable than the other person", and did not occur when the children were more ignorant ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12741755/)).

### For writers and teachers

Taken together, these are three laboratory settings: markets, a communication game and a child's knowledge task. None of the three abstracts tests explanations of expert jargon or essay drafts. Treating those as cases of the same bias is an inference, a reasonable one, and worth checking against readers rather than assuming.
