Self-deception is the situation of holding a belief one is also, in some way, working to keep from oneself. Its classical philosophical treatment is Sartre's account of bad faith (mauvaise foi) in Being and Nothingness (1943), where it is not an occasional accident but a standing risk built into the structure of consciousness.

> "If bad faith is possible, it is because it is an immediate, permanent threat to every project of the human being; it is because consciousness conceals in its being a permanent risk of bad faith. The origin of this risk is the fact that the nature of consciousness simultaneously is to be what it is not and not to be what it is" (Being and Nothingness, Part One, ch. 2, Barnes trans., [excerpt PDF](https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~degray/CP05/Sartre%20-%20Nothingness-Bad-Faith.pdf)).

### The waiter

Sartre's worked example is a café waiter who throws himself into his role. The failure is not the role itself but the treating of it as an essence: "the waiter in the café can not be immediately a café waiter in the sense that this inkwell is an inkwell, or the glass is a glass" (Being and Nothingness, Barnes trans.). A person can play at being a waiter, rehearse the gestures, let the role absorb the attention; what they cannot do is be one the way an inkwell is an inkwell. Bad faith is this confusion played out as identity: a freedom taking itself for a fixed thing.
