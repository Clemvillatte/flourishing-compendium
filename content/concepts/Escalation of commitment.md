Escalation of commitment is the tendency to keep investing in a course of action that is going badly. Sleesman and colleagues describe it as "the proclivity for decision makers to maintain commitment to losing courses of action, even in the face of quite negative news," a pattern of "throwing good money (or resources more generally) after bad". They note that researchers have studied it for over 35 years, since Staw (1976), and that Staw's article title "borrows from the 1967 Pete Seeger Vietnam War protest song, 'Waist Deep in the Big Muddy'" ([Sleesman, Conlon, McNamara and Miles, 2012, Academy of Management Journal](http://www.iot.ntnu.no/innovation/norsi-pims-courses/huber/Sleesman,%20Conlon%20&%20McNamara%20(2012).pdf)).

### Several explanations, not one

Sunk-cost thinking is the usual popular explanation. The 2012 meta-analysis treats it as one candidate among several. It lists "a sense of personal responsibility for the initial decision that led to the failing course of action (Staw, 1976), the extent of sunk costs (Arkes & Blumer, 1985; Thaler, 1980), and the extent to which the failing project is near completion (Conlon & Garland, 1993)". It also names performance trends, personality and experience of the decision maker as factors that have been studied, and it sorts the theories behind them: subjective expected utility, self-justification, prospect theory, goal substitution, self-presentation and agency theory.

The paper's stated aim is to compare how well these accounts explain escalation. This page draws on the paper's abstract and introduction, so it does not report which account explains escalation best.

### Why it matters here

The paper's own examples include a Boston highway project, a nuclear power plant, a failed stock-exchange IT system and rogue trading at a bank. People in ordinary life face smaller versions of the same decision about jobs, projects and relationships. That extension is an inference, and the meta-analysis covers organizational and laboratory studies.
