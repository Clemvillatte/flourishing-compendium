Anger reappraisal is the application of cognitive reappraisal to situations that elicit anger. Gross describes reappraisal as a form of cognitive change directed at a situation's meaning or its relevance to oneself.

> Cognitive change refers to modifying one's appraisal of a situation in order to alter its emotional impact. — Gross, ["Emotion regulation: Current status and future prospects" (2015), Psychological Inquiry 26(1), Cognitive Change subsection](http://www.johnnietfeld.com/uploads/2/2/6/0/22606800/gross_2015.pdf)

### Why venting fails the test

Brad Bushman's 2002 experiment compared ways of dealing with an existing anger. Angered participants hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who had angered them (rumination group) or about becoming physically fit (distraction group), alongside a no-punching-bag control. In the abstract's words, "Rumination increased rather than decreased anger and aggression. Doing nothing at all was more effective than venting anger." This experiment tested rumination and distraction during venting, not cognitive reappraisal.

### What reappraisal changes, and where it stops

When used to down-regulate negative emotions, the review finds reappraisal leads to decreased negative emotion experience and decreased or unchanged sympathetic responses, at least in low to moderate intensity situations. Gross notes that very high emotional intensity can make reappraisal less effective and suggests distraction before reappraisal in such situations. The review also records a context limit drawn from Troy, Shallcross and Mauss: reappraisal applied to stressors that can be controlled is less adaptive than reappraisal applied to stressors that cannot be controlled.
