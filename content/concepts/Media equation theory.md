Byron Reeves and Clifford Nass introduced the media equation in their 1996 book The Media Equation: How People Treat Computers, Television, and New Media Like Real People and Places. The International Encyclopedia of Communication entry explains that the equation, "media equal real life", means people process technology-mediated experiences as they would unmediated ones, because an "individual's interactions with computers, television, and new media are fundamentally social and natural, just like interaction in real life" (Reeves and Nass 1996, p. 5, as quoted in [Media Equation Theory, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781405186407.wbiecm035)).

### What it claims, and what it does not

A communication-theory textbook chapter stresses that the book "is not a science-fiction fantasy in which computers come alive". The authors regard the machines as inanimate, and the claim is only "that we respond to communication media as if they were alive" ([A First Look at Communication Theory, chapter PDF](https://www.afirstlook.com/docs/mediaeq.pdf)). The chapter notes that none of the studies "prove" the identity, and that the support is inductive. Nass gives the reasons computers draw social responses: "Computers use language, respond based on multiple prior inputs, fill roles traditionally filled by humans, and produce human-sounding voices." The rules people apply include gender stereotypes, similarity attraction, politeness and reciprocity ([Minding the source, Human Communication Research](https://academic.oup.com/hcr/advance-article/doi/10.1093/hcr/hqad034/7329158)).

### The mindlessness account

Nass and Moon (2000) later explained the effect. A paper on a stronger CASA quotes them: people respond mindlessly "to the extent that they apply social scripts... that are inappropriate for human-computer interaction, essentially ignoring the cues that reveal the essential asocial nature of the computer" ([Building a stronger CASA, INFORMIT PDF](https://search.informit.org/doi/pdf/10.3316/INFORMIT.097034846749023?download=true)). People show these responses "even though they understand that computers are not human". Reeves and Nass noted that people "can be trained to be more mindful of context cues" (Nass and Moon, 2000, p. 98, quoted in [a UCF paper on two paradigms](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1098&context=hmc)).

### Criticism

Critics raise four points. A review hosted on Dourish's site says that the authors "seem to systematically underestimate the intelligence of their subjects" ([Review of The Media Equation](https://www.dourish.com/publications/media-review.html)). A reviewer named Lucke objected that "people have been treating inanimate objects like other people since time immemorial", and the authors answered that he confuses their "systematic, empirical demonstration" with "the vacuous statement" ([ACM Digital Library reviews](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.5555/236605)). A theory paper in Human Communication Research says "mindlessness as a psychological state was not separately measured, nor was it directly tested", only "presumed or inferred" (Minding the source). Finally, studies suggest "in many cases, people still treat technology and humans as different kinds of communicators" ([Perceived authenticity of virtual characters, Frontiers in Virtual Reality](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/virtual-reality/articles/10.3389/frvir.2022.1033709/full)).

### For the essays

The finding is that social responses toward media are common, even among people who deny them. It does not show that machines have minds or that these responses are harmless.
