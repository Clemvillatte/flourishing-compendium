The ten thousand things is the Daoist way of pointing at the world's sheer abundance: every particular being, each with its own nature, all at once. The phrase translates wanwu, literally the ten thousand things, and the Daodejing's use of it is deliberately unbothered. In Legge's rendering of chapter 34: "All-pervading is the Great Tao! It may be found on the left hand and on the right. All things depend on it for their production, which it gives to them, not one refusing obedience to it" ([Laozi, Daodejing 34, Legge translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt)). The Tao does not produce a designed set; it produces everything, and nothing refuses.

### What the Dao does with them

The same chapter stresses the manner of the presiding: the Tao "clothes all things as with a garment, and makes no assumption of being their lord," and when its work is done "it does not claim the name of having done it" ([Daodejing 34, Legge](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt)). The ten thousand things are not managed. They are given birth, clothed, and left to their own ways, which is why ziran, naturalness or so-of-itself-ness, is the character the tradition assigns to each of them. Chapter 64 adds the other half of the picture: the things grow from small beginnings, "the tree which fills the arms grew from the tiniest sprout; the tower of nine storeys rose from a (small) heap of earth" ([Daodejing 64, Legge](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt)). The ten thousand is a count of what came from almost nothing.

### The ethical use of the number

The number matters because it is uncountable in practice. A view that takes in the ten thousand things cannot rest on one scale of value; the Zhuangzi's pipes of heaven passage hears every thing's own voice and refuses to rank them, which is the qiwu argument against rigid right and wrong. For practice the image does two jobs. It makes room for the variety of things, including the variety of selves and emotions a person contains, and it recommends the Tao's manner: to help without meddling, to clothe without lording. The compendium's Daoist principles are mostly glosses on those two jobs.
