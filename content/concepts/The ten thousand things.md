The ten thousand things is the Daoist way of pointing at the world's sheer abundance: every particular being, all at once. In Legge's rendering of chapter 34, the Daodejing treats the whole multitude without fuss: "All-pervading is the Great Tao! It may be found on the left hand and on the right. All things depend on it for their production, which it gives to them, not one refusing obedience to it" ([Laozi, Daodejing 34, Legge translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt)). The Tao does not produce a designed set; it produces everything, and nothing refuses.

### What the Dao does with them

The same chapter stresses the manner of the presiding: the Tao "clothes all things as with a garment, and makes no assumption of being their lord," and when its work is done "it does not claim the name of having done it" ([Daodejing 34, Legge](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt)). The chapter ends with the sage's version of the same manner: "Hence the sage is able (in the same way) to accomplish his great achievements. It is through his not making himself great that he can accomplish them" ([Daodejing 34.3, Legge](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt)). Chapter 64 adds the other half of the picture: the things grow from small beginnings, "The tree which fills the arms grew from the tiniest sprout; the tower of nine storeys rose from a (small) heap of earth" ([Daodejing 64, Legge](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt)). The ten thousand is a count of what came from almost nothing.

### The ethical use of the number

The number is uncountable in practice, and the tradition's practical counsel follows the Tao's manner toward the multitude: to give without lording, to accomplish without claiming, to help without meddling. The compendium's story of the pipes of heaven makes a related move in narrative form, hearing the different sounds without ranking them. Read that way, offered as the compendium's reading rather than a claim in the text, the image makes room for variety, including the variety of selves and emotions a person contains, while recommending the non-managing manner toward all of it.
