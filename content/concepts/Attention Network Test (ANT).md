The Attention Network Test (ANT) is a computer task built to measure the efficiency of three attentional networks — alerting, orienting, and executive attention — separately, in one sitting. It was introduced by Jin Fan, Bruce McCandliss, Tobias Sommer, Amir Raz and Michael Posner in 2002.

> "The Attention Network Test (ANT) is designed to evaluate alerting, orienting, and executive attention within a single 30-min testing session that can be easily performed by children, patients, and monkeys. A study with 40 normal adult subjects indicates that the ANT produces reliable single subject estimates of alerting, orienting, and executive function, and further suggests that the efficiencies of these three networks are uncorrelated. There are, however, some interactions in which alerting and orienting can modulate the degree of interference from flankers." ([Fan, McCandliss, Sommer, Raz and Posner, 2002](https://w3.ual.es/personal/tdaza/Fan%20y%20cols.pdf), abstract)

### How the task works

The ANT combines two older experimental paradigms: the cued reaction time task and the flanker task ([Fan et al., 2002](https://w3.ual.es/personal/tdaza/Fan%20y%20cols.pdf)). "The ANT requires participants to determine whether a central arrow points left or right. The arrow appears above or below fixation and may or may not be accompanied by flankers. Efficiency of the three attentional networks is assessed by measuring how response times are influenced by alerting cues, spatial cues, and flankers." Each network gets its own score by subtraction: how much a warning cue speeds responses (alerting), how much a spatial cue speeds them (orienting), and how much incongruent flankers slow them (executive attention).

### What the scores showed

In the 40-subject study, the three scores did not correlate with each other. As the authors put it: "Results demonstrate no correlation between any combination of alerting, orienting, and conflict resolution, suggesting that these are functionally orthogonal constructs." The abstract adds the caveat that there were "some interactions in which alerting and orienting can modulate the degree of interference from flankers" — the networks are separable but not entirely independent in operation.
