Guilt is a negative "self-conscious" emotion about a specific wrong. Tangney and colleagues' 2007 Annual Review of Psychology chapter names a triad of such emotions, "shame, guilt, and embarrassment", and reviews "the relative advantages and disadvantages of these two moral emotions" ([Tangney, Stuewig and Mashek, 2007, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16953797/)).

### Guilt versus shame

The same research group's inmate study draws the line: "Feelings of shame involve a negative evaluation of the global self; feelings of guilt involve a negative evaluation of a specific behavior." In their words, "Shame motivates a desire to 'duck the heat.' Guilt motivates a desire to 'face the music.'" Guilt "is typified by a sense of tension, remorse and regret over the 'bad thing done'", and "rather than motivating an avoidance response, guilt motivates confession and repair" ([Tangney et al., PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3092296/)).

### Evidence for the adaptive view

A review of offender research reports that studies "at various levels of measurement, with diverse populations, indicate that guilt is the more adaptive moral emotion, while shame is a moral emotion that can easily go awry". In that review, inmates' guilt-proneness was "positively associated with other-oriented empathy and negatively associated with externalization of blame and hostility" ([Shame, Guilt and Remorse: Implications for Offender Populations, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3328863/)). The inmate study found guilt-proneness negatively related to substance-abuse problems and risky sexual behavior, whereas shame-proneness was positively correlated with inmates' anxiety, depression and low self-esteem.

### A caution

This "social-adaptive" picture is contested. A 2017 paper compares it with a functionalist view, in which guilt "is more likely to produce moral behavior if conditions favoring direct reciprocity are met", for instance when future encounters with the person wronged are likely ([A Comparison of the Social-Adaptive Perspective and Functionalist Perspective on Guilt and Shame](https://digitalcommons.jsu.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1036&context=fac_res)). The same paper adds that "the majority of results found using the TOSCA do show support for the notion of guilt as adaptive". A study of anticipated group-based emotions reports results that "contrast with work by Tangney and colleagues ... suggesting that moral behavior is more likely to be guided by guilt than shame" ([PMC3657186](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3657186/)).

The claim the essays can make is bounded: in most of the research reviewed, guilt is the moral emotion linked to repair, and that link depends on conditions.
