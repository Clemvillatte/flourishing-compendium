Teleological bias is the tendency to explain things by their purpose. Deborah Kelemen calls the over-extended form "promiscuous teleology". A review of research on the cognitive science of religion notes that teleological thinking is applied "to domains where it is unwarranted", whereas "selective teleology" is the ability to use it where appropriate, such as artefacts and anatomical adaptations: "chairs are for sitting on and molars are for chewing with" ([Review of promiscuous teleology, CORE](https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/266990245.pdf)).

### Adults under time pressure

Kelemen and Rosset's 2009 study asked whether adults share children's bias. Undergraduates judged explanations as correct or incorrect in fast, moderately speeded or unspeeded conditions. The abstract reports that "participants in speeded conditions judged significantly more scientifically unwarranted teleological explanations as correct (e.g., 'the sun radiates heat because warmth nurtures life'), but were not more error-prone on control items" ([Kelemen & Rosset 2009, Cognition](https://www.bu.edu/cdl/files/2013/08/2009_KelemenRosset.pdf)). The authors suggest that "teleological explanation remains an explanatory default throughout development", suppressed and not replaced by scientific explanations.

A later study of professional physical scientists recaps that in the earlier work undergraduates accepted inaccurate teleological explanations 61% of the time under speed against 52% unspeeded, and reports that scientists too defaulted to such explanations when their cognitive resources were limited ([Kelemen, Rottman & Seston 2012](https://www.bu.edu/cdl/files/2013/08/2012_KelemenRottmanSeston.pdf)).

### Limits and critiques

The CORE review states that the hypothesis "faces a number of difficulties" despite substantial evidence. The tendency "can be subdued by education" ([CORE review](https://core.ac.uk/download/pdf/266990245.pdf)).

### Scope

Kelemen's experiments concern explanations of natural phenomena, not whether people read purpose into events in their own lives. The compendium's principle on reading purposes into events applies the idea to that second domain. The same review also covers the belief that things happen for a reason. The compendium's note cites Kelemen 2004, which was not consulted, so the sources here are Kelemen and Rosset 2009 and secondary reviews.
