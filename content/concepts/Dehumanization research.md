Dehumanization research asks how people come to see others as less than fully human, and what follows when they do. Haslam's 2006 review proposes two forms. They involve "the denial to others of 2 distinct senses of humanness: characteristics that are uniquely human and those that constitute human nature." In his model, "[d]enying uniquely human attributes to others represents them as animal-like, and denying human nature to others represents them as objects or automata." The abstract calls these the "animalistic" and "mechanistic" forms ([Haslam, 2006, PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16859440/)).

### An everyday process

The same review argues that dehumanization "is not unitary, is not restricted to the intergroup context, and does not occur only under conditions of conflict or extreme negative evaluation. Instead, dehumanization becomes an everyday social phenomenon, rooted in ordinary social-cognitive processes" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16859440/)). That makes it relevant to ordinary disagreements as well as to atrocity.

### Later review

Haslam and Loughnan (2014) survey the field around six questions: how people are dehumanized, who is dehumanized, who dehumanizes, when, with what consequences, and "what can be done to reduce dehumanization". On consequences, they stress "implications for prosocial and antisocial behavior and for moral judgment" ([Annual Review of Psychology, PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23808915/)).

### Scope

The two abstracts describe the forms, causes and consequences of dehumanization in general terms. Neither abstract states the specific claim that subtle denial of humanness predicts cruelty, which is how this topic is sometimes summarised. For that claim, the full articles and their cited studies are the place to check.
