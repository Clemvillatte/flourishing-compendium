“Concrete experience over abstraction” — Dewey, Art as Experience.

The label is the compendium's own summary, not Dewey's wording. In Art as Experience (1934), especially Chapter III, "Having an Experience", Dewey treats lived experience that runs its course to completion as the starting point of understanding, and treats the analytic distinctions of reflection as made from within it. The summaries here rely on the chapter text as reproduced online and on the Stanford Encyclopedia's account of Dewey's aesthetics.

### Experience that runs its course

> "In contrast with such experience, we have an experience when the material experienced runs its course to fulfillment." (Dewey, Art as Experience, ch. III, [reproduced text](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/philosophy/works/us/an-experience.htm))

His examples are ordinary: a piece of work finished satisfactorily, a problem solved, a game played through, a meal, a conversation, a political campaign. In these cases the close is "a consummation and not a cessation".

### Why abstraction comes second

Dewey's unity of "an experience" is not made of parts that can be pulled apart.

> "This unity is neither emotional, practical, nor intellectual, for these terms name distinctions that reflection can make within it." (Dewey, Art as Experience, ch. III)

The point for the compendium is the order he implies: the felt whole comes first, and the categories (emotion, practice, intellect) are what reflection afterwards draws out of it. The Stanford Encyclopedia adds that the structure holds even for "the thinker interacting with his or her ideas". ([Stanford Encyclopedia, section 2.3](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/win2020/entries/dewey-aesthetics/index.html))

### What Dewey does not say

Dewey is not arguing against abstraction as such. The same chapter treats thinking as an experience too: a conclusion, he writes, is "no separate and independent thing; it is the consummation of a movement". His claim is about where thinking is anchored and what makes an experience whole. He is also writing about aesthetic experience, so applying it to practical judgement is an extension the compendium makes.
