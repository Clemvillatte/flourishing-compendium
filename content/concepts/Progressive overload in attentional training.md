Progressive overload is a term borrowed from strength training: raise the demand on a system in steps so that it keeps adapting. Applied to attention, the idea is that gradually harder or longer practice builds capacity. The compendium's seed says this and cites Lutz et al. (2008) and Zanesco et al. (2016). I opened Lutz et al. and a 2018 Zanesco et al. paper, but found no 2016 Zanesco paper matching the sentence.

### What Lutz and colleagues describe

Lutz, Slagter, Dunne and Davidson review focused attention meditation, in which one voluntarily holds attention on a chosen object. They write that "progress in this form of meditation is measured in part by the degree of effort required to sustain the intended focus," that the novice "contends with more distractions," and that at the most advanced levels "the regulative skills are invoked less and less frequently, and the ability to sustain focus thus becomes progressively 'effortless'" ([Lutz et al., 2008, Trends in Cognitive Sciences 12(4):163-169, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2693206/)).

This is a progression, but it points the other way from overload. Difficulty is not stepped up for the practitioner. Effort falls as skill grows. In one fMRI study the authors describe, expert meditators with an average of 19,000 hours of practice showed stronger activation in attention-related areas than novices, while experts with an average of 44,000 hours "showed less activation," which they read as support for the idea that "after extensive FA meditation training, minimal effort is necessary to sustain attentional focus" ([Lutz et al., 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2693206/)). That comparison is between groups, not a training trial.

### What Zanesco and colleagues add

Zanesco, King, MacLean and Saron followed participants who did full-time meditation retreats and tested them on a sustained response inhibition task for up to 7 years. They report that "performance improvements observed during periods of intensive practice were partially maintained several years later," and that aging-related decrements were moderated by the level of continued practice. They also state that "participants did not generally improve over years of daily meditation practice" ([Zanesco et al., 2018, Journal of Cognitive Enhancement 2:259-275](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s41465-018-0068-1)). So maintenance of gains depends on continued practice, but the study does not show a ladder of ever-longer sessions.

### Adaptive difficulty in cognitive training

The nearest direct test of progressive difficulty comes from working memory training, where tasks are made harder as the trainee succeeds. A randomised study of 130 adults found neither near nor far transfer, and "neither training nor transfer effects were modulated by training procedure," so exposure to varying difficulty was enough ([Springer article](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s00426-015-0655-z.pdf)). A trial in children found "no evidence that adaptive working memory training maximizes children's outcomes" ([PMC11868676](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11868676/)).

### In the compendium

AT031 (Cultivate attentional endurance: build capacity gradually) reads best as advice grounded in practice traditions and the novice-to-expert description above. The sources do not show that lengthening sessions step by step produces more capacity than other schedules. The seed's reading of Zanesco 2016 is not confirmed.
