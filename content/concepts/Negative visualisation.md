Negative visualisation is the Stoic exercise of deliberately picturing loss and trouble before they arrive: not to brood, but to arrive at the loss already rehearsed. The compendium's principles carry it as a practice for keeping the mind in a state conformable to nature when things go wrong.

Epictetus states the exercise directly in the Enchiridion:

> "Let death and exile, and all other things which appear terrible be daily before your eyes, but chiefly death, and you win never entertain any abject thought, nor too eagerly covet anything."
>
> (Epictetus, Enchiridion 21, George Long translation. [MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html))

### How the exercise works
The mechanism is stated in the chapter before it: you take an action, remind yourself what usually happens in such actions, and pre-decide the state of your own mind. Epictetus's example is the bath — some people splash, push, use abusive language — so "you will more safely go about this action if you say to yourself, 'I will now go bathe, and keep my own mind in a state conformable to nature'" (Enchiridion 4). The visualisation does not prevent the event; it removes the surprise, and the surprise is where the damage enters. Seneca gives the same rationale in his ninety-first letter, after the burning of Lyons:

> "Therefore, nothing ought to be unexpected by us. Our minds should be sent forward in advance to meet all problems, and we should consider, not what is wont to happen, but what can happen."
>
> (Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius 91, Gummere translation. [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_91))

The near neighbour is premeditatio malorum, the premeditation of future ills, which this page is listed against for comparison. The difference is one of emphasis: the premeditation rehearses the trouble; the visualisation also rehearses one's own response to it. The exercise is not pessimism, since the point is to keep coveting and dread from running the mind.

### In everyday life (illustrative scenario)
A parent looks at a child asleep and privately notes that this is a loan, not a possession. The thought is not morbid; it is the Enchiridion's own practice of holding death "daily before your eyes", so that neither the child nor the bath, the commute or the paycheck owns the mind's peace. When the small losses arrive, they meet a person who had already met them.
