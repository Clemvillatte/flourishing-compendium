Ultradian performance rhythms are the roughly 90-minute oscillations in alertness and cognitive capacity that continue through the waking day. The compendium holds them for two reasons: attention is not a steady resource, and the day's troughs are rest, not waste.

### The Kleitman hypothesis

The claim traces to Nathaniel Kleitman, who proposed the basic rest-activity cycle (BRAC) in 1961 and elaborated it in Sleep and Wakefulness (1963). His own words on the waking form of the cycle:

> "This cycle is obscured during wakefulness by the great surge of cortical activity, but suggestions of its presence may be discerned in daytime oscillations in alertness, the often irresistible drowsiness after a big meal, and the relief that some persons get from brief catnaps."

([Kleitman 1961, p. 361, quoted in Lavie's BRAC chapter, Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4471-1969-2_13).)

The APA Dictionary defines the BRAC as "cyclic alternations between neurophysiological activity and nonactivity during waking and sleep, thought typically to involve a 90-minute cycle," first described by Kleitman in 1961 ([APA Dictionary of Psychology](https://dictionary.apa.org/basic-rest-activity-cycle)). Kleitman's later technical description gives the mechanism: "an alternation of EEG patterns, with a high-voltage slow activity (HVSA) during the rest phase and a low-voltage fast activity (LCFA) during the activity phase," completing in 85-90 minutes in adult humans ([Kleitman 1970, Springer chapter PDF](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-1-4684-1836-1_21.pdf)).

### What the rhythm does to performance

The cycle's structure — a high-performance phase followed by a 15-20 minute recovery trough — is why the compendium treats working in blocks as a practice rather than a productivity tip. The evidence base is the REM-NREM sleep literature: the 90-minute periodicity in sleep is well established, and the waking manifestation is what Kleitman predicted, a daytime oscillation in alertness ([Lavie, "Ultradian Cycles in Sleep Propensity," Springer](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4471-1969-2_13)). A candid note: the waking BRAC remains harder to measure than the sleeping one, and reviewers describe Kleitman as "somewhat vague about the properties of the BRAC" ([same](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-1-4471-1969-2_13)). The page's advice follows the oscillation, not a stopwatch.
