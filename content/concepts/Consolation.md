“Consolation” — Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy.

Consolation is the act of easing grief with reasons, and its classic text is the one Boethius wrote while awaiting execution in 524: a prison dialogue between the fallen statesman and Lady Philosophy. He begins in tears, and she begins by diagnosing his real wound: he is grieving his lost fortune as though fortune were something owed. Her first move is to re-describe his enemy:

> "Thou deemest Fortune to have changed towards thee; thou mistakest. Such ever were her ways, ever such her nature. Rather in her very mutability hath she preserved towards thee her true constancy." ([Boethius, The Consolation of Philosophy, H.R. James translation, Book II, prose I](https://archive.org/stream/theconsolationof00boetuoft/theconsolationof00boetuoft_djvu.txt))

Fortune then speaks in her own voice, and her speech is the most quoted description of chance in the tradition:

> "This is my art, this the game I never cease to play. I turn the wheel that spins. I delight to see the high come down and the low ascend. Mount up, if thou wilt, but only on condition that thou wilt not think it a hardship to come down when the rules of my game require it." (Book II, prose II)

With that settled, Philosophy can offer the consolation's core sentence, which survives every later translation dispute:

> "So true is it that nothing is wretched, but thinking makes it so, and conversely every lot is happy if borne with equanimity." (Book II, prose IV)

And she closes the argument by cutting consolation loose from comfort: happiness cannot be built out of things chance can take away:

> "And that thou mayst see that happiness cannot possibly consist in these things which are the sport of chance, reflect that, if happiness is the highest good of a creature living in accordance with reason, and if a thing which can in any wise be reft away is not the highest good... it is plain that Fortune cannot aspire to bestow happiness by reason of its instability." (Book II, prose IV)

The book's afterlife is enormous: for a thousand years it was the West's handbook for losing well, and its method, reason spoken gently to a grieving person, is still the shape of what a good counselor does. The compendium's principle [[B108 — Grieve with presence]] keeps a modern cousin, and [[Grief's oscillation model]] keeps the psychological one.
