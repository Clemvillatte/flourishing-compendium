Niko Tinbergen's four questions are the ethologist's test for a complete explanation of any behaviour: function, history, development, and mechanism. The PBT manual adopts them as the check that an evolutionary account of a change process is well-rounded:

> "In a well-rounded evolutionary account, these concepts are applied to any phenomenon using Niko Tinbergen’s (1963) four central questions of function, history, development, and mechanism." (Hofmann, Hayes & Lorscheid, 2021, Learning Process-Based Therapy, Chapter 3)

Applied to a stuck pattern, the four questions spread the inquiry: what is this behaviour for now (function), how did it arise over the species' history (history), how does it develop within a person's lifetime (development), and what carries it moment to moment (mechanism). The book's standard is the well-rounded account; a case description that answers only one of the four leaves the other questions open, and the model treats that openness as the next thing to investigate.

The questions were not invented for therapy. Tinbergen's 1963 paper extended Julian Huxley's three "major problems of Biology" — causation, survival value and evolution — with a fourth, ontogeny, and demanded equal billing for all four: "it is useful both to distinguish between them and to insist that a comprehensive, coherent science of Ethology has to give equal attention to each of them and to their integration" (Tinbergen, 1963, p. 411, quoted in Taborsky, 2014). Read clinically, the equal-attention demand turns into questions for any therapy: what is this pattern for, where did it come from over the species' history, how did it develop in this person's lifetime, and what carries it now? A therapy that adjusts mechanisms without the first two questions, or tells the origin story without the last one, is leaving Tinbergen's demand unmet — each is a different question, and none substitutes for another.
