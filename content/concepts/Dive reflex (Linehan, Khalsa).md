The dive reflex is the physiological hinge behind one of DBT's crisis-survival skills. A 2025 study of healthy adolescents states it plainly: "The diving reflex is a cardiorespiratory response to submersion under water, and is characterized by apnea, bradycardia, and peripheral vasoconstriction" ([Lundstrom et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12234767/)). The trigger is orofacial: "If water enters the orofacial area or if the head is submerged under water, resulting in apnea, the diving reflex is triggered. Subsequently, the parasympathetic nervous system (PNS) is activated through the vagal nerve, resulting in bradycardia and a decreased workload on the heart" (ibid.).

### The measurement

The reflex is robust enough to be a laboratory instrument. In a 2016 study, "All participants performed diving reflex tests twice in the sitting position with the face immersed in cold water (1.9-3.1 °C) and apnea at midinspiration for a minimum of 30 s", and "All participants showed bradycardia response to the two diving reflex tests" ([Konishi et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26795189/)). In that study every participant showed the slowing, with no training mentioned.

### Why Linehan reached for it

Linehan's DBT skills handout includes it among distress-tolerance skills, prescribing the technique as a distress-tolerance move: hold your breath and put your face in a bowl of cold water, or hold a cold pack on your eyes and upper cheeks, for about 30 seconds, keeping the water above 50°F and checking with a doctor first because very cold water decreases heart rate rapidly ([DBT skills handout](http://tri-life.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/TIPP-Skills_DBT.pdf)). It is a deliberate use of an ancient survival circuit: the brain reads cold water on the face as diving, and slows the heart whether or not the person is in crisis.
