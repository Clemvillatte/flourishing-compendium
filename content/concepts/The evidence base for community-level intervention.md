The evidence base for community-level intervention is the research record behind prevention programs that combine individual and environmental change strategies across multiple settings within a local community. The reference point is Abraham Wandersman and Paul Florin's 2003 American Psychologist review, which defines the object and then reports a mixed record ([Wandersman and Florin 2003](https://j.b5z.net/i/u/2043019/f/Community_Interventions_and_Effective_Prevention.pdf), American Psychologist 58(6/7)).

> "Community-level interventions are multicomponent interventions that generally combine individual and environmental change strategies across multiple settings to prevent dysfunction and promote well-being among population groups in a defined local community." (Wandersman and Florin, [Community interventions and effective prevention](https://j.b5z.net/i/u/2043019/f/Community_Interventions_and_Effective_Prevention.pdf), American Psychologist 58(6/7):441-448, 2003, section "Community-Level Interventions: Definition and Rationale")

### The mixed record

The review's abstract states the shape of the evidence plainly: the potential of community-level interventions "is illustrated by a number of successful studies", but "more inclusive reviews and multisite comparisons show that although there have been successes, many interventions did not demonstrate results" ([abstract](https://j.b5z.net/i/u/2043019/f/Community_Interventions_and_Effective_Prevention.pdf)). The review concerns prevention of problems among young people, including pregnancy, substance abuse, violence and delinquency. The authors also discuss how family, school and community systems influence youth substance-use problems, violence, delinquency and mental illness.

### Why results are hard to get

The article sorts the difficulties into three themes. Evaluation: community-level interventions carry methodological difficulties, including "questionable appropriateness of random assignment, difficulty in finding suitable (e.g., pure) comparison sites, and problems in making connections between immediate outcomes of specific programs and ultimate community-wide impacts". Coalitions: the process of developing and implementing a coalition is complex. Implementation: "community-level interventions are complex and difficult interventions to implement, whether they are community trials or community coalitions".

### A proposed improvement

The authors argue that prevention science "needs much further development" and that "a gap exists between science and practice in prevention". Their remedy is Getting To Outcomes, described as "a results-based accountability approach to help practitioners plan, implement, and evaluate their interventions to achieve results", built around answering 10 accountability questions. The authors hypothesize that some community-driven interventions fail to demonstrate better outcomes because they do not systematically address these accountability questions. All of this is a 2003 review's assessment of its literature, not a claim about the current state of the field.
