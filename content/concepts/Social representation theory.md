Social representation theory is Serge Moscovici's account of how a community builds the shared common sense it thinks and communicates with. Moscovici, a Romanian-born French social psychologist, coined the term in 1961 in his study of how psychoanalysis was received and taken up by different French publics. A social representation, in his definition:

> "system of values, ideas and practices with a twofold function; first, to establish an order which will enable individuals to orient themselves in their material and social world and to master it; and secondly to enable communication to take place among the members of a community by providing them with a code for social exchange and a code for naming and classifying unambiguously the various aspects of their world and their individual and group history" (Moscovici, quoted in [Wikipedia, Social representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_representation))

### Anchoring and objectification

The theory names two processes by which the unfamiliar is made familiar. Anchoring gives new things meaning by fitting them into what a community already knows:

> "Anchoring involves the ascribing of meaning to new phenomena – objects, relations, experiences, practices, etc. – by means of integrating it into existing worldviews, so it can be interpreted and compared to the 'already known'." ([same article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_representation))

Objectification is the second move, in which "something abstract is turned into something almost concrete" — an idea hardens into an image, a word, a thing people can point at.

### How the theory explains

The theory is an account of how knowledge moves between science and everyday life. Moscovici distinguished the reified universe of science, which produces knowledge by rule and procedure, from the consensual universe in which lay publics elaborate and circulate their own versions of what they receive. His founding study traced how three segments of 1950s France — urban-liberal, Catholic and communist milieus — each took up psychoanalytic ideas differently, with different communication styles and different resulting pictures of what psychoanalysis was. The Durkheim lineage is explicit: Moscovici built on Émile Durkheim's collective representations, adapting it to modern societies where the contents change faster.

An everyday-life case: when a new word such as "artificial intelligence" enters ordinary talk, a community anchors it to older ideas (assistants, robots, minds) and objectifies it into fixed images; those images then do quiet work whenever the subject comes up. This is an illustrative example, not one of Moscovici's cases.

### Status

The theory remains popular mainly among European social psychologists, and is less known in the United States, partly because much of Moscovici's original work was published in French. It is not a settled doctrine; it attracts ongoing debate.
