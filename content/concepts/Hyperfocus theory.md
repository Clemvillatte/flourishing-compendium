Hyperfocus is not a settled theory. Ashinoff and Abu-Akel describe it as "a phenomenon that reflects one's complete absorption in a task, to a point where a person appears to completely ignore or 'tune out' everything else", but add that it "is poorly defined within the literature" and that "there is no single consensus to what constitutes hyperfocus" ([Ashinoff and Abu-Akel, Psychological Research, 2021, abstract](https://cris.iucc.ac.il/en/publications/hyperfocus-the-forgotten-frontier-of-attention/)). The seed note's idea of absorption turning into lock-in matches how clinicians describe it, but the evidence is thinner than the name suggests.

### Operational definition

Their review proposes four testable features: hyperfocus is induced by task engagement; it involves "an intense state of sustained or selective attention"; there is "diminished perception of non-task relevant stimuli"; and task performance improves ([Ashinoff and Abu-Akel, open-access PDF](https://pure-oai.bham.ac.uk/ws/files/81055948/Ashinoff_Abu_Akel_2019_Hyperfocus_the_forgotten_frontier_of_attention_Psychological_Research.pdf)). They note that the term is "most often mentioned in the context of autism, schizophrenia, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder" but that research into its effects on cognition and neural functioning "is limited".

### The lock-in side

The clinical origin of the term emphasises difficulty switching. The review quotes a definition of hyperfocusing as "'locking on' to a task in patients with ADHD who have a difficulty of shifting their attention from one subject to another, especially if the subject is about their interests" (Conner, 1994, quoted there). Hupfeld, Abagis and Shah's working definition, as quoted in a student thesis, lists "timelessness, failure to attend to the world, ignoring personal needs, difficulty stopping and switching tasks, feelings of total engrossment in the task, and feeling 'stuck' on small details" ([University of Richmond honors thesis](https://scholarship.richmond.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2611&context=honors-theses)). That is the narrowing the seed describes.

### The strength side

A scoping review of ADHD-related strengths reports that definitions of hyperfocus range "from the more positive concept of flow to 'being in the zone,' which was seen as less uniformly positive by participants". It describes hyperfocus as "associated with ADHD but not included in diagnostic criteria". Studies in that review "identified hyperfocus as a strength associated with ADHD, citing its usefulness when harnessed for valued tasks relevant to the person's interests" ([ADHD-related strengths in adults: a scoping review, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13538910/)).

### Is it specific to ADHD?

Not clearly. A study of adults with and without ADHD found that patients and matched controls "did not differ in the occurrence, frequency, duration and pervasiveness of hyperfocus", and concluded that "hyperfocus experiences are not specific of ADHD patients". The authors suggest that "divergent findings might reflect multiple hyperfocus dimensions" ([Testing the relation between ADHD and hyperfocus experiences, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33126147/)). Earlier work by Hupfeld and colleagues had found ADHD participants reported it more often than controls (as reported in the PubMed abstract and the Ashinoff review).

### For the essays

Writers can use the term for a described experience, with a plain note that definitions vary and that its link to ADHD is disputed.
