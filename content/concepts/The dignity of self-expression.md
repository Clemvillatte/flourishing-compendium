“The dignity of self-expression” — Kant, Groundwork.

### What it is

The compendium uses this title for an application of Kant's Groundwork: the claim that saying what one needs and where one stands treats the speaker as the kind of being Kant says exists "as an end in himself, not merely as a means to be arbitrarily used by this or that will" (Kant, Groundwork, trans. Abbott, [Gutenberg #5682](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-0.txt)). The title is the compendium's; the grounding is Kant's distinction between value and dignity: "Whatever has a value can be replaced by something else which is equivalent; whatever, on the other hand, is above all value, and therefore admits of no equivalent, has a dignity" (Kant, Groundwork, trans. Abbott, [Gutenberg #5682](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-0.txt)).

### What it carries

On Kant's account, what has dignity cannot be traded off against convenience or another's comfort. The Groundwork locates that dignity in rational nature itself: "Thus morality, and humanity as capable of it, is that which alone has dignity" (Kant, Groundwork, trans. Abbott, [Gutenberg #5682](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-0.txt)). Applied to speech, the thought is that a person who consistently swallows her own needs in order to keep the peace is not being modest; she is pricing herself, treating her wishes as items that can be dropped whenever someone else's preferences bid higher. Expressing a need does not guarantee it will be met, and it does not license demanding that others obey. It is the refusal to pre-emptively strike one's own claim from the negotiation, which is what treating oneself as an end requires.

The assertiveness principle BE052 is the practical form of this: state the need, state the boundary, let the other person respond as an end in themselves too. The dignity is in the declaring, not in the winning.
