“The dignity of self-expression” — Kant, Groundwork.

### What it is

I use this title for an application of Kant's Groundwork: the claim that saying what one needs and where one stands treats the speaker as the kind of being Kant says exists "as an end in himself, not merely as a means to be arbitrarily used by this or that will" (Kant, Groundwork, second section, trans. Abbott, [Gutenberg #5682](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-0.txt)). The title is my; the grounding is Kant's distinction between value and dignity: "Whatever has a value can be replaced by something else which is equivalent; whatever, on the other hand, is above all value, and therefore admits of no equivalent, has a dignity" (Kant, Groundwork, second section, trans. Abbott, [Gutenberg #5682](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-0.txt)).

### What it carries

On Kant's account, what has dignity cannot be traded off against convenience or another's comfort. The Groundwork locates that dignity in rational nature itself: "Thus morality, and humanity as capable of it, is that which alone has dignity" (Kant, Groundwork, second section, trans. Abbott, [Gutenberg #5682](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-0.txt)). I connect this with BE052's practice of expressing needs and boundaries directly, without demanding that others comply. The connection is an application rather than something the quoted passages conclude: what Kant's text establishes is the incomparable worth of rational nature, and I draw from that the narrower lesson that a person's own claims need not always yield to another's comfort.
