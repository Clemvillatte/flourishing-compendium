“The jar of the Danaids” — Greek myth, as literary parallel.

### What it is

In the myth, the Danaids, the daughters of Danaus, killed their husbands on their wedding night, and "were then condemned to spend eternity carrying water in a sieve or perforated jug" ([Wikipedia, "Danaïdes"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dana%C3%AFdes)). Ancient Apulian underworld vases show them atoning "in Hades by eternally pouring water into a leaky vessel" ([Getty Museum didactic](https://www.getty.edu/art/exhibitions/ancient_underworld/downloads/underworld_krater_didactic.pdf)). The image the compendium anchors to it is Plato's, in the Gorgias: "the place in the souls of the uninitiated in which the desires are seated, being the intemperate and incontinent part, he compared to a vessel full of holes, because it can never be satisfied" (Plato, Gorgias 493b, trans. Jowett, [Gutenberg #1672](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1672/1672-0.txt)). Plato's passage does not name the Danaids; the association of the leaking jar with them belongs to the later tradition ([The History of Art](https://www.thehistoryofart.org/john-william-waterhouse/danaides/)).

### What it carries

The allegory: "they pour water into a vessel which is full of holes out of a colander which is similarly perforated. The colander, as my informer assures me, is the soul, and the soul which he compares to a colander is the soul of the ignorant, which is likewise full of holes, and therefore incontinent, owing to a bad memory and want of faith" (Plato, Gorgias 493b-c, trans. Jowett, [Gutenberg #1672](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1672/1672-0.txt)). Socrates presses the image against Callicles' life of maximum gratification: "the more you pour in, the greater the waste; and the holes must be large for the liquid to escape" (Plato, Gorgias 494a, trans. Jowett, [Gutenberg #1672](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1672/1672-0.txt)). And the life so depicted "is not that of a dead man, or of a stone, but of a cormorant; you mean that he is to be hungering and eating" (Plato, Gorgias 494a, trans. Jowett, [Gutenberg #1672](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1672/1672-0.txt)).

The compendium connects this image with SP025, which reads greed, luxury and drunkenness as species of madness: the leak is in the vessel, not the supply, so no increase of supply can end the labor. A compendium exercise drawn from the image is to ask, before the next pour, what the wanting is for.
