“The jar of the Danaids” — Greek myth, as literary parallel.

### What it is

The seed anchors the title in the Greek myth of the Danaids, the daughters of Danaus whose underworld punishment was endless, futile water-carrying. The philosophical use the compendium leans on is Plato's, in the Gorgias, where Socrates quotes a Sicilian allegory of the leaky soul: "the place in the souls of the uninitiated in which the desires are seated, being the intemperate and incontinent part, he compared to a vessel full of holes, because it can never be satisfied" (Plato, Gorgias 493a-b, trans. Jowett, [Gutenberg #1672](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1672/1672-0.txt)).

### What it carries

The allegory makes the punishment fit the crime precisely: "they pour water into a vessel which is full of holes out of a colander which is similarly perforated. The colander, as my informer assures me, is the soul, and the soul which he compares to a colander is the soul of the ignorant, which is likewise full of holes, and therefore incontinent, owing to a bad memory and want of faith" (Plato, Gorgias 493b, trans. Jowett, [Gutenberg #1672](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1672/1672-0.txt)). Socrates presses the image against Callicles' life of maximum gratification: "the more you pour in, the greater the waste; and the holes must be large for the liquid to escape" (Plato, Gorgias 494a, trans. Jowett, [Gutenberg #1672](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1672/1672-0.txt)). The cormorant's life, thirsting and drinking without end, is the Danaids' jar on earth.

The SP025 principle reads greed, luxury and drunkenness as species of madness, and the jar is its diagram: the leak is not in the supply but in the vessel, so no increase of supply can end the labor. The practice the image suggests is repair, not refilling, asking what the wanting is for rather than reaching again for the pitcher.
