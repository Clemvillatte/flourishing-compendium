Parasympathetic activation is the engagement of the parasympathetic branch of the autonomic nervous system, the vagal half that slows the heart, settles arousal and supports digestion and recovery. In contemporary research the state is tracked indirectly, through heart rate variability (HRV) and respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA), the speeding and slowing of the heart that follows the breath.

### The breath as the lever

The most reliable voluntary route is breathing. A systematic review of slow-breathing techniques concluded:

> “Slow breathing techniques seem to promote a predominance of the parasympathetic autonomic system with respect to the sympathetic one, mediated by the vagal activity (Streeter et al., 2012; Brown et al., 2013).” ([Zaccaro et al., 2018, Front Hum Neurosci 12:353](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6137615/))

The same review links slow breathing to rising HRV and RSA, with the modulation depending on respiration frequency: variability increases as the breath slows.

### Biofeedback and the baroreflex

Heart-rate-variability biofeedback (HRVB) trains people to breathe slowly while watching their heart-rate oscillations, aiming to maximise RSA. The effect on the oscillations is immediate:

> “During HRV biofeedback, the amplitude of heart rate oscillations grows to many times the amplitude at rest, while the pattern becomes simple and sinusoidal.” ([Lehrer and Gevirtz, 2014, Front Psychol 5:756](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4104929/))

Lehrer and Gevirtz attribute the method's clinical effects chiefly to the baroreflex: “The most supported possible mechanism is the strengthening of homeostasis in the baroreceptor.” On this account the activation is trainable reflex activity: each slow breath stimulates the baroreflex, which operates more strongly during the practice.
