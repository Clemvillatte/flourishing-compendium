Enchiridion 5 in Elizabeth Carter's translation reads: "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things. Death, for instance, is not terrible, else it would have appeared so to Socrates. But the terror consists in our notion of death that it is terrible. When therefore we are hindered, or disturbed, or grieved, let us never attribute it to others, but to ourselves; that is, to our own principles" ([Epictetus, The Enchiridion, section 5, Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html)). The passage ties disturbance to the judgment we hold about an event. It does not use the word passion or the technical Stoic vocabulary, which comes from the wider school.

### The school's doctrine

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says that "each passion can be described as an opinion or judgment or weak supposition, whose content includes two elements, both of which (in the standard case) are false: (i) an ascription of value to an object that is present or in prospect and (ii) an assessment that a particular course of action is appropriate to perform in response" ([Stoicism, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/)). The error is about value: the passionate agent "mistakes something he currently possesses or expects to possess as either good or bad, when really it is indifferent". The four main types are distress (a present perceived bad), fear (a future perceived bad), appetite (a future perceived good) and delight (a present perceived good).

### Chrysippus and the word false

Diogenes Laertius reports: "They say that the passions are judgments, according to Chrysippus in his book On Passions". A paper on Stoic psychopathology argues that the Stoics "do not, in fact, define passions as false judgments" and that "where they are explicit, they characterize the judgment as weak, not false"; it concludes that a passion is "a weak, fresh judgment that something good or bad for me is present or potential" ([Stoic Psychopathology, PhilArchive PDF](https://philarchive.org/archive/BROSP)). The Stanford entry, by contrast, says both elements are false in standard cases, so scholars differ on how to word the claim.

### Not an absence of feeling

The Stoics did not aim at an unfeeling sage. The Stanford entry describes "good feelings" (eupatheiai) as emotional responses to genuine goods, and pre-emotions (propatheiai) as reactions "without the intervention of assent". The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy says "a better reading of Stoicism is that the goal is not absence of emotion, but a well-disposed emotional life" ([Stoic Philosophy of Mind, IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/stoicmind/)).
