Hans Eysenck's three-factor model, often called PEN, describes personality along three broad dimensions: psychoticism (P), extraversion (E) and neuroticism (N). A methods paper on the Eysenck Personality Questionnaire-Revised sums up his view this way:

> "the structure of personality may be effectively described by three main traits: psychoticism (P), extraversion (E), and neuroticism (N)." (Colledani, Anselmi and Robusto, [Frontiers in Psychology, 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6190847/))

The same paper calls these dimensions "basic, independent, and biologically founded traits", and says that "PEN traits do not represent pathological dimensions in themselves".

### The two earlier dimensions

Eysenck first proposed two major dimensions, extraversion and neuroticism (Eysenck, 1947), and later added psychoticism (Eysenck and Eysenck, 1968; Eysenck, 1970), according to a [textbook overview on ScienceDirect Topics](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/social-sciences/eysenck). In the same overview, extraversion is tied to arousability: to the extent that a person's ascending reticular activating system (ARAS) "admits little stimulation, one feels underaroused and thus seeks stimulation, producing an extraverted personality".

### The biological claims

The overview links neuroticism to the limbic system and its handling of stress. For psychoticism, it reports that Eysenck "suggested that persons high in Psychoticism tended to have high levels of the hormone called testosterone" and low levels of monoamine oxidase.

A paper in Personality Neuroscience describes Eysenck (1967) as "one of the first investigators to explore and develop a neurobiological model for personality". It adds that the arousal theory generated studies showing "moderate support at most" for the extraversion-arousal link, "suggesting that this relationship is not as simple as originally proposed by Eysenck" ([Leshem and colleagues, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7219696/)).

### A caution on the biology

The Personality Neuroscience paper's wording means the arousal account of extraversion should be read as a hypothesis with partial support, not a settled mechanism.
