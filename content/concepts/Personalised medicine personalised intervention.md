Personalised or precision medicine "is predicated on the assumption that the average response to treatment is not necessarily representative of the response of each individual". The same paper contrasts the approaches: "evidence-based medicine approaches focus on average data to define one-size-fits-all treatments, whereas personalized or precision medicine aims to obtain customized treatments for each specific individual or group" ([Mental disorders of known aetiology and precision medicine in psychiatry, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5182164/)).

### Personalised versus precision

Terms vary. A psychiatry review says the two "are often used synonymously" but describe different approaches: personalised medicine considers a patient's sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, while precision medicine uses objective measures from genetics, blood biomarkers, imaging or neuropsychological tests to choose treatment ([Precision pharmacotherapy: psychiatry's future direction, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6250105/)). It adds that some personalisation has long existed, since physicians considered age, comorbidities, symptoms and preferences, but that this depends on the clinician's experience, with a degree of trial and error. A Cambridge paper holds that "personalised medicine" is the broader term and that precision psychiatry places patients "into biologically or clinically meaningful subgroups" ([Precision medicine in mental health, Cambridge, PDF](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/D6B9178E543D2CD1B189968ECC56E181/S092493382612210Xa.pdf/div-class-title-precision-medicine-in-mental-health-applications-challenges-and-recommendations-div.pdf)).

### Why individual variation matters

The Cambridge paper says patients with the same diagnosis "often show highly variable responses to the same intervention". A neuropsychiatry review states: "What we need now are medications for targeted subgroups of patients within diagnostic categories who share biology, not just symptoms" ([Pharmacogenomics and personalized medicine in neuropsychiatry, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3407812/)). Tailoring is not only genetic. The Psychological Medicine paper separates precision psychiatry, which builds models to inform patients about expected outcomes, from personalised psychiatry, which aims to tailor interventions to specific individuals ([Precision psychiatry: predicting predictability, Cambridge](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/precision-psychiatry-predicting-predictability/7A51DBDF832F47502DE677D0FE0A9BE6)).

### What is not yet shown

A psychiatric genetics paper says that "for the most part, treatment selection in psychiatric practice remains a trial-and-error proposition", and that two GWAS meta-analyses of antidepressant response (N > 2200) found no genome-wide significant associations, which suggests response is highly polygenic ([Psychiatric Genetics and the Future of Personalized Treatment, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4632650/)). The seed's claim that "monitoring and adapting is more effective than one-size-fits-all" rests on Cheyne and Poulsen (2019) and Ferrer et al. (2020). I could not open either, and nothing I opened measures the effectiveness of monitoring and adapting, so treat that claim as unverified. For essays, the safer statement is the one the sources support: responses vary within a diagnosis, and matching treatments to individuals is an aim whose predictors are still being developed.
