Hormesis is a biological concept. Mattson's 2008 paper defines it and reviews the cellular mechanisms. Applying it to psychological or lifestyle stress ("voluntary discomfort") is a step the compendium takes beyond that paper, and the sources below treat that step separately and cautiously.

### The definition

> "In the fields of biology and medicine hormesis is defined as an adaptive response of cells and organisms to a moderate (usually intermittent) stress." (Mattson 2008, Ageing Research Reviews 7:1-7, [PubMed 18162444](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18162444/))

Mattson notes that toxicologists use the term for "a biphasic dose-response to an environmental agent characterized by a low dose stimulation or beneficial effect and a high dose inhibitory or toxic effect". His examples include ischemic preconditioning, exercise, dietary energy restriction and low doses of certain phytochemicals. In the cells, the response involves kinases, deacetylases and transcription factors such as Nrf-2 and NF-kappaB, and increases production of cytoprotective proteins such as growth factors, antioxidant enzymes and protein chaperones. The two features that matter for the seed are "moderate" and "usually intermittent": the benefit depends on the dose and its pattern.

### A psychological parallel

A nationally representative longitudinal study asked whether adversity history relates to later mental health. It found U-shaped relationships.

> "People with a history of some lifetime adversity reported better mental health and well-being outcomes than not only people with a high history of adversity but also than people with no history of adversity." (Seery, Holman & Silver 2010, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 99:1025-1041, [PubMed 20939649](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20939649/))

People with some adversity were also the least affected by recent adverse events. This is observational evidence about life events that happened to people, not about stressors chosen as a practice, so it cannot show that deliberately seeking discomfort works.

### What this page does not claim

Mattson's paper is about cells and organisms and does not test cold exposure, fasting regimes or psychological challenges for wellbeing in humans. The useful range of a stressor is narrow and varies between people, and a dose that is too high is harmful by the definition itself. Treat the compendium's "hormetic exposure" practices as a hypothesis from this mechanism, not an established result.
