Adaptation research is the longitudinal literature on whether people return to baseline happiness after life events. The field was long dominated by the set-point model, which held that people adapt to almost anything and that happiness fluctuates around a biologically determined set point. Richard Lucas's 2007 review is the standard citation for the revision of that view, and its abstract carries the correction in three sentences: "Although inborn factors certainly matter and some adaptation does occur, events such as divorce, death of a spouse, unemployment, and disability are associated with lasting changes in SWB. These recent studies also show that there are considerable individual differences in the extent to which people adapt. Thus, happiness levels do change, and adaptation is not inevitable" ([Lucas 2007, Current Directions in Psychological Science](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/current-directions/j.1467-8721.2007.00479.x/)).

### What the panel studies found

The evidence base is large-scale longitudinal panel data. Lucas's disability study followed participants for an average of seven years before and after onset: "Disability was associated with moderate to large drops in happiness (effect sizes ranged from 0.40 to 1.27 standard deviations), followed by little adaptation over time" ([Lucas 2007, JPSP, PDF](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/2007-lucas.pdf)). The general discussion states the conclusion directly: "The results from these studies show that happiness can change and that adaptation is not inevitable" ([same](https://gwern.net/doc/psychology/2007-lucas.pdf)).

The pattern is not uniform. Adaptation to positive events like marriage is well supported — satisfaction returns to pre-marriage levels within about a year — while widowhood, unemployment, and disability show lasting drops ([Lucas 2007 review](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/current-directions/j.1467-8721.2007.00479.x/); [Japanese-language summary of the field](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jsre/21/2/21_92/_pdf)).

### Why I hold it

The Buddhist principle of impermanence (anicca) is often read as a claim that all states pass. The research gives that reading a needed limit: some states do not fully pass, and treating suffering as always self-resolving is a factual error with consequences for how help is offered. The two claims are compatible — nothing is fixed, but not everything returns to where it was.
