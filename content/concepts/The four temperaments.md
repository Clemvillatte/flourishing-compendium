The four temperaments are the personality typology Galen built on the Hippocratic humours: four named dispositions, each tied to a bodily fluid and a pair of qualities. The National Library of Medicine's summary of the historical system:

> "He described four basic human temperaments: 'sanguine' disposition was hot/moist and associated with blood; ... 'melancholic' disposition was cold/dry and associated with black bile; and 'phlegmatic' disposition was moist/cold and associated with phlegm." (Galen, *De temperamentis*, as summarised by the [US National Library of Medicine](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/exhibition/shakespeare-and-the-four-humors/digitalgallery.html))

The fourth, choleric, is given in the same sentence as dry/hot and associated with yellow bile.

### What the system carries

Galen (ca. 131–ca. 201) wrote De temperamentis on the basis of the Hippocratic theory of the four humours, blood, phlegm, yellow bile and black bile, adding the qualities dry, moist, hot and cold; as the same source puts it, "In combination, a balance of these elements and temperaments would foster better health." The enduring part is not the physiology, which is false, but the typology's survival: sanguine, choleric, melancholic and phlegmatic stayed in European psychology for eighteen centuries, and the modern dimensions of personality research, including Cloninger's temperament-and-character model, still work the same ground with better instruments. I keep the concept for what it honestly is: the first systematic claim that moods and behaviours have stable constitutional patterns, and a caution that the pictures attached to them were guesses about mechanism. A compendium exercise drawn from the typology is to notice temperament-talk in oneself and others, and to separate the observed pattern from the assumed cause. What the doctrine does not give: any evidence that body fluids cause dispositions; the mapping is historical, not physiological.
