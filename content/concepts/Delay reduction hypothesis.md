The delay reduction hypothesis comes from animal-learning research on choice. In the words of a later study, Fantino's delay reduction theory "posits that stimuli that signal a reduction in the delay to reinforcement, such as the 5-s stimulus that occurred immediately prior to reinforcement, serve as strong conditioned reinforcers and should be preferred" ([Zentall, Case and Andrews, 2018, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29188447/)). Fantino's original paper is cited there as the Journal of the Experimental Analysis of Behavior, volume 12, 1969, pages 723-730.

### An example with pigeons

Zentall and colleagues gave pigeons a choice between two sequences that both ended in food after 20 seconds. One sequence ran 15 seconds and then 5 seconds, and the other ran 5 and then 15. The pigeons "procrastinated", preferring the 15-5 chain, and they "pecked most at the 5-s stimulus that led immediately to reinforcement." The authors conclude that the theory, "a theory that emphasizes the attraction to stimuli that predict reinforcement with a short delay, also may contribute to human procrastination behavior because when task completion comes just before the deadline, it may become a stronger conditioned reinforcer than if task completion comes earlier" ([abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29188447/)). The human link is a suggestion, because the experiment used pigeons.

### A boundary

Fantino and Romanowich (2007) review conditioned reinforcement and choice. They report that adding more conditioned reinforcers to a choice does not raise preference for it, and that "if anything, the opposite preference is likely to occur", because the extra signals "are not correlated with a reduction in time to primary reinforcement nor with an increase in value" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17575906/)). On this view the thing that matters is whether a signal shortens the wait to the real reward, and signals as such are not what draws choice.

### Scope
