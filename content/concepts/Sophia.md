Sophia (Greek: theoretical or philosophic wisdom) is, in Aristotle's catalogue of the virtues of thought, the intellectual virtue that knows the necessary and eternal. The original entry's definition stands, and Book VI of the Nicomachean Ethics gives it a precise shape: it is not one faculty but a union. "Therefore wisdom must be intuitive reason combined with scientific knowledge-scientific knowledge of the highest objects which has received as it were its proper completion." ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.7, tr. Ross](http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.6.vi.html))

### What sophia knows

The object of sophia is the highest by nature, not the most useful to us: "From what has been said it is plain, then, that philosophic wisdom is scientific knowledge, combined with intuitive reason, of the things that are highest by nature." Aristotle is blunt about the trade this involves: "This is why we say Anaxagoras, Thales, and men like them have philosophic but not practical wisdom, when we see them ignorant of what is to their own advantage, and why we say that they know things that are remarkable, admirable, difficult, and divine, but useless; viz. because it is not human goods that they seek." The original entry's remark that Spinoza's third kind of knowledge is sophia's heir is left as the original entry's framing; my use of the concept is the pairing: [[Phronesis|practical wisdom]] deliberates about what can be otherwise, sophia contemplates what cannot, and a flourishing life needs to know which mode a given moment calls for.
