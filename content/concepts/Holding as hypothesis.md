“Holding as hypothesis” — Peirce, "The Fixation of Belief".

Holding as hypothesis is the practice of treating a strongly held belief as a proposition to be tested rather than a possession to be defended. Its classical grounding is Peirce's 1877 paper, which supplies the machinery: belief is a habit of mind that settles into calm, doubt is the irritation that reopens inquiry, and the method of science is the only method of fixing belief that submits to something independent of what we happen to think. The time-boxed exercise in the compendium's kernel, treating the belief as a hypothesis for a defined period and noticing what changes when certainty is suspended, is the compendium's own operationalization of this machinery; the essay itself gives no such instruction.

### The theory of belief and doubt (Peirce)

Peirce's contrast between the two states:

> “Doubt is an uneasy and dissatisfied state from which we struggle to free ourselves and pass into the state of belief; while the latter is a calm and satisfactory state which we do not wish to avoid, or to change to a belief in anything else. On the contrary, we cling tenaciously, not merely to believing, but to believing just what we do believe.” (Peirce, [The Fixation of Belief, Popular Science Monthly 12 (1877): 1-15](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p107.html), the essay's opening discussion of belief and doubt)

The engine that connects them:

> “The irritation of doubt causes a struggle to attain a state of belief. I shall term this struggle inquiry... With the doubt, therefore, the struggle begins, and with the cessation of doubt it ends. Hence, the sole object of inquiry is the settlement of opinion.” (Peirce, [The Fixation of Belief, Popular Science Monthly 12 (1877): 1-15](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p107.html), the essay's opening discussion of belief and doubt)

Peirce adds a qualification the practice needs: doubt cannot be manufactured at will.

> “the mere putting of a proposition into the interrogative form does not stimulate the mind to any struggle after belief. There must be a real and living doubt, and without this all discussion is idle.” (same paper, section V)

The paper's diagnosis is that the calm of belief is available by cheaper methods than truth: tenacity (clinging to what one already thinks), authority (enforcing it), and the a priori method, whose propositions seemed "agreeable to reason," an expression Peirce glosses as meaning "that which we find ourselves inclined to believe" rather than anything that agrees with experience. The method of science is the one that accepts an external standard:

> “There are Real things, whose characters are entirely independent of our opinions about them; those Reals affect our senses according to regular laws, and, though our sensations are as different as are our relations to the objects, yet, by taking advantage of the laws of perception, we can ascertain by reasoning how things really and truly are; and any man, if he have sufficient experience and he reason enough about it, will be led to the one True conclusion.” (same paper, the presentation of the method of science)

The closing moral is the concept's warrant:

> “what is more wholesome than any particular belief is integrity of belief, and that to avoid looking into the support of any belief from a fear that it may turn out rotten is quite as immoral as it is disadvantageous.” (same paper, the closing paragraphs)
