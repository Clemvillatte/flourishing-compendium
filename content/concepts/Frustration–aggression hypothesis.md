The frustration-aggression hypothesis is one of the most seminal theories in aggression research. The Wiley Handbook of Violence and Aggression entry says it was first formulated in the late 1930s and that its basic assumption is still "that frustration, typically understood as an event instead of an emotion, increases the tendency to act or react aggressively" ([Breuer and Elson, Wiley Handbook of Violence and Aggression](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/9781119057574.whbva040)).

### The 1939 version

Dollard and colleagues built the theory on one core assumption: "Aggression is always a consequence of frustration". For them frustration was "an obstacle blocking the attainment of an expected gratification", and aggression was an action aimed at injuring the source of the frustration ([Klandermans, Wiley-Blackwell Encyclopedia of Social and Political Movements, entry "Frustration-aggression"](https://dn760107.eu.archive.org/0/items/scott19482/klandermans2013.pdf)).

### Berkowitz's reformulation

In 1989, Leonard Berkowitz reviewed the evidence. His abstract says the original formulation's main proposition is limited to "interference with an expected attainment of a desired goal on hostile (emotional) aggression", and that "Although some studies have yielded negative results, others support the core proposition". He found that "Frustrations can create aggressive inclinations even when they are not arbitrary or aimed at the subject personally", and proposed that "frustrations generate aggressive inclinations to the degree that they arouse negative affect" ([Berkowitz, 1989, PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2667009/)).

Breuer and Elson explain what changes. In the reformulated theory, "not the frustration but negative affect is the proximal cause of aggressive responses", and frustration becomes "a sufficient rather than a necessary criterion for aggression". The outcome is also reworded: Berkowitz names "aggressive inclinations" rather than aggression or aggressive behaviour, and these inclinations "are not behaviors but comprise both an affective and a cognitive component" ([Breuer and Elson, open-access chapter](https://www.ssoar.info/ssoar/bitstream/handle/document/61070/ssoar-2017-breuer_et_al-Frustration-Aggression_Theory.pdf?sequence=1)).

### Justified frustration

A 1995 test supported the reformulation. Even justified frustration "produced more hostile aggression than no frustration at all", though less than unjustified frustration ([Dill and Anderson, 1995, Aggressive Behavior](http://www.craiganderson.org/wp-content/uploads/caa/abstracts/1995-1999/95DA.2.html)).

### A limit

A later review notes that the hypothesis "has difficulty, for example, explaining some of the more instrumental forms of aggression (e.g., cutting in line)" ([Aversive events and aggression review](http://www.craiganderson.org/wp-content/uploads/caa/abstracts/2015-2019/17GA.pdf)).
