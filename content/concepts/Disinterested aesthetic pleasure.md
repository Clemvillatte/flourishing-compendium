Kant's term for the distinctive pleasure taken in beauty: a satisfaction that neither depends on a desire for the object nor produces one. In the Critique of the Power of Judgment the point is made through a thought experiment. Asked whether the palace before him is beautiful, a person may dislike showy buildings, prefer the cook-shops like the Iroquois sachem in Paris, or rebuke the vanity of the great like Rousseau; all of that is beside the question ([Kant, Critique of Judgement, section 2, Bernard translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/48433/48433-h/48433-h.html)):

> "We wish only to know if this mere representation of the object is accompanied in me with satisfaction, however indifferent I may be as regards the existence of the object of this representation." (Kant, [Critique of Judgement, section 2](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/48433/48433-h/48433-h.html))

The indifference requirement is strict:

> "Every one must admit that a judgement about beauty, in which the least interest mingles, is very partial and is not a pure judgement of taste." (Kant, [ibid.](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/48433/48433-h/48433-h.html))

### What the interest-free pleasure is contrasted with

The Stanford Encyclopedia's account of Kant's aesthetics places the disinterested pleasure between two other kinds of liking: "The pleasure, however, is of a distinctive kind: it is disinterested, which means that it does not depend on the subject's having a desire for the object, nor does it generate such a desire" ([Kant's Aesthetics and Teleology, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, section 2.1.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-aesthetics/)). The same entry explains that the disinterested character distinguishes judgments of beauty from judgments of the agreeable, such as liking a food or drink, and from judgments of the good ([ibid.](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kant-aesthetics/)).
