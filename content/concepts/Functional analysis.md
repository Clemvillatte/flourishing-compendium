Functional analysis asks what a behavior accomplishes for the person: which antecedent occasions set it off, and which consequences keep it coming back. Read behavior by its function rather than its form, and the next question becomes empirical — what, exactly, is this behavior doing? The method comes from applied behavior analysis, and its review article states its focus plainly:

> "Functional analysis methodology focuses on the identification of variables that influence the occurrence of problem behavior and has become a hallmark of contemporary approaches to behavioral assessment." (Hanley, Iwata and McCord, [Functional analysis of problem behavior: a review, Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis 36 (2003), pp. 147-185, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12858983/))

### How the analysis is run

The review's inclusion criteria show the method's discipline:

> "Studies included in the present review were those in which (a) a pretreatment assessment based on (b) direct observation and measurement of (c) problem behavior was conducted under (d) at least two conditions involving manipulation of an environmental variable in an attempt (e) to demonstrate a relation between the environmental event and behavior." (Hanley, Iwata and McCord, [2003, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12858983/))

The discipline those criteria impose is worth spelling out. A functional analysis does not ask what a behavior means; it arranges conditions and watches what changes. The manipulation is the method: the same behavior, observed under conditions that differ in one environmental variable at a time, until a relation shows itself. A single watched behavior tells you nothing about influence; a behavior watched under changed conditions can show it. The point is not to label the behavior but to find the lever that moves it.
