Righteousness (yì, 义) is the Confucian virtue of fitting response: doing what the situation demands of a person, where profit and convenience do not get a vote. Mencius gives it its strongest statement in the fish-and-bear's-paws passage:

> "So, I like life, and I also like righteousness. If I cannot keep the two together, I will let life go, and choose righteousness. I like life indeed, but there is that which I like more than life, and therefore, I will not seek to possess it by any improper ways. I dislike death indeed, but there is that which I dislike more than death, and therefore there are occasions when I will not avoid danger." (Mencius, Gaozi I, trans. James Legge, [Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/mengzi/gaozi-i/ens))

### Not saints only

Mencius is explicit that the capacity is not the sages' private property. Of the heart that will not take life by improper means or avoid danger at the price of righteousness, he says: "All men have it; what belongs to such men is simply that they do not lose it." Righteousness on this account is a native possession, kept or squandered.
