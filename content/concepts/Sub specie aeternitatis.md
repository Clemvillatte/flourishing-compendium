"Under the aspect of eternity" — Spinoza's Latin phrase for the mind's highest perspective: understanding things as necessary, as contained in God and following from the necessity of the divine nature, rather than as contingent objects of hope and fear. The phrase itself appears at Ethics II, Prop. XLIV, Corollary II, where it is reason that perceives things this way; the Fifth Part ties the same perception to the mind's highest knowledge.

> "It is in the nature of reason to perceive things under a certain form of eternity (sub quâdam æternitatis specie)." (Ethics II, Prop. XLIV, Corollary II, Elwes translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3800/pg3800.txt))

The note to Ethics V, Prop. XXIX draws the contrast the phrase turns on:

> "Things are conceived by us as actual in two ways; either as existing in relation to a given time and place, or as contained in God and following from the necessity of the divine nature." (Ethics V, Prop. XXIX, Note, same translation and source)

Whatsoever is conceived in the second way, Spinoza continues, is conceived under the form of eternity. The next proposition states the payoff for the mind: "Our mind, in so far as it knows itself and the body under the form of eternity, has to that extent necessarily a knowledge of God, and knows that it is in God, and is conceived through God." This is why the phrase belongs to the third kind of knowledge and grounds [[Blessedness]]: to see a thing sub specie aeternitatis is to see it as it follows from necessity, and the seeing itself is the mind's eternity.
