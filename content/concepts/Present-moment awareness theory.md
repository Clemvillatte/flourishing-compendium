**Present-moment awareness theory** — contact with the current moment, rather than the remembered past or imagined future, underpins wellbeing.

## Field

Attention · Psychological science

The theory's most direct empirical support comes from a smartphone experience-sampling study by Killingsworth and Gilbert, which sampled people at random moments to ask what they were doing, thinking and feeling. What they found: people are thinking about what is not happening almost as often as about what is, and doing so typically makes them unhappy.

> "We developed a smartphone technology to sample people's ongoing thoughts, feelings, and actions and found (i) that people are thinking about what is not happening almost as often as they are thinking about what is and (ii) found that doing so typically makes them unhappy." (Killingsworth & Gilbert, 2010, Science 330, 932, [abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21071660/))

The acceptance side of the theory has experimental support in a dismantling study of mindfulness training. In two randomized controlled trials comparing Monitor+Accept training to Monitor Only and control conditions, only the Monitor+Accept group increased happiness:

> "There was a significant advantage of MA over MO in increasing happiness (χ²(1)=4.08, p=.043, g=.39)..."
> — [Lindsay & Creswell, "How mindfulness training promotes positive emotions", PMC6296247](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6296247/)

The same study's conclusion names the mechanism this page's theory claims:

> "These studies provide the first experimental evidence that developing an orientation of acceptance toward present-moment experiences is a central mechanism of mindfulness interventions for boosting positive emotions in daily life."
> — [same source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6296247/)

The theory's scope is honest about what the evidence does and does not show. The Killingsworth and Gilbert finding is correlational (the study sampled what people were doing and feeling, not whether training attention to the present improves wellbeing); the Lindsay and Creswell trials test acceptance training inside mindfulness packages, not present-moment attention on its own. The theory is best read as a convergent account: several lines of evidence point the same direction without any single one isolating the mechanism.
