A plan designed from the outset to be revised as the future unfolds, rather than a fixed schedule of actions followed until it fails.

> "Adaptive planning means that plans are designed from the outset to be adapted over time in response to how the future may actually unfold." (Marchau et al., [Supporting DMDU: A Taxonomy of Approaches and Tools](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-05252-2_15), in *Decision Making under Deep Uncertainty*, Springer, 2019, ch. 15)

### What it holds

The adaptation is announced together with the plan itself. The designers say in advance what signals they will watch, which actions suit which conditions, and when they will switch, so the plan carries its own instructions for revising itself. The timing of a change is deliberately left open: "the timing of plan adaptation is not known a priori; it depends on how the future unfolds" ([Marchau et al. 2019](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-030-05252-2_15)). A scheduled review every five years is not the same thing, because changes there happen at predetermined moments regardless of conditions.

The approach answers a specific problem: deep uncertainty, where no probability distribution over futures is available and predicting-and-optimising breaks down. Faced with that, one designs a short-term action that is robust now, keeps long-term commitments reversible, and specifies triggers based on observed conditions rather than dates for the next move.

### Where the contrast lives

The claim is not that plans are useless. A specific plan often helps: in the lab, participants with a specific plan acted on a goal more often than those holding a broad intention when time was ample. The same specific plan became a liability when recognising and seizing an alternative opportunity was essential, because "participants failed to capitalize on an alternative opportunity for achieving the goal" ([Masicampo and Baumeister, 2012](https://users.wfu.edu/masicaej/MasicampoBaumeister2012SocialCognition.pdf), abstract). The value of a plan depends on how costly it is to change mid-course. Where changing course is cheap, hold plans loosely. Where it is expensive, buy the flexibility in advance, in options, slack or staged commitments, rather than discover the need later.

### How it explains

Mechanistically, an adaptive plan is a commitment device with an escape clause: it protects attention from constant re-deciding, and it names the observation that releases the protection. Its nearest neighbour in cognitive science is Gary Klein's Flexecution, the finding that expert practitioners rarely execute a pre-made plan lock-step; they "clarify and adapt their goals while pursuing them," with re-planning as the normal case rather than the exception ([Klein, cited in Macrocognition review](https://cdn.aaai.org/ocs/5724/5724-24573-1-PB.pdf)). The adaptive-planning literature generalises that observation from the individual practitioner to the multi-year plan.

### How it relates to flourishing

For the writer, the distinction is a piece of wisdom infrastructure. Most suffering around plans comes from treating them as promises. A plan you intend to keep regardless of evidence is an identity commitment; one you intend to revise is an instrument. Naming the revision conditions in advance removes the shame of changing one's mind later, and turns noticing you were wrong into an ordinary act of the plan working rather than a failure of character. Suffering arrives via rigidity. Wisdom here is the capacity to distinguish a plan's core from its contingent parts, and to mourn the latter lightly.

### Practice

Implement as three layers. First, name the commitment: what part of the plan stays fixed. Second, name the trigger: the observable condition that calls for revision. Third, name the revision: the alternative you would switch to. Without the trigger, adaptive remains an aspiration; without the revision, the trigger fires into a vacuum.
