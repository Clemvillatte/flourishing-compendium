A plan designed from the outset to be revised as the future unfolds, rather than a fixed schedule of actions followed until it fails.

> "Adaptive planning means that plans are designed from the outset to be adapted over time in response to how the future may actually unfold." (Kwakkel and Haasnoot, ch. 15 in Marchau et al., eds., *Decision Making under Deep Uncertainty*, Springer, 2019, p. 358; [TU Delft repository PDF](https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_b12e7e51-2d7b-4741-9508-c58dcda02c4a))

### What it holds

The adaptation is announced together with the plan itself. The designers say in advance what signals they will watch, which actions suit which futures, and when to switch, so the plan carries its own instructions for revising itself. The chapter states this directly: "The way a plan is designed to adapt in the face of potential changes in conditions is announced simultaneously with the plan itself rather than taking place in an ad hoc manner post facto" (Kwakkel and Haasnoot 2019, p. 358).

The timing of a change is deliberately left open. "The timing of plan adaptation is not known a priori; it depends on how the future unfolds," and "In this sense, adaptive planning differs from planned adaptation (see Chapter 13), where changes generally occur at predetermined moments (e.g., every 5 years) and which entails a review of conditions that results in an adaptation of the original plan" (Kwakkel and Haasnoot 2019, p. 359).

### Where the contrast lives

A specific plan is not automatically bad. In one experiment, participants with a specific plan achieved the goal more often than those holding a broad intention when time was unlimited. The same specific plan impaired attainment under insufficient time, when recognising and seizing an alternative opportunity was essential, because "participants failed to capitalize on an alternative opportunity for achieving the goal" (Masicampo and Baumeister 2012, abstract; [author PDF](https://users.wfu.edu/masicaej/MasicampoBaumeister2012SocialCognition.pdf)).

### Practice

The design has three layers: a commitment (what stays fixed), a trigger (the observable condition that calls for revision) and a revision (the alternative arrangement). An illustrative application: a writer who plans to write before work can name repeated interruptions as the trigger for reviewing that arrangement, try a lunchtime or evening slot when it fires, compare how much uninterrupted writing each allows, and keep the workable arrangement until conditions change again. This is an illustrative application, not a tested intervention.
