context dominates in real moral choice (Doris, Lack of Character).

Doris's book argues against what he calls globalism — the idea that people possess robust character traits that hold steady across situations. Against it he sets situationism, which the review summarizes in these terms:

> "Situationism holds that behavioral differences are due less to individual dispositional differences than to situational ones; that 'to a surprising extent,' people behave similarly in similar situations; that people 'typically' behave without the consistency required for trait attributions." (review of Lack of Character, Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews, 2003)

The studies the book draws on, as the review recounts them, are the classic helping experiments. In one, 87% of subjects who found a dime in a phone booth helped a confederate who dropped her papers, against 4% of those who did not find a dime. In another, the presence of two passive confederates reduced appropriately responsive behavior from 70% to 7%. In a third, seminary students hurrying to an appointment helped a seemingly distressed person 10% of the time when in a high hurry, against 63% when in a low hurry.

The book's practical lesson, as the reviewer reads it, is that ethical attention should focus on situations: arranging circumstances so that one is not influenced to behave badly, rather than expecting a global transformation of character to hold under any pressure.
