context dominates in real moral choice (Doris, Lack of Character).

Doris's book argues against what he calls globalism — the idea that people possess robust character traits that hold steady across situations. Against it he sets situationism: behavioral differences are due less to individual dispositional differences than to situational ones. The studies the book draws on are the classic helping experiments. In one, 87% of subjects who found a dime in a phone booth helped a confederate who dropped her papers, against 4% of those who did not find a dime. In another, the presence of two passive confederates reduced appropriately responsive behavior from 70% to 7%. In a third, seminary students hurrying to an appointment helped a seemingly distressed person 10% of the time when in a high hurry, against 63% when in a low hurry.

> "behavior is – contra the old saw about character and destiny – extraordinarily sensitive to variation in circumstance." (Doris, Lack of Character, ch. 1)

The book's practical lesson, as reviewers read it, is that ethical attention should focus on situations: arranging circumstances so that one is not influenced to behave badly, rather than expecting a global transformation of character to hold under any pressure.
