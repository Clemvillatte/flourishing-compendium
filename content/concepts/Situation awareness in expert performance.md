Situation awareness in expert performance is the study of what experts perceive in an unfolding situation that novices miss, and how. The situation-awareness half comes from Endsley's model, defined as "the perception of the elements in the environment considering time and space, the understanding of their meaning, and the prediction of their status in the near future" ([Wikipedia, Situation awareness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Situation_awareness), citing Endsley 1995). Its formal definition is three ascending levels: "Perception of the elements in the environment, Comprehension or understanding of the situation, and Projection of future status" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Situation_awareness)). The expert-performance half comes from Gary Klein, whose 2009 book Streetlights and Shadows argues that the skills involved are mostly tacit.

### What the expert sees

Klein's own summary of the skill: "Unlike explicit knowledge of names, facts and rules, tacit knowledge is being able to do things without being able to explain how. With experience, we learn to see things that others don't notice. Tacit knowledge includes the ability to recognize typical and unusual situations based on one's experience" ([Klein, interviewed by Science News, 2010](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/jumping-conclusions-can-make-good-decisions)). Perception here is not passive reception. "Good decision makers then construct a mental story to understand what's going on" ([Klein, Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/jumping-conclusions-can-make-good-decisions)), a story that directs what to look at next.

Klein also marks the difference between real experts and people who merely look like experts: "One way to tell real experts from people who look like experts is to ask them about the last mistake that they made. The expert is still chewing over that last mistake and asking 'What should I have been watching for?'" ([Klein, Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/jumping-conclusions-can-make-good-decisions)). The expertise includes the error-correction loop.

### The dots come first

The page's sharpest Klein line pushes against the picture of decision making as data processing: "I hate the notion of the mind as an assembly line where data get refined and processed until we can 'connect the dots.' That trivializes the expertise of people, from firefighters to intensive-care unit nurses, who know how to identify the dots in the first place so that they can make sense of a situation" ([Klein, Science News](https://www.sciencenews.org/article/jumping-conclusions-can-make-good-decisions)). Situation awareness is the dot-identifying skill, upstream of any analysis.
