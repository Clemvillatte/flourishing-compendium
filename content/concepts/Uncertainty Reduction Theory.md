**Uncertainty Reduction Theory** — Berger, Calabrese — communication aims to reduce uncertainty about others, using passive, active and interactive strategies.

Charles Berger and Richard Calabrese published the theory in 1975 to explain the communication process of initial interaction: when strangers meet, they proactively seek to reduce the uncertainty between them, and the interaction itself is the instrument. The theory is built as a quasi-formal system of seven axioms and twenty-one derived theorems linking uncertainty to verbal output, nonverbal warmth, information seeking, intimacy of disclosure, reciprocity, similarity and liking. The first axiom runs:

> "Given the high level of uncertainty present at the onset of the entry phase, as the amount of verbal communication between strangers increases, the level of uncertainty for each interactant in the relationship will decrease. As uncertainty is further reduced, the amount of verbal communication will increase." (Berger & Calabrese 1975, pp. 101–102)

The axioms give the theory its practical shape: uncertainty drives information seeking and question-asking; similarities between persons reduce uncertainty while dissimilarities raise it; and increases in uncertainty level produce decreases in liking, which is why first conversations matter so much. The three strategies come from Berger's later development of the theory, defined "according to the level of participation": passive strategies gather information "through unobtrusive observation", active strategies work through "manipulations of the interaction environment" and third-party sources, and interactive strategies involve "direct, face-to-face contact" (Berger 1987, p. 46). My use in [[K260 — Wise-reasoning prompts]] draws on the theory's core insight for wisdom: much social friction is not disagreement but unresolved uncertainty, and the reduction of it, not the winning of it, is what relationships run on.
