The absurd is Camus's name for the confrontation between the human demand for meaning and the world's silence. His own definition, from The Myth of Sisyphus:

> "In a universe suddenly divested of illusions and lights, man feels an alien, a stranger. His exile is without remedy since he is deprived of the memory of a lost home or the hope of a promised land. This divorce between man and his life, the actor and his setting, is properly the feeling of absurdity."

([Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus, trans. Justin O'Brien, quoted at the Colorado PDF](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil150/Camus.pdf).)

### Where the absurd lives

Camus is explicit that the absurd is a relation, not a property of either side: "The absurd is essentially a divorce. It lies in neither of the elements compared; it is born of their confrontation" ([same](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil150/Camus.pdf)). The SEP summary gives the two terms: the human "wild longing for clarity" against the "unreasonable silence of the world" ([SEP, Albert Camus](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/camus/)). The philosophical consequence Camus draws is a prohibition: "The absurd has meaning only in so far as it is not agreed to" — both suicide and philosophical suicide (the leap to faith) are refusals of the confrontation ([Loyola Chicago excerpt](https://webpages.cs.luc.edu/~dennis/106/22-Existentialism-Camus.pdf)).

### The happiness

The essay's ending, in the O'Brien translation:

> "But Sisyphus teaches the higher fidelity that negates the gods and raises rocks. He too concludes that all is well. This universe henceforth without a master seems to him neither sterile nor futile. Each atom of that stone, each mineral flake of that night-filled mountain, in itself forms a world. The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy."

([Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus, quoted at Wikiquote](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Absurdism).) The link between the absurd and happiness is stated earlier in the book: "Happiness and the absurd are two sons of the same earth. They are inseparable" (MS 122; [SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/camus/)). My principle on Sisyphus holds this as a practice: the happiness is not the reward for solving the absurd but the stance taken toward it.
