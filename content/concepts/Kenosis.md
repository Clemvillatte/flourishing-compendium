Kenosis (Greek κένωσις) means the act of emptying. In Christian theology it names the self-emptying of Christ described in Philippians 2:5-9, where the Greek verb ἐκένωσεν (ekénōsen, "he emptied himself") occurs:

> "Let the same mind be in you that was in Christ Jesus, who, though he was in the form of God, did not regard equality with God as something to be exploited, but emptied himself, taking the form of a slave, being born in human likeness. And being found in human form, he humbled himself and became obedient to the point of death—even death on a cross. Therefore God also highly exalted him and gave him the name that is above every name." (Philippians 2:5-9, NRSV, quoted in [Wikipedia, "Kenosis"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenosis))

The noun form kénōsis is post-biblical; the verb form kenóō occurs five times in the epistles (Romans 4:14; 1 Corinthians 1:17, 9:15; 2 Corinthians 9:3; Philippians 2:7), and the noun and its doctrinal development arise from patristic and later theological reflection on this verse ([Wikipedia, "Kenosis"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenosis)).

### The contested readings

The meaning is contested. The less controversial reading is that Christ emptied himself of his own desires and became entirely receptive to God's will, obedient to the point of death. A stronger kenotic Christology holds that Christ voluntarily bound his use of divine powers in some way in order to live a genuinely human life: he slept, ate, was tempted, and could be frustrated. A third line, the kenotic ethic, treats the passage not as a theory about God but as a moral example: an instruction to Christians to practise the same self-emptying toward others.

### What it is not

Kenosis is not self-erasure, self-hatred, or the annihilation of the person. It is a voluntary, purposeful relinquishment of status or prerogative, not a loss of agency. It is also not identical to humility in the modern sense of low self-regard; the Philippians passage presupposes that the one who empties himself has something to empty. Vincent's commentary, quoted in the leadership literature, is explicit:

> "His self-emptying was not self-extinction, nor was the divine Being changed into a mere man. In His humanity He retained the consciousness of deity, and in His incarnate state carried out the mind which animated Him before His incarnation. He was not unable to assert equality with God. He was able not to assert it." (Vincent, quoted in [Regent, "The Christ Hymn in Philippians 2"](https://www.regent.edu/journal/journal-of-biblical-perspectives-in-leadership/christ-hymn-philippians-2/))

In Eastern Orthodoxy, "Kenosis is only possible through humility and presupposes that one seeks union with God" ([Wikipedia, "Kenosis"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenosis)).

### Practical implications

The Regent paper develops the leadership application directly:

> "leaders who work in at-risk communities practice kenosis, or radical subordination, to be effective in empowering the poor to rise from poverty by holding back the leader's use of power, knowledge, expertise or resource availability in order for the impoverished to develop their abilities and recognize their local assets." ([Regent, "The Christ Hymn in Philippians 2"](https://www.regent.edu/journal/journal-of-biblical-perspectives-in-leadership/christ-hymn-philippians-2/))

The practical form is not abasement but non-exploitation: not using what you have (status, skill, information) to dominate the situation. This is the ethical core shared with wu wei (non-forcing) and anatta (non-identification). As a psychological concept, kenosis is often compared to modern research on humility, self-transcendence, and the paradox that self-focus can impair performance and well-being, though this is an interpretive analogy rather than a genealogical claim.
