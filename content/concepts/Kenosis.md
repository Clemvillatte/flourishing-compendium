Kenosis (Greek κένωσις) means the act of emptying. In Christian theology it names the self-emptying of Christ described in Philippians 2:5–9, where the Greek verb ἐκένωσεν (ekenōsen, "he emptied himself") occurs. The passage reads: Christ Jesus, "though he was in the form of God, did not regard equality with God as something to be exploited, but emptied himself, taking the form of a slave, being born in human likeness" (NRSV). The New Testament does not use the noun kenōsis; the verb kenóō occurs five times, of which Philippians 2:7 is the theologically central instance. The noun and its doctrinal development are post-biblical, arising from patristic and later theological reflection on this verse ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenosis)). The verb's other uses include Romans 4:14, where faith is said to be voided if works suffice, and 2 Corinthians 8:9, where Jesus becomes poor so that others might become rich ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenosis)).

The meaning is contested. The less controversial reading is that Christ emptied himself of his own desires and became entirely receptive to God's will, obedient to the point of death. A stronger kenotic Christology holds that Christ voluntarily bound his use of divine powers in some way in order to live a genuinely human life: he slept, ate, was tempted, and could be frustrated. A third line, the kenotic ethic, treats the passage not as a theory about God but as a moral example: an instruction to Christians to practise the same self-emptying toward others.

## What it is NOT

Kenosis is not self-erasure, self-hatred, or the annihilation of the person. It is a voluntary, purposeful relinquishment of status or prerogative, not a loss of agency. It is also not identical to humility in the modern sense of low self-regard; the Philippians passage presupposes that the one who empties himself has something to empty. In Eastern Orthodoxy, kenosis is associated with humility, seeking union with God, and following Christ's example ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kenosis)).

## Practical implications

As a psychological concept, kenosis is often compared to modern research on humility, self-transcendence, and the paradox that self-focus can impair performance and well-being, though this is an interpretive analogy rather than a genealogical claim. It supplies a mechanism for the Daoist and Buddhist convergence in this library: the claim that the ego's projects of self-aggrandisement are the obstacle, and that the good life requires a deliberate loosening of the grip. The practical form is not abasement but non-exploitation: not using what you have — status, skill, information — to dominate the situation. This is the ethical core shared with wu wei (non-forcing) and anatta (non-identification).
