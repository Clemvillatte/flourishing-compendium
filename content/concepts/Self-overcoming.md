“Self-overcoming” — Nietzsche, Thus Spoke Zarathustra.

## What it is

The idea belongs to a single chapter of Zarathustra. In Thomas Common's translation the chapter is titled "Self-Surpassing", the German Selbstüberwindung. Zarathustra follows living things and reports what he heard. All living things are obeying things. Whatever cannot obey itself is commanded. And when a living thing commands even itself, it must atone for its commanding, becoming the judge and avenger and victim of its own law.

Then Life herself speaks the secret.

> And this secret spake Life herself unto me. “Behold,” said she, “I am that WHICH MUST EVER SURPASS ITSELF. ... Whatever I create, and however much I love it,—soon must I be adverse to it, and to my love: so willeth my Will.
>
> — Nietzsche, Thus Spoke Zarathustra, "Self-Surpassing," trans. Thomas Common (capitals in the translation)

The chapter binds self-overcoming to the will to power. Wherever Zarathustra found a living thing, he found will to power. Only where there is life is there will, and the will is not a will to life but a will to power. Self-overcoming is not a one-time act. It is the standing condition of whatever lives, the drive that makes each achievement the platform for the next surpassing.

Values are included in the motion. Good and evil that would be everlasting do not exist, and must of their own accord surpass themselves anew. Whoever would be a creator in good and evil must first be a destroyer and break values in pieces.

## In the compendium

The principle on practising self-overcoming takes this chapter as its text, quoting the obedience-and-command triad and the word of Life. Its caution is worth keeping: the doctrine is descriptive before it is prescriptive. Life overcomes itself; the practice asks what to do with that fact.

The concept also meets the compendium's psychology of becoming. The possible-selves principle holds that people carry ideas of what they might become, what they would like to become, and what they fear becoming. The self-actualization principle carries Maslow's line that a musician must make music, an artist must paint, and what a man can be, he must be. Nietzsche's version is harsher than either. The surpassing is not toward a fixed ideal self that could, once reached, be possessed. Even what the will creates and loves, it must soon be adverse to. Rest is not the destination of the process.
