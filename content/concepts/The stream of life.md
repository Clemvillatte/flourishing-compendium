James's Principles of Psychology refuses the picture of consciousness as beads on a string. His chapter on the stream of thought insists it is nothing jointed: it flows. The stream runs under life itself; he writes of "the river of life, of the river of elementary feeling," and says of it, with Heraclitus, that we never descend twice into the same stream.

> "Consciousness, then, does not appear to itself chopped up in bits. Such words as 'chain' or 'train' do not describe it fitly as it presents itself in the first instance. It is nothing jointed; it flows. A 'river' or a 'stream' are the metaphors by which it is most naturally described." (William James, The Principles of Psychology, ch. IX, [Classics in the History of Psychology, York University](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin9.htm))

The chapter's cases run on through sensation: even if sensations came pure and single, we never in strict accuracy get the same one twice, and brain-changes being continuous, the consciousnesses melt into each other "like dissolving views," one protracted consciousness, one unbroken stream. The Buddhist teaching of impermanence makes the same point from the other shore: no moment repeats, and the self riding the stream is a becoming rather than a thing.
