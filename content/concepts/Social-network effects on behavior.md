Social-network effects on behavior is the finding, associated with Nicholas Christakis and James Fowler, that health-related behaviour clusters across social ties and appears to spread along them. The seed cites their 2007 and 2008 papers. Both papers used the Framingham Heart Study network of 12,067 people assessed repeatedly from 1971 to 2003; the 2007 paper is on obesity and the 2008 paper on smoking (the seed does not say which 2008 paper it means; Christakis and Fowler published more than one that year, so smoking is the one covered here).

### What the two papers report

On obesity, they found clusters that "extended to three degrees of separation" and that "did not appear to be solely attributable to the selective formation of social ties among obese persons". A person's chances of becoming obese rose by 57% if a friend became obese; the 95% confidence interval was wide (6 to 123).

> "Smoking behavior spreads through close and distant social ties, groups of interconnected people stop smoking in concert, and smokers are increasingly marginalized socially." (Christakis & Fowler 2008, [PubMed 18499567](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18499567/))

In the smoking study, a spouse quitting lowered a person's chances of smoking by 67%, a friend quitting by 36%, and a sibling quitting by 25%. Neither study found the effect among neighbours in the immediate geographic area. The 2007 obesity paper is [PubMed 17652652](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17652652/).

### The causal question

The word "spread" claims more than an observed cluster can show. Shalizi and Thomas argue that, in observational network data, homophily (people forming ties with similar people), social contagion and the effect of an individual's own traits are generically confounded with each other.

> "Distinguishing them from one another requires strong assumptions on the parametrization of the social process or on the adequacy of the covariates used (or both)." (Shalizi & Thomas (2011), [PubMed 22523436](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22523436/))

Christakis and Fowler say their obesity clusters were not solely due to selective tie formation. Shalizi and Thomas argue that identifying contagion from such data depends on strong assumptions. The honest summary is a dispute about identification, not a settled result.
