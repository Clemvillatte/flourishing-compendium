Gene Sharp's three-part The Politics of Nonviolent Action (1973) pairs a theory of power with a catalogue of methods. Brian Martin's review says it "presented a theory for understanding the effectiveness of nonviolent action" and "documented 198 methods of nonviolent action, classifying them into types and subtypes" ([Martin, Review of Gene Sharp, The politics of nonviolent action](https://www.bmartin.cc/pubs/17BRjrs.html)).

### The consent theory of power

Sharp contrasts two pictures. In the monolithic one, power is held by those in higher positions. In the consent theory, "those in positions of authority have power only so far as subordinates and subjects grant it to them", so that "all that is required to bring down the ruler is to cease cooperating" (Martin's review). Martin's later summary of Sharp's theory states that "nonviolent action constitutes a refusal by subjects to obey" ([Martin, Nonviolence theory: insights for communication, PDF](https://www.bmartin.cc/pubs/03nvs/nvs-5-nv-theory.pdf)).

### The methods

The 198 methods fall into three main groups: protest and persuasion, noncooperation, and nonviolent intervention. Martin's review counts six chapter-level types, since noncooperation splits into social, economic (boycotts), economic (strikes) and political forms. A University of Wisconsin page lists five categories and gives the same broad idea ([The Nonviolence Project, Wisconsin](https://thenonviolenceproject.wisc.edu/2025/05/03/leading-nonviolence-scholar-gene-sharp/)). For each method Sharp gave historical examples, among them revenue refusal in ancient China and fasting and sit-ins as interventions (Martin's PDF; [Flintoff](https://flintoff.org/power-and-how-to-seize-it)).

### Mechanisms and debate

A summary on Philopedia lists four mechanisms of change: conversion, accommodation, nonviolent coercion and disintegration ([Philopedia, Gene Sharp](https://philopedia.org/thinkers/gene-sharp/)). It also defines nonviolent action as deliberate, organized conflict that excludes physical violence but "may nonetheless be highly coercive". The civil resistance bibliography notes the consent theory "has since been much debated" ([Civil Resistance bibliography](https://civilresistance.info/section/introduction-nonviolent-action/1-theory-methods-and-examples/1b-strategic-theory-dynamics)).

### Reading the seed note

The seed is a bare citation, so the page above states what the work actually argues. In the sources I opened, nonviolent action is presented as a method of struggle that works through withdrawn consent; I did not find a moral argument in them, so treat any ethical claim as outside this entry.
