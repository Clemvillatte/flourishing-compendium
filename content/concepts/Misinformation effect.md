The misinformation effect is the finding that exposure to misleading information after an event impairs memory for the event itself. Elizabeth Loftus, who reviewed roughly 30 years of the research programme in Learning & Memory, defines it this way:

> "The misinformation effect refers to the impairment in memory for the past that arises after exposure to misleading information." ([Loftus, "Planting misinformation in the human mind", Learning & Memory 12(4), 2005, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16027179/))

### What the 30-year review covers

Loftus's abstract lists the questions the field has worked through: the conditions under which people are especially susceptible to misinformation and when they resist it; whether warnings help (they sometimes inhibit the damaging effects, but only under limited circumstances); the observation of the effect across a variety of human and nonhuman species; and the fate of the original memory traces after misinformation appears to have made them inaccessible ([same record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16027179/)). The review closes with the newer line of work on rich false memories: the processes by which people come to believe they experienced complex events that never occurred.
