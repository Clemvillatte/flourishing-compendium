Carol Gilligan's ethics-of-care theory holds that moral reasoning has more than one voice. The Inamori Foundation's Kyoto Prize citation summarises her 1982 book In a Different Voice: she "noted that women's ways of thinking include 'a different voice' that offers rich insights into moral reasoning", and "called this relationship-oriented reasoning the 'ethic of care' and contrasted it with the 'ethic of justice,' which often entails imposing universal principles or rights" ([Kyoto Prize, Carol Gilligan](https://www.kyotoprize.org/en/laureates/carol_gilligan/)).

### The challenge to Kohlberg

Gilligan's argument was aimed at Lawrence Kohlberg's stage theory. Em Griffin's textbook chapter reports her objection that Kohlberg's theory was "conceived by a man and tested on an all-male sample". She "has no quarrel with its validity for those who see ethics in terms of justice", but objects that psychology has "tried to fashion women out of a masculine cloth", and she insists women speak in "a different - but not inferior - moral voice" ([A First Look at Communication Theory, "Different Voice"](https://www.afirstlook.com/docs/diffvoice.pdf)).

### What care and justice emphasise

A reading of Gilligan from the University of the Free State puts it this way: "Whereas the logic of justice emphasises the application of abstract rules and principles (by the autonomous individual), the focus of the ethic of care is on relationships and the interdependence between human beings in specific contexts" ([Gilligan and complexity: reinterpreting the "ethic of care"](https://scholar.ufs.ac.za/server/api/core/bitstreams/536b529b-21e1-4633-a0ff-06cb314600cd/content)). The Kyoto citation adds that the care ethic "seeks the most desirable solution for each individual while considering each individual involved from the viewpoint of 'relationships' between interdependent individuals".

### Not a simple opposition

The Kyoto citation stresses that Gilligan "did not merely contrast the 'ethic of care' to the 'ethic of justice'". She has been concerned with "an enmeshment of these two differently positioned ethical frameworks for a more fully realized human maturity". It also says she saw the ethic of care, which aims to respond fairly to the needs of others and to one's own, as the key to liberating both women and men "from modern patriarchy". Griffin's chapter notes that Gilligan assumed there would be different perspectives within an ethic of care, as there are within a justice-based model.

### Criticism worth knowing

A recent paper on Cambridge Core notes that the "dualistic framing of care versus justice has faced significant criticism over the years, particularly from feminist scholars who argue that it risks perpetuating essentialist views of gender" ([Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/5AE774301EE8D7DEF46CD0358FA96914/S0887536725100329a.pdf/abstract_other_exploring_the_care_ethical_potential_of_contextualized_abstraction.pdf)). For this reason, the essays should present care and justice as two orientations that people of any gender may use, not as a male and a female morality.
