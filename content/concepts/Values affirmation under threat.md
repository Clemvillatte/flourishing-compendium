Values affirmation under threat is the practice, and the research finding, that writing about one's core values blunts the sting of threats to the self. It began with Claude Steele's self-affirmation theory and was consolidated in Cohen and Sherman's review:

> "People have a basic need to maintain the integrity of the self, a global sense of personal adequacy. Events that threaten self-integrity arouse stress and self-protective defenses that can hamper performance and growth. However, an intervention known as self-affirmation can curb these negative outcomes. Self-affirmation interventions typically have people write about core personal values."
> — [Cohen & Sherman, "The Psychology of Change," Annual Review of Psychology 65 (2014)](https://labs.psych.ucsb.edu/sherman/david/sites/labs.psych.ucsb.edu.sherman.david/files/pubs/cohen_sherman_2014.pdf)

The key term is global. What a threat menaces is usually one domain — a grade, a diagnosis, a slur — but what it unsettles is the whole story of being a good, adequate person. Because the story is global, it can be shored up from anywhere: an act of self-affirmation is "an act that manifests one's adequacy and thus affirms one's sense of global self-integrity," and it need have nothing to do with the threatened domain. Affirming one's role as a friend can steady a person facing a health message.

The mechanism explains why the intervention looks too small to work. Threats set off defensive responses — denial, avoidance, motivated reasoning — that protect the self but block learning. Affirmation reduces the need for defense, and with the alarm quieted, performance and openness improve. The effects are not momentary pep: reviews report benefits in education, health and relationships that persist for months and years, as an early success improves the environment, which improves the person, and round again.
