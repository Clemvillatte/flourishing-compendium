“Existential confrontation” — Tillich, 1952; Yalom, 1980.

Existential confrontation means facing the basic conditions of human life, such as death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness, instead of avoiding them. The two sources in the seed supply the underlying theories: Paul Tillich's account of anxiety as awareness of nonbeing in The Courage to Be (1952), and Irvin Yalom's four ultimate concerns in Existential Psychotherapy (1980). The compendium's phrase is a label; neither author is shown here using it as a technical term.

### Tillich: three directions of threat

Tillich proposes that anxiety takes three forms.

> "I suggest that we distinguish three types of anxiety according to the three directions in which nonbeing threatens being." (Tillich, The Courage to Be, 1952, [excerpt at the Fordham Internet History Sourcebooks](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/1952tillich.asp))

The threats are to ontic self-affirmation (fate and death), spiritual self-affirmation (emptiness and meaninglessness) and moral self-affirmation (guilt and condemnation). He marks the difference from clinical anxiety.

> "In all three forms anxiety is existential in the sense that it belongs to existence as such and not to an abnormal state of mind as in neurotic (and psychotic) anxiety." (Tillich, same excerpt)

The book's answer to that anxiety is courage, which a secondary summary defines as self-affirmation in spite of the threat of nonbeing ([EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/literature-and-writing/courage-be-paul-tillich/)).

### Yalom: four ultimate concerns

The publisher's summary of Yalom's book says it is organised around what he calls the four "ultimate concerns of life", which are death, freedom, existential isolation and meaninglessness, and it examines the conflict that springs from confronting each. ([Yalom, Existential Psychotherapy](https://www.yalom.com/existential-psychotherapy/)) Yalom also draws on empirical research, philosophy and literature, according to the same summary.

### What the evidence says about therapy

A meta-analysis of existential therapies (15 studies with unique data, 1,792 participants) found that meaning therapies had large effects on positive meaning in life immediately after treatment (d = 0.65) and at follow-up (d = 0.57), and moderate effects on psychopathology (d = 0.47) and self-efficacy (d = 0.48). Supportive-expressive therapy had small effects on psychopathology (d = 0.20 post-treatment). Experiential-existential and cognitive-existential therapies had no significant effects. ([Vos, Craig & Cooper 2015, Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology 83(1):115-128](https://clinica.ispa.pt/sites/default/files/22._vos_et_al_2015.pdf))

The authors note that the number of studies is small and that effects varied widely between individuals within studies. The meta-analysis does not test Tillich's or Yalom's theories directly, and it does not show that confronting death or freedom in itself produces the benefits.

### What this page does not claim

The sources give a descriptive theory of what is confronted, and mixed evidence for therapies built on it. They do not show that confrontation is safe or helpful for everyone. Tillich's frame is theological as well as philosophical, which should be borne in mind before using it as a general psychological claim.
