Praxis is action that carries its own meaning, in the lineage that runs from Aristotle's ethics through Marx. The Greek word Aristotle uses for it marks doing as something other than making: making aims at a product beyond itself, while doing, praxis, aims at the doing. In the Nicomachean Ethics he writes that "only that which is done is that; for good action is an end, and desire aims at this" (VI.2), and that the choice such action springs from is "either desiderative reason or ratiocinative desire" (VI.2). A good act is not raw movement; it is desire and thinking fused into a chosen end.

> "The origin of action -- its efficient, not its final cause -- is choice, and that of choice is desire and reasoning with a view to an end. This is why choice cannot exist either without reason and intellect or without a moral state; for good action and its opposite cannot exist without a combination of intellect and character." (Aristotle, [Nicomachean Ethics VI.2](https://home.csulb.edu/~cwallis/382/readings/160/Nicomachean%20Ethics/chapter%206.html), translated by W. D. Ross)

### What distinguishes praxis

Aristotle distinguishes it from both theory and production. Intellect alone, he says, "moves nothing, but only the intellect which aims at an end and is practical; for this rules the productive intellect, as well, since every one who makes makes for an end, and that which is made is not an end in the unqualified sense" (VI.2). For Aristotle, then, production aims at a product, while good action has its end in acting well. The virtue that guides it, practical wisdom, is "a true and reasoned state of capacity to act with regard to the things that are good or bad for man" (VI.5), and "plainly, then, practical wisdom is a virtue and not an art" (VI.5).

### Marx's turn

Marx takes the concept and turns it against philosophy itself. Marx wrote the theses in the spring of 1845, and they were first published in 1888, slightly edited by Engels, as an appendix to his Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy. The eleventh thesis reads:

> "The philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways; the point is to change it." (Marx, [Theses on Feuerbach](https://www.marxists.org/archive/marx/works/1845/theses/theses.htm), Thesis XI)

The second thesis explains the ground of the break: "The question whether objective truth can be attributed to human thinking is not a question of theory but is a practical question. Man must prove the truth... in practice." Theory that never meets practice, Marx argues, remains scholastic.
