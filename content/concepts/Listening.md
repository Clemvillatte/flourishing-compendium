Listening, in the dialogical tradition, is the act by which another person becomes more than noise. The seed names Buber's I and Thou and Levinas's Totality and Infinity, and the tradition's clearest statements are Buber's, from The Knowledge of Man:

> Genuine conversation, and therefore every actual fulfilment of relation between men, means acceptance of otherness.

(Martin Buber, "Elements of the Interhuman," in The Knowledge of Man, [quoted at Jenny Connected](https://jennymackness.wordpress.com/tag/martin-buber/))

The same essay describes the speaker who wants to influence the other and yet "unreservedly accepts and confirms him in his being this man." Levinas's version is starker: the face of the other commands before you speak, so listening is not optional courtesy but the primary obligation. The I-Thou relation, Buber says elsewhere, "can only be spoken with the whole being."

The psychological research line takes the same structure and measures it. Rogers and Farson's 1957 Active Listening names the attitude: "I'm not trying to change you or evaluate you. I just want to understand you," and adds the precondition, that you cannot begin to be an effective listener until you "genuinely respect the potential worth of the individual." Elliott et al.'s 2011 meta-analysis, pooling 59 samples with 3,599 clients, found empathy a moderately strong predictor of therapy outcome.

The compendium's [[R092 — Practise listening as a moral act]] treats the act as respect rather than technique; this page is that principle's concept ground.
