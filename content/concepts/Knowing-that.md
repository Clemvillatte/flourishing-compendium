Knowing-that is the propositional side of Gilbert Ryle's contrast from The Concept of Mind (1949): knowledge that something is the case, the kind that can be true or false, stated and imparted. Ryle's point was not to disparage it but to deny that all intelligence reduces to it: "Intelligent" cannot be defined in terms of "intellectual" or "knowing how" in terms of "knowing that" ([Ryle 1949: 20, quoted in SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/knowledge-how/)).

### The intellectualist reply

Ryle called his position anti-intellectualism: knowledge-how and knowledge-that are distinct kinds of knowledge ([SEP, Knowledge How](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/knowledge-how/)). The intellectualist tradition he opposed holds that abilities are underwritten by a grasp of propositions, a view the SEP entry traces through the later debate, where intellectualists argue that knowing-how is itself propositional knowledge of a special sort ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/knowledge-how/)). Facts are also, in Ryle's wording, all-or-nothing: "we never speak of a person having partial knowledge of a fact or truth... it is proper and normal to speak of a person knowing in part how to do something" ([Ryle 1949: 46, quoted in SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/knowledge-how/)).
