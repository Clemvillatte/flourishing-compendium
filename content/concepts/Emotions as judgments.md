“Emotions as judgments” — the SEP entry on emotion describes the most parsimonious cognitivist theory as following the Stoics in identifying emotions with judgments: my anger at someone is the judgment that I have been wronged by that person ([Scarantino & de Sousa, SEP "Emotion", §5](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotion/)). The entry attributes this approach to Robert Solomon, Jerome Neu and Martha Nussbaum, and notes that Nussbaum and Neu have explicitly rejected the label of judgmentalism while clarifying what sorts of judgments emotions are ([SEP, "Emotion", §5](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotion/)).

> "On a common interpretation of their view, my anger at someone is the judgment that I have been wronged by that person. To generalize, the proposal is that an emotion E is a judgment that the formal object of E is instantiated (by some particular object X)." — [SEP, "Emotion", §5](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotion/)

### The Nussbaum refinement

The entry describes the judgmentalists' response to these critiques: judgments understood as "enclosing a core desire" (Solomon), dynamic and able to "house…the disorderly motions of emotion" (the entry's citation of Nussbaum 2001: 45), available to infants and animals through pre-linguistic acceptance of how the world seems, and capable of being held jointly with contradictory judgments — which, on this theory, is how recalcitrance to reason is explained ([SEP, "Emotion", §5](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/emotion/)).
