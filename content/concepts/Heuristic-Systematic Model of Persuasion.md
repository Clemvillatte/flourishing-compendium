The heuristic-systematic model (HSM), developed by Shelly Chaiken, "proposes two distinct modes of thinking about information". The abstract of Chaiken and Ledgerwood's chapter explains: "Systematic processing involves attempts to thoroughly understand any available information through careful attention, deep thinking, and intensive reasoning, whereas heuristic processing involves focusing on salient and easily comprehended cues that activate well-learned judgmental shortcuts" ([Chaiken and Ledgerwood, "A theory of heuristic and systematic information processing", SciSpace](https://scispace.com/papers/a-theory-of-heuristic-and-systematic-information-processing-10c2hu1qkt)).

### The trade-off

Heuristic processing "is a more efficient and relatively automatic mode of processing, but more often than not confers less judgmental confidence", while systematic processing "confers more confidence but is relatively effortful and time-consuming". So "individuals tend to engage in heuristic processing unless they are both motivated and able to think carefully about information, in which case the two modes of processing can have additive, attenuating, or interactive effects". Examples of heuristic cues in the chapter are a communicator's credentials, the communicator's group membership, the number of arguments presented, and audience reactions.

### Two principles set the effort

The chapter names two principles. The least-effort principle holds that people try to reach attitudinal decisions as efficiently as possible. The sufficiency principle holds that people are sometimes motivated to put in extra effort "to reach a certain level of judgmental confidence". The balance point is a "sufficiency threshold", the confidence a person aspires to, and "effortful information processing should only occur when actual confidence falls below the sufficiency threshold". The textbook chapter adds that the desired level "is likely to be higher for issues of great personal relevance than for trivial issues" ([social psychology textbook, chapter 7](https://www.blackwellpublishing.com/content/hewstonesocialpsychology/chapters/chapter7.pdf)).

An experiment on the sufficiency principle found that "high-task-importance Ss exhibited a great deal of systematic processing regardless of congruency, whereas low-importance Ss processed systematically only when they received incongruent messages; in the congruent conditions heuristic processing dominated" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1890583/)).

### How the two modes interact

Unlike an either-or model, the HSM lets both operate together. The Hewstone chapter says it "does not assume a trade-off between these processing modes" and that "both processing modes affect persuasion at the high-effort end of the processing continuum, whereas only heuristic processing dominates at the low-effort end". Three hypotheses describe how they combine:

- Additivity: "both heuristic cues and content information exert independent main effects on persuasion".
- Attenuation: a wealth of strong arguments can submerge a cue, so that its effect "may no longer be detectable".
- Bias: heuristic processing can colour how arguments are read. In one account, "if you trust the source heuristically, you read their argument charitably" ([Yu-kai Chou's behavioural-analysis summary](https://yukaichou.com/behavioral-analysis/heuristic-systematic-model-chaiken-dual-process-persuasion/)). That page is a practitioner summary, not a primary source.

### Relation to the ELM

The Hewstone chapter says "the HSM and the ELM agree in their assumptions about the factors which determine processing motivation, but the HSM makes more explicit assumptions about processes which mediate the impact of these determinants on processing motivation".
