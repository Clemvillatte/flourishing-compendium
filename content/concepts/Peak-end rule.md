Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber and Redelmeier (1993) exposed subjects to two aversive experiences: in the short trial, one hand in water at 14 °C for 60 seconds; in the long trial, the other hand at 14 °C for 60 seconds and then 30 seconds more while the water was gradually raised to 15 °C, "still painful but distinctly less so for most subjects". A "significant majority chose to repeat the long trial, apparently preferring more pain over less". The abstract concludes that duration "plays a small role in retrospective evaluations of aversive experiences; such evaluations are often dominated by the discomfort at the worst and at the final moments of episodes" ([Kahneman et al., When More Pain Is Preferred to Less: Adding a Better End, Psychological Science 4(6), 401-405, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x)). The wording matters: "often dominated", not "always determined".

### The rule in plain terms

An Oxford note describes it this way: "Painful episodes are evaluated on the basis of how bad the pain is at its worst and how bad it is at the end. The duration of the pain counts for almost nothing", so "an episode with more pain than another can easily be judged less bad" ([More pain or less? Oxford, PDF](https://users.ox.ac.uk/~sfop0060/pdf/More%20pain%20or%20less.pdf)). The seed's "disproportionately shaped by the peak and the final moments" matches the abstract.

### Pleasant experiences and other uses

Do, Rupert and Wolford (2008) reported "strong evidence that the peak-end rule applies to both material goods and pain", finding that people reported lower overall pleasure after mild pleasure was added at the end of an experience ([Evaluations of pleasurable experiences: The peak-end rule, Psychonomic Bulletin and Review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/PBR.15.1.96?error=cookies_not_supported&code=59ea7587-1af2-436c-8053-e467f8b1fc8e)). They cite Fredrickson and Kahneman (1993) for the finding that people "essentially disregard the duration of an affective episode" in retrospective assessments. A study of effortful studying found that a more strenuous study period extended by a moderate interval was preferred to a shorter one, despite better test performance after the shorter interval ([Ending on a high note, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2970645/)).

### Limits

The laboratory trials involved cold water and short films, so applying the rule to life as a whole is an extension. I did not open Redelmeier and Kahneman (1996), the seed's second reference, so the medical-procedure evidence is not summarised here.
