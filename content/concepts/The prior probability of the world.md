In Section X, Part I of the [Enquiry](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9662), Hume describes degrees of evidence through the balance of past experiments and observations:

> "All probability, then, supposes an opposition of experiments and observations, where the one side is found to overbalance the other, and to produce a degree of evidence, proportioned to the superiority. A hundred instances or experiments on one side, and fifty on another, afford a doubtful expectation of any event; though a hundred uniform experiments, with only one that is contradictory, reasonably beget a pretty strong degree of assurance." (Hume, Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, X.1, [Project Gutenberg #9662](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9662))

In Section V, Part I, Hume attributes our expectation that future events will resemble past ones to custom ([Enquiry](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9662)). The heading is my own phrase for the background of past observations his account starts from; Hume writes of probability and experience, not of a "prior probability of the world". The custom passage states the mechanism plainly:

> "Custom, then, is the great guide of human life. It is that principle alone which renders our experience useful to us, and makes us expect, for the future, a similar train of events with those which have appeared in the past." (Hume, Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, V.1, [Project Gutenberg #9662](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9662))

His discussion of miracles in Section X weighs the evidence supplied by testimony against experience concerning the reported event, and states the balance as a rule:

> "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish" (Hume, Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding, X.I, [Project Gutenberg #9662](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/9662))

Read as a compendium concept, the page is about where confidence should start from. Before any new report arrives, the world has already run the experiments: illness usually follows certain patterns, people usually behave certain ways, and the tally of past observations sets how much a new claim has to overcome. The base-rate principle takes this literally, and the calibration principle asks that stated confidence stay proportioned to the superiority of the evidence. Hume's rule for testimony is the same discipline at full strength: the more the report deviates from the accumulated tally, the better the testimony must be. The concept guards against starting the argument from the desired conclusion and working backwards.
