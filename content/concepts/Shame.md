Shame, in psychological research, is a self-conscious emotion in which the whole self is judged negatively, as distinct from guilt, which attaches to a specific act. Tangney, Stuewig and Mashek review the field in the Annual Review of Psychology: "According to Lewis (1971), shame involves a negative evaluation of the global self; guilt involves a negative evaluation of a specific behavior" ([Tangney, Stuewig & Mashek, 2007, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3083636/)).

> "On the whole, empirical evidence evaluating the action tendencies of people experiencing shame and guilt suggests that guilt promotes constructive, proactive pursuits, whereas shame promotes defensiveness, interpersonal separation, and distance." (Tangney, Stuewig & Mashek, 2007, [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3083636/))

### What the review reports

The authors say the difference of emphasis, on the self in "I did that horrible thing" and on the behavior in "I did that horrible thing," "sets the stage for very different emotional experiences and very different patterns of motivations and subsequent behavior" (same paper; the original marks the emphasised word in italics). Shame "corresponds with attempts to deny, hide, or escape the shame-inducing situation," while guilt "corresponds with reparative actions including confessions, apologies, and undoing the consequences of the behavior." Guilt "goes hand in hand with other-oriented empathy," and feelings of shame "apparently disrupt individuals' ability to form empathic connections with others." They add that guilt turns maladaptive "when it becomes fused with shame."

### Qualifications

The review does not present this as settled for every group or case. It says the link of shame and guilt to moral behavior "may not generalize across all populations with respect to all behaviors," and notes that one study, which "focused on a unique, homogeneous sample (convicted drunk drivers ...) and a single type of transgression," found no evidence that shame and guilt form distinct factors. It also reports that group-based shame "may have a kinder, gentler side than personal shame." Much of the evidence concerns proneness to shame as a trait, for example that it "is positively correlated with anger, hostility, and the propensity to blame factors beyond the self." None of this tests whether a given episode of shame can be useful.

### In the compendium

Several principles treat shame, and they come from different traditions. SE030 (shame is about the self, not the act), AF053 and SP034 (Distinguish shame from guilt's signal) follow the psychological distinction above. A021 (Practice shame, aidos) and A118 (Understand shame, aischynē) draw on Greek ethics, a different sense of the word that the compendium keeps on its own pages, [[Shamefastness (aidos)]] and [[Shamefastness as quasi-virtue]]. E036 treats shame as ontological, which is a phenomenological claim, and E504 and SE082 are ethical and spiritual claims. The 2007 review supports only the psychological ones.
