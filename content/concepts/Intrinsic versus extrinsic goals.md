intrinsic goals predict well-being (Kasser and Ryan, 1996; Deci and Ryan, 2000).

Intrinsic versus extrinsic goals is the distinction between goals whose pursuit satisfies basic psychological needs directly and goals whose value depends on other people's reactions. The two anchor sources are Kasser and Ryan's 1996 two-study paper, which introduced the three-by-four classification the distinction rests on, and Deci and Ryan's 2000 synthesis, which places the finding inside self-determination theory's account of needs. The later paper compresses the classification into one sentence:

> "T. Kasser and Ryan (1993, 1996) distinguished between intrinsic aspirations (i.e., goals such as affiliation, personal growth, and community contribution, which are closely associated with basic need satisfaction) and extrinsic aspirations (i.e., goals such as attaining wealth, fame, and image, which are more related to obtaining contingent approval or external signs of worth, and thus are, on average, expected to be less likely to yield direct need satisfaction and may even distract from it)." (Deci and Ryan, [The "What" and "Why" of Goal Pursuits, Psychological Inquiry 11(4):227-268](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_DeciRyan_PIWhatWhy.pdf), the goal-contents section)

The original 1996 wording, from the image-scan PDF:

> "Extrinsic goals, such as financial success, are those that depend on the contingent reactions of others. Further, they are typically engaged in as means to some other end. Conversely, intrinsic goals, such as self-acceptance, are expressive of desires congruent with actualizing and growth tendencies natural to humans. As such, intrinsic goals are likely to satisfy basic and inherent psychological needs." (Kasser and Ryan, [Further Examining the American Dream, PSPB 22(3):280-287](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/1996_KasserRyan_AmericanDream.pdf), introduction)

### The studies

The 1996 paper rated seven life-goal domains. Four intrinsic: self-acceptance (growth), affiliation (relatedness), community feeling (helpfulness), and physical fitness (health). Three extrinsic: financial success (money), social recognition (fame), and appealing appearance (image). In an adult sample, and in a second study of 192 undergraduates with daily-affect diaries, the relative centrality of extrinsic goals was associated with lower self-actualization and vitality and more depression and physical symptoms, with the opposite pattern for intrinsic goals; the associations survived checks against social desirability and held across gender. The 2000 synthesis adds the attainment literature: rated and actual attainment of intrinsic aspirations was associated with well-being, whereas attainment of extrinsic aspirations generally was not, in US and Russian samples.

Three explanations for the negative correlation are discussed in the 1996 paper rather than settled: extrinsic aspirations as a marker of earlier insecurity, extrinsic goals leading to more controlled and driven daily behavior, and the greater difficulty of attaining extrinsic goals at all. The evidence throughout is correlational and self-report based, and the measure works with relative centrality, each person's importance ratings partialled against their own mean.
