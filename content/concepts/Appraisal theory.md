Appraisal theory is the family of emotion theories holding that emotions arise from evaluations, called appraisals, of what an event means for the person, rather than from the event itself. Richard Lazarus built the canonical version: a relational theory in which emotion is a response to the fate of one's goals in an encounter with the environment.

> "Appraisal consists of an evaluation of the personal significance of what is happening in an encounter with the environment." (Lazarus 1991, [Emotion and Adaptation, chapter PDF](https://dn760106.eu.archive.org/0/items/lazarus_richard-s/lazarus1991_2.pdf))

### Primary and secondary appraisal

Lazarus splits the evaluation into two parts. Primary appraisal "concerns the stakes one has in the outcomes of an encounter" — goal relevance, goal congruence or incongruence, and type of ego-involvement: "without a stake there is no potential for an emotion" (Lazarus 1991, [chapter PDF](https://dn760106.eu.archive.org/0/items/lazarus_richard-s/lazarus1991_2.pdf)). Secondary appraisal "concerns the options and prospects for coping" — blame or credit, coping potential, and future expectations. The stress version of the same idea is transactional: "psychological stress refers to a relationship with the environment that the person appraises as significant for his or her well being and in which the demands tax or exceed available coping resources" (Lazarus and Folkman 1986, p. 63, as quoted in [Krohne, "Stress and Coping Theories"](https://www.uv.mx/cendhiu/files/2015/12/Krohne_Stress.pdf)).
