In her 2010 article, Sarah Clark Miller develops and argues for an account she calls cosmopolitan care. Her abstract announces the project in her own words:

> "I develop the foundation for cosmopolitan care, an underexplored variety of moral cosmopolitanism." — Miller, "Cosmopolitan Care", Ethics and Social Welfare 4(2), 2010 ([abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17496535.2010.484258))

She proceeds by characterizing contemporary cosmopolitanism from the justice tradition, then "employ[s] a feminist philosophical perspective to provide a critical evaluation of the moral foundations of cosmopolitan justice, with an eye toward demonstrating the need for an alternative account of moral cosmopolitanism as cosmopolitan care" ([abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17496535.2010.484258)). She explains "how care ethics in connection with Kantian ethics generates a duty to care", develops the theory of obligation the account endorses, and applies it to gender violence in conflict zones ([abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17496535.2010.484258)).

Virginia Held supplies the global reach of the care tradition. Where "such other moral theories as Kantian morality and utilitarianism demand impartiality above all, the ethics of care understands the moral import of ties to families and groups" ([Oxford Academic, book abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/2881)), Held argues that:

> "a globalization of caring relations would help enable people of different states and cultures to live in peace, to respect each others' rights, to care together for their environments, and to improve the lives of their children." — Held, quoted in the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy's "Care Ethics" entry ([link](https://iep.utm.edu/care-ethics/))
