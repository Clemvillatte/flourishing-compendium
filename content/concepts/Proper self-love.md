Proper self-love is the Aristotelian claim that loving oneself is not itself a fault: the question is which part of oneself one loves and what one assigns to oneself. In Nicomachean Ethics IX.8 Aristotle starts from the ordinary reproach and then narrows it.

> "Those who use the term as one of reproach ascribe self-love to people who assign to themselves the greater share of wealth, honours, and bodily pleasures." (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IX.8, W. D. Ross translation, [Internet Classics Archive](@N@))

### The argument in IX.8

Aristotle says such people "gratify their appetites and in general their feelings and the irrational element of the soul," and that the word takes its meaning "from the prevailing type of self-love, which is a bad one." He then offers a contrast case: "if a man were always anxious that he himself, above all things, should act justly, temperately, or in accordance with any other of the virtues... no one will call such a man a lover of self or blame him." He goes further and says such a man "would seem more than the other a lover of self," because he "assigns to himself the things that are noblest and best, and gratifies the most authoritative element" in him, which Aristotle identifies with reason. The conclusion is that the good man "is most truly a lover of self, of another type than that which is a matter of reproach" ([Aristotle, NE IX.8](@N@)).

### Self-love and love of others

Aristotle does not treat this as selfishness. He writes that "the good man should be a lover of self (for he will both himself profit by doing noble acts, and will benefit his fellows), but the wicked man should not." The good man "does many acts for the sake of his friends and his country, and if necessary dies for them," and in doing so he "is therefore assigning the greater good to himself," because the nobility is his. He sums it up: "In this sense, then, as has been said, a man should be a lover of self; but in the sense in which most men are so, he ought not" ([Aristotle, NE IX.8](@N@)).

### In the compendium

B097 (Wish well, starting with yourself) and P166 (Offer unconditional regard, to yourself and others) borrow the idea that care for oneself and care for others are compatible. Aristotle's version is conditional. The self worth loving is the rational, virtue-seeking part, and the passage does not describe unconditional regard or self-compassion in the modern sense. Those extensions are the compendium's. Only IX.8 was opened for this revision, not the rest of Book IX.
