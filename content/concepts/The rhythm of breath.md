“The rhythm of breath” — Daoism.

The rhythm of breath is the Daoist image of breathing as a deep, whole-body rhythm rather than a shallow action of the throat. Its locus classicus is Zhuangzi chapter 6, where the "pure men of old" are described by contrast with ordinary breathing.

> "The pure men of old slept without dreams, and waked without anxiety. They ate without discrimination, breathing deep breaths. For pure men draw breath from their uttermost depths; the vulgar only from their throats." (Zhuangzi, ch. 6, [Giles translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/59709))

Giles, the translator, notes in the footnote to this passage that "uttermost depths" is literally "heels", and that commentators take the sentence to mean that pure men breathe with their whole being, "not as it were superficially, from the throat only".

The passage's contrast, read as text, is a claim about depth rather than about how much air a person takes in. A breath drawn from the throat is described as a breath that stops at the throat; a breath drawn from the uttermost depths is described as a breath the whole body is doing. Giles's note, which the passage itself invites, is that the image is about breathing "with their whole being, not as it were superficially, from the throat only". The comparison does not say the vulgar are doing something wrong so much as that they are doing something small, and the smallness is what the image is built to point at. What the passage does not supply is a physiology of breath distribution or of attention changing respiration, so the description is best kept textual rather than treated as a clinical instruction.

The reading the passage supports is about noticing rather than about training. A person can notice where a given breath ends, and noticing is something the passage's own image is about, since a whole-body breath is a breath a person is doing rather than a breath a person is having. What the text does not establish is that the noticing itself changes the breath, so the practical reading is a reading rather than a claim about respiration, and the concept's contribution is the image of depth that the attentional anchor can be read alongside.
