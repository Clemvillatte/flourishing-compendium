The specious present is E.R. Clay's term for the part of time actually given in experience, quoted and discussed by William James in The Principles of Psychology. In Clay's words, the experienced present is "really a part of the past--a recent past--delusively given as being a time that intervenes between the past and the future" (quoted in James 1890). James's own summary:

> In short, the practically cognized present is no knife-edge, but a saddle-back, with a certain breadth of its own on which we sit perched, and from which we look in two directions into time.
>
> (James, The Principles of Psychology, vol. 1, 1890, [Project Gutenberg #57628](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/57628))

Clay's illustration is musical: "All the notes of a bar of a song seem to the listener to be contained in the present" (quoted in James 1890). The intuited duration is short, and its content is in flux, while "the specious present, the intuited duration, stands permanent, like the rainbow on the waterfall, with its own quality unchanged by the events that stream through it" (James 1890). In the Essays in Radical Empiricism, James connects the present to his doctrine of pure experience: "The instant field of the present is at all times what I call the 'pure' experience. It is only virtually or potentially either object or subject as yet. For the time being, it is plain, unqualified actuality, or existence, a simple _that_" (James 1904/1912).
