The moral situation is John Dewey's term for where morality actually happens: not in the rule, not in the inner self, but in the concrete meeting of a person's dispositions with an environment ([Human Nature and Conduct, 1922, Gutenberg full text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/41386/pg41386.txt)). The term is defined in the book's discussion of custom and morality ([Gutenberg full text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/41386/pg41386.txt)). Dewey's formulation:

> "Common-sense in short never loses sight wholly of the two facts which limit and define a moral situation. One is that consequences fix the moral quality of an act. The other is that upon the whole, or in the long run but not unqualifiedly, consequences are what they are because of the nature of desire and disposition." (Dewey, Human Nature and Conduct, Part I, [Gutenberg full text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/41386/pg41386.txt))

Two consequences follow. Judgment of a deed has to take in both the deed's concrete results and the disposition behind it, because "disposition is habitual, persistent... it shows itself therefore in many acts and in many consequences" ([Gutenberg full text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/41386/pg41386.txt)). And the unit of moral repair is the situation, not the soul: "all conduct is interaction between elements of human nature and the environment, natural and social" ([Gutenberg full text](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/41386/pg41386.txt)). The compendium's principles on acting in the situation rather than from the rulebook (P084) and seeing the particular (A046) work this ground.
