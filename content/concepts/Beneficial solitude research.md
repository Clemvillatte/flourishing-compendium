“Beneficial solitude research” — Nguyen et al., 2018.

### The deactivation effect

Nguyen, Ryan and Deci's 2018 paper on solitude as affective self-regulation reports four studies:

> "In this research, we showed that solitude generally has a deactivation effect on people's affective experiences, decreasing both positive and negative high-arousal affects." (Nguyen, Ryan & Deci, 2018, [Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167217733073))

The same abstract gives the conditions:

> "In Study 1, we found that the deactivation effect occurred when people were alone, but not when they were with another person. Study 2 showed that this deactivation effect did not depend on whether or not the person was engaged in an activity such as reading when alone. In Study 3, high-arousal positive affect did not drop in a solitude condition in which participants specifically engaged in positive thinking or when they actively chose what to think about. Finally, in Study 4, we found that solitude could lead to relaxation and reduced stress when individuals actively chose to be alone." (Nguyen, Ryan & Deci, 2018, [Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167217733073))

The finding is not that solitude is good or bad in itself. It is that solitude reduces high-arousal affect of both kinds, and that choice matters.
