Faculties of the mind is Kant's term for the mind's distinct powers of knowing. In the Critique of Pure Reason the two that supply knowledge are sensibility, the receptivity through which objects are given to us, and understanding, the spontaneity through which they are thought. In Kant's phrasing, "through the first an object is given to us; through the second, it is, in relation to the representation (which is a mere determination of the mind), thought", and "intuition and conceptions constitute, therefore, the elements of all our knowledge, so that neither conceptions without an intuition in some way corresponding to them, nor intuition without conceptions, can afford us a cognition" ([Critique of Pure Reason](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/4280/4280-h/4280-h.htm)).

> "Our knowledge springs from two main sources in the mind, first of which is the faculty or power of receiving representations (receptivity for impressions); the second is the power of cognizing by means of these representations (spontaneity in the production of conceptions)." (Kant, [Critique of Pure Reason](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/4280/4280-h/4280-h.htm), Transcendental Logic, Introduction I; Meiklejohn translation)

### How the faculties cooperate

Kant's rule is that neither faculty can do the other's work: "Without the sensuous faculty no object would be given to us, and without the understanding no object would be thought." His summary line: "Thoughts without content are void; intuitions without conceptions, blind." A third faculty sits above the two:

> "All our knowledge begins with sense, proceeds thence to understanding, and ends with reason, beyond which nothing higher can be discovered in the human mind for elaborating the matter of intuition and subjecting it to the highest unity of thought." — Kant, Critique of Pure Reason ([Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/4280/4280-h/4280-h.htm))
