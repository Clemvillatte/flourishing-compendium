The term comes from Greenwald and Banaji's 1995 review, which proposed "implicit social cognition" as a name for a body of findings in which attitudes, stereotypes and self-esteem operate outside the person's awareness. Two sentences from the review's abstract carry the core:

> "The identifying feature of implicit cognition is that past experience influences judgment in a fashion not introspectively known by the actor." (Greenwald and Banaji 1995, Psychological Review 102(1): 4-27, [abstract, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7878162/))

> "However, considerable evidence now supports the view that social behavior often operates in an implicit or unconscious fashion." (Greenwald and Banaji 1995, [abstract, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7878162/))

The review's conclusion was that attitudes, self-esteem and stereotypes all have such implicit modes of operation, which extends both the construct validity and the predictive usefulness of these major theoretical constructs of social psychology, in the abstract's words. An implicit attitude, in this framework, is a favourable or unfavourable disposition whose past-experience sources the holder cannot report. Methodologically, the review called for increased use of indirect measures, since asking people directly about what they cannot introspect is the wrong instrument. The review also noted a practical lever, quoted here from the same abstract: implicit cognitive effects are often reduced by focusing judges' attention on their judgment task, which is why the review took seriously the evaluation of countermeasures against unintended discrimination.

**Plain words** You can like or dislike a group, a food, a name, without knowing where the liking came from. Asking yourself why you feel it will not always find the answer, because the cause sits where introspection does not reach.
