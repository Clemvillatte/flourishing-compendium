Jane Addams's phrase for a pragmatist stance toward one's own life: that living is itself the experiment, and that deferring life while preparing for it is a trap.

The figure comes from Twenty Years at Hull-House. Addams describes the Settlement as an ongoing experiment rather than a finished program:

> "The Settlement then, is an experimental effort to aid in the solution of the social and industrial problems which are engendered by the modern conditions of life in a great city." (Addams, Twenty Years at Hull-House, ch. VI, "The Subjective Necessity for Social Settlements," pp. 113–128, [Gutenberg #1325](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1325))

The same book holds her caution about the laboratory metaphor itself. She writes that she has "always objected to the phrase 'sociological laboratory' applied to us, because Settlements should be something much more human and spontaneous than such a phrase connotes" ([Addams, ch. XIII, "Public Activities and Investigations," Gutenberg #1325](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1325)). Life as laboratory, for Addams, names the experimental attitude of the work, not a license to treat people as specimens.

The stance is sharpened by her account of "the snare of preparation" — Tolstoy's phrase, which she adopts, for the habit of endless preparation that keeps young people "in a curious inactivity at the very period of life when they are longing to construct the world anew" ([Addams, ch. IV, "The Snare of Preparation," pp. 65–88, Gutenberg #1325](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1325)). Against that deferral she reports her own turning point: the period of mere passive receptivity had come to an end, and she "had at last finished with the ever-lasting 'preparation for life,' however ill-prepared I might be" ([Addams, ch. IV, Gutenberg #1325](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1325)).
