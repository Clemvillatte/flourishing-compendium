### Definition

Object relations theory is the branch of psychoanalysis that locates the roots of personality in the relationships a baby forms, loses, and carries inward. Where classical drive theory treated other people as means toward tension-reduction, Ronald Fairbairn reversed the claim: the person is built for contact, and pleasure follows from it rather than driving it. The clearest statement is Fairbairn's principle, as set out in Gomez's introduction to the tradition:

> Libido, or the person in her libidinal capacity, is primarily not pleasure-seeking but object-seeking. Fairbairn is suggesting that our deepest motivation is for contact with others and that this need overrides our wish for physical gratification. ([Gomez, An Introduction to Object Relations](https://tcf-website-media-library.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/20131935/An-Introduction-To-Object-Relations-1997-Chap-3.pdf))

Early relationships are internalised as "objects", templates of self-and-other that keep operating inside us and quietly script later ones. The theory's founders are Melanie Klein, Ronald Fairbairn, and Donald Winnicott.

### Origin

Klein (1946) proposed that infants already relate to part-objects, good and bad, in phantasy. Fairbairn (1941-44) rebuilt libido theory around the object-seeking claim and described the splitting of the ego into subegos bonded to internal objects. Winnicott, in Sutherland's summary, "suggested more specifically how the infant's relationships at the earliest stages patterned its whole subsequent personal development" ([Sutherland, JAPA 1980, PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7009698/)). The Klein-Fairbairn contrast runs deep: for Klein, the internal object world is "a natural, inevitable and continual accompaniment of all experience"; for Fairbairn, "internal objects are neither primary nor inevitable (theoretically). They are compensatory substitutes for unsatisfactory relations with real, external objects" ([Mitchell, Contemporary Psychoanalysis 1981](https://archive.wawhite.org/uploads/PDF/Mitchell-Origin_of_Nature.pdf)).

### How it explains suffering and growth

Psychopathology, in Fairbairn's account, is a closed system: a child who cannot afford to see her parent as bad takes the badness inside, splits the ego, and remains loyal to those inner figures because they preserve the outer tie they replaced. The greatest resistance to change, he believed in 1943, "lay in the patient's loyalty to her internal objects" (Gomez). Growth is the reverse movement: opening the closed system, tolerating real others, and re-owning the split-off capacities for anger and need. Therapy works through the relationship itself.

### A real-life example

A man whose warmth was met with coldness grows fluent in coldness. He expects it, provokes it, reads it into kind faces, and feels oddly safe inside it. His inner template, not the person in front of him, is doing the relating. Noticing that loop, and risking warmth anyway, is object relations theory in action.
