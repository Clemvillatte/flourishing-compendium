Throwing away the ladder is Wittgenstein's own instruction about how to read his Tractatus. Proposition 6.54, the second-to-last remark, says that anyone who has understood him will finally recognize the book's propositions as senseless once they have climbed out through them, and that the reader must, so to speak, throw away the ladder after climbing up on it ([Tractatus 6.54, Ogden translation](https://www.kfs.org/jonathan/witt/t654en.html)).

> "My propositions are elucidatory in this way: he who understands me finally recognizes them as senseless, when he has climbed out through them, on them, over them. (He must so to speak throw away the ladder, after he has climbed up on it.) He must surmount these propositions; then he sees the world rightly."
> — [Wittgenstein, Tractatus 6.54 (Ogden translation)](https://www.kfs.org/jonathan/witt/t654en.html)

The image is not a licence to discard ideas on a whim. It says a teaching can be useful and true-in-use while it is being used, and still be something you put down once it has done its work.

Buddhist texts make the same point with the raft simile: the Buddha compares his teaching to a raft used to cross a flood, and notes that once you are across you do not carry the raft around on your head; you leave it ([B014 — Use views as tools, not possessions (the raft)]], [[B127 — Dismantle the raft when you've crossed]]).
