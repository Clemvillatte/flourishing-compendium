Relapse prevention is a cognitive-behavioural approach, developed by Marlatt and Gordon (1985), that treats relapse as a process unfolding over time and plans for lapses before they happen. The page draws on Larimer, Palmer and Marlatt's 1999 overview of the model, written for alcohol treatment.

### Relapse as a process

Traditional treatment, the overview says, often treats relapse as an end-state equal to treatment failure. Models like Marlatt and Gordon's instead emphasise "relapse as a transitional process, a series of events that unfold over time", and the process "begins prior to the first posttreatment alcohol use and continues after the initial use". That wider frame gives clinicians more places to intervene.

### The parts of the model

The model names immediate determinants of relapse and the interventions that go with them.

> "The RP model proposed by Marlatt and Gordon suggests that both immediate determinants (e.g., high-risk situations, coping skills, outcome expectancies, and the abstinence violation effect) and covert antecedents (e.g., lifestyle factors and urges and cravings) can contribute to relapse." (Larimer, Palmer & Marlatt 1999, [PMC6760427](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6760427/))

Coping is central. The overview says it is the person's response to a high-risk situation, not the situation itself, that determines whether a lapse happens, and that successful coping raises self-efficacy. Interventions include identifying each client's high-risk situations, building coping skills, managing lapses, urge-management techniques such as urge surfing, and relapse road maps, which are analyses of high-risk situations that lay out the choices available and their consequences.

### The lapse is not the relapse

The abstinence violation effect is the model's account of how a first slip becomes a collapse. The overview separates the first violation of the abstinence goal (a lapse) from a return to uncontrolled drinking (a relapse), and says that the progression "is not inevitable". People who blame a lapse on stable, global, internal flaws ("I have no willpower") are more likely to abandon the attempt than people who see it as a failure to cope with a specific situation.

### How well it holds up

The overview reports mixed results. In the large Relapse Replication and Extension Project, the original taxonomy of high-risk situations had only moderate inter-rater reliability at its finest level, and its predictive validity was modest, though the authors argue the study was not a good test of it. Support was better for other parts: how people coped strongly predicted relapse, and the abstinence violation effect predicted who relapsed after a lapse. The sources opened here are about alcohol treatment; the page does not claim the findings carry over to other behaviours.
