Emotional inertia describes how strongly feelings carry over from one observation to the next. Kuppens, Nicholas Allen and Lisa Sheeber describe it in terms of how well an earlier emotional state predicts a later one:

> "Emotional inertia can be defined as resistance to emotional change, formalized as the degree to which a person's current emotional state can be predicted by his or her emotional state at a previous moment (with high predictability reflecting high inertia)." ([Kuppens, Allen and Sheeber, 2010, p. 985](https://ppw.kuleuven.be/okp/_pdf/Kuppens2010EIAPM.pdf))

### Origin and evidence

Kuppens, Allen and Sheeber examined emotional inertia in their 2010 Psychological Science article. Their two naturalistic studies found greater inertia among people with low self-esteem, in experience-sampling data on daily emotion, and among depressed adolescents, in second-by-second coded family interactions ([Kuppens et al., 2010](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/0956797610372634/)). In a 2012 prospective study, researchers assessed 165 early adolescents during videotaped interactions with a parent. Greater inertia of both positive and negative emotional behavior predicted the emergence of clinical depression 2.5 years later ([Kuppens et al., 2012](https://ppw.kuleuven.be/okp/_pdf/Kuppens2012EIPPT.pdf)).

### Core claims

On what high inertia amounts to:

> "High emotional inertia means that a person's emotional state is likely to persist from one moment to the next, which suggests that it may be highly resistant to external influences (e.g., changes in the environment) or internal influences (e.g., regulation efforts)." ([Kuppens, Allen and Sheeber, 2010, p. 985](https://ppw.kuleuven.be/okp/_pdf/Kuppens2010EIAPM.pdf))

On where inertia is thought to come from:

> "Theoretically, emotional inertia was conceived to reflect both decreased emotional reactivity and impaired emotion regulation skills (Kuppens et al., 2010), but empirically, inertia seems to mainly and most consistently stem from difficulties regulating one's emotions." ([Kuppens and Verduyn, 2015](https://ppw.kuleuven.be/okp/_pdf/Kuppens2015LAERT.pdf))

### Persistence across observations

Inertia is measured as an autocorrelation, the tie between consecutive readings of the same emotion. The same article notes the low end of the measure: low inertia means an emotional state is more susceptible to change and more responsive to regulatory control ([Kuppens et al., 2010, p. 985](https://ppw.kuleuven.be/okp/_pdf/Kuppens2010EIAPM.pdf)). A 2015 meta-analysis found that more inert emotions co-occurred with lower psychological well-being (overall ρ̂ = -.151), with stronger effects for negative than positive emotions ([Houben, Van Den Noortgate and Kuppens, 2015](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/25822133)). This is an association observed across studies, not a demonstration that changing inertia treats anything.

### In everyday life

Illustrative scenario: irritation after a difficult meeting persists into a later conversation. The research question is whether earlier irritation predicts later irritation across repeated observations. This scenario illustrates carryover; it does not demonstrate a treatment effect or forecast an individual's depression risk.
