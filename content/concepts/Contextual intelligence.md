“Contextual intelligence” — Sternberg, 2019.

## Facets

- **and situational judgment** — Sternberg, 2019

Contextual intelligence is the external-world member of Robert Sternberg's triarchic theory of intelligence: the part of the theory that treats intelligence as how well a person manages the actual environment their life runs in. Where the componential sub-theory looks inside the head at learning, planning and evaluation, the contextual sub-theory locates intelligent behaviour in adaptation to a setting. The original entry's second facet, situational judgment, names the same idea from the assessment side: reading what a situation calls for before acting.

A peer-reviewed summary of the theory:

> "Sternberg (1984, 1985) proposed the triarchic theory of intelligence highlighting three sub-theories (i.e., contextual—intelligence related to the external world of the individual involving adaptation to the environment, componential—intelligence related to the individuals' internal world involving learning, planning, execution, and evaluation of intelligent behavior, and the two-facet sub-theory focusing on intelligence as attributed to the external and internal worlds." ([Suzuki et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11050987/))

The same article records where Sternberg later took the idea, in the theory of successful intelligence, defined as "one's ability to lead a meaningful life that is in line with their values by adapting to their sociocultural context, capitalizing on their strengths, and compensating for their weaknesses" — intelligence judged against a person's own context and values instead of a generic test. The article adds that on Sternberg's account intelligence "could not simply be understood as a cognitive trait as it could include practical and wisdom-based skills" depending on context. That is why the compendium's principles lean on the term: A038 (cultivate practical judgment), A048 (act in the right way, at the right time) and A104 (practise equity over strict legality) each turn on judging what the situation itself demands, with equity and timing read out of the circumstances rather than applied blind.
