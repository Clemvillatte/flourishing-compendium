Rumination is Susan Nolen-Hoeksema's term for a way of responding to distress: repetitively and passively focusing on one's symptoms and on their possible causes and consequences, rather than acting on the situation. The theory behind it, response styles theory (1991), was built to explain why this style of thinking and depression travel together.

> "According to the response styles theory, rumination is a mode of responding to distress that involves repetitively and passively focusing on symptoms of distress and on the possible causes and consequences of these symptoms. Rumination does not lead to active problem solving to change circumstances surrounding these symptoms. Instead, people who are ruminating remain fixated on the problems and on their feelings about them without taking action." (Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco and Lyubomirsky 2008, p. 400, [Perspectives on Psychological Science](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x))

### What the evidence supports

The 2008 review lists what survives testing: rumination "exacerbates depression, enhances negative thinking, impairs problem solving, interferes with instrumental behavior, and erodes social support." Experimentally induced rumination prolongs negative mood in dysphoric people. The measurement instrument, the Ruminative Responses Scale, asks how often a person thinks thoughts like "Why do I react this way?" or "I won't be able to do my job if I don't snap out of this" when feeling sad.

### What it does not

The original theory predicted rumination would extend depressive episodes; the evidence mostly disagrees. The review's verdict: "rumination appears to more consistently predict the onset of depression rather than the duration, but rumination interacts with negative cognitive styles to predict the duration of depressive symptoms." Self-reported rumination does not reliably predict how long an episode lasts. The scale itself has since been split into brooding, the maladaptive part, and reflection, a more deliberate self-focus (Treynor, Gonzalez and Nolen-Hoeksema 2003). The compendium's seed line, "past-focused repetitive thought predicts depression," is best read as a prediction of onset.
