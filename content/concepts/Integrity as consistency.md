“Integrity as consistency” — Emerson, Self-Reliance.

Emerson's essay is an odd source for this label, because it attacks one kind of consistency. The label is the compendium's summary, not Emerson's phrase. Read closely, the essay separates a consistency of surface (repeating what you said before) from a consistency of character, and it prizes only the second. The Stanford Encyclopedia's treatment of integrity helps say what kind of consistency is at stake.

### What Emerson rejects

> "A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds, adored by little statesmen and philosophers and divines. With consistency a great soul has simply nothing to do." (Emerson, Self-Reliance, [text at Emerson Central](https://emersoncentral.com/texts/essays-first-series/self-reliance/))

He asks, "Why drag about this corpse of your memory, lest you contradict somewhat you have stated in this or that public place?" His instruction is to "speak what you think now in hard words, and to-morrow speak what to-morrow thinks in hard words again, though it contradict every thing you said to-day." He adds that "to be great is to be misunderstood".

### What he keeps

In the same passage he claims a deeper unity.

> "A character is like an acrostic or Alexandrian stanza; — read it forward, backward, or across, it still spells the same thing." (Emerson, same essay)

He holds that "no man can violate his nature", and that a person's honest thoughts, recorded day by day, "will be found symmetrical, though I mean it not, and see it not". The consistency he trusts is not maintained by effort. It appears across the record of a life lived honestly.

### Philosophical accounts of integrity

The Stanford Encyclopedia describes two formal accounts. On the self-integration view, "integrity is a matter of persons integrating various parts of their personality into a harmonious, intact whole". On the identity view, to act with integrity "is just to act in a way that accurately reflects your sense of who you are; to act from motives, interests and commitments that are most deeply your own". ([Cox, La Caze & Levine, Integrity, sections 1 and 2](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/integrity/))

Both views are formal. The entry's objection is that someone can be wholly integrated and still lack integrity: "a person who sells used-cars for a living and is wholeheartedly dedicated to selling cars for as much money as possible" and will lie to do so "may well be perfectly integrated in Frankfurt's sense, but we should feel no temptation at all to describe them as having exemplary integrity". Consistency alone is therefore not enough.

The entry also lists rigidity, dogmatism and fanaticism among the traits that "can defeat integrity" because they suppress critical assessment. Emerson's attack on foolish consistency fits that warning.

### What this page does not claim

Emerson does not define integrity, and his essay praises nonconformity and self-trust. The compendium's principle [[A030 — Practice integrity — consistency between thought, word, and deed]] reads integrity as consistency between thought, word and deed. That reading belongs to the philosophy of integrity, as in the Stanford entry, and Emerson only supports the part about acting from one's own character.
