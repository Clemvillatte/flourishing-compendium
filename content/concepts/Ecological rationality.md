Ecological rationality is the idea that a simple decision rule is good or bad only relative to the environment it is used in. Gigerenzer and Gaissmaier's review defines heuristics as "efficient cognitive processes, conscious or unconscious, that ignore part of the information." The classical view held that because using heuristics saves effort, heuristic decisions "imply greater errors than do 'rational' decisions as defined by logic or statistical models." The authors answer that "for many decisions, the assumptions of rational models are not met, and it is an empirical rather than an a priori issue how well cognitive heuristics function in an uncertain world" ([Annual Review of Psychology, 2011, PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21126183/)).

### What the review concludes

The review reports "that (a) individuals and organizations often rely on simple heuristics in an adaptive way, and (b) ignoring part of the information can lead to more accurate judgments than weighting and adding all information, for instance for low predictability and small samples." It names as the next challenge a "systematic theory of the building blocks of heuristics as well as the core capacities and environmental structures these exploit" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21126183/)).

### Scope

The abstract supports the match between heuristic and setting: more information does not always give better judgments, and low predictability and small samples are named as conditions where less can do better. The review covers business organizations, health care and legal institutions. A fuller statement of the framework in Todd and Gigerenzer (2012) is not summarised here.
