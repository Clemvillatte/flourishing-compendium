Amor sui is Latin for love of self. Spinoza names the affect in the scholium to [Ethics IIIp55](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3800/3800-h/3800-h.htm): "the pleasure, which springs from the contemplation of ourselves, is called self-love or self-complacency."

Part IV gives this joy a rational footing. Reason "demands, that every man should love himself, should seek that which is useful to him" (IVp18, scholium): what is really useful preserves and strengthens one's being.

### What makes the love right

The difference lies in what the joy rests on. Self-approval is joy born of contemplating one's own power of action; the form arising from reason is "the highest possible" (IVp52), and its scholium calls it "the highest object for which we can hope". Pride is pleasure "arising from the false opinion, whereby a man may consider himself superior to his fellows" (IVp57, scholium); dejection, the false opinion of inferiority, is its twin.

"Extreme pride or dejection indicates extreme infirmity of spirit" (IVp56); the proof runs through ignorance of oneself, the foundation of virtue. The Stanford Encyclopedia reads Part IV as psychological and ethical egoism: virtue is power of acting.

Right self-love turns outward on its own. "The good which every man, who follows after virtue, desires for himself he will also desire for other men" (IVp37), and men guided by reason "desire for themselves nothing, which they do not also desire for the rest of mankind" (IVp18, scholium).

### A contested term

The phrase did not always carry this friendly sense. Augustine's [City of God](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120114.htm) draws its line between two loves, "the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self" (City of God XIV.28).

Aristotle had split the concept: the good man "should be a lover of self" for gratifying the most authoritative element in himself, the blamed self-lover for gratifying appetite ([Nicomachean Ethics IX.8](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.9.ix.html)). Kierkegaard's [Works of Love](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34339/chapter/327339576) (1847) argues the commandment to love the neighbour as oneself, properly understood, "also says the opposite: You shall love yourself in the right way" (Works of Love, p. 22).

### In the compendium

The compendium carries amor sui in its self-relationship principles. AF066 and SE025, "Cultivate self-compassion: treat yourself as you would a good friend," read right self-love as a practice. SE004, "Understand that self-esteem is not the same as self-worth," draws a parallel distinction between feedback-dependent and stable self-regard. SE012 and SE017, on self-relation as a trainable skill and on the inner critic, work this page's original facet, right self-love against self-contempt.

B097, "Wish well, starting with yourself," is the metta parallel; A015, "Practice magnanimity (megalopsychia)," the Aristotelian one. These are comparisons, not claims of influence.

Spinoza called honour rooted in popular approval "really empty, being nothing" (IVp58, scholium). Next to modern work on contingent self-esteem it reads like an early diagnosis of the fragile kind; that link is interpretation, not history.
