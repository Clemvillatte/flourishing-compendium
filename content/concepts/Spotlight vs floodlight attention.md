Spotlight vs floodlight attention names the aperture metaphor in the attention literature: attention can be narrowed to a detail or broadened to the whole, with different consequences for what is seen. The experimental basis is Navon's 1977 paper, "Forest before trees: The precedence of global features in visual perception," whose abstract states the finding: "The idea that global structuring of a visual scene precedes analysis of local features is suggested, discussed, and tested" ([Navon 1977, Cognitive Psychology 9(3), University of Haifa](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/forest-before-trees-the-precedence-of-global-features-in-visual-p)). The paper's own words on the temporal organization:

> "The idea put forward in this paper is that perceptual processes are temporally organized so that they proceed from global structuring towards more and more fine-grained analysis. In other words, a scene is decomposed rather than built up."

([Navon 1977, p. 354, PDF at cognitionandculture.net](https://cognitionandculture.net/wp-content/uploads/Navon.pdf).)

### The asymmetry

The finding is directional, not symmetrical. When the global and local levels of a compound figure conflict, "global cues which conflicted with the local ones did inhibit the responses to the local level," while the identity of the small characters had no effect on recognition of the large ones ([Navon 1977 abstract](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/forest-before-trees-the-precedence-of-global-features-in-visual-p)). Navon concluded that this supports "the notion that global processing is a necessary stage of perception prior to more fine-grained analysis" ([quoted in the SDU review](https://findresearcher.sdu.dk:8443/ws/files/157108036/Whos_got_the_global_advantage_R2.pdf)).

### The regulative half

Förster and Higgins's regulatory mode work adds the second half of my pairing: the attentional aperture widens or narrows with the person's regulatory state, so that "attending broadly versus narrowly" is a state that can be induced ([Förster and Higgins 2005, as summarized in the Lehigh thesis](https://preserve.lehigh.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2313&context=etd)). My principle on using attention as a spotlight holds both halves: narrow for focus, broad for context, and the evidence that the broad view has temporal priority in the first pass.
