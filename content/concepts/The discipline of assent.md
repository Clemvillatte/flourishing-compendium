“The discipline of assent” — Epictetus, Discourses.

The discipline of assent is the Stoic training in treating every impression as a candidate for judgment rather than a judgment already made. Epictetus places it third among the three exercises of the philosopher:

> "There are three things (topics, τόποι) in which a man ought to exercise himself who would be wise and good. The first concerns the desires and the aversions, that a man may not fail to get what he desires, and that he may not fall into that which he does not desire. The second concerns the movements (towards an object) and the movements from an object, and generally in doing what a man ought to do, that he may act according to order, to reason, and not carelessly. The third thing concerns freedom from deception and rashness in judgment, and generally it concerns the assents (συγκαταθέσεις)." (Epictetus, Discourses 3.2, [Long translation, Standard Ebooks](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/epictetus/discourses/george-long/text/single-page))

The third topic guards the other two, "so that not even in sleep any appearance unexamined may surprise us, nor in intoxication, nor in melancholy" (same chapter). The handbook's most quoted line gives the underlying claim:

> "Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things; for example, death is nothing terrible, for if it were it would have seemed so to Socrates; for the opinion about death that it is terrible, is the terrible thing." (Epictetus, Enchiridion 5, [Long translation, Project Gutenberg ebook #10661](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10661/10661-h/10661-h.htm))

### The watch at the gate

The training itself is a pause before the impression is accepted, in the chapter on exercise:

> "For as Socrates said, we ought not to live a life without examination, so we ought not to accept an appearance without examination, but we should say, 'Wait, let me see what you are and whence you come;' like the watch at night (who says) 'Show me the pass (the Roman tessera). Have you the signal from nature which the appearance that may be accepted ought to have?'" (Discourses 3.12, [same translation](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/epictetus/discourses/george-long/text/single-page))

The discipline is possible because the understanding has a nature to work with:

> "What is the cause of assenting to anything? The fact that it appears to be true. It is not possible then to assent to that which appears not to be true. Why? Because this is the nature of the understanding: to incline to the true, to be dissatisfied with the false, and in matters uncertain to withhold assent." (Discourses 1.28, [same translation](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/epictetus/discourses/george-long/text/single-page))

The work, as the chapter on progress puts it, lies "in assent and suspension of assent, that you be not deceived" (Discourses 1.4, same translation). The seed's Spinoza line is preserved in the formulations below; the page's quotes are all Epictetus, the anchor the seed names.
