“The free spirit” — Nietzsche, Human, All Too Human.

Nietzsche subtitled Human, All Too Human "A Book for Free Spirits". The preface is dated Nice, Spring, 1886. This page draws on that preface in Alexander Harvey's English translation, from the Project Gutenberg text, which was opened. The main text of the book was not read for this page, so the page does not reconstruct Nietzsche's wider theory.

### A figure Nietzsche needed

The preface's most striking claim is that the free spirit is partly a construction.

> "Such "free spirits" do not really exist and never did exist." ([Nietzsche, Human, All Too Human, Preface, section 2](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/38145))

He explains that he needed them, as company in illness and loneliness, "to serve as gay spirits and comrades, with whom one may talk and laugh when one is disposed to talk and laugh". He also writes that he does not doubt such free spirits may come, and that he sees them "already coming, slowly, slowly". The free spirit is thus both an invented companion and a hope.

### The liberation

In section 3 he conjectures that

> "a soul in which the type of "free spirit" can attain maturity and completeness had its decisive and deciding event in the form of a great emancipation or unbinding"

and that before this event the soul seemed bound to its place. He asks "What binds strongest?" and answers that for noble natures the strongest cords are duty, reverence and gratitude toward what they were taught to honour. The liberation "comes suddenly to such prisoners, like an earthquake".

### Limits

The preface is a later, retrospective piece in Nietzsche's own voice, and is not a definition of the free spirit within the aphorisms of the book. My practices of detachment and wandering (D078, B125) are drawn from other traditions, and Nietzsche's passage does not discuss them. Whether liberation of this kind is available to most people, or desirable, is not addressed by the sources opened.
