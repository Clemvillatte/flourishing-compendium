Studies of heart-rate variability coherence — synchronized heart-rhythm patterns — as a physiological marker of composure and regulatory capacity.

This line of research trains people to breathe slowly while watching their own heart rate, with the aim of producing a large, regular, wave-like heart-rhythm pattern. The training procedure, as the field's main review describes it:

> "The procedure consists of feeding back beat by beat heart rate data during slow breathing maneuvers such that the participant tries to maximize RSA, create a sine-wave-like curve of peaks and valleys, and match RSA to heart rate patterns." (Lehrer and Gevirtz, [Heart rate variability biofeedback: how and why does it work?](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00756/full), Frontiers in Psychology 5, 2014)

### Why slow breathing produces the pattern

The mechanism is resonance. The cardiovascular system, stimulated through the baroreflex, oscillates with the greatest amplitude near 0.1 Hz, roughly six breaths per minute. When breathing is paced at that individual frequency, heart rate, blood pressure and respiration fall into a fixed phase relationship, and the oscillations compound rather than cancel. This is the physiology behind the "coherence" pattern: not a mystical state, but a large-amplitude rhythm the system produces when driven at its resonant frequency.

The evidence base is promising but uneven. The same review notes that "few areas have extensive support by way of controlled studies" while finding growing evidence for lower blood pressure in hypertensive patients, some improvement in athletic performance and emphysema symptoms, and possible reductions in depression and anxiety symptoms.
