Obedience theory, in Stanley Milgram's sense, is the finding that ordinary people will carry out destructive commands when authority supplies the frame. The founding paper is "Behavioral Study of Obedience" (Journal of Abnormal and Social Psychology, 1963). Its own abstract:

> It consists of ordering a naive S to administer increasingly more severe punishment to a victim in the context of a learning experiment. Punishment is administered by means of a shock generator with 30 graded switches ranging from Slight Shock to Danger: Severe Shock. The victim is a confederate of the E. The primary dependent variable is the maximum shock the S is willing to administer before he refuses to continue further. 26 Ss obeyed the experimental commands fully, and administered the highest shock on the generator.

(Milgram, 1963, [PDF](https://www.gvsu.edu/cms4/asset/F51281F0-00AF-E25A-5BF632E8D4A243C7/milgram_behavioral_study_1963.pdf))

Twenty-six of forty subjects went to the end of the generator. Milgram's own summary of the first surprise: "The experiment yielded two findings that were surprising. The first finding concerns the sheer strength of obedient tendencies manifested in this situation." The procedure also produced "extreme levels of nervous tension in some Ss," with "Profuse sweating, trembling, and stuttering."

The compendium's [[SO032 — Treat social influence as inevitable and manageable]] and [[B160 — Question authority — even the Buddha's]] state the practice this research grounds: the pressure to obey is a normal feature of situations, and the countermeasure is a rehearsed capacity to refuse.
