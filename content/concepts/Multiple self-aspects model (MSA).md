This entry concerns Allen R. McConnell's multiple self-aspects framework (MSF), presented in a 2011 review in Personality and Social Psychology Review. The framework proposes that the self-concept is not one store of self-knowledge but a collection of context-dependent self-aspects, such as roles, relationships, goals and group identities, represented in an associative memory network.

> "The multiple self-aspects framework (MSF) conceives of the self-concept as a collection of multiple, context-dependent selves." (McConnell, [The Multiple Self-Aspects Framework: Self-Concept Representation and Its Implications](https://allenmcconnell.net/pdfs/msf-PSPR-2011.pdf), Personality and Social Psychology Review 15(1), 2011, p. 3)

### Origin

McConnell, a social psychologist at Miami University in Ohio, built the MSF out of his earlier research on self-complexity, the line of work begun by Linville in 1985 that asked who copes better with stress. In the article he reports that the self-complexity literature's depiction of the self-concept struck him as underspecified and incomplete, and that this led him to develop "a more comprehensive account of self-concept representation grounded in self-complexity but extending into new areas" (p. 4). The paper was written against a backdrop he describes with Greenwald and Banaji's 1989 verdict that "the self's structure has not yet been well described" (p. 5).

### Core claims

The framework's first three principles, in McConnell's own words:

> "The self is a collection of multiple, context-dependent self-aspects." (p. 5)

> "Self-aspects are associated with personal attributes, which become more accessible when the self-aspect is activated and vice versa." (p. 5)

> "Overall affect reflects the evaluation of one's self-aspects weighted by their accessibility, and thus feedback about a self-aspect will affect general affective states to the extent that the information has implications for one's evaluation of that self-aspect." (p. 6)

### How the framework explains

The MSF is a mechanistic account: an associative network in memory, with activation doing the work. Context switches on some self-aspects rather than others, and the attributes linked to an active aspect become more accessible, shaping how events are read. Through spreading activation, feedback aimed at one aspect can reach connected aspects. The framework predicts that people whose self-aspects overlap heavily in attributes feel life events more strongly, because the feedback reaches a larger share of the self-concept; self-complexity research had observed this pattern and the MSF carries it as a direct consequence of Principle 3.

McConnell also compares the framework with compartmentalization research (Showers, 1992; Showers & Zeigler-Hill, 2003), which distinguishes self-aspects holding mostly positive or mostly negative attributes from aspects mixing both. His review discusses evidence that compartmentalized structures suit positive events and integrative ones cushion negative events, and argues the MSF adds mechanisms to explain why.

### In everyday life

The paper's worked example is a student it calls Rachel. Her self-aspects include roles (daughter, student), social identities (being Jewish, sorority sister) and a relationship (Mike's girlfriend). At the library she is the student; at dinner with Mike's family she is the girlfriend. An unpleasant breakup is bad feedback for the girlfriend aspect, and how far it colors her general mood depends on how much of her self-concept that aspect touches.
