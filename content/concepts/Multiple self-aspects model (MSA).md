The multiple self-aspects model is a social-psychological account of how self-knowledge is organised. Its fullest statement is Allen McConnell's multiple self-aspects framework (MSF), set out in a 2011 review in Personality and Social Psychology Review. The self-concept is a collection of context-dependent self-aspects, such as roles, relationships, goals and group identities, stored in an associative memory network. The context a person is in activates some aspects and leaves others dormant, and the active aspects supply the attributes through which events are interpreted.

> "The multiple self-aspects framework (MSF) conceives of the self-concept as a collection of multiple, context-dependent selves." (McConnell, [The Multiple Self-Aspects Framework: Self-Concept Representation and Its Implications](https://allenmcconnell.net/pdfs/msf-PSPR-2011.pdf), Personality and Social Psychology Review 15(1), 2011, p. 3)

### What the aspects do

Self-aspects vary in accessibility, and only the accessible ones shape the moment. Affect in the MSF reflects the evaluation of whichever aspects are active, so the same piece of feedback lands differently depending on which aspect receives it. The framework grew out of earlier self-complexity research, which had asked who copes better with stress; McConnell judged that literature's picture of the self "underspecified and incomplete" and built the MSF as a fuller account, grounded in self-complexity but extending it.

The MSF also absorbs the compartmentalization literature (Showers, 1992; Showers & Zeigler-Hill, 2003), which asks whether a self-aspect's attributes are mostly of one valence or mixed. Compartmentalized self-concepts amplify positive events and integrated ones cushion negative ones, a pattern McConnell treats as consistent with his framework while adding mechanisms of his own.
