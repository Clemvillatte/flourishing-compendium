Diversion is Pascal's name for the whole apparatus of activity — play, war, hunting, office, society — by which human beings keep themselves from thinking about their own condition. His most quoted sentence comes from the long fragment numbered 139 in the Trotter translation:

> "I have discovered that all the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber." (Pascal, Pensées §139, [Trotter translation, Project Gutenberg #18269](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/18269/18269-0.txt))

Pascal's diagnosis is not moral but psychological: the king surrounded by pleasure, "if he be without what is called diversion, he is unhappy, and more unhappy than the least of his subjects who plays and diverts himself". The activity does not produce happiness; it produces forgetting. "We do not seek that easy and peaceful lot which permits us to think of our unhappy condition... but the bustle which averts these thoughts of ours, and amuses us."

The mechanism is drawn with hunter's precision: "The hare in itself would not screen us from the sight of death and calamities; but the chase which turns away our attention from these, does screen us." And Pascal is careful not to blame the seekers: "their error does not lie in seeking excitement, if they seek it only as a diversion; the evil is that they seek it as if the possession of the objects of their quest would make them really happy." The test is stated in one line: "If our condition were truly happy, we would not need diversion from thinking of it in order to make ourselves happy."
