Diversion is Pascal's name for the whole apparatus of activity — play, war, hunting, office, society — by which human beings keep themselves from thinking about their own condition. The long fragment numbered 139 in the Trotter translation states it:

> "I have discovered that all the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber." (Pascal, Pensées §139, [Trotter translation, Project Gutenberg #18269](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/18269/18269-0.txt))

Pascal imagines a king attended with every pleasure he can feel: "if he be without what is called diversion, he is unhappy, and more unhappy than the least of his subjects who plays and diverts himself". On his account the sought-after bustle averts the thoughts of one's condition: "We do not seek that easy and peaceful lot which permits us to think of our unhappy condition", but "the bustle which averts these thoughts of ours, and amuses us".

The hare passage gives the mechanism: "The hare in itself would not screen us from the sight of death and calamities; but the chase which turns away our attention from these, does screen us." Pascal adds that "we are wrong in blaming them": "Their error does not lie in seeking excitement, if they seek it only as a diversion; the evil is that they seek it as if the possession of the objects of their quest would make them really happy." The test is stated in one line: "If our condition were truly happy, we would not need diversion from thinking of it in order to make ourselves happy."
