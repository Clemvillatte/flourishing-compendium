Guarding the senses is the practice of taking care over what the eyes, ears and other senses take in, and over how the mind then reacts to it. Two ancient traditions in the compendium's sources state it in different ways, and they should not be merged into one idea.

### In the Dhammapada

The Dhammapada's chapter on the monk begins with restraint at each sense door:

> Good is restraint over the eye; good is restraint over the ear; good is restraint over the nose; good is restraint over the tongue. Good is restraint in the body; good is restraint in speech; good is restraint in thought. Restraint everywhere is good. The monk restrained in every way is freed from all suffering.

(Dhammapada 360-361, trans. Acharya Buddharakkhita, [Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/dhp/dhp.25.budd.html).) The list runs from the senses to speech and thought, so restraint here covers the whole of conduct and not only perception.

### Indriya-samvara in the discourses

The Pali term the seed uses, indriya-samvara, appears as "restraint over the sense faculties" in the Samannaphala Sutta (Digha Nikaya 2). Its account of how a monk guards the doors of his senses:

> On seeing a form with the eye, he does not grasp at any theme or details by which — if he were to dwell without restraint over the faculty of the eye — evil, unskillful qualities such as greed or distress might assail him.

(DN 2, trans. Thanissaro Bhikkhu, [Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/dn/dn.02.0.than.html).) The text goes on to say the same of the ear, nose, tongue, body and intellect. Note what is being guarded against: grasping at themes and details, which then let greed or distress in. The instruction is about the mind's reaction, not about keeping the eyes shut. The sutra places this restraint alongside mindfulness and alertness and contentedness in its sequence of training.

### In the Daodejing

Chapter 12 uses the same human facts for a different argument. Legge's verse rendering:

> Colour's five hues from th' eyes their sight will take;
> Music's five notes the ears as deaf can make;
> The flavours five deprive the mouth of taste;

(Daodejing 12, trans. James Legge, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt).) Its prose line then says the sage "seeks to satisfy (the craving of) the belly, and not the (insatiable longing of the) eyes." Here the worry is that overstimulation dulls the senses and feeds craving. This text does not mention evil qualities or a training sequence.

### What the two share, and where they differ

Both texts treat unbridled sensory appetite as costly. The Buddhist passages describe a trained way of meeting experience inside a monastic path. The Daoist chapter describes a cost of excess and a preference for what satisfies a basic need. The compendium's modern principles on phones, news and interruptions are applications of this theme; neither text speaks of them, and any claim that they do would be the compendium's own.
