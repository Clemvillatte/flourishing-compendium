Golman and Loewenstein describe an information-gap account in which curiosity arises when a person becomes aware of a gap in their knowledge that information could fill ([author-hosted paper](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf)). Aristotle writes that people began to philosophize through wonder at difficulties ([Ross translation, ELLOPOS page 5](https://www.ellopos.net/elpenor/greek-texts/ancient-greece/aristotle/metaphysics.asp?pg=5)).

> "For it is owing to their wonder that men both now begin and at first began to philosophize; they wondered originally at the obvious difficulties, then advanced little by little and stated difficulties about the greater matters, e.g. about the phenomena of the moon and those of the sun and of the stars, and about the genesis of the universe." (Aristotle, Metaphysics, W. D. Ross translation, [ELLOPOS full text, page 5](https://www.ellopos.net/elpenor/greek-texts/ancient-greece/aristotle/metaphysics.asp?pg=5), the passage beginning "For it is owing to their wonder")

### The information gap

Golman and Loewenstein describe the information-gap account: "In a review of the psychological literature on curiosity, one of the authors of this paper (Loewenstein, 1994) introduced the concept of an information gap and proposed that curiosity occurs when an individual becomes aware of a gap in his or her knowledge that could potentially be filled by information. That is, a gap opens when a question becomes activated but the answer is not known with certainty" (Golman and Loewenstein, [Curiosity, Information Gaps, and the Utility of Knowledge](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/golman/golman_loewenstein_curiosity.pdf), author-hosted paper dated August 4, 2014, section 4.2 "Curiosity").
