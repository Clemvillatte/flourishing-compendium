"Naming the passions" is the compendium's heading for the project Spinoza carries out in Part III of the Ethics: the passions are not merely suffered, they can be named, defined and known like anything else in nature. Spinoza opens the part with the complaint that "no one, so far as I know, has defined the nature and strength of the emotions, and the power of the mind against them for their restraint" ([Spinoza, Ethics III, Preface](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3800/3800-h/3800-h.htm)). His remedy is the geometric method: "I shall consider human actions and desires in exactly the same manner, as though I were concerned with lines, planes, and solids" ([Spinoza, Ethics III, Preface](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3800/3800-h/3800-h.htm)).

> "I shall, therefore, treat of the nature and strength of the emotions according to the same method, as I employed heretofore in my investigations concerning God and the mind. I shall consider human actions and desires in exactly the same manner, as though I were concerned with lines, planes, and solids." (Spinoza, Ethics III, Preface, [Elwes translation, Project Gutenberg #3800](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3800/3800-h/3800-h.htm))

What makes a passion nameable is that it follows law rather than caprice: "the passions of hatred, anger, envy, and so on, considered in themselves, follow from this same necessity and efficacy of nature; they answer to certain definite causes, through which they are understood, and possess certain properties as worthy of being known as the properties of anything else" ([Spinoza, Ethics III, Preface](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3800/3800-h/3800-h.htm)). A named passion is one whose cause and properties can be inspected instead of merely undergone.

### The definitions that do the naming

Part III ends with a formal catalogue, the "Definitions of the Emotions," in which each passion receives a definition the way a figure receives its properties in geometry. Desire is defined first: "Desire is the actual essence of man, in so far as it is conceived, as determined to a particular activity by some given modification of itself" ([Spinoza, Ethics III, Definitions of the Emotions I](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3800/3800-h/3800-h.htm)). Pleasure and pain are defined as transitions: "Pleasure is the transition of a man from a less to a greater perfection" and "Pain is the transition of a man from a greater to a less perfection" ([Spinoza, Ethics III, Definitions of the Emotions II-III](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3800/3800-h/3800-h.htm)). Love gets the same treatment: "Love is pleasure, accompanied by the idea of an external cause" ([Spinoza, Ethics III, Definitions of the Emotions VI](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3800/3800-h/3800-h.htm)).

The catalogue is not an unbounded list. Spinoza reduces the whole vocabulary to a small base: "I, therefore, recognize only three primitive or primary emotions (as I said in the note to III. xi.), namely, pleasure, pain, and desire" ([Spinoza, Ethics III, Definitions of the Emotions, after IV](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3800/3800-h/3800-h.htm)). To name a passion on his model is to trace it to one of these three together with its cause — the compendium's link from this practice to the modern study of naming feelings precisely is carried by the AF004 principle, which cites this page.
