**Mixed emotions theory Miyamoto** — mixed positive and negative emotion is culturally patterned; East Asian contexts invite co-occurrence more than Western ones.

This page covers the cross-cultural research programme on mixed emotions associated with Yuri Miyamoto, here represented by the study she conducted with Yukiko Uchida and Phoebe Ellsworth, published in Emotion in 2010. The abstract's summary of what the studies found:

> "The findings suggest that both Americans and Japanese feel mixed emotions, but the kinds of situation in which they typically do so depends on culture." ([Miyamoto, Uchida & Ellsworth, Emotion 10(3), 2010, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20515228/))

### The specific pattern

The cultural difference is situational, not general. When respondents described situations where they felt mixed emotions, Japanese and Americans listed mostly similar situations. But when those situations were presented to another group, "Japanese reported more mixed emotions than Americans in the predominantly pleasant situations, whereas there were no cultural differences in mixed emotions in the predominantly unpleasant situations or the mixed situations" ([same record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20515228/)). The appraisal of self-agency mediated that cultural difference in the pleasant situations, and a second study replicated the pattern by asking participants to recall past pleasant, unpleasant and mixed situations.

The abstract situates the work against earlier correlation studies: previous cross-cultural comparisons had found East Asians more likely than Americans to feel dialectical emotions, while this study examined co-occurrence in a given situation.
