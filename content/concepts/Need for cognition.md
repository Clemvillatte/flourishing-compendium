Need for cognition is a stable individual difference in how much people engage in and enjoy effortful thinking. Cacioppo and Petty introduced it in 1982 with a scale that asked about exactly that tendency, "the tendency for an individual to engage in and enjoy thinking" (Cacioppo and Petty, "The need for cognition," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 42(1), 1982, abstract, via [SciSpace](https://scispace.com/papers/the-need-for-cognition-90br3uqkcp)).

> "NC was conceptualized as reflecting a stable intrinsic motivation that developed over time rather than a need in the traditional sense (i.e., a source of energy that motivates behavior)." (Petty, Brinol, Loersch and McCaslin, "The Need for Cognition," in Leary and Hoyle (eds.), Handbook of Individual Differences in Social Behavior, Guilford, 2009, pp. 318-329, [PDF](https://richardepetty.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/01/2009-need-cog-petty-brinol-loersch-mccaslin.pdf))

### Motivation, not ability

The same chapter says the construct taps differences in motivation rather than ability, because need for cognition "is only moderately related to measures of cognitive ability (e.g., verbal intelligence) and continues to predict relevant outcomes after cognitive ability is controlled." The 1982 abstract is consistent with this: in its third study, need for cognition correlated positively with general intelligence, weakly and negatively with close-mindedness, and not at all with social desirability.

### What the 1982 studies show

The scale was built and validated across four studies. Faculty members stood in for high need for cognition and assembly-line workers for low, and the scale did not track test anxiety. Study 4 reported that attitudes toward simple and complex versions of a cognitive task looked the same until the participants' need for cognition was taken into account.
