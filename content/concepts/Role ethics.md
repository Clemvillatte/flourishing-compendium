Role ethics is the family of views on which a person's roles and relationships, such as parent, child, citizen or member of a profession, are central to what is morally required and to who the person is. The compendium's seed cites two very different sources, Confucius's Analects and MacIntyre's After Virtue, so this page keeps them apart.

### The Confucian version

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy says Confucian ethics can be characterised "as a relational ethic, meaning that it is in part distinguished by its placement of relationships at the center of a well-lived life," and it points to Ames (2011, 2021) for an "alternative role-ethic characterization" ([SEP, Chinese Ethics, sections 2.3 and 2.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/)). The Confucius entry reports that Henry Rosemont and Roger Ames "argue that prescribed social roles are a defining characteristic of the 'Confucian tradition'," and contrast this with a "virtue ethics" approach that they say needs rational calculation ([SEP, Confucius, section 5](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/confucius/)). The Analects passage usually brought to this theme is a reply about government:

> "There is government, when the prince is prince, and the minister is minister; when the father is father, and the son is son." (Analects XII.11, Legge translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3330/pg3330.html))

The passage itself concerns government. Reading it as a statement about persons and roles is an interpretation. A recent philosophical paper summarises what defenders of Confucian role ethics claim: that persons are constituted by their social roles and relationships, and that roles and relationships are the source of ethical normativity. It quotes Rosemont (1991): "I am the totality of roles I live in relation to specific others" ([Springer paper on the metaphysics of personhood in Confucian role ethics](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s44204-023-00120-z.pdf)). That paper presents these as claims defenders make, and it treats them as contested.

### MacIntyre's version

In After Virtue, MacIntyre writes that "we all approach our own circumstances as bearers of a particular social identity. I am someone's son or daughter, someone else's cousin or uncle; I am a citizen of this or that city, a member of this or that guild or profession... Hence what is good for me has to be the good for one who inhabits these roles." The same passage says these inherited debts and obligations "constitute the given of my life, my moral starting point" (After Virtue, p. 220, [PDF excerpt](https://epistemh.pbworks.com/f/4.+Macintyre.pdf)). Earlier he says "we enter human society, that is, with one or more imputed characters- roles into which we have been drafted" (p. 216, [PDF excerpt](https://epistemh.pbworks.com/f/4.+Macintyre.pdf)).

The two views overlap on the claim that identity and obligation come partly from inherited roles. The sources opened here do not show that MacIntyre belongs to a "role ethics" school in the Ames and Rosemont sense. The label is applied to the pairing by the compendium.

### In the compendium

The principles listed (for example K284, S011 and SO027) use roles as a practical frame. The sources establish what Ames, Rosemont and MacIntyre say, not whether role-based ethics is correct or whether it can be reconciled with individual autonomy.
