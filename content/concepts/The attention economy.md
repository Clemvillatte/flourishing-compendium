mere smartphone presence reduces cognitive capacity (Ward et al., 2017).

The seed states as settled a finding that is contested. Ward and colleagues reported it in two experiments. Later replications and meta-analyses disagree about whether it holds, so this page presents it as an influential but unresolved result. The seed also bears on the "attention economy" as a concept, which the study does not test. It concerns one effect of a phone being nearby, not the market for attention.

### The original study

> "Results from two experiments indicate that even when people are successful at maintaining sustained attention—as when avoiding the temptation to check their phones—the mere presence of these devices reduces available cognitive capacity." (Ward, Duke, Gneezy & Bos 2017, Journal of the Association for Consumer Research 2(2):140-154, [abstract](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/ucpjacres/doi_3a10.1086_2f691462.htm))

The authors add that the cognitive costs were highest for people highest in smartphone dependence. They proposed that the phone occupies "limited-capacity cognitive resources". Their sample was nearly 800 smartphone users.

### Replications and meta-analyses

Two replications of Ward et al.'s second experiment, by Ruiz Pardo and Minda, did not replicate it: "there was no difference between smartphone location conditions on performance on either the o-span task or the go/no-go task" ([FORRT replication atlas](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1086/691462/)). A Swiss study with 302 participants found no effect of phone presence on short-term or prospective memory, though the authors say people should not take that as a reason to put phones on desks ([British Psychological Society Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/revisiting-brain-drain-effect)).

The meta-analyses point in different directions. A 2023 meta-analysis of 22 studies (43 effects) reported "a significant overall negative effect of smartphone use and presence", with memory and attention more affected than other domains ([Böttger, Poschik & Zierer 2023, Behavioral Sciences, PMC 10525686](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10525686/)). A four-level meta-analysis of 166 effect sizes from 33 studies and 4,368 participants found that mere presence "had no significant effect on cognitive outcomes (d = -0.02, SE = 0.02, 95% CI [-0.06, 0.01], p = .246)". ([Singapore Management University repository](https://ink.library.smu.edu.sg/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=5176&context=soss_research)) The first pooled "use and presence" together, which may explain part of the difference.

### Practical reading

The evidence does not support a firm claim that a phone on the desk costs working-memory capacity. A weaker claim stands: attention can be captured by personally relevant cues, and phones are built to be such cues. Whether this reduces measured cognitive capacity is not settled.

### What this page does not claim

Simon's argument that information consumes attention, which the seed's related-concept line attributes to this idea, was not opened here, and the market side of the attention economy is not covered.
