In Human Nature and Conduct, John Dewey treats impulse as the part of a person that can change old habits. "Impulses are the pivots upon which the re-organization of activities turn, they are agencies of deviation, for giving new directions to old habits and changing their quality." They bring "the possibility but not the assurance of a steady reorganization of habits to meet new elements in new situations" (Dewey, Human Nature and Conduct, Part Two, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/41386/pg41386.txt)).

### Impulse is a source, not a goal

Dewey rejects two positions. One sets up static goals "even when they are called perfection". The other idealizes "raw impulse" and finds in its spontaneities "an adequate mode of human freedom." His own view: "Impulse is a source, an indispensable source, of liberation; but only as it is employed in giving habits pertinence and freshness does it liberate power" ([Part Two](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/41386/pg41386.txt)).

### What makes an activity creative

In Part Two Dewey takes up a division of instincts into the creative and the acquisitive, and calls it "convenient, yet mistaken". His account: "native activity is both creative and acquisitive, creative as a process, acquisitive in that it terminates as a rule in some tangible product which brings the process to consciousness of itself." And: "Activity is creative in so far as it moves to its own enrichment as activity, that is, bringing along with itself a release of further activities." He lists "[s]cientific inquiry, artistic production, social companionship" as having this trait "to a marked degree", and adds that "there is here no antagonism between creative expression and the production of results which endure" ([Part Two](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/41386/pg41386.txt)).

His complaint about much industrial work is that the worker "neither understands the machines nor cares for their purpose". Where a person sees "the whole process of which their special work is a necessary part" and cares for the whole, "the mechanizing effect is counteracted" ([Part Two](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/41386/pg41386.txt)).
