Dispositional awe is the tendency to feel awe often, as a lasting trait and not only on the occasions when something vast appears. Piff and colleagues (2015) define awe as "an emotional response to perceptually vast stimuli that transcend current frames of reference" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25984788/)).

### What the studies found

Across five studies with 2,078 participants, the authors tested whether awe can diminish "the individual self and its concerns" and increase prosocial behavior. In a representative national sample, "dispositional tendencies to experience awe predicted greater generosity in an economic game above and beyond other prosocial emotions (e.g., compassion)." In experiments, inducing awe raised ethical decision-making, generosity and prosocial values. In the fifth study, participants who stood in a grove of towering trees showed more helping behavior and less entitlement than a control group. "Mediational data demonstrate that the effects of awe on prosociality are explained, in part, by feelings of a small self" ([PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25984788/)).

### Reading the result

The "small self" here is a feeling of being small next to something larger, and, according to the mediation data, it accounts in part for the extra generosity in these tasks. The abstract does not describe it as a loss of worth. The authors conclude that awe "may help situate individuals within broader social contexts and enhance collective concern."

The studies measure generosity in economic games and brief helping tasks. They do not follow people over years, so what the trait does across a whole life remains open.
