Conatus is the striving by which a thing persists in its being, which Spinoza identifies with that thing's actual essence in Ethics III.7 ([R. H. M. Elwes's translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3800/3800-h/3800-h.htm)). The preceding proposition states the general claim:

> "Everything, in so far as it is in itself, endeavours to persist in its own being." (Ethics III.6, [translated by R. H. M. Elwes](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/3800/3800-h/3800-h.htm).)

### Striving and conscious desire

The doctrine applies to finite things generally; it does not attribute conscious intentions to all of them ([Michael LeBuffe, Spinoza's Psychological Theory, section 1](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2022/entries/spinoza-psychological/)). In the human case, Spinoza defines desire as appetite together with consciousness of that appetite, at III.9's scholium ([LeBuffe, section 1](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2022/entries/spinoza-psychological/)).

LeBuffe cautions against reducing this account to a conscious wish for survival: striving can include changing one's condition to know more and live with greater power ([section 1.3](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2022/entries/spinoza-psychological/)). Particular desires may also follow confused ideas, so having a desire does not guarantee understanding what will preserve one's being ([LeBuffe, section 1.3](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2022/entries/spinoza-psychological/)).

### Joy and sadness

In Spinoza's account, joy and sadness concern transitions to greater and lesser perfection, understood through increases and decreases in the power of acting ([LeBuffe, section 2.1](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2022/entries/spinoza-psychological/)). This is a claim within his philosophical psychology, not a modern clinical measure of someone's health.
