Situationism is the challenge to trait psychology from experimental social psychology: situations move behavior more than stable character traits predict. The SEP article on moral character states the philosophical reading:

> Philosophers impressed by this tradition in experimental social psychology - which is often labeled "situationism" - have denied that traits of character are stable, consistent, or evaluatively integrated in the way that ancient or contemporary philosophers suggest.

(Marcia Homiak, "Moral Character," [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/moral-character/))

The article's own verdict on the tradition's evidence: "Perhaps most damning for the robust view of character are the results of the experiments conducted by Stanley Milgram in the 1960s." The studies behind the label include the Milgram obedience series, Darley and Batson's Good Samaritan study (seminarians in a hurry), and the classic person-situation findings summarized by Ross and Nisbett, The Person and the Situation (1991).

The compendium works this as a caution rather than a refutation: [[P005 — You are an organism in transaction with an environment]] runs on the situationist ground, and the owner's seed reads it against Aristotle's virtue account ([[Virtue]]). The honest framing is that situationism showed trait talk needs situational qualifiers, not that character does not exist.

- Doris, Lack of Character
