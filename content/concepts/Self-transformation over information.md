'Self-transformation over information' names Pierre Hadot's account of philosophy as a way of life, in which the schools taught doctrines but the doctrines served a practice whose object was the practitioner. The Stoics supply the sharpest version of the distinction: logic, physics and ethics are parts of philosophical discourse, cut up for teaching, while philosophy itself stays whole.

> "For the Stoics, the parts of philosophy—physics, ethics, and logic—were not, in fact, parts of philosophy itself, but rather parts of philosophical discourse. But philosophy itself—that is, the philosophical way of life—is no longer a theory divided into parts, but a unitary act, which consists in living logic, physics, and ethics" (Hadot, Philosophy as a Way of Life, [full text](https://vdoc.pub/documents/philosophy-as-a-way-of-life-spiritual-exercises-from-socrates-to-foucault-5e96amgvfim0)).

The same text describes philosophy proper, as an exercise of wisdom, as a single act renewed at every instant, describable without breaking its unity as the exercise of logic, of physics or of ethics, according to the directions in which it is exercised. The parts appear distinct and separate only in the discourse of teaching and exposition of the philosophical dogmas ([Hadot, Philosophy as a Way of Life](https://vdoc.pub/documents/philosophy-as-a-way-of-life-spiritual-exercises-from-socrates-to-foucault-5e96amgvfim0)).

### What the exercises are for

The instrument of that act is what Hadot calls spiritual exercises: "The object of spiritual exercises is precisely to bring about this transformation" (Philosophy as a Way of Life). The model is athletic, not academic: "just as, by dint of repeated physical exercises, athletes give new form and strength to their bodies, so the philosopher develops his strength of soul, modifies his inner climate, transforms his vision of the world, and, finally, his entire being" (Philosophy as a Way of Life).
