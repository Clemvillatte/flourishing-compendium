The PEN model is Hans Eysenck's biological theory of personality: three superfactors, Psychoticism, Extraversion and Neuroticism, "comprised of three personality dimensions based on psychophysiology" ([personalityresearch.org](http://www.personalityresearch.org/pen.html)). Descriptively, "the descriptive aspect of the model is a hierarchical taxonomy based on factor analysis", with the superfactors at the top of the hierarchy.

### Each dimension, one arousal system

The model's signature move is tying each dimension to a biological system. Extraversion is "based on cortical arousal": "extraverts are chronically under-aroused and bored and are therefore in need of external stimulation to bring them UP to an optimal level of performance" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eysenck_Personality_Questionnaire)). Neuroticism is "based on activation thresholds in the sympathetic nervous system or visceral brain", the system "responsible for the fight-or-flight response". Psychoticism, the least-researched factor, was given a hormonal basis: "the research that has been done has indicated that Psychoticism too has a biological basis: increased testosterone levels" ([personalityresearch.org](http://www.personalityresearch.org/pen.html)).

### The model's status

The taxonomy is now considered outdated: the psychoticism entry calls it "his outdated P–E–N... model of personality", with psychoticism including "aggression, coldness, egocentrism, impulsivity, lack of empathy, tough-mindedness, and being antisocial" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychoticism)). The five-factor model superseded it as the standard taxonomy, though Eysenck's E and N dimensions survive inside it.
