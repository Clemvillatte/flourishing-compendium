Pu (朴) is Laozi's image for original simplicity: wood that has not yet been cut into anything. The Daodejing uses the word repeatedly, in chapters 15, 19, 28, 32 and 37. In James Legge's translation, which renders it as "simplicity" and "unwrought material", chapter 28 gives the figure its edge:

> "The unwrought material, when divided and distributed, forms vessels. The sage, when employed, becomes the Head of all the Officers (of government); and in his greatest regulations he employs no violent measures." (Daodejing 28, Legge translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/216))

The block is not empty. Cutting it makes vessels, which is useful, but each vessel is one shape and the block was many. The sage who holds to the block can serve in a role without being fixed by it.

### Desire and the nameless

Chapter 37 ties the block to stillness. After the line about doing nothing and leaving nothing undone, Legge has the speaker say:

> "If this transformation became to me an object of desire, I would express the desire by the nameless simplicity. Simplicity without a name Is free from all external aim. With no desire, at rest and still, All things go right as of their will." (Daodejing 37, Legge translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/216))

Here pu is what remains when naming, wanting and aiming fall quiet. Elsewhere, in chapter 19, Legge's version urges "simple views, and courses plain and true" in place of the cultivated virtues of the day.

### Reading it as a practice

One reading, by interpretation rather than translation, takes pu as a practice of subtraction. Roles, credentials, opinions and performances are layers carved onto a person, and the exercise is to notice which of them are being kept up out of habit or fear, then act from what remains. The Daodejing does not set out such a method, so treat this as a modern use of the image, not a claim about what Laozi taught.

### What it is not

Pu is not ignorance or childish naivety. In chapter 28 it pairs with the "simple infant", but the sage in the same chapter holds high office. And it is not a claim that experience leaves a person untouched; the images suggest only that the uncut state is the one from which any shape remains possible.
