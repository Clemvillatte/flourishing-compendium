Pragmatism is a philosophical tradition that understands knowing the world as inseparable from agency within it — it views language and thought as tools for prediction, problem solving, and action, rather than as mirrors of a pre-existing reality ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/)). The tradition began in the United States around 1870; its classical figures were Charles Sanders Peirce (1839–1914), William James (1842–1910), and John Dewey (1859–1952) ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/)).

## The Pragmatic Maxim

The core of pragmatism, as Peirce originally conceived it, was the Pragmatic Maxim — a rule for clarifying the meaning of hypotheses by tracing their practical consequences, their implications for experience in specific situations ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/)). Peirce's 1878 formulation reads: "Consider what effects, which might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then, our conception of those effects is the whole of our conception of the object" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/)). The Maxim treats concepts as instruments: their content consists in what they predict and how they guide conduct. For Peirce, those consequences are general and intelligible — they concern laws governing how things behave and how people might act in light of those laws — and he treated the Maxim as a logical principle of scientific methodology ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/)).

## Inquiry and the Method of Science

Peirce describes inquiry as a struggle to replace doubt with settled belief, contrasting three other methods of fixing belief — the Method of Tenacity (refusing to consider contrary evidence), the Method of Authority (accepting an institution's dictates), and the A Priori Method (developing the most elegant-seeming belief-set) — and arguing that only the Method of Science, which draws on the Pragmatic Maxim, can withstand scrutiny of its own correctness ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/)). He emphasizes the communal, fallible, and self-correcting character of inquiry: "We individually cannot reasonably hope to attain the ultimate philosophy which we pursue; we can only seek it, therefore, for the community of philosophers" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/)).

## James on truth and meaning

Pragmatism is both a theory of meaning and a theory of truth ([Routledge Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://www.rep.routledge.com/articles/biographical/james-william-1842-1910/v-1/sections/pragmatism-1)). As a theory of meaning it aims at clarity — our thoughts of an object are clear when we know what effects it will have and what reactions we are to prepare ([Routledge Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://www.rep.routledge.com/articles/biographical/james-william-1842-1910/v-1/sections/pragmatism-1)). James presents pragmatism as "a method for settling metaphysical disputes that might otherwise be interminable" — a dispute is idle if no practical difference would follow from either side being correct ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/)). Importantly, contrary to a widespread misunderstanding, this does not mean that James defines truth as that which is useful; rather, he points out that it is, in fact, useful to believe what is true ([Routledge Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://www.rep.routledge.com/articles/biographical/james-william-1842-1910/v-1/sections/pragmatism-1)).

## Dewey's reconstruction

Dewey reconstructs inquiry as beginning with a problem in an objectively indeterminate situation, whose aim is "the controlled or directed transformation of an indeterminate situation into one that is so determinate in its constituent distinctions and relations as to convert the elements of the original situation into a unified whole" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/)). He rejects a sharp division between theoretical belief and practical deliberation — in some sense all inquiry is practical, concerned with transforming and evaluating the situations in which we find ourselves ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/)).

## What it is NOT

Pragmatism is not the crude claim that "truth is whatever is useful" — that caricature is explicitly rejected ([Routledge Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://www.rep.routledge.com/articles/biographical/james-william-1842-1910/v-1/sections/pragmatism-1)). It is not a licence for wishful thinking or short-term expediency; beliefs are held to the standard of the long-run outcomes of the community's inquiry ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/)). And it is not a refusal to make truth-claims — it is a theory about what truth-claims mean and how they are tested.

## Practical implications

For flourishing: (1) treat your beliefs as instruments — when stuck on an abstract question, ask what concrete difference each answer would make to how you live; (2) prefer experiment to argument; (3) treat your life itself as an ongoing inquiry, transforming indeterminate situations into determinate ones ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pragmatism/)).
