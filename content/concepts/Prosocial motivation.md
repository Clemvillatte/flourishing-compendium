Prosocial motivation is the desire to benefit other people. Grant and Berg define it as "the desire to protect and promote the well-being of others," and add that it "is distinct from altruism and independent of self-interested motivations": it "refers to a concern for others, not a concern for others at the expense of self-interest" ([Grant & Berg, "Prosocial Motivation at Work: When, Why, and How Making a Difference Makes a Difference," chapter, author copy](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/06/GrantBerg_ProSocialHandbookFinal.pdf)).

> "Researchers have obtained conflicting results about the role of prosocial motivation in persistence, performance, and productivity. To resolve this discrepancy, I draw on self-determination theory, proposing that prosocial motivation is most likely to predict these outcomes when it is accompanied by intrinsic motivation." (Grant, 2008, Journal of Applied Psychology 93(1), 48-58, [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18211134/))

### What Grant found

Grant's two field studies used working samples. "In Study 1, intrinsic motivation strengthened the relationship between prosocial motivation and the overtime hour persistence of 58 firefighters. In Study 2, intrinsic motivation strengthened the relationship between prosocial motivation and the performance and productivity of 140 fundraising callers." Callers high in both motivations "raised more money 1 month later" (same abstract). The result concerns work outcomes, and it says the combination matters. It does not show that prosocial motivation improves well-being, which is the page's facet.

### Helping and well-being

The well-being link comes from Weinstein and Ryan (2010). Their four studies tested autonomous versus controlled motivation for helping, and "findings support the idea that autonomous motivation for helping yields benefits for both helper and recipient through greater need satisfaction" ([Weinstein & Ryan, 2010, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 98(2), 222-244, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20085397/)). The point is the quality of the motivation: helping that feels chosen, not pressured, is the condition under which the benefits appear in that work.

### In the compendium

The facet "and collective well-being" has no direct support in the abstracts opened here. Grant's studies measured persistence, performance and productivity, and Weinstein and Ryan measured helper and recipient well-being in laboratory and diary settings. Statements about collective well-being are the compendium's extension.
