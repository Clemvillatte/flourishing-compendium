### Definition

The Kalama principle is the Buddha's instruction, given to a clan beset by competing preachers, to test every teaching against one's own experience of its fruits. The sutta's core passage:

> Of course you are uncertain, Kalamas. Of course you are in doubt. When there are reasons for doubt, uncertainty is born. So in this case, Kalamas, don't go by reports, by legends, by traditions, by scripture, by logical conjecture, by inference, by analogies, by agreement through pondering views, by probability, or by the thought, 'This contemplative is our teacher.' When you know for yourselves that, 'These qualities are unskillful; these qualities are blameworthy; these qualities are criticized by the wise; these qualities, when adopted & carried out, lead to harm & to suffering' — then you should abandon them. ([AN 3.65, tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an03/an03.065.than.html))

### Origin

The instruction appears in the discourse to the Kalamas of Kesaputta (AN 3.65). The Buddha there does not recommend doubt as a resting place; he recommends it as a starting gate. The test is not personal taste but observable consequence: whether a practice, adopted and carried out, leads to harm or to welfare.

### A real-life example

A person inherits a harsh view of themselves from family and tradition, the kind of thing "everyone knows". The Kalama question is empirical: what actually happens when I live by this? Carried out, the inherited judgment produces constriction and misery. That is data. The principle says the tradition, however ancient, does not outrank it.
