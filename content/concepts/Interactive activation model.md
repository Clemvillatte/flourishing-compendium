The interactive activation model is a model of letter and word perception published by James L. McClelland and David E. Rumelhart in 1981. It proposes detectors at three levels (visual features, letters and words) whose excitatory and inhibitory interactions, running in parallel, produce perception.

> "The basic idea is that the presentation of a string of letters begins the process of activating detectors for letters that are consistent with the visual input. As these activations grow stronger, they begin to activate detectors for words that are consistent with the letters, if there are any. The active word detectors then produce feedback, which reinforces the activations of the detectors for the letters in the word." (McClelland & Rumelhart, [An interactive activation model of context effects in letter perception: Part 1](https://stanford.edu/~jlmcc/papers/McClellandRumelhart81.pdf), Psychological Review 88(5), 1981, pp. 375-407)

### How the pieces interact

Each detector has a resting level of activation that reflects its long-term frequency of use: in the paper's example, nodes for high-frequency words rest higher than nodes for low-frequency words. Active neighbours push a detector up or pull it down, and word detectors feed back to reinforce the letter detectors inside them. This feedback is the model's account of the word superiority effect: letters in words are more perceptible because they receive more activation than letters presented alone or in unrelated contexts.

The model is associated with the parallel distributed processing group at the time, and McClelland and Rumelhart later described the interactive activation and competition variant in detail in their book Explorations in Parallel Distributed Processing ([Wikipedia, interactive activation and competition networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interactive_activation_and_competition_networks)).
