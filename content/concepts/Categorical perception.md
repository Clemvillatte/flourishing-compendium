Categorical perception is the finding that the categories we hold bend perception itself: differences within a category shrink and differences across a boundary grow, even when the physical stimuli are equal. Harnad's classic statement:

> "'Categorical perception' (CP) is the name given to a suggestive perceptual phenomenon that may serve as a useful model for categorization in general: For certain perceptual categories, within-category differences look much smaller than between-category differences even when they are of the same size physically." ([Harnad 1987](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/250386/))

### What the perception carries

The examples are everyday. "In color perception, differences between reds and differences between yellows look much smaller than equal-sized differences that cross the red/yellow boundary; the same is true of the phoneme categories /ba/ and /da/." As Harnad adds, the effect of the category boundary is qualitative as well as quantitative. A listener does not hear a smooth slide from ba to da but two things with a jump between them, and the same warping applies to colour, music and learned categories generally. The philosophical stakes are Whorfian in miniature: the labels a perceiver owns partly decide what differences they can see, which is why the contemplative tradition's caution about concepts is also a claim about perception. The compendium principle E007 carries the applied reading: practise eidetic seeing, meaning the effort to notice the particular before the category finishes it. A compendium exercise drawn from the phenomenon is to take one person or situation one has filed under a label, and look for a difference within the category that the filing had compressed. What the perception does not give: an argument that categories are dispensable; they are the groundwork of cognition, and the point is their cost, not their abolition.
