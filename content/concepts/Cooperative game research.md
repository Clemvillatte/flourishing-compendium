Cooperative game research is my label for the line of work Robert Axelrod assembled in The Evolution of Cooperation (1984), which asked how cooperation could arise among self-interested players. The problem, as the Science paper with William D. Hamilton put it: "The theory of evolution is based on the struggle for life and the survival of the fittest. Yet cooperation is common between members of the same species and even between members of different species" ([Axelrod and Hamilton 1981](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~axe/research/Axelrod%20and%20Hamilton%20EC%201981.pdf)).

Axelrod ran a tournament of strategies for the iterated prisoner's dilemma, inspired, he later explained, by computer chess tournaments of the 1970s: "Inspired by computer chess, I wondered what a good computer program for playing the iterated Prisoner's Dilemma would look like" ([Axelrod 2011](https://websites.umich.edu/~axe/Launching%20EC%20JTB%20Axelrod%20April%208%202011.pdf)). The outcome, in the book's account:

> "TIT FOR TAT, submitted by Professor Anatol Rapoport of the University of Toronto, won the tournament. This was the simplest of all submitted programs and it turned out to be the best! TIT FOR TAT, of course, starts with a cooperative choice, and thereafter does what the other player did on the previous move." — Axelrod, The Evolution of Cooperation ([open copy](https://monoskop.org/images/b/b8/Axelrod_Robert_The_Evolution_of_Cooperation.pdf))

Its staying power rested on being "never the first to defect" — "it was never the first to defect, it was provocable into retaliation by a defection of the other, and it was forgiving after just one act of retaliation" ([Axelrod and Hamilton 1981](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~axe/research/Axelrod%20and%20Hamilton%20EC%201981.pdf)). The book's summary of the rule: "It is nice, forgiving, and retaliatory. It is never the first to defect; it forgives an isolated defection after a single response; but it is always incited by a defection no matter how good the interaction has been so far" ([open copy](https://monoskop.org/images/b/b8/Axelrod_Robert_The_Evolution_of_Cooperation.pdf)).

Whether cooperation can get a foothold depends on the probability that the players will meet again: "there is some probability, w, that after the current interaction the same two individuals will meet again" ([Axelrod and Hamilton 1981](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~axe/research/Axelrod%20and%20Hamilton%20EC%201981.pdf)). The zero-repeat case is stark: "With two individuals destined never to meet again, the only strategy that can be called a solution to the game is to defect always" (ibid.). The book calls this stake in the future the shadow of the future, and one of its chapters asks how to promote cooperation "by enlarging the shadow of the future" ([open copy](https://monoskop.org/images/b/b8/Axelrod_Robert_The_Evolution_of_Cooperation.pdf)).

Cooperation also does not require sophisticated minds. The book's framing is broad:

> "The actions that players take are not necessarily even conscious choices." and "There is no need to assume deliberate choice at all." — Axelrod, The Evolution of Cooperation ([open copy](https://monoskop.org/images/b/b8/Axelrod_Robert_The_Evolution_of_Cooperation.pdf))

Even "an organism does not need a brain to employ a strategy" ([open copy](https://monoskop.org/images/b/b8/Axelrod_Robert_The_Evolution_of_Cooperation.pdf)).
