Cooperative game research is the compendium's label for the line of work Robert Axelrod assembled in The Evolution of Cooperation (1984), which asked how cooperation could arise among self-interested players. Axelrod ran a tournament of strategies for the iterated prisoner's dilemma. The winner, submitted by Anatol Rapoport, was tit for tat, a strategy that "cooperates on the first move, and subsequently echoes (reciprocates) what the other player did on the previous move" ([Wikipedia, The Evolution of Cooperation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_of_cooperation)). Across the tournaments and replays, "the best-performing strategies were nice: that is, they were never the first to defect" (Wikipedia).

Whether cooperation can get a foothold depends on the probability that the players will meet again, the discount parameter Axelrod figuratively calls the shadow of the future (Wikipedia). With a negligible chance of meeting again, each round is effectively a one-shot game and defection dominates. Cooperation also does not require sophisticated minds: "rationality and deliberate choice are not necessary, nor trust nor even consciousness, as long as there is a pattern that benefits both players" (Wikipedia).
