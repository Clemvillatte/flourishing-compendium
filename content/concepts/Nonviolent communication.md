Nonviolent communication (NVC) is a communication process developed by clinical psychologist Marshall Rosenberg in the 1960s and 1970s, based on the principles of nonviolence and humanistic psychology; it aims to increase empathic understanding and reduce conflict in everyday interactions ([Wikipedia, "Nonviolent Communication"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolent_Communication)). Its development was highly influenced by Carl Rogers's person-centered therapy, particularly the value of congruence, empathic listening, and realness ([Wikipedia, "Nonviolent Communication"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolent_Communication)). Its closest Buddhist cousin in the compendium is [[Right speech]], and the [[Compassion]] the compendium defines as being moved by suffering and moved to act is the register it works in. The practice is often reached for in the hard places: repairing a marriage, negotiating at work, or helping a community find shared ground ([[K216 — Nonviolent communication]]).

Jim and Jori Manske's summary sheet of NVC describes the first step this way:

> "A report of \"what is\", free of judgment, criticism or other forms of analysis." ([4 Components of NVC, CNVC trainers Jim & Jori Manske](https://www.nonviolentcommunication.com/pdf_files/4_components_of_NVC.pdf))

### The four steps

An observation is specific to time and context, not an evaluation. A feeling is physical sensations and emotions, which the sheet distinguishes from thoughts such as beliefs, concepts and opinions. A need is "resources required to sustain and enrich life", something all human beings share. A request is "an opportunity to contribute to the well-being of ourself and/or others", stated in positive and immediately doable terms ([4 Components of NVC](https://www.nonviolentcommunication.com/pdf_files/4_components_of_NVC.pdf)). A structural analogy, not a claimed influence: the [[Four Noble Truths]] run suffering, cause, cessation, path; NVC runs observation, feeling, need, request. In both, the diagnosis precedes the remedy, and [[Right effort]] — preventing the unskillful state before it arises — is the effort of not blaming in the first place.

### Request, not demand

The distinction sits with the speaker, not with what happens next. The trainers' summary notes that demands "include a threat of punishment or the promise of reward linked to a behavior", while its line for requests is "Open to outcome" ([4 Components of NVC](https://www.nonviolentcommunication.com/pdf_files/4_components_of_NVC.pdf)): a request can be refused and still be useful, because the need behind it can be met another way. In an illustrative scenario (not a reported case), a housemate's unwashed dishes stop being "she is lazy" and become "the dishes from Tuesday are in the sink; I feel tense; I need shared space we can both use; would you be willing to put them in the dishwasher before tonight?"

### Evidence status

NVC has been applied in clinical, educational, organisational and community settings, and has been the subject of small trials and scoping reviews that report improvements in self-reported empathy and interpersonal outcomes; however, the evidence base is heterogeneous and reviewers have called for larger, longer and more rigorous studies, and for attention to cultural adaptation and trauma-sensitive implementation ([Wikipedia, "Nonviolent Communication"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolent_Communication)). Trauma specialists also warn that empathy-focused techniques like NVC do not by themselves address structural power imbalances and may risk re-traumatising vulnerable participants.
