Nonviolent communication (NVC) is Marshall Rosenberg's four-part practice for speech that connects rather than attacks: (1) observation — what happened, stated without evaluation; (2) feeling — the emotion it provokes, named distinctly from thought; (3) need — the underlying human need the feeling points to; (4) request — a concrete, doable ask, distinct from a demand ([Rosenberg, the four components of NVC](https://www.nonviolentcommunication.com/pdf_files/4_components_of_NVC.pdf)). Rosenberg's founding distinction is between observation and evaluation: "she is lazy" is evaluation, "she did not do the agreed task this week" is observation — and only the observation can be discussed without triggering defensiveness.

The mechanism: moralistic judgment is what provokes counterattack; an observation without evaluation gives the other person nothing to defend against. The need step translates blame ("you abandoned me") into shared human vocabulary ("I need support"), and the request keeps the other's autonomy intact — demands provoke resistance, requests invite. Its compendium form is the kernel [[K216 — Nonviolent communication]], and its kin pages are the speech disciplines ([[B023 — Practice right speech]]; [[B148 — Speak lovingly]]).

The distinction to hold: the request is not a demand. A demand punishes noncompliance; a request can hear "no" and stay in conversation. NVC's difficult cases — anger, refusal, disagreement — are handled by the same four steps, which is the point: the structure is for the hard conversations, not instead of them.

An example at its smallest: one sentence with all four parts — "when the dishes were left (observation), I felt tired (feeling), because I need some order to think (need), would you stack them before bed? (request)".

Its contemplative kin are [[Right speech]] — the Buddhist discipline of the gate — and [[Compassion]] as the motive that the structure serves.

observation, feeling, need, request defuses attack (Rosenberg, 2003).
