Nonviolent communication (NVC) is Marshall Rosenberg's practical method for compassionate connection, particularly under conflict: speaking in observations rather than evaluations, naming needs and feelings rather than blame, and making requests rather than demands. It grew out of the clinical psychology of Carl Rogers's person-centered approach in the 1960s, and sits close to the Buddhist [[Right speech]] and the Christian [[Compassion]] carried in the cited-in lists below. The practice is often reached for in the hard places: repairing a marriage, negotiating at work, or helping a community find shared ground ([[K216 — Nonviolent communication]]).

Rosenberg's summary sheet describes the first step this way:

> "A report of 'what is', free of judgment, criticism or other forms of analysis." ([4 Components of NVC, CNVC trainers Jim & Jori Manske](https://www.nonviolentcommunication.com/pdf_files/4_components_of_NVC.pdf))

### The four steps

An observation is specific to time and context, not an evaluation; a feeling is distinguished from a thought ("I feel ignored" is a judgment of the other, not an emotion); a need is what sustains and enriches life, something all human beings share; and a request is "an opportunity to contribute to the well-being of ourself and/or others", stated in positive and immediately doable terms ([4 Components of NVC](https://www.nonviolentcommunication.com/pdf_files/4_components_of_NVC.pdf)). The [[Four Noble Truths]] parallel is structural: suffering, cause, cessation, path; observation, feeling, need, request. The diagnosis precedes the remedy, and [[Right effort]] is the effort of not blaming in the first place.

### Request, not demand

What separates a request from a demand is the outcome: the trainers' summary notes that demands "include a threat of punishment or the promise of reward linked to a behavior", while requests remain "open to outcome" ([4 Components of NVC](https://www.nonviolentcommunication.com/pdf_files/4_components_of_NVC.pdf)). A rejected request still gives information: the need behind it can be met another way. In an illustrative scenario (not a reported case), a housemate's unwashed dishes stop being "she is lazy" and become "the dishes from Tuesday are in the sink; I feel tense; I need shared space we can both use; would you be willing to put them in the dishwasher before tonight?"

### Evidence status

NVC has been applied in clinical, educational, organisational and community settings. The trials that exist are small: scoping reviews report improvements in self-reported empathy and interpersonal outcomes, but the evidence base is heterogeneous, and reviewers call for larger, longer and more rigorous studies, with attention to cultural adaptation and trauma-sensitive implementation ([Wikipedia, "Nonviolent Communication"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonviolent_Communication)). The method's power in the compendium is as a structure for hard conversations, not a therapy.
