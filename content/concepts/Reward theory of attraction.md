Wikipedia states the core idea: "people are attracted to individuals exhibiting behaviors that are rewarding to them, whom they associate with rewarding events, or have positive, fulfilling interactions with" ([Reward theory of attraction, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reward_theory_of_attraction)). Byrne and Clore's version, the reinforcement-affect model, adds that the liking runs through feeling. An EBSCO overview says people are attracted to those who reward them and to those with whom they associate rewards, so "it is possible to like someone not so much for themselves but rather as a consequence of that person's merely being part of a rewarding situation" ([Attraction theories, EBSCO Research Starters](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/psychology/attraction-theories/)).

### The four assumptions

Clore and Byrne (1974), quoted on Wikipedia, state: "(a) a variety of social communications and other interpersonal events can be classed as either reinforcing or punishing; (b) reinforcing events elicit positive affect, while punishing events generate negative affect; (c) stimuli associated with positive or negative affect develop the capacity to evoke that affect; and (d) stimuli that evoke positive affect are liked, while stimuli that evoke negative affect are disliked." A study of similarity and attraction applies the model: similarity "functions as a reward", producing positive affect toward similar others ([Extending the Similarity-Attraction Effect, Journal of Computer-Mediated Communication](https://academic.oup.com/jcmc/article/19/3/342/4067515)).

### The balance of rewards and costs

The seed's "balance" corresponds to the Byrne and Rhamey (1965) law of attraction: attraction "toward a person is a positive linear function of the proportion of weighted positive reinforcements associated with him" over all weighted positive and negative reinforcements ([Reinforcement, Affect, and Interpersonal Attraction, J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/psysoc/18/3/18_1975.142/_pdf/-char/ja)). It is a ratio of reinforcements, not an accounting of relationship costs, which is the territory of social exchange theory. An A-level factsheet adds that, in the model, "praise, attention, or admiration" keep attraction going, which is the nearest to the seed's "simple attention" (the factsheet is a school resource, not a primary source).

### Evidence and limits

Dittes and Kelley (1956) found that discussion-group members who thought others liked them were more attracted to them, and Lewicki (1985) found that rewarding interactions raised attractiveness and unrewarding ones lowered it (A-level factsheet, [Psychological theories of attraction, PDF](https://pmt.physicsandmathstutor.com/download/Psychology/A-level/Notes/AQA/11-Relationships/Set-A/Psychological%20Theories%20of%20Attraction.pdf)). The same factsheet warns that what counts as a reward varies by person, so "we cannot predict whether an attraction will form in any particular situation". A review of mate selection argues that reinforcement theory and equity theory "lack external validity when applied to romantic relationships" and that liking research has questionable construct validity ([Mate selection, SciSpace PDF](https://scispace.com/pdf/mate-selection-the-process-of-developing-relationships-qgrjlj85n4.pdf)).
