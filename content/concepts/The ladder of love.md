The ladder of love is the staged ascent Diotima describes in Plato's Symposium (roughly 210a-212a), the speech Socrates reports as having learned from her. A person begins by loving one beautiful body, notices that the beauty of all bodies is akin, moves on to the beauty of souls, then of practices and laws, then of knowledge, and finally beholds Beauty itself. The ladder is Plato's answer to a practical question: how does an ordinary attraction become a philosophical life? By steps, and by each step quietly devaluing the one before it.

### The ascent in Diotima's words

The stages are given in order, and the climb is described as a discipline of widening:

> "He who would proceed aright in this matter should begin in youth to visit beautiful forms; and first, if he be guided by his instructor aright, to love one such form only—out of that he should create fair thoughts; and soon he will of himself perceive that the beauty of one form is akin to the beauty of another [...] and will become a lover of all beautiful forms; in the next stage he will consider that the beauty of the mind is more honourable than the beauty of the outward form [...] until he is compelled to contemplate and see the beauty of institutions and laws [...] and after laws and institutions he will go on to the sciences [...] drawing towards and contemplating the vast sea of beauty."

([Plato, Symposium, Diotima's speech, Jowett translation, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1600/1600-h/1600-h.htm).)

The top rung is not another beautiful thing but Beauty itself:

> "He who has been instructed thus far in the things of love, and who has learned to see the beautiful in due order and succession, when he comes toward the end will suddenly perceive a nature of wondrous beauty [...] a nature which in the first place is everlasting, not growing and decaying, or waxing and waning; secondly, not fair in one point of view and foul in another [...] but beauty absolute, separate, simple, and everlasting."

([Symposium, 210e-211a, Jowett translation, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1600/1600-h/1600-h.htm).)

And the image of the ladder is explicit: the beginner is to use beautiful things as steps, "from one going on to two, and from two to all fair forms, and from fair forms to fair practices, and from fair practices to fair notions, until from fair notions he arrives at the notion of absolute beauty" ([Symposium 211b-c, Jowett translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1600/1600-h/1600-h.htm)).

### Why the image matters

Two features of the image do real work. First, each rung is a real attachment: one body, then all bodies, then souls, then laws, then knowledge. Nothing is skipped, and the earlier rungs are not shameful but the necessary start. Second, the rungs are steps to be left behind: the point of loving one beautiful body is to be moved off it, since "personal beauty is a trifle" once the beauty of laws and institutions comes into view ([Symposium 210b-c](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1600/1600-h/1600-h.htm)). I read Diotima's sequence as a warrant for a rule of practice: on her account, transformation runs through ordered stages, and the stages are climbed, not skipped. The Yoga-Vedanta principle Expect stages, not leaps makes a similar point from a different tradition; whether every transformation obeys such a rule is not something this passage establishes.

The concept's nearest neighbour is [[Eros]] as the desire that does the climbing; The principle pages Pursue kalokagathia and Virtue aims at the beautiful track the destination; Understand love and hate as attached affects tracks the affective side.
