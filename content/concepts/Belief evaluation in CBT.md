Belief evaluation in cognitive-behavioural therapy and its third-wave successors is the process of examining what a thought does, not only whether it is true. The two traditions the compendium pairs here differ in what they evaluate: cognitive therapy evaluates the content of a belief, and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy evaluates the function of the relationship to it.

### The ACT version

Hayes, Pistorello and Levin's 2011 article summarises the ACT reading of belief evaluation. The problem it names is cognitive fusion:

> "In ACT this is termed cognitive fusion." (Hayes, Pistorello & Levin, 2011, [APA](https://www.apa.org/education-career/ce/acceptance-commitment.pdf))

The same article gives the remedy's aim:

> "In ACT, cognitive defusion and mindfulness techniques are used to attempt to create more flexibility in the presence of difficult thoughts, in part by making the ongoing process of thinking much more evident." (Hayes, Pistorello & Levin, 2011, [APA](https://www.apa.org/education-career/ce/acceptance-commitment.pdf))

And the article states what success looks like:

> "The desired result of such methods and many others like these is a decrease in believability of, attachment to, or impact of private thoughts and experiences rather than an immediate change in their frequency." (Hayes, Pistorello & Levin, 2011, [APA](https://www.apa.org/education-career/ce/acceptance-commitment.pdf))

The same article notes that traditional cognitive-behavioural therapy might attempt to reduce the frequency of a thought, challenge its validity, or ask for it to be tested in the real world, and that all these approaches treat the thought itself as if it is important.
