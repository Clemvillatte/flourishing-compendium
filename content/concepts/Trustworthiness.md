Trustworthiness, in the compendium's Aristotelian frame, is the virtue Aristotle calls truthfulness: the settled state of a person whose word can be taken without checking it, in matters small enough that no contract enforces it. His account, in Chase's translation:

> "Now since falsehood is in itself low and blameable, while truth is noble and praiseworthy, it follows that the Truthful man (who is also in the mean) is praiseworthy, and the two who depart from strict truth are both blameable, but especially the Exaggerator." (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book IV, the chapter on truthfulness, D.P. Chase translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt))

### What the truthful man is

Aristotle is careful about the virtue's scope: the truthful man "is not now meaning the man who is true in his agreements nor in such matters as amount to justice or injustice (this would come within the province of a different virtue), but, in such as do not involve any such serious difference as this, the man we are describing is true in life and word simply because he is in a certain moral state" ([Chase translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt)). The state itself is a love of truth: "he that has a love for Truth as such, and is guided by it in matters indifferent, will be so likewise even more in such as are not indifferent" ([Chase translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt)). Trustworthiness on this account is not a policy of honesty but a character that finds falsehood base even when it is profitable.
