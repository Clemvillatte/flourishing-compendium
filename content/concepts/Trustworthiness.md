Trustworthiness, in my Aristotelian frame, is the character that makes a person's word dependable. Aristotle's nearest virtue is the one he calls truthfulness, and he is careful to mark its limits: truth in life and word where no agreement or court enforces it, while keeping faith in contracts belongs to justice, a different virtue. His account, in Chase's translation:

> "Now since falsehood is in itself low and blameable, while truth is noble and praiseworthy, it follows that the Truthful man (who is also in the mean) is praiseworthy, and the two who depart from strict truth are both blameable, but especially the Exaggerator." (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book IV, the chapter on truthfulness, D.P. Chase translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt))

### What the truthful man is

Aristotle marks the virtue's scope himself: "I call him Truthful, because we are not now meaning the man who is true in his agreements nor in such matters as amount to justice or injustice (this would come within the province of a different virtue), but, in such as do not involve any such serious difference as this, the man we are describing is true in life and word simply because he is in a certain moral state" ([Chase translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt)). The state itself is a love of truth: "he that has a love for Truth as such, and is guided by it in matters indifferent, will be so likewise even more in such as are not indifferent" ([Chase translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt)). Trustworthiness on this account is not a policy of honesty but a character that finds falsehood base even when it is profitable.
