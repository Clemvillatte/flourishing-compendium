The spotlight model compares visual attention to a beam of light that can be moved across the visual field. Michael Posner's 1980 paper "Orienting of Attention" describes a task in which participants commit attention to a position other than fixation. The abstract says this is done by "orienting a covert (attentional) mechanism that seems sufficiently time locked to external events that its trajectory can be traced across the visual field in terms of momentary changes in the efficiency of detecting stimuli" ([Posner 1980, abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1080/00335558008248231)).

### The metaphor

An Oxford handbook chapter summarises the account: attention "operates like a beam of light that enhances processing of stimuli that occupy regions that fall within its boundary, and inhibits, or attenuates, processing of stimuli at locations outside its boundary". Detection was more efficient at the cued location because resources were allocated there before the target appeared ([Oxford Handbook chapter on spatial attention](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/38606/chapter/334712704)). Another Oxford chapter quotes Posner's own description of the spotlight as one that "enhances the efficiency of the detection of events within its beam" ([Oxford chapter on spatial orienting](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/41256/chapter/350821842?guestAccessKey=)).

### Narrow and broad: the zoom lens

In the strict spotlight metaphor, the beam size is fixed. The zoom lens theory of Eriksen and colleagues "suggests that the spatial extent of attention can be focused or diffuse, depending on the demands of the task". Focusing yields higher resolution over a narrow area, and widening yields lower resolution over a broader one ([Oxford Handbook chapter](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/38606/chapter/334712704)). A Cambridge text adds that the spotlight metaphor "is imperfect as a model of attention, because this privileged region does not have a sharp edge" ([Goodhew, The Breadth of Visual Attention](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811087/96217/excerpt/9781108796217_excerpt.pdf)).

### Scope

These sources concern the spatial allocation of visual attention in laboratory tasks. They do not test whether people can deliberately narrow or broaden attention in daily life to flourish. The "narrow for focus, broad for context" idea in the compendium's principle is a metaphorical extension. The compendium's earlier note attributes the model to Posner, and the sources here also credit Eriksen and colleagues for the zoom lens refinement.
