**Error management theory** — Haselton, Buss — cognitive biases are tuned to make cheap errors rather than costly ones under uncertainty.

## Field

Overt Behavior · Psychological science

Error management theory (EMT) is Martie Haselton and David Buss's account of why predictable reasoning errors persist: under uncertainty, a decision system that cannot be right all the time can still be tuned to be wrong in the cheaper direction. The founding article states the proposal:

> "A new theory of cognitive biases, called error management theory (EMT), proposes that psychological mechanisms are designed to be predictably biased when the costs of false-positive and false-negative errors were asymmetrical over evolutionary history." (Haselton & Buss, [Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10653507/), abstract)

### Origin

The theory was introduced in a 2000 Journal of Personality and Social Psychology article on biases in cross-sex mind reading, published from the Department of Psychology at the University of Texas at Austin ([JPSP 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10653507/)).

### Core claims in the authors' words

The abstract compresses the theory's explanatory and predictive program into one sentence: the theory "explains known phenomena such as men's overperception of women's sexual intent, and it predicts new biases in social inference such as women's underestimation of men's commitment" ([JPSP 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10653507/)). The two studies then test those predictions: "In Study 1 (N = 217), the authors documented the commitment underperception effect predicted by EMT. In Study 2 (N = 289), the authors replicated the commitment bias and documented a condition in which men's sexual overperception bias is corrected" ([JPSP 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10653507/)).

### How it explains

The mechanism is cost asymmetry, not sloppiness. Where a false positive and a false negative had different costs over evolutionary history, a system biased toward the cheaper error outperforms an unbiased one; the bias is the design, not the malfunction. The article closes by setting this against the standard program: "Discussion contrasts EMT with the heuristics and biases approach and suggests additional testable hypotheses based on EMT" ([JPSP 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10653507/)).

### In everyday life

The theory's documented cases are ordinary social inferences: reading sexual intent and commitment in a partner's behavior ([JPSP 2000](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10653507/)). On the EMT account, a systematically misread signal is not a random failure but a hint about which mistakes were historically cheaper.
