In an analysis of knowledge workers' diaries, Amabile and Kramer found that "nothing contributed more to a positive inner work life (the mix of emotions, motivations, and perceptions that is critical to performance) than making progress in meaningful work" ([The Power of Small Wins, Harvard Business Review, May 2011](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins)).

The publisher's summary adds: "Even small wins can boost inner work life tremendously. On the flip side, small losses or setbacks can have an extremely negative effect. And the work doesn't need to involve curing cancer in order to be meaningful. It simply must matter to the person doing it." ([same source](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins))

### A test you can run on yourself

The publisher's summary turns the finding into a diagnostic: "If a person is motivated and happy at the end of the workday, it's a good bet that he or she achieved something, however small. If the person drags out of the office disengaged and joyless, a setback is likely to blame." It names the conditions that support progress as "setting clear goals, providing sufficient time and resources, and offering recognition" (Amabile & Kramer, [Harvard Business Review](https://hbr.org/2011/05/the-power-of-small-wins)). Read as a personal practice, this suggests an evening question: what moved forward today, however slightly? The summary does not report effect sizes, and the page only claims the pattern for knowledge workers, so the question is a prompt for attention and not a measurement.

### What it is not

The principle is not a call for relentless productivity. The summary is explicit that the work "simply must matter to the person doing it", so progress on something indifferent to you is not what is described. It also says that small setbacks have "an extremely negative effect", which is a reason to notice them kindly and not to ignore them.
