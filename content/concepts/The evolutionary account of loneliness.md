The evolutionary account of loneliness treats loneliness not as a personal defect or a mere unhappy feeling, but as an aversive biological signal that evolved to keep members of a social species connected to others. The account was developed by John Cacioppo and his colleagues; this page is carried by the review below, and the original entry's later papers (Cacioppo and Cacioppo, 2018; Cacioppo et al., 2011) are cited without being opened here.

### The signal

The account starts from the species:

> "The species Homo sapiens is fundamentally social, and in ontogeny and phylogeny humans need others to survive and prosper." (Cacioppo & Hawkley, "Perceived Social Isolation and Cognition", [Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 2009, PMC2752489](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2752489/))

The analogy that organizes the theory is pain:

> "Physical pain is an aversive signal that evolved to motivate one to take action to minimize damage to one's body. Loneliness, a social pain, is an aversive signal that evolved to motivate one to take action that minimizes threats or damage to one's social body." (same review)

And the social structures the signal protects predate any individual:

> "Social species create emergent organizations beyond the individual– structures ranging from dyads and families to institutions and cultures." (same review)

On this account the discomfort of loneliness is doing a job: like hunger, it is unpleasant on purpose, and the unpleasantness is the motive to reconnect. The reframe matters for how loneliness is met. A defect invites concealment or repair of the self, while a signal invites attention to what it indicates; the compendium's principle takes the second path, treating the ache as information about connection rather than as a verdict on the person feeling it.
