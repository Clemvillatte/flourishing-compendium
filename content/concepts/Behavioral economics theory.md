## In plain words

Behavioral economics is the field that studies the mind inside the market: "the study of the psychological (e.g. cognitive, behavioral, affective, social) factors involved in the decisions of individuals or institutions, and how these decisions deviate from those implied by traditional economic theory" ([Wikipedia, Behavioral economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_economics)). Where classical theory assumed a rational chooser, this field maps the chooser's actual habits.

## Where it comes from

The roots include Herbert Simon's bounded rationality, "the idea that when individuals make decisions, their rationality is limited by the tractability of the decision problem, their cognitive limitations and the time available" ([Wikipedia, Behavioral economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_economics)). A landmark publication came in 1979: "[[T022 — Kahneman, Daniel|Kahneman]] and Tversky published Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision Under Risk, that used cognitive psychology to explain various divergences of economic decision making from neo-classical theory" ([Wikipedia, Behavioral economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_economics)). Their three generalisations: "gains are treated differently than losses, outcomes received with certainty are overweighed relative to uncertain outcomes and the structure of the problem may affect choices" ([Wikipedia, Behavioral economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_economics)).

## How it works

The core of prospect theory is reference dependence: "when evaluating outcomes, the decision maker considers a 'reference level'. Outcomes are then compared to the reference point and classified as 'gains' if greater than the reference point and 'losses' if less than the reference point," and losses loom larger: "losses are avoided more than equivalent gains are sought" ([Wikipedia, Behavioral economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_economics)). The applied arm is choice architecture. In Nudge (2008), Thaler and Sunstein defined their unit: "A nudge, as we will use the term, is any aspect of the choice architecture that alters people's behavior in a predictable way without forbidding any options or significantly changing their economic incentives. To count as a mere nudge, the intervention must be easy and cheap to avoid. Nudges are not mandates. Putting fruit at eye level counts as a nudge. Banning junk food does not" (quoted in [Wikipedia, Behavioral economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Behavioral_economics)).

## What it explains

The field's account of decision-making is that its deviations from classical predictions are systematic rather than random noise. Its practical promise is that what is predictable can be designed around, for the chooser's own benefit or against it.

## Everyday life

The fruit placed at eye level in the nudge definition is the everyday face of this field. The personal version is to become your own choice architect: arrange defaults so the better choice is the easy one.
