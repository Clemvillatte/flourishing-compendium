The circumplex model of affect, introduced by James Russell in 1980, is one of the two great ways of mapping feeling. Instead of a list of basic emotions, it gives feeling two axes, and every emotional state becomes a position on the plane they span. A 2005 review by Posner, Russell and Peterson states the claim:

> "One particular dimensional approach, termed the circumplex model of affect, proposes that all affective states arise from two fundamental neurophysiological systems, one related to valence (a pleasure–displeasure continuum) and the other to arousal, or alertness (Russell, 1980)."

And what that buys:

> "Each emotion can be understood as a linear combination of these two dimensions, or as varying degrees of both valence and arousal (see Figure 1)."

> "In interpreting this 2-D structure, proponents of the circumplex model of affect suggest that all affective states arise from two independent neurophysiological systems, which, for the purposes of discussion here, we term the valence and arousal systems." ([Posner, Russell and Peterson 2005, Development and Psychopathology 17(3):715-734](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2367156/))

The model takes sides in an old dispute. Its rival, the basic-emotions tradition, gives each emotion its own dedicated neural machinery; the circumplex model says the machinery is shared, two systems that combine:

> "This model stands in contrast to theories of basic emotions, which posit that a discrete and independent neural system subserves every emotion." (same paper, abstract)

The two axes are meant to be independent, which is what makes the plane informative: a state can be pleasant and sleepy (contentment), unpleasant and sleepy (dejection), pleasant and alert (excitement), unpleasant and alert (alarm). For the compendium, the payoff is practical: [[AF003 — Learn the structure of your affect — valence and arousal]] uses the two axes as a first vocabulary for naming what one feels, before any finer emotional word is available.
