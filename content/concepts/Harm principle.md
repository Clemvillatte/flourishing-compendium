The harm principle is Mill's answer to when society may coerce an adult. It appears in the opening chapter of On Liberty as "one very simple principle":

> That principle is, that the sole end for which mankind are warranted, individually or collectively, in interfering with the liberty of action of any of their number, is self-protection. That the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others. His own good, either physical or moral, is not a sufficient warrant. (Mill, On Liberty, chapter I, [Gutenberg #34901](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/34901/34901-0.txt))

The principle covers both legal penalties and "the moral coercion of public opinion", which makes it a limit on majorities and on culture, not only on the state.

### What it rules out

Mill immediately names the rejected grounds: "He cannot rightfully be compelled to do or forbear because it will be better for him to do so, because it will make him happier" (ibid.). Paternalism is the standing target. The doctrine "is meant to apply only to human beings in the maturity of their faculties" — children and those unable to decide for themselves are outside its scope, a boundary Mill draws explicitly in the same chapter.

### In the compendium

The principle is the philosophy side of Y028: if non-harm is the ground of legitimate coercion, then the ethical work of a life becomes noticing, and declining to act on, the many pressures that do harm while claiming to help.
