Critical thinking research is the empirical study of how well people reason and decide, and of what separates good reasoners from poor ones. The compendium's claim — that the capacity for analytical reasoning predicts better decision-making — is grounded in Maggie Toplak, Richard West and Keith Stanovich's 2011 study of the Cognitive Reflection Test, a short measure of the tendency to check an intuitive first answer.

> "In this study, we showed that the CRT is a more potent predictor of performance on a wide sample of tasks from the heuristics-and-biases literature than measures of cognitive ability, thinking dispositions, and executive functioning." (Toplak, West & Stanovich 2011, [PubMed 21541821](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21541821/))

The test works by inviting a wrong-but-tempting answer and rewarding the second look. The authors' explanation for why the CRT predicts so well is that it taps "the tendency toward miserly processing", the habit of accepting the first answer that comes to mind, which intelligence tests miss because they measure capacity rather than the disposition to use it. The abstract is careful about what this does and does not show: the CRT correlates with cognitive ability, but a series of regression analyses indicated it "was a unique predictor of performance on heuristics-and-biases tasks" and "accounted for substantial additional variance after the other measures of individual differences had been statistically controlled". The authors also note a practical advantage of the measure: it is "a performance measure rather than a self-report measure", so it cannot be gamed by knowing what a humble answer sounds like. The claim the compendium attaches to this research, that analytical reasoning predicts better decision-making, is the study's central result, with decision quality read off performance on heuristics-and-biases tasks.
