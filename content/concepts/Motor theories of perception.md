Motor theories of perception hold that to perceive something is, in part, to recognize how you would produce it. The flagship is the motor theory of speech perception, launched by Liberman and colleagues at Haskins Laboratories and stated in their 1967 Psychological Review paper, "Perception of the Speech Code." A later review compresses its claims into three sentences:

> The three main claims of the theory are the following: (1) Speech processing is special (Liberman & Mattingly, 1989; Mattingly & Liberman, 1988); (2) perceiving speech is perceiving vocal tract gestures (e.g., Liberman & Mattingly, 1985); (3) speech perception involves access to the speech motor system (e.g., Liberman et al., 1967).

(Galantucci, Fowler, & Turvey, "The motor theory of speech perception reviewed," Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2746041/))

The motivation was coarticulation: the vocal tract gestures for successive consonants and vowels overlap in time, so the same sound can carry different phonemes. As the review puts it, "if information for the phonetic segments overlaps, information for each segment can span a longer interval of time, and the ear can resolve the segments temporally." Liberman's own metaphor was that speech is not an acoustic "alphabet" or "cipher," but an efficient "code."

The compendium's [[E015 — Practise motor intentionality]] and [[ST263 — The Phantom Hand]] draw the broader lesson: perception is action-shaped, and the body's own capacities are part of what the world shows up as.
