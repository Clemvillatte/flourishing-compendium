“The fearless mind” — Buddhism, Abhayaraja-kumara Sutta.

### What it is

I use this title for the composure the Buddha shows in the Abhayaraja-kumara Sutta (MN 58). Prince Abhaya, sent by his scheming teacher, lays a trap question designed to make the Buddha lose face whatever he answers. The Buddha's reply is a set of criteria for speech, held without anger and without evasion: "In the case of words that the Tathagata knows to be factual, true, beneficial, but unendearing & disagreeable to others, he has a sense of the proper time for saying them" (MN 58, trans. Thanissaro, [Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.058.than.html)), while "In the case of words that the Tathagata knows to be unfactual, untrue, unbeneficial (or: not connected with the goal), unendearing & disagreeable to others, he does not say them" (MN 58, trans. Thanissaro, [Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.058.than.html)). The ground of the policy is given plainly: "Why is that? Because the Tathagata has sympathy for living beings" (MN 58, trans. Thanissaro, [Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.058.than.html)).

### What it carries

The fearlessness is not boldness but the absence of the fear that usually does the talking. Speech governed by truth, benefit and sympathy has no room left for the fear of displeasing, and the trap loses its grip: there is no face to lose when the aim is the listener's welfare. The Buddha seals the point with a simile from the prince's own lap, a baby with a pebble in its mouth: "If I couldn't get it out right away... I would take it out, even if it meant drawing blood. Why is that? Because I have sympathy for the young boy" (MN 58, trans. Thanissaro, [Access to Insight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.058.than.html)). The AF049 principle works the same ground on the panic side: fear of fear is what escalates a wave into a storm, and sympathy for oneself in the wave is what ends the escalation.
