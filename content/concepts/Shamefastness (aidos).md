Shamefastness is the old English rendering of aidos, the Greek sensitivity to honor that restrains a person from acting in ways that would lower them in their own and others' eyes. [[T002 — Aristotle|Aristotle]]'s treatment in Nicomachean Ethics IV.9 gives the concept its careful limits. His verdict:

> "Shame should not be described as a virtue; for it is more like a feeling than a state of character. It is defined, at any rate, as a kind of fear of dishonour, and produces an effect similar to that produced by fear of danger; for people who feel disgraced blush, and those who fear death turn pale." (Aristotle, [Nicomachean Ethics IV.9](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.4.iv.html), translated by W. D. Ross)

### Why it suits the young

Aristotle restricts aidos to a stage of life. "The feeling is not becoming to every age, but only to youth. For we think young people should be prone to the feeling of shame because they live by feeling and therefore commit many errors, but are restrained by shame." The reasoning is practical: the young lack settled character, so a fast-acting social emotion does the work habituation has not yet done. For the mature person the need should have passed: "an older person no one would praise for being prone to the sense of disgrace, since we think he should not do anything that need cause this sense."

The deeper point is that aidos is remedial. "For the sense of disgrace is not even characteristic of a good man, since it is consequent on bad actions." A good person has no use for a guard against disgraceful acts because such acts are no longer in their repertoire. Aristotle's concession keeps the concept alive for practice: "But shame may be said to be conditionally a good thing; if a good man does such actions, he will feel disgraced."
