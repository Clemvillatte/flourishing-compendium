The intrinsic value of nature is the claim that living things matter for their own sake and not only as means to human ends. Paul Taylor gave the best-known version of it for individual organisms. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy summarises his view: each living thing "is a 'teleological-center-of-life' having a good or well-being of its own which can be enhanced or damaged", and all of them "have equal intrinsic value (or what he calls 'inherent worth') which entitles them to moral respect" ([SEP, Environmental Ethics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-environmental/)).

### Two kinds of value

The encyclopedia explains the vocabulary. Instrumental value "is the value of things as means to further some other ends", while intrinsic value is "the value of things as ends in themselves regardless of whether they are also useful as means to other ends" (same source). Because the intrinsically valuable is good as an end in itself, "it is commonly agreed that something's possession of intrinsic value generates a prima facie direct moral duty on the part of moral agents to protect it or at least refrain from damaging it." This is a claim in moral philosophy about what deserves moral concern. It is a different use of "intrinsic" from the one in psychology, where intrinsic motivation concerns why a person acts.

### Taylor's wording

In his 1981 article Taylor writes that the principle of intrinsic value "states that, regardless of what kind of entity it is in other respects, if it is a member of the Earth's community of life, the realization of its good is something intrinsically valuable." He explains: "This means that its good is prima facie worthy of being preserved or promoted as an end in itself and for the sake of the entity whose good it is." Combining it with a second principle, he defines inherent worth like this: to possess it "is to say that its good is deserving of the concern and consideration of all moral agents, and that the realization of its good has intrinsic value, to be pursued as an end in itself and for the sake of the entity whose good it is" ([Taylor, 1981, The Ethics of Respect for Nature, copy hosted at the University of Colorado](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil308/Taylor.pdf)).

Organisms need not be conscious for this: "conscious or not, all are equally teleological centers of life in the sense that each is a unified system of goal-oriented activities directed toward their preservation and well-being" (same source).
