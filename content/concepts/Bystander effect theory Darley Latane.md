## In plain words

The bystander effect is the finding that witnesses can be each other's excuse. Its standard statement: "individuals are less likely to offer help to a victim in the presence of other people" ([Wikipedia, Bystander effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bystander_effect)). John Darley and Bibb Latané built the theory in 1968 from the smoke-filled-room experiments.

## Where it comes from

The theory grew from Columbia experiments by Latané and Darley (1968), in which students filling in a questionnaire were exposed to smoke pumped through a wall vent: "when students were working alone they noticed the smoke almost immediately (within 5 seconds). However, students who were working in groups took longer (up to 20 seconds) to notice the smoke," and "only one participant in the group condition reported the smoke within the first four minutes" ([Wikipedia, Bystander effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bystander_effect)).

## How it works

Two mechanisms carry the effect. The first is diffusion of responsibility: "when people believe that there are other people around, they are less likely or slower to help a victim because they believe someone else will take responsibility" ([Wikipedia, Bystander effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bystander_effect)). The second is pluralistic ignorance: in the smoke experiment, groups "that did not report the smoke" read the situation as benign, "some preferring less serious explanations, such as that the air-conditioner was leaking" ([Wikipedia, Bystander effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bystander_effect)). The theory's process model runs through five steps: noticing that something is going on, interpreting the situation as an emergency, arriving at a degree of responsibility, choosing a form of assistance, and implementing that choice ([Wikipedia, Bystander effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bystander_effect)).

## What it explains

The effect explains why emergencies can go unhelped in crowds, and later work described in the article adds boundary conditions: people are more likely to accept responsibility "when the emergency is unambiguous or when they feel uniquely capable of helping," and group identity matters, as bystanders intervene more "when the victim is perceived as part of their ingroup" ([Wikipedia, Bystander effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bystander_effect)).

## Everyday life

An illustration of the model in use (not a tested prescription): if you are the bystander, name the situation to yourself and out loud ("this person needs help") to break the diffusion; if you are the one in trouble, single someone out ("you, in the blue jacket, call an ambulance").
