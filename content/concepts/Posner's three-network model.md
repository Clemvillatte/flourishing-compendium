Posner's three-network model is the framework Michael Posner and Steven Petersen introduced in 1990, dividing the brain's attention system into three anatomically distinct networks with different functions. Their 2012 update, twenty years later, restates the division:

> "The three networks we described in 1990 included an alerting network, which focused on brain stem arousal systems along with right hemisphere systems related to sustained vigilance; an orienting network focused on, among other regions, parietal cortex; and an executive network, which included midline frontal/anterior cingulate cortex." (Petersen and Posner, [The attention system of the human brain: 20 years after, Annual Review of Neuroscience 35, 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3413263/), "The Original Networks" section)

### The framework's three claims

The original review's premises, as the 2012 update summarizes them, are that the attention system is anatomically separate from the processing systems that handle stimuli, decisions and outputs; that attention operates through networks of anatomical areas; and that these areas carry out functions that can be specified in cognitive terms (Petersen and Posner, [2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3413263/), Introduction). The update adds that research since has supported new networks: a frontoparietal network distinct from the orienting network, and a cingulo-opercular network overlapping the original executive network (Petersen and Posner, 2012, "The Original Networks" and later sections).
