Premeditatio malorum is the Stoic exercise of rehearsing future misfortunes in advance: poverty, illness, exile, loss, death. The Latin means "the premeditation of evils". The rehearsal is deliberate and bounded; its purpose is to strip misfortune of the strangeness that makes it heavy.

> "Therefore, nothing ought to be unexpected by us. Our minds should be sent forward in advance to meet all problems, and we should consider, not what is wont to happen, but what can happen." (Seneca, Letters 91.4, Gummere translation, [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_91))

### Why rehearsal works, on Seneca's account

The reasoning comes in the same letter: "it is the unexpected that puts the heaviest load upon us. Strangeness adds to the weight of calamities, and every mortal feels the greater pain as a result of that which also brings surprise." A misfortune already run through the mind arrives as a partial acquaintance rather than a shock. Seneca's instruction is to summon "the very greatest evil that possibly can happen" beforehand, not the average case, so that nothing actual exceeds the rehearsal.

### The limit: rehearsal is not worry

The same author warns against the exercise's shadow. "There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality," and "we are in the habit of exaggerating, or imagining, or anticipating, sorrow." The difference is boundedness: premeditation runs the loss through the mind and returns to the present; worry loops on it.
