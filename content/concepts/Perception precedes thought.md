“Perception precedes thought” — Aristotle, De Anima II.

The label is the compendium's summary, not an Aristotelian phrase. The text of De Anima could not be opened for this page, because the Internet Classics Archive page returned no text. The account below rests on Christopher Shields's article on Aristotle's psychology in the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, which cites the relevant passages, so Aristotle's own wording is not quoted.

### A hierarchy of capacities

Aristotle lists the soul's capacities as nutrition, perception and mind. The Stanford Encyclopedia says they "form a kind of hierarchy", and states the dependence in one direction only.

> "Any creature with reason will also have perception; any creature with perception will also have the ability to take on nutrition and to reproduce; but the converse does not hold." ([Shields, Aristotle's Psychology, section 4](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/aristotle-psychology/))

So, in this sense, perception comes before thought: no thinking creature lacks perception, and some perceiving creatures do not think. This is a claim about what a being must have, and does not say that each act of thinking is preceded in time by a perception.

### An analogy between the two

Shields reports that Aristotle models thinking on perception: "Just as perception involves the reception of a sensible form by a suitably qualified sensory faculty, so thinking involves the reception of an intelligible form by a suitably qualified intellectual faculty" (De Anima iii 4, 429a13-18, as cited by Shields, section 7). He adds that Aristotle sometimes holds that thought is of universals while perception is of particulars, and reads this as thought operating "at a higher level of generality than perception".

### Where the label overreaches

Shields also records that Aristotle does not treat the mind as simply another bodily power. The mind "is not 'mixed with the body', insofar as it, unlike the perceptual faculty, lacks a bodily organ" (De Anima iii 4, 429a24-7). The compendium's use of the label for embodiment (as in the principle on thinking being embodied) therefore needs care: Aristotle makes thought depend on perception, and he also sets thought apart from the body. A passage in De Anima Book III about thinking and mental images was not found in the opened sources, so it is not used.
