Epistemic curiosity is curiosity about knowledge itself: the drive to learn, resolve gaps and solve intellectual problems, as distinct from curiosity about sensations or thrills. Jordan Litman, who built the standard measures of the trait, defines it with its two faces:

> "Epistemic curiosity (EC) is the desire for new knowledge (Berlyne, 1954, 1978), aimed at stimulating pleasurable feelings of situational interest (I-type) or relieving negative affective conditions of feeling deprived of knowledge (D-type; Litman, 2005)." ([Litman and Mussel, 2013](https://drjlitman.net/papers/Litman-Mussel-2013.pdf))

### The two types

The distinction matters because the two kinds of wanting feel different and lead to different study habits:

> "As an emotional-motivational state, I-type EC is activated by opportunities for intellectual exploration expected to lead to new discoveries and corresponds to the intrinsic pleasure of learning. D-type EC is theorized to manifest as an uncomfortably intense 'need to know,' activated when people find they lack very specific pieces of information needed to solve a problem or complete a knowledge-set (Litman, Hutchins, & Russon, 2005)." ([Litman and Mussel, 2013](https://drjlitman.net/papers/Litman-Mussel-2013.pdf))

The interest type is the pleasure of finding out; the deprivation type is the itch of an unanswered question. In the German validation study, the two scales behaved as distinct factors: interest-type curiosity correlated with enjoying new discoveries and ideas, deprivation-type with persistence and solving problems ([Litman and Mussel, 2013](https://drjlitman.net/papers/Litman-Mussel-2013.pdf)).
