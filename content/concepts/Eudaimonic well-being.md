Eudaimonic well-being is the half of well-being research that defines the good life by functioning rather than feeling. Where the hedonic approach counts pleasure and pain, the eudaimonic approach asks whether a life is going somewhere. Ryan and Deci's field review draws the line:

> Current research on well-being has been derived from two general perspectives: the hedonic approach, which focuses on happiness and defines well-being in terms of pleasure attainment and pain avoidance; and the eudaimonic approach, which focuses on meaning and self-realization and defines well-being in terms of the degree to which a person is fully functioning.
>
> (Ryan and Deci, "On Happiness and Human Potentials: A Review of Research on Hedonic and Eudaimonic Well-Being", Annual Review of Psychology 52, 2001, p. 141)

The review traces the label to eudaimonism: "It lies instead in the actualization of human potentials. This view has been called eudaimonism (Waterman 1993), conveying the belief that well-being consists of fulfilling or realizing one's daimon or true nature." For measurement, it records Ryff and Keyes's six aspects of psychological well-being, "autonomy, personal growth, self-acceptance, life purpose, mastery, and positive relatedness" (all quotes: Ryan and Deci 2001).

The compendium's principles use the concept as the modern-psychology name for what [[A001 — Make eudaimonia the organising end of your life]] argues from Aristotle, and as the outcome behind [[P014 — The good life is a process, not a state]] and [[P164 — Judge your life as a direction, not a destination]].
