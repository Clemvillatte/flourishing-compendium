Exposure therapy is the treatment approach in which a person repeatedly approaches the things they fear, in the absence of the bad outcome they expect, until fear loses its grip. Craske and colleagues call it "repeated approach toward fear provoking stimuli" and note it has been a mainstay of cognitive behavioral therapy for anxiety disorders since its inception ([Craske et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4114726/)).

### How it works

In Craske's account, exposure is the clinical counterpart of extinction in fear-conditioning research. The old fear is not erased. The paper describes it this way: the original association "is not erased during extinction, but rather is left intact as new, secondary inhibitory learning about the CS-US develops" ([Craske et al., 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4114726/)). The feared cue then carries two meanings at once, the old threat and a newer "no threat". That is why fear can come back after treatment.

Craske's group set this inhibitory learning model against two older accounts: habituation, which emphasises fear dropping during an exposure, and behavioural tests that disconfirm threatening beliefs. They also say the mechanisms are likely mixed, and that habituation is probably involved too.

### What changes in practice

The inhibitory learning paper lists eight ways to strengthen the new learning: expectancy violation, deepened extinction, occasional reinforced extinction, removal of safety signals, variability, retrieval cues, multiple contexts and affect labeling ([Craske et al., 2014, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864005/)). Two are easy to picture:

- Expectancy violation: design the exposure around "what do you need to learn" rather than "stay in the situation until fear declines".
- Removing safety signals: the paper names another person, a phone, medication, or food or drink as common ones, and says that safety signals relieve distress in the short term, but the fear returns when they are gone. It also reports contradictory findings on safety behaviours, so the point is stated with some caution.

### Foa's strand

Foa and McLean describe how emotional processing theory led to two protocols: prolonged exposure for posttraumatic stress disorder, and exposure and response (ritual) prevention for obsessive-compulsive disorder. They review efficacy, then the mechanisms behind it ([Foa and McLean, 2016, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26565122/)). Only the abstract of that review was read for this page, so the efficacy figures are not reproduced here.
