The cardinal virtues are the four hinge virtues on which, in the classical and scholastic tradition, the whole of moral life turns: prudence, justice, fortitude and temperance. Thomas Aquinas, systematizing a line that runs from Plato's Republic through Cicero and Augustine, names them directly: "We know that there are four cardinal virtues, viz. temperance, justice, prudence, and fortitude" ([Summa Theologiae I-II q61](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2061.htm)). The tradition treats them as principal because the other virtues are contained under them: "Many, both holy doctors, as also philosophers, speak about these virtues in this sense: and in this way the other virtues are contained under them" ([Summa Theologiae I-II q61](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2061.htm)).

Their subordination is ordered: prudence governs the rest. As Aquinas puts it, "Prudence is the principal of all the virtues simply. The others are principal, each in its own genus" ([Summa Theologiae I-II q61](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2061.htm)) — prudence is chief absolutely, while justice, fortitude and temperance each head their own domain of matter. Cicero had already "reduce[d] all other virtues to these four (De Invent. Rhet. ii)" ([Summa Theologiae I-II q61](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2061.htm)), which is why the scheme could pass from pagan philosophy into Christian theology almost without remainder.

The virtues also mark stages of ascent. Aquinas reports Plotinus — "together with Plato foremost among teachers of philosophy" — distinguishing "social virtues," "perfecting virtues," "perfect" virtues and "exemplar virtues" ([Summa Theologiae I-II q61](https://www.newadvent.org/summa/2061.htm)): the virtues of ordinary civic life, the virtues that purify the soul, the virtues of the purified soul, and the virtues as they exist in God. The cardinal four therefore sit inside a larger map, running from the political person toward the divine exemplar.

For the flourishing project the scheme matters as an early structural answer to the question "which excellences organize the rest?" Its claim is that a small, closed set of master virtues can carry the whole of character. Its weakness is the mirror of its elegance: a four-fold hinge assumes the moral life has a small fixed number of load-bearing points.
