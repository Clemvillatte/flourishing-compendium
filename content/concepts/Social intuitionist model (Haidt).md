The social intuitionist model is Haidt's 2001 account of moral judgment: moral intuitions come first, and reasoning is usually post-hoc. His definition of the intuition: "the sudden appearance in consciousness of a moral judgment, including an affective valence (good-bad, like-dislike), without any conscious awareness of having gone through steps of searching, weighing evidence, or inferring a conclusion" ([Haidt 2001, p. 818, quoted in the Revista de Historia de la Vida analysis](https://revistas.uv.cl/index.php/RHV/article/download/2947/version/2642/3141/12783)). The model's claim: "The model proposes that moral judgments appear in consciousness automatically and effortlessly as the result of moral intuitions" ([Haidt 2001, p. 818, quoted in the same analysis](https://revistas.uv.cl/index.php/RHV/article/download/2947/version/2642/3141/12783)).

### The two illusions

Haidt names the phenomenology with two dog images. The wag-the-dog illusion: "we believe our own moral judgment (the dog) is driven by our own moral reasoning." The wag-the-other-dog's-tail illusion: "we expect the rebuttal of an opponent's argument to change the opponent's mind. Such a belief is like thinking that forcing a dog's tail to wag by moving it with your hand will make the dog happy" ([Haidt 2001, quoted in Protevi's notes](https://www.protevi.com/john/Morality/Haidt.pdf)). The 2001 abstract's summary: "moral reasoning is usually a post hoc construction, generated after a judgment has been reached" ([Haidt 2001, PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11699120/)).

### What follows for persuasion

The practical consequence is in the 2004 Dædalus piece with Craig Joseph: "If you focus on the reasons people give for their judgments, you are studying the rational tail that got wagged by the emotional dog" ([Haidt and Joseph, "Intuitive Ethics," Dædalus](https://bec.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/108/archive/papers/Haidt_1-31-05.pdf)). Since intuitions come first, you will rarely convince someone by reasoning them out of a strong intuition; the plausible route is to provoke a different intuition that clashes with the first and prompts a reshuffling of the affective structure. The model's "social" half states this in interpersonal terms: "the social part of the social intuitionist model proposes that moral judgment should be studied as an interpersonal process" ([Haidt 2001, p. 814, quoted in the Revista analysis](https://revistas.uv.cl/index.php/RHV/article/download/2947/version/2642/3141/12783)).

The model has been criticized on the reasoning side — later work argues that "moral reasoning may sometimes disrupt the automatic process of judgment formation described by the SIM" ([Altukhil and colleagues, Macquarie summary](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/is-the-emotional-dog-wagging-its-rational-tail-or-chasing-it-reas/)) — and the honest reading is that intuitions usually come first, not always.
