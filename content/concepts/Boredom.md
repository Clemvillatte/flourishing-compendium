Boredom is "the aversive experience of wanting, but being unable, to engage in satisfying activity" ([Eastwood et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4217586/), p. 483, as quoted in Elpidorou). The dominant attentional account holds that boredom arises when engagement fails: "Boredom is particularly likely to occur when a task provides little external support for keeping attention engaged, such that performance relies instead on self-sustained attention" ([Eastwood et al. 2012, as discussed in PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3842844/)).

The state is aversive but functional. On the adaptive theory, "boredom acts as an emotional signal that current goal pursuits should be abandoned and that new goals should be actively selected and pursued" ([Elpidorou, "On the Function of Boredom"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4217586/)). Boredom "results from recognition that the current goal is no longer stimulating ... and is characterized by motivation to change the current situation and seek alternatives" ([Elpidorou](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4217586/)). Even its negative side is regulatory: boredom pushes toward exploration even when "those experiences might result in negative emotions" ([Elpidorou](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4217586/)).

For the flourishing project, boredom is a signal to read, not a void to fill: an excess of unclaimed meaning, alerting the agent that current goals have stopped earning attention.
