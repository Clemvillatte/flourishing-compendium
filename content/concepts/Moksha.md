Moksha is, in Britannica's words, "in Indian philosophy and religion, liberation from the cycle of death and rebirth (samsara)". The term is "derived from the Sanskrit word muc ('to free')" and "literally means freedom from samsara", a concept "shared by a wide spectrum of religious traditions, including Hinduism, Buddhism, and Jainism" ([Britannica, moksha](https://www.britannica.com/topic/moksha-Indian-religion)). In Vedanta the best-known reading identifies [[Atman]] with [[Brahman]]; Wikipedia states that in Hindu traditions "moksha is a central concept and the utmost aim of human life" ([Wikipedia, Moksha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moksha)).

### What is released from what

Wikipedia gives the range: moksha is freedom "from 'impurities' (klesa) such as ignorance (avidhya) and 'earthly desires', and from saṃsāra". It adds that "the understanding of moksha varies between various schools of Indian religions": Hinduism and Jainism "presuppose the existence of an enduring self, while Buddhism rejects the existence of such an enduring self". Liberation may be attained "while one is on earth (jivanmukti), or eschatologically (karmamukti, videhamukti)".

### In the Upanishads

According to the Wikipedia entry, the word moksha appears in the middle and later Upanishads, such as the Svetasvatara and Maitri, and "by the middle to late Upanishadic period, the emphasis shifted to knowledge". Britannica's article on Hinduism describes the path of knowledge (jnana-marga) as gaining "a supraintellectual insight into one's identity with brahman", beside the paths of action and devotion, which are "interactive and potentially available to all" ([Britannica, Karma, Samsara, Moksha](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Hinduism/Karma-samsara-and-moksha)). A university paper quotes the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad on the state of liberation: "the person when in the embrace of the Intelligent self knows nothing without or within" ([Concept of Moksha in Hinduism and Buddhism, NBU repository](https://ir.nbu.ac.in/server/api/core/bitstreams/4b171164-12ab-44f8-8e6b-e4ba207883cd/content)).

### A correction to the earlier text

The earlier line said the Upanishads describe moksha "as freedom here and now, not a place". I could not support that in the sources I opened. They show two views: living liberation (jivanmukti) and liberation after death. Britannica's article on the afterlife says the definition varies by sect, "but in most cases it can be described as a kind of heaven" ([Britannica, Afterlife](https://www.britannica.com/topic/afterlife-religion)). Keep the claim out of essays unless a primary text is quoted.
