Social Judgment Theory explains responses to persuasion in terms of how a listener evaluates a message against existing attitudes ([Atkin and Smith, 2008](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781405186407.wbiecs060)). The model describes both a person's preferred position and the range of positions they find tolerable ([A First Look at Communication Theory, ch. 14 outline](https://www.afirstlook.com/edition-11/theory-resources/by-theory/Social-Judgment-Theory/outline)).

> Sherif et al. (1965) claimed that an individual's attitude toward a particular issue or behavior is not adequately reflected by a single alternative or position among those available.
>
> Charles K. Atkin and Sandi W. Smith, “Social Judgment Theory,” [The International Encyclopedia of Communication, abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781405186407.wbiecs060).

### Latitudes and judgment

The theory distinguishes three latitudes: acceptance covers ideas the person finds reasonable, rejection covers objectionable ideas, and noncommitment covers ideas they neither accept nor reject ([chapter outline](https://www.afirstlook.com/edition-11/theory-resources/by-theory/Social-Judgment-Theory/outline)). It also predicts assimilation, perceiving an acceptable message as closer to one's position than it is, and contrast, perceiving a rejected message as more distant ([“Judging the message”](https://www.afirstlook.com/edition-11/theory-resources/by-theory/Social-Judgment-Theory/outline)).

The textbook outline cautions that the proposed mental structures are not directly observable ([“Critique”](https://www.afirstlook.com/edition-11/theory-resources/by-theory/Social-Judgment-Theory/outline)). It identifies this as a difficulty for testing the theory's causal explanations ([“Critique”](https://www.afirstlook.com/edition-11/theory-resources/by-theory/Social-Judgment-Theory/outline)).
