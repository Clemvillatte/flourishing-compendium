Pure experience is experience before it is split into a subject who experiences and an object experienced. The term has two famous homes: William James's radical empiricism (essays of 1904-05, collected 1912) and Nishida Kitaro's An Inquiry into the Good (1911), the founding work of the Kyoto School. Both use it against the same assumption: that experience arrives already divided into an inner watcher and an outer world.

> "The instant field of the present is at all times what I call the 'pure' experience. It is only virtually or potentially either object or subject as yet. For the time being, it is plain, unqualified actuality, or existence, a simple that." (James, "Does 'Consciousness' Exist?", Essays in Radical Empiricism, [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/32547/32547-h/32547-h.htm))

### James: the flux before the categories

James's fuller definition: "'Pure experience' is the name which I gave to the immediate flux of life which furnishes the material to our later reflection with its conceptual categories." He concedes the literal sense is rare: only "new-born babes, or men in semi-coma from sleep, drugs, illnesses, or blows" may be assumed to have it. For everyone else purity is a matter of degree, "meaning the proportional amount of unverbalized sensation which it still embodies." The split into mind and world is a later act: "the doubling of it in retrospection into a state of mind and a reality intended thereby, is just one of the acts."

### Nishida: the Kyoto School development

Nishida's Inquiry opens with the same move: experience prior to judgment. In the Stanford Encyclopedia's quotation, "the moment of seeing a color or hearing a sound" is prior not only to the thought that the color or sound is the activity of an external object or that one is sensing it, but also to the judgment of what the color or sound might be (Nishida 1911c: 3). The entry notes his notion of pure experience "clearly shows the influence of William James, Ernst Mach, and others, but it differs from their notions" by its emphasis on the non-individuated character and seamless development of such experience. For Nishida, pure experience reaches further than awareness: it is "the fundamental form of reality, indeed the 'one and only reality' from which all differentiated phenomena are to be understood."
