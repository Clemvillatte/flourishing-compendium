Responsiveness names the interpersonal act of answering, and the compendium gets it from two directions. In care ethics, Tronto makes it the fourth moral element of care, and it is about being alert to abuse: "The moral precept of responsiveness requires that we remain alert to the possibilities for abuse that arise with vulnerability" (Tronto, Moral Boundaries, quoted on the compendium's [[R073 — Practise responsiveness]] page). In Levinas, the face of the other person is a summons that calls you to account before you have chosen anything. The Stanford Encyclopedia's account of Levinas:

> In responding, I discover my responsibility to them.

(Bergo & Bettina, "Emmanuel Levinas," [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/levinas/), quoting and paraphrasing Totality and Infinity)

The two uses are not the same. Tronto's responsiveness is a practice with a failure mode (paternalism, parochialism): the one cared-for can lose autonomy through the care itself, so the caregiver must keep checking the receiver's response. Levinas's responsibility is not chosen at all: "Above all, we do not choose to be responsible. Responsibility arises as if elicited, before we begin to think about it, by the approach of the other person." Noddings's Caring sits between them, treating the cared-for's response as what completes the caring relation.
