threat-based influence breeds compliance plus resentment.

The research behind this seed line is the ultimatum game, the laboratory miniature of a threat. One player proposes a split of real money; the other can accept or reject; rejection leaves both with nothing. Standard economic theory predicts the responder accepts any positive amount, since something beats nothing. Instead, across 15 societies on five continents, people reject low offers at real cost:

> "For every population studied, the probability of rejection decreased as offers increase from 0% to 50%. At the lowest offer for which punishment is costly (10% offers), 56.5% of players rejected overall." (Henrich et al. 2006, Science 312, p. 1768)

The rejection is the resentment of the seed line made visible and priced: responders give up their own share to deny the proposer the gains of an unfair, coercive split. A threat can extract compliance, but the person threatened will pay to punish the threatener when the chance appears. What the game adds to the seed is the other half of the finding: proposers who anticipate this resentment offer near-fair splits, which is why influence that feels like partnership lasts longer than influence that feels like an ultimatum.
