Feng shui is a traditional Chinese form of geomancy concerned with choosing and orienting buildings, dwellings and burial sites according to beliefs about qi. Its name literally means "wind-water" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feng_shui): "The term feng shui means, literally, 'wind-water' (i.e., fluid)"; [New World Encyclopedia](https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Feng_shui): "Feng shui literally translates as 'wind-water.'"). Yuan Ding describes kanyu as the term used in classical texts, where the popular name feng shui does not appear: "Kanyu is the term always used in classical text instead of its most popular name, Feng-shui" ([Kanyu (Feng-shui): A Case of Re-interpretation of the Spirit of Space](https://publ.icomos.org/publicomos/jlbSai?html=Bur&base=technica&ref=41507&file=146.pdf&path=77-y1bV-92.pdf)). The authoritative Ci-hai lexicon, reproduced in the same paper, states the dismissive definition outright:

> "a superstition in ancient China, according to which the surrounding situation of residences and graves can decide the fortune of the residents and the offspring of the dead." (Ci-hai lexicon definition, as reproduced in Yuan Ding, [Kanyu (Feng-shui): A Case of Re-interpretation of the Spirit of Space](https://publ.icomos.org/publicomos/jlbSai?html=Bur&base=technica&ref=41507&file=146.pdf&path=77-y1bV-92.pdf), PDF pp. 1-2)

The practice's own aim, in Yuan Ding's rendering, is a search for a spot: "The aim of Kanyu is to discover Xue, an analogous term meaning 'lair', 'cave', 'hole' and used also for critical acupuncture points, where Qi concentrates" (same paper). The rule the tradition is best known for is read there as practical rather than mystical: "Sitting north and facing south was traditionally a fundamental ingredient in properly siting dwellings, and it is employed widely throughout China even today. Such an orientation is a device for obtaining the best advantage of sun and wind" (same paper).

### Siting and orientation

The Form branch is the oldest; it was originally concerned with the location and orientation of tombs, and later took up homes and other buildings: "The Form branch was originally concerned with the location and orientation of tombs (Yin House feng shui), which was of great importance. The branch then progressed to the consideration of homes and other buildings (Yang House feng shui)" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feng_shui)). The Compass branch uses the luopan, "a disc marked with formulas in concentric rings around a magnetic compass" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feng_shui)).

### Modern re-reading

The paper's own project is a re-interpretation: feng shui is "facing two problems: one is the ideological matter in its birthplace, China; the other is the re-interpretation by modern scientific framework due to the international impact" (same paper). Some scholars, the paper reports, "re-defined Wudang architectural group with the western theories of environmental science, environmental aesthetics, contextism, bio-architecture, and so on," finding that "many principles of Kanyu in Wudang architectural group are also found fitting well into the framework of modern science" (same paper). The New World Encyclopedia records the same movement from the other side: "Environmental scientists and landscape architects have researched traditional feng shui and its methodologies," and architects surveyed in Sydney and Hong Kong "generally concurred with the ideal feng shui model" ([New World Encyclopedia](https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Feng_shui)). For the compendium the interest is exactly this double reading: a tradition that sits buildings by an invisible flow, and a modern reader who finds in the same rules an empirical fit with sun, wind and shelter.

**Plain words** Place things as if the land had currents: find where the good air pools, sit the house with its back to the cold and its face to the sun. The tradition said qi; the modern reading says sun and wind; the siting advice comes out much the same.
