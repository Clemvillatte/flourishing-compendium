“Janis” — 1972.

Irving Janis's 1972 book on groupthink laid out when a cohesive group is likely to decide badly, what it looks like from the inside, and what follows. The definition and the procedural fixes are on the [[Groupthink theory]] page; this page covers the structure of the model and the evidence behind it, as summarised in Aldag and Fuller's 1993 review in Psychological Bulletin (the Janis books themselves were not opened).

### When it is supposed to happen

Janis (1982) divided the antecedent conditions into three categories. In Aldag and Fuller's account, "a moderate to high level of group cohesion is a necessary but not sufficient condition for groupthink". The second category is structural faults: insulation of the group, no tradition of impartial leadership, no norms requiring methodical procedures, and homogeneous social backgrounds and ideology. The third is a provocative situational context, mainly stress from external threats combined with a low hope of finding a better solution than the leader's, or from members' recent failures and a sense that no morally correct alternative exists. ([Aldag & Fuller 1993](https://web.mit.edu/writing/2012/Grad_Summary_Readings/beyond_fiasco_final.pdf))

### What it looks like

> "There are several supposed symptoms of groupthink (cf. Janis & Mann, 1977, pp. 130-131), including an illusion of invulnerability, rationalization to discount warnings and other negative feedback, belief in the inherent morality of the group, stereotyped views of members of opposing groups, pressure on dissenters, self-censorship, illusion of unanimity, and self-appointed 'mindguards' acting to shield the group from adverse information." (Aldag & Fuller 1993)

The consequences they list from Janis's work include limited discussion of alternatives, ignoring new information about the risks of a chosen course, and a failure to consider what might go wrong and so to make contingency plans.

### The evidence base

Early support, Aldag and Fuller say, rested almost entirely on retrospective case studies of five American foreign-policy crises: the Marshall Plan, the invasion of North Korea, the Bay of Pigs invasion, the Cuban Missile Crisis, and the escalation of the Vietnam conflict. Tetlock's 1979 content analysis of the same crises was partly consistent with Janis's theory (groupthink decision makers were more simplistic in their perceptions) and partly not (they did not make significantly more negative references to Communist states). The review also quotes Moorhead's 1982 remark that there had been "no systematic research testing any of its propositions" since the book appeared.
