Ockham's razor is the medieval methodological rule named after William of Ockham (c. 1287-1347). The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry, by Sharon Kaye, defines it: "Ockham's Razor is the principle of parsimony or simplicity according to which the simpler theory is more likely to be true" ([IEP, section 2](https://iep.utm.edu/ockham/)). The entry's section on the razor also notes the standard maxim associated with it: "Entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity" ([same entry, section 2](https://iep.utm.edu/ockham/)).

### Ockham's own relation to the principle

The entry is explicit that the name is later than the principle and that Ockham did not originate it: "Ockham did not invent this principle; it is found in Aristotle, Aquinas, and other philosophers Ockham read" and "Nor did he call the principle a 'razor.'" In fact, the first known use of the term occurs in 1852, in the work of the Scottish philosopher Sir William Hamilton. For Ockham himself, the entry says, "the principle of simplicity limits the multiplication of hypotheses not necessarily entities", and he favored the formulation "It is useless to do with more what can be done with less," which the entry reads as implying that "theories are meant to do things, namely, explain and predict, and these things can be accomplished more effectively with fewer assumptions" ([all from section 2](https://iep.utm.edu/ockham/)). The entry adds that the assumption that the world is maximally simple is itself problematic, and that Ockham never makes it ([section 2](https://iep.utm.edu/ockham/)).
