Ockham's razor is the medieval methodological rule associated with William of Ockham, an English Franciscan philosopher (the entry infers a birth between 1280 and 1285 and a death between 1347 and 1349). The Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy's entry, by Sharon Kaye, defines it:

> "Ockham's Razor is the principle of parsimony or simplicity according to which the simpler theory is more likely to be true." (Kaye, [IEP, section 2](https://iep.utm.edu/ockham/))

The maxim most often attached to the principle runs: "Entities should not be multiplied beyond necessity" ([same entry, section 2](https://iep.utm.edu/ockham/)).

### Ockham's own relation to the principle

The entry is explicit that the name is later than the principle: "Ockham did not invent this principle; it is found in Aristotle, Aquinas, and other philosophers Ockham read", and "Nor did he call the principle a 'razor.'" The first known use of the term occurs in 1852, in the work of the Scottish philosopher Sir William Hamilton.

For Ockham himself, "the principle of simplicity limits the multiplication of hypotheses not necessarily entities", and he favored the formulation "It is useless to do with more what can be done with less". The entry reads that formulation as implying that "theories are meant to do things, namely, explain and predict, and these things can be accomplished more effectively with fewer assumptions" ([all from section 2](https://iep.utm.edu/ockham/)).

The entry adds a caution: the assumption that the world is maximally simple is itself suspicious, and "Ockham never makes this assumption" ([section 2](https://iep.utm.edu/ockham/)).
