Neurobiology of addiction is the study of what changes in the brain's circuits when a person becomes addicted to drugs. Its question is why quitting is so hard for so many people, and the review's answer is a three-part one. George Koob and Nora Volkow's 2016 review, "Neurobiology of addiction: a neurocircuitry analysis" (Lancet Psychiatry, 3(8), 760-773), describes a three-stage framework:

> "Drug addiction represents a dramatic dysregulation of motivational circuits that is caused by a combination of exaggerated incentive salience and habit formation, reward deficits and stress surfeits, and compromised executive function in three stages." (Koob & Volkow 2016, [abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27475769/))

### The three stages

The abstract maps each stage to its circuitry:

> "The rewarding effects of drugs of abuse, development of incentive salience, and development of drug-seeking habits in the binge/intoxication stage involve changes in dopamine and opioid peptides in the basal ganglia." ([abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27475769/))

> "The increases in negative emotional states and dysphoric and stress-like responses in the withdrawal/negative affect stage involve decreases in the function of the dopamine component of the reward system and recruitment of brain stress neurotransmitters, such as corticotropin-releasing factor and dynorphin, in the neurocircuitry of the extended amygdala." ([abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27475769/))

> "The craving and deficits in executive function in the so-called preoccupation/anticipation stage involve the dysregulation of key afferent projections from the prefrontal cortex and insula, including glutamate, to the basal ganglia and extended amygdala." ([abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27475769/))

Read plainly, the stages run from drug reward to drug distress to drug craving. In the first, the rewarding effects of drugs and the building of drug-seeking habits involve dopamine and opioid-peptide changes in the basal ganglia. In the second, negative emotional states involve a drop in reward-system dopamine and the recruitment of brain stress neurotransmitters. In the third, craving and deficits in executive function involve dysregulated projections from the prefrontal cortex and insula. Each stage has its own circuit, and the review's title, "a neurocircuitry analysis", signals its method: addiction is analyzed circuit by circuit.
