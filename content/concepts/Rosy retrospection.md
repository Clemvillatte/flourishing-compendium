Rosy retrospection is the "rosy view" finding: people's later recollection of an event comes out more positive than the experience actually was while they were living it. Mitchell, Thompson, Peterson and Cronk tested it directly, following people through a trip to Europe, a Thanksgiving vacation and a 3-week bicycle trip in California, asking them before, during and after. Their abstract's summary sentence:

> The results of all three studies supported the hypothesis that people's expectations of personal events are more positive than their actual experience during the event itself, and their subsequent recollection of that event is more positive than the actual experience.

(Mitchell, Thompson, Peterson & Cronk, "Temporal Adjustments in the evaluation of events: The 'Rosy View'," Journal of Experimental Social Psychology 33(4), 1997, [Kellogg abstract page](https://www.kellogg.northwestern.edu/academics-research/research/detail/1997/temporal-adjustments-in-the-evaluation-of-events-the-rosy/))

The mechanism the abstract proposes ties the gap to what happens during the event: more negative thoughts, caused by distractions, disappointment and a less positive view of the self. The authors add that these effects are short-lived, because "within days after the event, people have much more positive evaluations of the event."

The scope is worth keeping in view: these studies concerned trips and holidays of a few weeks. The finding supports my [[CO059 — Recognise rosy retrospection — the past is remembered more fondly than it was experienced]], which extends it with a caution: the long-past "good old days" are an extrapolation from this result rather than something the studies demonstrated.

## Related concepts

- [[Fading affect bias]] — the complementary mechanism by which negative affect fades faster than positive affect in memory
- [[Hindsight bias]] — the retrospective distortion that runs in the opposite direction, about knowledge rather than warmth
