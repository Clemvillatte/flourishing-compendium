Nihilism is the belief that all values are baseless and that nothing can be known or communicated. It is often associated with extreme pessimism and a radical skepticism that condemns existence ([Pratt, Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/nihilism/)). Its most common current form is existential nihilism, defined in the same article:

> "Existential nihilism is the notion that life has no intrinsic meaning or value, and it is, no doubt, the most commonly used and understood sense of the word today." ([Pratt, IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/nihilism/))

### Beauvoir's reading

The compendium's principle E072 (Practise avoiding the nihilist) is built on Beauvoir's analysis in The Ethics of Ambiguity, whose formula the principle quotes:

> "Nihilism is disappointed seriousness which has turned back upon itself." (de Beauvoir, [The Ethics of Ambiguity, Part II](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/ethics/de-beauvoir/ambiguity/ch02.htm))

On her account the nihilist is close to the spirit of seriousness: unable to be anything, he decides to be nothing, wanting to rid himself of the anxiety of his freedom by denying the world and himself ([Part II](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/ethics/de-beauvoir/ambiguity/ch02.htm)). The IEP article's Nietzsche summary gives the other side of the compendium's pairing: "For Nietzsche, there is no objective order or structure in the world except what we give it", and he believed "we could–at a terrible price–eventually work through nihilism" ([Pratt, IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/nihilism/)).
