In their 1987 paper, Lane and Schwartz present a cognitive-developmental theory of emotional awareness. They describe emotional awareness as a type of cognitive processing that develops through five structural levels, deriving the proposed sequence from the theories of Piaget and Werner ([Lane & Schwartz, 1987](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3812780/)).

> "The five levels of structural transformation are awareness of bodily sensations, the body in action, individual feelings, blends of feelings, and blends of blends of feelings." (Lane & Schwartz, American Journal of Psychiatry 144(2), 133-143, 1987, [abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3812780/))

### What the differentiation studies measure

Barrett and colleagues' 2001 study examined emotion differentiation: how distinctly people report emotions of the same valence across repeated ratings. The researchers investigated whether differentiation was associated with the frequency of emotion regulation ([Barrett et al., 2001](https://www.affective-science.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/01MaprelationDiffReg.pdf)).

> "Individuals differ considerably in their emotion experience. Some experience emotions in a highly differentiated manner, clearly distinguishing among a variety of negative and positive discrete emotions. Others experience emotions in a relatively undifferentiated manner, treating a range of like-valence terms as interchangeable." (Barrett, Gross, Christensen & Benvenuto, Cognition and Emotion 15(6), p. 713, 2001, [full PDF](https://www.affective-science.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/01MaprelationDiffReg.pdf))

Barrett and colleagues analysed diary ratings from 53 participants, who rated their most intense emotional experience each day for 14 days using nine affect terms. Emotion regulation was assessed through a recall-based laboratory measure obtained before the diary period. Greater negative-emotion differentiation was associated with more frequent reported regulation of negative emotions, particularly at higher emotional intensity. ([Barrett et al., 2001](https://www.affective-science.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/01MaprelationDiffReg.pdf))

Positive differentiation was not significantly related to the positive-emotion regulation strategy index. These associations do not establish that teaching emotion labels improves regulation. ([Barrett et al., 2001](https://www.affective-science.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/01MaprelationDiffReg.pdf))

The authors' proposed rationale:

> "Knowing how we feel helps to inform us about the significance of the immediate situation, to work out what we should do next, and to indicate what, if anything, we should do about changing how we feel." (Cognition and Emotion 15(6), p. 714, 2001, [full PDF](https://www.affective-science.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/01MaprelationDiffReg.pdf))
