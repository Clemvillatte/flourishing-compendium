The suspension of the natural attitude is Husserl's name for the move that starts phenomenology: holding back the automatic assumption that the world exists just as we take it, so that experience itself can be examined. The Greek term is epoché.

> "Husserl's transcendental turn also involved his discovery of the method of epoché (from the Greek skeptics' notion of abstaining from belief). We are to practice phenomenology, Husserl proposed, by 'bracketing' the question of the existence of the natural world around us. We thereby turn our attention, in reflection, to the structure of our own conscious experience." (Smith, "Phenomenology", Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, section "The History and Varieties of Phenomenology")

### What the suspension does

The abstention is methodological: the existence question is bracketed while attention turns to the structure of experience. David Woodruff Smith's SEP entry places the method in Husserl's Ideas I (1913), where phenomenology became the study of consciousness approached in the first person. The suspension is what makes that first-person study possible, because the natural attitude's automatic acceptance of the world would otherwise carry every description along with it.
