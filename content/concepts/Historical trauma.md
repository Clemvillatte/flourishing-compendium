“Historical trauma” — Brave Heart and DeBruyn, 1998.

Historical trauma is the name Maria Yellow Horse Brave Heart gave to the injury that colonization leaves in a people: losses so massive and so unresolved that the grief is handed down, and shows up in the living as depression, substance abuse, and despair with no personal cause.

> "American Indians experienced massive losses of lives, land, and culture from European contact and colonization resulting in a long legacy of chronic trauma and unresolved grief across generations." — [Brave Heart & DeBruyn, American Indian and Alaska Native Mental Health Research 8(2) (1998), full text](https://coloradosph.cuanschutz.edu/docs/librariesprovider205/journal_files/vol8/8_2_1998_60_yellowhorsebraveheart.pdf)

### What it is and how it is treated

The general definition Brave Heart later fixed: historical trauma as "cumulative and psychological wounding over the life span and across generations, emanating from massive group trauma experience" ([NCTSN, Conversations about Historical Trauma](https://www.nctsn.org/sites/default/files/resources/spotlight_on_culture_conversations_about_historical_trauma_part_one.pdf)). The treatment model Brave Heart built is a ceremony-grounded answer rather than a diagnosis: "The model relies on four components designed to foster healing from grief: 1) confronting the history; 2) understanding the trauma and its effects; 3) releasing the pain; and 4) transcending the trauma." The two original citations in my parallels, on practising healing from historical trauma and treating intergenerational trauma as a real mechanism, both turn on this pair of ideas — that the wound is transgenerational and that healing must go through the history rather than around it.
