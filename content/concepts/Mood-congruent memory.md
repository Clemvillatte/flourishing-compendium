Mood-congruent memory is the family of hypotheses claiming that affect biases recall toward affectively congruent material. Its canonical source is Bower's 1981 "Mood and Memory" (American Psychologist 36, 129-148); the paper's own abstract is not indexed in PubMed, so the hypotheses are quoted here as later testers state them. The testers distinguish two versions: "the encoding mood congruent hypothesis which states that information semantically related to mood at encoding is retrieved more readily than information unrelated to mood at encoding", and "the recall mood congruent hypothesis which states that information semantically related to mood at recall is retrieved more readily than information unrelated to mood at recall" ([Foa, McNally and Murdock 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2930439/)).

### The evidence is not one-sided

The same paper is a useful caution. In speech-anxious students with induced anxiety, "No support was obtained for any of the three hypotheses" tested — state-dependent, encoding-congruent and recall-congruent — and the authors cautiously conclude: "This suggests that attention to threat information may diminish in aroused nonclinical subjects" (ibid.). Later work on anxiety and cognition continued to frame the bias in Bower's terms while arguing about the mechanism (see [MacLeod and Mathews 1991](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1759958/), which reviews the question through "Bower's (1981) network" theory).

My use is epistemic rather than therapeutic: recall run through a mood is a sample, not a record, which is the working premise of AF023 and CO054.
