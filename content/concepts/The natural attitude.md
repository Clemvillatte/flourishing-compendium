The natural attitude is Husserl's name for the stance we are in almost all the time: absorbed in the world, taking its reality for granted. The Stanford Encyclopedia's summary: "In ordinary life we are so absorbed in the world that we simply take its reality for granted; it is simply there waiting to be discovered and investigated. This stance towards the world is so fundamental and deeply rooted that Husserl calls it the natural attitude" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Edmund Husserl (Zahavi)](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). The concept comes from Ideas I (1913), and it is not a defect of everyday life but its condition: science itself runs on it, though it "must be contrasted with the properly philosophical attitude, which critically questions the very foundation of experience and scientific thought" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)).

### The suspension

The epoché is the method's countermove to this absorption. Of the world taken for granted: "We keep it, in order to be able to investigate it, but we bracket its validity. It is this procedure that is known by the name of epoché" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). The bracketing is not doubt or denial: "The purpose of the epoché is not to doubt, neglect, abandon, or exclude reality from our research; rather its aim is to suspend or neutralize a certain dogmatic attitude towards reality" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). What is lost is only "a certain naivety, the naivety of simply taken the world for granted, thereby ignoring the contribution of consciousness" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)).

The reduction is the analysis the epoché makes possible. The entry glosses it as a move that "leads from the natural sphere back to (re-ducere) its transcendental foundation" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). The lifeworld, "the world we live and feel at home in," which "forms the basis for our daily actions," marks the later limit of the movement: even science's abstractions presuppose the world as lived, which remains "the basis and meaning-foundation for scientific research" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)).
