The valuing happiness paradox is the empirical finding that making happiness one's goal can reduce it. Mauss and colleagues' two studies found that people who valued happiness more felt worse precisely in the situations where happiness was most available, and the mechanism was disappointment:

> "This effect was mediated by participants' disappointment at their own feelings. Paradoxically, therefore, valuing happiness may lead people to be less happy just when happiness is within reach."
> — [Mauss, Tamir, Anderson & Savino, Emotion 11 (2011), PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21517168/)

The mechanism is a feature of goal pursuit generally: a goal sets the standard against which its own achievement is measured. With most goals, falling short and feeling disappointed is compatible with continuing to pursue the goal. Happiness is the exception — the disappointment at not feeling happy is itself the opposite of the goal, so the evaluation poisons what it evaluates.

The studies sharpened where the effect appears. In conditions of high life stress, people who valued happiness were no less happy than others; they could attribute a shortfall to circumstances. In low-stress conditions, where happiness seemed obtainable, valuing it predicted lower happiness. Experimentally, participants induced to value happiness reacted less positively to a happy film clip, and their disappointment at their own feelings carried the effect.

The practical lesson is not to abandon happiness but to stop aiming at it directly — the paradox is about pursuit, not about the state. Happiness arrives more reliably as a by-product of engagement, connection and meaning than as a monitored target.
