Status quo bias is the tendency to stick with a current or previous choice more often than a neutral comparison would predict. William Samuelson and Richard Zeckhauser introduced the term in a 1988 paper in the Journal of Risk and Uncertainty. Their abstract begins: "Most real decisions, unlike those of economics texts, have a status quo alternative—that is, doing nothing or maintaining one's current or previous decision." Experiments showed that "individuals disproportionately stick with the status quo", and data on faculty members' health plans and retirement programs showed the bias to be "substantial in important real decisions" ([Springer, abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF00055564)).

### The experiments

A review of the literature describes the investment task. In one version, participants inherited money and chose among four investment options. In another, they learned that the money was already invested in one option, and they were more likely to choose that pre-selected option ([Godefroid et al., Management Review Quarterly](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/307394/1/s11301-022-00283-8.pdf)). A replication paper adds that an option was chosen more often when framed as the status quo than when framed neutrally or as a departure from it, and that the preference grew as the number of options increased ([Xiao et al., Meta-Psychology preprint](https://mgto.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/04/Xiao-et-al-status-quo-bias-Samuelson-Zeckhauser-1988-replication-final-preprint.pdf)).

### Three kinds of explanation

According to the same review, Samuelson and Zeckhauser grouped explanations into rational decision-making, cognitive misperception and psychological commitment. The first covers the wish to avoid uncertainty and transition costs. The second is mainly loss aversion, where potential losses from change loom larger than gains. The third is most often framed as the sunk cost effect ([Godefroid et al.](https://www.econstor.eu/bitstream/10419/307394/1/s11301-022-00283-8.pdf)).

### Replication

The replication ran two pre-registered experiments (n = 311 and n = 316) on four of the original scenarios. It found "strong empirical support" in three, covering budget allocation, investment portfolios and college job offers, but not in the wagon colour scenario ([Xiao et al., Europe PMC](https://europepmc.org/article/ppr/ppr325535)).

### Scope

The original abstract does not say that people choose the status quo "even when alternatives are better". Because rational explanations are among the proposed causes, sticking with the status quo is not always an error. This page draws on the abstract and secondary accounts, not the full paper.
