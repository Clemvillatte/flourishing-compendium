Social penetration theory is Irwin Altman and Dalmas Taylor's account of how relationships deepen: through self-disclosure that moves from outer layers toward inner ones ([Wikipedia, Social penetration theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_penetration_theory)). The article on the theory states its core claim:

> "SPT posits that relationship development progresses through stages characterized by increasing breadth and depth of self-disclosure." ([Wikipedia, Social penetration theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_penetration_theory))

### Origin

The theory was formulated by psychologists Altman of the University of Utah and Taylor of the University of Delaware in 1973 to understand the development of relationships between individuals ([Wikipedia, Social penetration theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_penetration_theory)). Altman and Taylor noted that relationships "involve different levels of intimacy of exchange or degree of social penetration" ([same article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_penetration_theory)).

### Core claims

The theory's picture of the person is layered:

> "Social penetration theory uses the onion metaphor to show how we reveal deeper parts of ourselves as a relationship grows." ([same article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_penetration_theory))

The same article records the process factors the theory names — uncertainty reduction, disclosure reciprocity, the assessment of rewards and cost, potential barriers, and de-penetration, the winding-down counterpart ([Wikipedia, Social penetration theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_penetration_theory)).

An everyday-life illustration (imagined, not a reported case): two colleagues start with shop talk — the commute, the project. Weeks in, one mentions a parent's illness and the other answers with something of her own; the exchange has moved a layer in. Where it stalls at shop talk, the relationship stays at the breadth of the workday.
