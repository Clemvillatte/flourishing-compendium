James's chapter "Habit" in The Principles of Psychology (1890) ties repeated conduct to the formation of character ([James 1890](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin4.htm)). The chapter runs on two tracks: a physiological account of how habits form, and a moral argument about what the forming adds up to.

### The mechanism

In James's physiological account, habit rests on plasticity, which he defines as "the possession of a structure weak enough to yield to an influence, but strong enough not to yield all at once" ([James 1890](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin4.htm)). His first proposition is that "the phenomena of habit in living beings are due to the plasticity of the organic materials of which their bodies are composed" ([James 1890](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin4.htm)). He borrows Carpenter's summary of the fact: "our nervous system grows to the modes in which it has been exercised" ([James 1890](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin4.htm)). The payoff is efficiency: habit "simplifies the movements required to achieve a given result, makes them more accurate and diminishes fatigue" ([James 1890](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin4.htm)). James presses the point socially:

> "Habit is thus the enormous fly-wheel of society, its most precious conservative agent." ([James 1890](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin4.htm))

### The moral

Repeated acts accumulate, and James counts the accumulation in both directions:

> "We are spinning our own fates, good or evil, and never to be undone. Every smallest stroke of virtue or of vice leaves its never so little scar." ([James 1890](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin4.htm))

> "As we become permanent drunkards by so many separate drinks, so we become saints in the moral, and authorities and experts in the practical and scientific spheres, by so many separate acts and hours of work." ([James 1890](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin4.htm))

### The maxims

James's advice for education and habit aims at making "our nervous system our ally instead of our enemy", which means making "automatic and habitual, as early as possible, as many useful actions as we can" ([James 1890](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin4.htm)). Once a habit is in training: "Never suffer an exception to occur till the new habit is securely rooted in your life", and "Seize the very first possible opportunity to act on every resolution you make" ([James 1890](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin4.htm)). James adds a maxim for the will itself: "Keep the faculty of effort alive in you by a little gratuitous exercise every day" ([James 1890](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin4.htm)). The person who skips all this is the one James describes as "in whom nothing is habitual but indecision" ([James 1890](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/James/Principles/prin4.htm)).

## Related concepts

- [[Habit]] — the general phenomenon, which this page reads through James's chapter
- [[Habit formation]] — the acquisition question in its modern form, automaticity growing with repetition; James's chapter asks it physiologically
- [[Habit maintenance]] — the stage that begins where the rooting maxim ends: keeping a habit going once it is secure
- [[Habit stacking]] — a modern recipe anchoring a new behaviour to an existing routine; its act-now step echoes James's maxim to seize the first opportunity to act
- [[Shaping (Skinner)]] — builds complex behaviours by reinforcing successive approximations; resembles James's counting of separate acts, with reinforcement where James has nervous-system plasticity
- [[Behavior analysis]] — the Skinnerian tradition whose three-term contingency is its own answer to the same phenomenon
