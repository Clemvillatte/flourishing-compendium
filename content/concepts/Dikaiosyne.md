Dikaiosyne is the Greek virtue-word usually rendered justice or righteousness; the personified Dike carries the same idea as a goddess, and the derived name Dikaiosyne was her epithet ([Wikipedia, Dike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dikaiosyne)). As a virtue the word has two classical shapes. For [[T002 — Aristotle|Aristotle]] it is complete virtue turned toward others: "This form of justice, then, is complete virtue, but not absolutely, but in relation to our neighbour" ([Nicomachean Ethics V](http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.5.v.html)). His reason: "many men can exercise virtue in their own affairs, but not in their relations to their neighbour" (ibid.), so that "justice is often thought to be the greatest of virtues", with the proverb "'in justice is every virtue comprehended'" (ibid.).

For Plato, in the Republic's fourth book, dikaiosyne is the right order of the soul's parts:

> "The just man does not permit the several elements within him to interfere with one another, or any of them to do the work of others — he sets in order his own inner life, and is his own master and his own law, and at peace with himself." — Plato, Republic IV (Jowett translation, [Internet Classics Archive](http://classics.mit.edu/Plato/republic.5.iv.html))

The formula that keeps recurring in the dialogue is "doing one's own business, and not being a busybody" (ibid.), and the summary runs: "Then virtue is the health and beauty and well-being of the soul, and vice the disease and weakness and deformity of the same" (ibid.). One virtue, then, seen from two angles: the soul's internal order, and that order exercised toward other people.
