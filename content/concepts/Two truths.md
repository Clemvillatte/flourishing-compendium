Two truths is Nāgārjuna's doctrine that the Buddha's teaching operates on two levels at once: a conventional truth, in which things exist, act and are talked about in the ordinary way, and an ultimate truth, in which those same things are empty of inherent existence. Neither cancels the other. The verses that state it, in the middle of his defense of emptiness against the charge that it destroys the Four Noble Truths:

> "The Buddha's teaching of the Dharma / Is based on two truths: / A truth of worldly convention / And an ultimate truth. // Those who do not understand / The distinction drawn between these two truths / Do not understand / The Buddha's profound truth. // Without a foundation (reliance) in conventional truth / The significance of the ultimate cannot be taught. / Without understanding the significance of the ultimate, / Liberation (nirvana) is not achieved."
> — [Nāgārjuna, Mūlamadhyamakakārikā 24.8–10, trans. Jay Garfield](https://kokyohenkel.weebly.com/uploads/1/2/7/4/127410773/nagarjuna_-_mulamadhyamakakarika_ch_24__garfield_.pdf)

The point of the doctrine is the relation between the two levels, and that relation is dependence. Emptiness is not a deeper world behind appearances; it is the way appearances work — things are empty because they arise dependently, and their dependence is visible only in conventional description. So the ultimate truth cannot even be stated without borrowing the conventional, and someone who rejects the conventional outright has no ladder to climb.

The practical error the doctrine guards against runs in both directions. Taking only the conventional, one reifies: things seem self-made and graspable. Taking only the ultimate, one goes numb: if all is empty, nothing matters. Nāgārjuna's middle way holds both — act in the conventional world, understanding its emptiness — which is why the doctrine is introduced precisely to defend, not dissolve, the Buddha's practical teaching.
