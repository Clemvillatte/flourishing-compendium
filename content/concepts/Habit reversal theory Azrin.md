Habit reversal is a behavioural treatment developed by Nathan Azrin and Ronald Nunn in 1973 for nervous habits and tics. Their rationale was that "nervous habits persist because of response chaining, limited awareness, excessive practice and social tolerance". The treatment countered these: "the client practiced movements which were the reverse of the nervous habit, he learned to be aware of each instance of the habit and to differentiate it from its usual response chain and he was given social approval for his efforts to inhibit the habit" ([Azrin and Nunn, 1973, Behaviour Research and Therapy 11, 619-628](http://www.nathanazrin.com/uploads/5/8/0/9/5809267/1973_habit_reversal__a_method_of_eliminating_nervous_habits_and_tics.pdf)).

### The two core steps

Awareness comes first. In the original study "the client was required to describe the details of the movement to the counselor, using a mirror if necessary, while he re-enacted several instances of the typical movement". Then a competing response is practised: "a physically competing response should be established to interfere with the habit". Clients were told to use it "for about 3 min" after "either the temptation to perform a tic or the actual occurrence of a tic."

Later work narrowed the package. A review for a broad audience notes that "a simplified HRT consisting of two core elements, awareness training and competing response training, is as effective as the full package" ([Reducing risky behavior with habit reversal, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7404378/)). It describes awareness training as built from response description, response detection and early warning training, and says the competing response "physically prevents the completion of the habit".

### What the evidence shows

The 1973 study treated 12 clients with diverse nervous habits. The authors report that "for every client the habit was reduced by at least 90 per cent". It was an open trial with no control group, so it cannot isolate the method's effect.

A meta-analysis of 575 participants in 18 studies found habit reversal therapy "efficacious" for "a wide variety of maladaptive repetitive behaviors, including stuttering, tics, nail biting, temporomandibular disorder, thumb sucking, and mixed repetitive oral-digital habits", with "a large effect size pre-treatment to final post-treatment assessment, d=0.80" compared to control conditions ([Bate et al., 2011, Clinical Psychology Review, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21549664/)).

### Which part does the work?

A 1998 review in the Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis analysed "the behavioral processes involved in habit reversal" and "the limitations of habit reversal" ([Miltenberger et al., Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1901/jaba.1998.31-447)). A Western Michigan University thesis abstract notes that awareness training alone reduced or eliminated tics and nervous habits in four subjects, "suggesting that a portion of the effect attributed to the competing response may be due to the awareness training component" ([Western Michigan University](https://scholarworks.wmich.edu/masters_theses/1275/)). That is a four-person study and should be read as a pointer.

### Scope

Habit reversal comes from clinical work on tics and repetitive habits. Using it as a metaphor for everyday habit change is an extension: the evidence above concerns repetitive behaviours such as tics, nail biting and stuttering.
