Self-serving attribution bias is the tendency to explain your own successes by what you did and your failures by what the world did. The Mezulis, Abramson, Hyde and Hankin 2004 meta-analysis in Psychological Bulletin states: "The authors conducted a meta-analysis of 266 studies, yielding 503 independent effect sizes. The average d was 0.96, indicating a large bias. The bias was present in nearly all samples" ([Mezulis et al. 2004, Psychological Bulletin 130(5), 711-747](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15367078/)).

> "Researchers have suggested the presence of a self-serving attributional bias, with people making more internal, stable, and global attributions for positive events than for negative events. This study examined the magnitude, ubiquity, and adaptiveness of this bias. The authors conducted a meta-analysis of 266 studies, yielding 503 independent effect sizes. The average d was 0.96, indicating a large bias. The bias was present in nearly all samples."
> — [Mezulis, Abramson, Hyde & Hankin 2004, Psychological Bulletin 130(5), 711-747 (PubMed abstract)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15367078/)

The same review finds the bias is not uniform. Asian samples display significantly smaller biases (d = 0.30) than U.S. (d = 1.05) or Western (d = 0.70) samples, and psychopathology is associated with an attenuated bias, smallest for depression (0.21) ([Mezulis et al. 2004](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15367078/)).

For a reader, the two findings point in opposite directions. The bias is large and present in nearly all samples, so any account of one's own record that assigns the successes to skill and the failures to circumstance deserves suspicion on arrival. The exceptions are equally instructive: where the bias is smallest, in depression, the self-serving direction of the explanation has gone missing. The review examined the bias's adaptiveness rather than assuming it, which is the right posture for a reader deciding where to hold it.
