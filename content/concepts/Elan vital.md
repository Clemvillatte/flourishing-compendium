"Elan vital" — Bergson, Creative Evolution.

## Facets

- **read as creativity** — Bergson, Creative Evolution

The élan vital is Henri Bergson's name, introduced in Creative Evolution (1907), for the push behind life. The term resists translation — "vital impetus" is the standard rendering — and Bergson offered it as an image rather than a force: life behaves like a single impulsion that divides as it grows, branching into plants, animals and minds without following a pre-set plan. The standard reading of the term, as the facet read-as-creativity notes, is the one Bergson himself insisted on: evolution, on his account, produces genuinely new forms rather than unfolding what was already there.

Bergson arrived at the idea by way of lived time. A conscious being never merely repeats itself; each moment of a life modifies the person living it. The passage he built the whole book on reads:

> "for a conscious being, to exist is to change, to change is to mature, to mature is to go on creating oneself endlessly." (Bergson, Creative Evolution, ch. 1, [Mitchell 1911 translation, Brock University](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Bergson/Bergson_1911a/Bergson_1911_01.html))

If that is what existence feels like from inside, Bergson argued, evolution may work the same way from outside — a creative movement running through the generations:

> "So we come back, by a somewhat roundabout way, to the idea we started from, that of an original impetus of life, passing from one generation of germs to the following generation of germs through the developed organisms which bridge the interval between the generations." ([same text](https://brocku.ca/MeadProject/Bergson/Bergson_1911a/Bergson_1911_01.html))

The idea drew sharp criticism — biologists read it as vitalism, a hidden force doing the work that natural selection was already explaining — and Bergson's followers conceded the word invited that reading. The Stanford Encyclopedia records the dispute in these terms: mechanistic and teleological frameworks "fail to account for the efficacy of duration in biological evolution," and the élan vital was Bergson's attempt to name what they miss ([SEP, Bergson](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2012/entries/bergson/)). The concept survives less as biology than as a way of talking about openness: the future of life is not fixed in advance.
