Shaping is B. F. Skinner's method for building a behavior that does not yet exist. Instead of waiting for the finished response to occur so it can be rewarded, the teacher reinforces each small step toward it, raising the criterion as the learner gets closer. Skinner's own analogy, from Science and Human Behavior:

> "Operant conditioning shapes behavior as a sculptor shapes a lump of clay. Although at some point the sculptor seems to have produced an entirely novel object, we can always follow the process back to the original undifferentiated lump, and we can make the successive stages by which we return to this condition as small as we wish." (Skinner, [Science and Human Behavior](https://www.bfskinner.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/02/ScienceHumanBehavior.pdf), 1953, p. 91)

### Why the method works

The chapter's worked example is a pigeon learning to strike a spot: reinforce turning toward the spot first, then a step toward it, then the head movement, then contact, each time withholding reward for the cruder form. The passage states the payoff plainly: "The original probability of the response in its final form is very low; in some cases it may even be zero. In this way we can build complicated operants which would never appear in the repertoire of the organism otherwise. By reinforcing a series of successive approximations, we bring a rare response to a very high probability in a short time." The trick is that the criterion moves only as fast as the behavior does, which is why I pair it with gradual-training principles from other traditions.
