### Definition

Cognitive reappraisal, reframing an emotional situation so it lands differently, is usually counted among the healthiest regulation strategies. Troy, Shallcross and Mauss showed the counting is incomplete: the same skill helps or harms depending on whether the stressor can be changed. Their 170-participant study found:

> For participants with uncontrollable stress, higher cognitive-reappraisal ability was associated with lower levels of depression. In contrast, for participants with controllable stress, higher cognitive-reappraisal ability was associated with greater levels of depression. ([Troy, Shallcross & Mauss 2013](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~eerlab/pdf/papers/A_Person_by_Situation_Approach_to_Emotion_Regulation.pdf))

### Origin

The paper's theoretical frame is a person-by-situation approach: no strategy is good in itself. The mechanism is motivational. When a situation is controllable, the negative emotion is partly a signal to act, and reframing can blunt the signal along with the pain. The authors' own summary line:

> These findings support a theoretical model in which particular emotion-regulation strategies are not adaptive or maladaptive per se; rather, their adaptiveness depends on the context. ([Troy et al. 2013](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~eerlab/pdf/papers/A_Person_by_Situation_Approach_to_Emotion_Regulation.pdf))

### A real-life example

Two employees face bad reviews. The first's problem is a fixed restructuring; reframing it as survivable weather helps her sleep and changes nothing that needed changing. The second's problem is late deliveries he could fix; reframing it as "not that important" soothes the sting and robs the fix of its urgency. Same skill, opposite outcomes.
