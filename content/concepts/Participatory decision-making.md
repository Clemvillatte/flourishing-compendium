### Definition

Participatory decision-making is the practice of shaping choices with the people who must live with them, rather than doing things to or for them. Its psychological backbone, in Bandura's 2001 agentic account, is collective agency: people acting together on shared beliefs. As the paper's abstract opens:

> The capacity to exercise control over the nature and quality of one's life is the essence of humanness. ([Bandura 2001](https://dl.icdst.org/pdfs/files/72609bc781d8ddccf1e412b46f36d596.pdf))

Participation is that capacity exercised in company. Bandura's map of agency has three modes:

> Social cognitive theory distinguishes among three modes of agency: direct personal agency, proxy agency that relies on others to act on one's behest to secure desired outcomes, and collective agency exercised through socially coordinative and interdependent effort. ([Bandura 2001](https://dl.icdst.org/pdfs/files/72609bc781d8ddccf1e412b46f36d596.pdf))

Participatory decision-making lives in the third mode: not handing one's life over to a proxy, not going it alone.

### Origin

The idea draws on a long line running from democratic theory to Bandura's social cognitive account of agency, where the group level is real but not mystical: "People's shared belief in their collective power to produce desired results is a key ingredient of collective agency" (Bandura 2001). He is careful to add that no disembodied group mind does the thinking; it is people acting conjointly. A group deciding together therefore pools more than opinions. It pools efficacy, the felt sense that "we" can pull this off, which no amount of expert decree from outside can install.

### A real-life example

A clinic redesigns its appointment system. The managers' version is elegant and fails; the receptionists and patients, asked to co-design it, trade ideas the managers never had, and the change holds. What made the difference was not extra talent but authorship: the people inside the flow of the day were the ones deciding.
