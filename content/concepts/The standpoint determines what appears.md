Husserl's lesson, pressed hardest in the late work, is that the world is never given from nowhere. Experience arrives from an embodied, historically situated standpoint, and what shows up is shaped by it. His name for that standpoint is the lifeworld: the world we live and feel at home in, a subject-relative world of cups to drink from and chairs to sit in, historically and culturally shaped, a world of cumulative traditions and sedimented meaning.

> "It is thus that all things of the surrounding world possess an orientation to the body [...]. The 'far' is far from me, from my body; the 'to the right' refers back to the right side of my lived body." (Husserl, Ideas II, quoted in the [SEP entry "Edmund Husserl"](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/))

The body is the reason. Spatial objects never appear in their totality, only from a limited perspective, so there is no view from nowhere, only an embodied point of view: the subject is the zero-point from which far and near, right and left are measured. The lifeworld adds the historical depth. Prior experiences leave traces that guide and motivate the ones that follow, and we are socialized into a tradition passed down over generations, one that regulates and organizes experience and action.
