"The examined life" is Socrates' name for a life held up to questioning. He gives it at his trial, in answer to a jury that offers to spare him if he stops talking. Benjamin Jowett's translation of Plato's Apology has him say that to refuse would disobey the god, and that:

> "daily to discourse about virtue, and of those other things about which you hear me examining myself and others, is the greatest good of man, and that the unexamined life is not worth living" (Plato, Apology 38a, Jowett translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1656))

For Socrates examination is not a hobby but the daily business of a person who takes the question of how to live seriously, tested in conversation against anyone who claims to know the answer.

### The method and its honesty

The method is questioning. Take a claim about how to live, ask what it really means, watch it fail under cases, and hold the revised claim more lightly than before. What keeps it honest is Socrates' profession of ignorance. After questioning a man with a reputation for wisdom he says to himself:

> "I neither know nor think that I know. In this latter particular, then, I seem to have slightly the advantage of him." (Plato, Apology 21d, Jowett translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/1656))

That stance is the ancestor of [[Fallibilism]]. Its twin on this site is [[The unexamined life]], and the Delphic self-knowledge behind it connects to [[Self-as-context]].

### Examination is not rumination

Rumination circles and changes nothing. Examination is structured, answerable to evidence and aimed at living better. The compendium pairs it with metacognition, thinking about one's own thinking, and with practice rules that give it a shape.

A small case: each evening, five minutes with three written questions. What did I do today? What did it serve? What will I do differently? Written down, tomorrow's self can examine today's. (Illustrative scenario, not a reported case.)
