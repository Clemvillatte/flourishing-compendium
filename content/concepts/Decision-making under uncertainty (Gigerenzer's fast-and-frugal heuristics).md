Fast-and-frugal heuristics are the subject of Gigerenzer's research program on decision making under uncertainty: simple rules that ignore part of the available information and still judge well. The compendium's page for the program's 2011 review by Gigerenzer and Gaissmaier defines the object of study this way:

> "Heuristics are efficient cognitive processes, conscious or unconscious, that ignore part of the information." (Gigerenzer & Gaissmaier, [Heuristic decision making, Annual Review of Psychology 62 (2011)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21126183/))

The work comes out of the Center for Adaptive Behavior and Cognition at the Max Planck Institute for Human Development, whose review summarizes tests of formal models of heuristic inference "in business organizations, health care, and legal institutions" ([abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21126183/)).

### The core claim

The classical view held that heuristic decisions imply greater errors than "rational" decisions as defined by logic or statistical models. The review's summary reverses the burden of proof: "it is an empirical rather than an a priori issue how well cognitive heuristics function in an uncertain world" ([ibid.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21126183/)). Two findings anchor the program:

- individuals and organizations often rely on simple heuristics in an adaptive way
- "ignoring part of the information can lead to more accurate judgments than weighting and adding all information, for instance for low predictability and small samples" ([ibid.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21126183/))

### How the theory explains

The form of the theory is mechanistic and ecological at once: heuristics are studied through formal models, and their success is explained by the match between a heuristic's building blocks and the environmental structures they exploit. The review states that formal models are "indispensable" for answering both which heuristics people use and when people should rely on them ([ibid.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21126183/)).

For a person facing a thin-evidence choice (low predictability, few comparable cases), the practical reading is that doing more calculation is not automatically doing better: the extra inputs may add noise.
