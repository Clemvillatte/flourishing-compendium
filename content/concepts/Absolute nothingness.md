“Absolute nothingness” — Nishida, An Inquiry into the Good.

Absolute nothingness (zettai mu) is the term Nishida Kitarō, founder of the Kyoto School, gave to the ultimate "place" (basho) of his mature philosophy. The concept first became central to his thought in the mid-1920s, and he kept reworking it through his last completed essay of 1945, The Logic of Place and the Religious Worldview ([SEP, Kyoto School](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kyoto-school/)). His premise is that the meaning of "to be" is "to be within"; the ultimate "within" is the topos of absolute nothingness ([SEP, Nishida Kitarō](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nishida-kitaro/)).

> “absolute nothingness transcends all that is, but at the same time all that is arises through it” (Nishida Kitarō, NKZ IX, 6, quoted in [SEP, Nishida Kitarō](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nishida-kitaro/))

The concept turns on a distinction between two nothingnesses. Relative nothingness, such as the field of consciousness, is still opposed to being; absolute nothingness underlies both sides of that opposition ([SEP, Nishida Kitarō](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nishida-kitaro/)). Nishida plays on the Japanese word zettai, which literally means breaking through or overcoming opposition, so his absolute nothingness is not one term in a pair ([SEP, Nishida Kitarō](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nishida-kitaro/)). Rather than a mere absence of being, it is active and creative in forming the actual world, an uncommon kind of foundation that functions through self-negation ([SEP, Nishida Kitarō](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nishida-kitaro/)).

In his final years Nishida suggested that absolute nothingness is the best way to understand God or the absolute, which he defines as that which contains its own absolute self-negation within itself ([SEP, Kyoto School](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kyoto-school/)). In the parlance of Zen he writes that because this is absolute nothingness, the mountain is mountain, the river is river, and all beings are just as they are (NKZ V, 182, quoted in [SEP, Kyoto School](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/kyoto-school/)). The pages citing this concept place the Daoist empty space of the wheel and the room beside this place in which things can appear as what they are.
