Basic emotion theory holds that a small number of emotions are universal, biologically grounded, and distinct from one another in expression, physiology and adaptive function. The version associated with Paul Ekman treats each emotion as a family of related states rather than a single state, as he put it in his 1993 review of the facial expression research:

> "Just as it is useful to think of expressions as constituting families, I have proposed (Ekman, 1992a) that we consider each emotion as constituting a family of related affective states, which share commonalities in their expression, physiological activity, and in the types of appraisal that call them forth. These shared characteristics within an emotion family should distinguish one emotion family from another." (Paul Ekman, [Facial Expression and Emotion, American Psychologist 48(4), 1993](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/~pr10/iui/ekman93.pdf))

The theory's evidence base is the cross-cultural work on faces: Ekman reports robust, consistent evidence of a distinctive, universal facial expression for anger, fear, enjoyment, sadness, and disgust, from both labelling studies across literate and preliterate cultures and studies of actual expression. Distinctive universal expressions have been identified for anger, fear, disgust, sadness, and enjoyment. The criteria that mark an emotion as basic include distinctive universal signals, presence in other primates, distinctive physiology, distinctive universals in antecedent events, coherence among emotional response systems, quick onset, brief duration, automatic appraisal, and unbidden occurrence. For the wiki this theory sits opposite constructed theories of affect on the same shelf: it claims a small evolved set with dedicated machinery, which is the position the constructed-affect page argues against.
