Self-legislation is Kant's name for the structure of moral authority: the principle that binds the will is one the will must be regarded as giving itself. The concept pairs with its failure — heteronomy, the will taking its law from something else.

> "Thus the will is not subject simply to the law, but so subject that it must be regarded as itself giving the law and, on this ground only, subject to the law (of which it can regard itself as the author)" (Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, Abbott trans., [Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-h/5682-h.htm)).

### The two-sided structure

The passage is built on a double claim: the will is subject to law, and it is the author of that law. Kant introduces the principle behind this as "the idea of the will of every rational being as a universally legislative will" (Groundwork, Abbott trans.). Kant connects autonomy with dignity: "Autonomy then is the basis of the dignity of human and of every rational nature" (Groundwork, Abbott trans.).

### The failure mode

Heteronomy is the will going out of itself for its law: "If the will seeks the law which is to determine it anywhere else than in the fitness of its maxims to be universal laws of its own dictation, consequently if it goes out of itself and seeks this law in the character of any of its objects, there always results heteronomy. The will in that case does not give itself the law, but it is given by the object through its relation to the will" (Groundwork, Abbott trans.).
