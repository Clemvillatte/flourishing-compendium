Empathy — the felt understanding of another's experience. Its tradition-spanning family: Buddhist compassion, Confucian shu, Hume's sympathy, Levinas's responsibility.

Empathy is the label for a family of capacities rather than a single thing. The Stanford Encyclopedia entry opens with the range: knowing what other people are thinking and feeling, emotionally engaging with them, sharing their thoughts and feelings, and caring for their well-being. The word itself is young for so old an idea: the psychologist Edward Titchener introduced "empathy" in 1909 as the English translation of the German Einfühlung ("feeling into"); before that "sympathy" was the common term, and the capacities had been studied since the eighteenth century, particularly under the influence of David Hume and Adam Smith.

> "The concept of empathy is used to refer to a wide range of psychological capacities that are thought of as being central for constituting humans as social creatures, allowing us to know what other people are thinking and feeling, to emotionally engage with them, to share their thoughts and feelings, and to care for their well–being." (Karsten R. Stueber, [Empathy, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/empathy/))

### What empathy is not

The entry separates empathy from two nearby states. Emotional contagion is "a resonance phenomenon of a relatively basic form": a mood spreads to another person, who experiences it as their own without registering whose it is. Genuine empathy presupposes the ability to differentiate between oneself and the other, and awareness that one is resonating with the thoughts and feelings of the other person. Sympathy, or empathic concern, is a different state again: it is care for the other's well-being from one's own perspective, and it does not require feeling what the other feels.

### Its moral standing is contested

Martin Hoffman's developmental account treats empathic responses as a continuum that starts with the reactive newborn cry and matures into perspective-taking, with sympathy and prosocial behavior appearing at the fourth stage. C. Daniel Batson's experiments argue that empathic concern produces genuinely altruistic motivation, where the other's welfare is the goal of helping. Critics reply that empathy is biased: Hoffman's "here and now" biases mean we tend to empathize more with persons perceived to be closer to us, subjects in an in-group study reported more empathy for in-group than out-group targets, and Paul Bloom's spotlight criticism holds that empathy illuminates the one identifiable person rather than the many. On the entry's assessment, empathy is on its own not sufficient to keep us reliably moral.
