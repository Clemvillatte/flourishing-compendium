Community psychology theory studies well-being at the level of settings and systems. Its founding move is to place the person in context: community psychologists "seek to understand people within the social contexts of their lives in order to promote a better quality of life for all people" ([Kloos et al.](https://www.apa.org/pubs/books/community-psychology-fourth-edition-sample-chapter.pdf)). The field's two signature constructs, sense of community and empowerment, both live at that collective level.

> "Community psychology concerns the relationships of individuals with communities and societies. By integrating research with action, it seeks to understand and enhance quality of life for individuals, communities, and societies." (Kloos, Hill, Thomas, Case, Scott and Wandersman, [Community Psychology: Linking Individuals and Communities, 4th ed., APA, sample chapter](https://www.apa.org/pubs/books/community-psychology-fourth-edition-sample-chapter.pdf))

### The two key constructs

Sense of community is "a feeling of belongingness, interdependence, and mutual commitment that links individuals as a collective", a perception the textbook traces to McMillan and Chavis and to Sarason ([sample chapter](https://www.apa.org/pubs/books/community-psychology-fourth-edition-sample-chapter.pdf)). Empowerment is "the process of enhancing the possibilities for people to control their own lives (Rappaport, 1987)" ([sample chapter](https://www.apa.org/pubs/books/community-psychology-fourth-edition-sample-chapter.pdf)). Both appear among the field's eight core values, where sense of community and "empowerment and citizen participation" sit alongside social justice, respect for human diversity, collective wellness, collaboration, empirical grounding, and a multilevel strengths-based perspective ([Exhibit 1.1](https://www.apa.org/pubs/books/community-psychology-fourth-edition-sample-chapter.pdf)).
