Evolutionary personality theory asks why human trait variation persists at all: if natural selection keeps the best version of everything, why is everyone not the same? The answer it gives is that traits are trade-offs, and the payoffs of a trade-off shift with circumstances. Daniel Nettle's version is summarized in the evolutionary literature:

> "Nettle (2006) proposed concrete cost-benefit trade-offs associated with five of the major dimensions of personality variation in humans. For example, he proposed that high extraversion conferred the benefits of greater mating and social success, which were balanced by increased risk of accident and injury due to greater novelty seeking behaviour." (Verweij et al. 2012, [Evolution](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3518920/))

On this view a trait level is a strategy with costs attached, not a defect or an ideal. Variation is then maintained rather than depleted when selection favors rarer trait values or shifts across time, place and sex: "under balancing selection, genetic variation is maintained rather than depleted by selection; for example by selection pressures that fluctuate over time and space... or that favour rarer trait values (negative frequency-dependent selection)". What is common in a population has its advantage discounted; what is rare gains one.
