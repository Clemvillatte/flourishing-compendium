Anger management is the clinical field that treats problematic anger as its own target, rather than as a symptom of something else. Its reference text is DiGiuseppe and Tafrate's Understanding Anger Disorders (2007), which opens by noting that anger has been "written off as merely an emotional symptom and excluded from most accepted systems of psychiatric diagnosis" ([Oxford University Press, book page](https://books.google.com/books?vid=9780195170795)).

### What is being managed

The field's working definition of the emotion comes from the book itself: "Anger is a subjectively experienced emotional state with high sympathetic autonomic arousal. It is initially elicited by a perception of a threat (to one's physical well-being, property, present or future resources, self-image, social status or projected image to one's group, maintenance of social rules that regulate daily life, or comfort), although it may persist even after the threat has passed" ([DiGiuseppe & Tafrate 2007, book preview PDF](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9780198038160_A23605861/preview-9780198038160_A23605861.pdf)).

### The treatment gap

The field exists partly because help is hard to reach: an Australian evidence summary reports that "the majority of people experiencing problematic anger do not seek support from health services," that even when they do they are "unlikely to receive an effective intervention," and that clinicians "may lack confidence in their ability to assess and provide interventions for this client group" ([Headspace evidence summary](https://headspace.org.au/assets/download-cards/OR17177-EvidenceSummary-Anger-WEB.pdf)).
