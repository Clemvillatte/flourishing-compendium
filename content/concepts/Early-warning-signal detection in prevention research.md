Early-warning-signal detection is the research program that treats relapse as a process with a beginning. In psychosis care, its founding observation is that small changes in how a person thinks, feels and acts tend to arrive before a full relapse does. Max Birchwood and colleagues built the program's monitoring systems around that observation.

> "Investigations (e.g. Herz & Melville, 1980; Birchwood et al, 1989; Jørgensen, 1998) have consistently determined that subtle changes in thought, affect and behaviour precede development of frank psychosis." (Birchwood, Spencer & McGovern, ["Schizophrenia: early warning signs", APT 2000](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/advances-in-psychiatric-treatment/article/schizophrenia-early-warning-signs/17FEA980ACA38BD6B3C41CFB92C61C80))

### Signs, not prodrome

The signals are mostly quiet ones. "'Dysphoric' symptoms (depressed mood, withdrawal, sleep and appetite problems) are most commonly reported, while psychotic-like symptoms (for example, a sense of being laughed at or talked about) are less frequent" ([same article](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/advances-in-psychiatric-treatment/article/schizophrenia-early-warning-signs/17FEA980ACA38BD6B3C41CFB92C61C80)). The word choice carries the theory: the authors argue these are "more accurately conceptualised as 'early warning signs' of psychotic relapse, since the concept of a 'prodrome' (a term derived from the medical literature) implies a disease progression that cannot be interrupted," whereas people with psychosis "actively use coping strategies to intervene in the onset of psychosis" (same text). A sign is something a person can notice and act on; a prodrome is a fate.

The approach's aim, in their summary: "The 'early warning signs' approach to relapse prevention seeks to identify the earliest signs of impending psychotic relapse and to offer timely and effective intervention to arrest their progression towards frank psychosis" (same text). The signal is not the relapse; it is the window.
