Hypo-egoic phenomena are psychological states in which self-focused processing is reduced: less self-preoccupation, less introspection about one's thoughts and motives, and less concern with how one is perceived and evaluated by others. The umbrella term was brought together by Kirk Warren Brown and Mark R. Leary in the Oxford Handbook of Hypo-egoic Phenomena, which frames it as an integrative construct for seemingly disparate states including flow, equanimity, mindfulness, compassion, and mystical states.

> "For centuries, many philosophers, scientists, spiritual leaders, and social activists have advocated a 'hypo-egoic' way of being, a psychological mindset characterized by less self-preoccupation in favor of a more inclusive, 'we first' mode of functioning." — [Brown & Leary (eds.), The Oxford Handbook of Hypo-egoic Phenomena, volume abstract, Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/28342)

On the compendium's reading, the construct matters here because it names, in one frame, what the contemplative traditions describe as the fruits of practice: presence without self-commentary, concern without self-concern, and action that is not first of all about the actor.

### Two sources of evidence

The idea has two roots in Mark Leary's work. The first is a self-regulation argument. In a 2006 paper, Leary, Adams and Tate observe that "intentionally trying to control one's behavior sometimes reduces the likelihood of achieving one's goals", and examine hypo-egoic self-regulation, "in which people relinquish deliberate, conscious control over their own behavior so that they will respond more naturally, spontaneously, or automatically". They suggest two pathways: reducing "the proportion of time that they are self-aware", for example by "practicing meditation", or "increasing the concreteness of their self-thoughts" through "practicing mindfulness" ([Leary, Adams & Tate, 2006, Journal of Personality, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1467-6494.2006.00429.x)). That paper is theoretical, and a later article on mindfulness and self-regulation noted that the construct was then "in the most preliminary stages of validation" ([Journal of Transpersonal Psychology, PDF](https://www.atpweb.org/jtparchive/trps-40-08-01-088.pdf)).

The second root is a description of the person. In the Handbook chapter on dispositional hypo-egoicism, Leary, Brown and Diebels report that hypo-egoic people "tend to be more focused on stimuli in the present moment, which they process in an experiential fashion with minimal internal commentary", and "show less concern with how they are evaluated by others, and display greater emotional equanimity" ([Oxford Academic, chapter abstract](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/28342/chapter/215152834)). Interpersonally, the authors say it "appears to be associated with an agreeable, attentive, and caring style of relating to other people". The chapter's language is cautious ("appears", "probably"), and so should ours.

### What it is not

As defined here, hypo-egoic refers to reduced self-focus and self-preoccupation. It is a statement about where attention goes, and says nothing about how a person rates themselves.
