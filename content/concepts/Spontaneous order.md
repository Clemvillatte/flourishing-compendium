Spontaneous order is F. A. Hayek's name for the kind of order that forms itself. Markets, languages, common law and traffic patterns arrange themselves without a designer, and Hayek's Law, Legislation and Liberty is built on the difference between such orders and the made kind. The book announces the project at its start: "The central concept around which the discussion of this book will turn is that of order, and particularly the distinction between two kinds of order which we will provisionally call 'made' and 'grown.'"

> "It would be no exaggeration to say that social theory begins with — and has an object only because of — the discovery that there exist orderly structures which are the product of the action of many men but are not the result of human design." (Hayek, Law, Legislation and Liberty, vol. 1, ch. 2, [via Wikiquote](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Law,_Legislation_and_Liberty))

### What the difference is

The two orders differ in origin: the made order is put together for a purpose, while "A spontaneous order of this kind has in many respects properties different from those of a made order" (p. 36). The practical consequence Hayek draws is epistemic humility for planners: the order's detail is produced by adjustments no planner directs, so interventions can shape its general character while the details exceed any designer's grasp.
