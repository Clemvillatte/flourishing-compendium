“Dispositional optimism” — Scheier and Carver, 1985.

Dispositional optimism is Michael Scheier and Charles Carver's name for a stable, broad expectation about how one's life will go. Their 1985 article introduces the construct and the Life Orientation Test (LOT) that measures it:

> This article describes a scale measuring dispositional optimism, defined in terms of generalized outcome expectancies. (Scheier and Carver, "Optimism, Coping, and Health: Assessment and Implications of Generalized Outcome Expectancies," Health Psychology 4(3), 1985, p. 219, [full text](https://www.deempathischeorganisatie.nl/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/Beroemd-artikel-van-Scheier-en-Carver-uit-1985-over-de-effecten-van-optimisme.pdf))

The word to watch is "generalized." This is not confidence about any particular goal, skill or situation. It is the overall tilt of a person's expectancies — good things will happen rather than bad — held across situations, which is why it counts as a disposition rather than a mood or a judgment.

### Why expectancies do work

The construct sits inside the authors' expectancy-value model of self-regulation. People compare where they are with where they want to be; the result is an outcome expectancy, and it drives effort:

> If expectancies are favorable, the result is renewed effort. If expectancies are sufficiently unfavorable, the result is reduced effort, or even complete disengagement from further attempts. (ibid., p. 222)

In the 1985 study, undergraduates who scored optimistic in week one of a four-week window were less likely to report being bothered by physical symptoms at semester's end, even after correcting for their initial symptom levels (ibid., pp. 219-220). The practical stake here: optimism is a difference that shows up in persistence, coping and health behaviour, and it is distinct from the optimism bias, which is a specific cognitive distortion (PP016).
