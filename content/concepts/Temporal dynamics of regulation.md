## What the chapter argues

Emotions unfold in a series of temporal stages. Regulation can step in at any of them, and where it steps in shapes what it can achieve. Gal Sheppes and James Gross made timing the center of their account of what works when.

The clearest test pits reappraisal against suppression.

> The basic logic of this comparison is that reappraisal which alters the emotional trajectory early on, should be a more effective regulation strategy than suppression, which intervenes later, and thus has to combat a higher intensity emotional response.
> — Sheppes & Gross, "Emotion Regulation Effectiveness: What Works When," Handbook of Psychology, 2nd ed., 2012

The evidence ran in that direction across affective, cognitive and social domains. Suppression, relative to reappraisal, did not reduce negative emotional experience, and it increased sympathetic nervous system arousal. Fighting a fully developed emotion is expensive, and the costs show up in memory, social connection and physiology.

The chapter then adds a twist the simple early-is-better story misses. Intensity changes the arithmetic. Reappraisal lets emotional information be attended and given meaning before it is modulated, so it is effective in the context of a low intensity emotional response, but it becomes costly and effortful in the context of a high intensity emotional response. At high intensity the early blocking strategy, distraction, succeeds where reappraisal struggles, though distraction buys short term relief with minimal effort and can prove maladaptive in the long run.

## In the compendium

The principle on regulating early, not late carries this concept directly. Intervening early in an emotional episode beats intervening at the peak, and once an emotion is highly activated, cognitive strategies weaken while behavioral ones carry the load. Timing matters more than intensity.

The principle on the temporal structure of affect is the wider frame. Emotions have intensity, duration, variability and recovery, and regulation is one of the levers that moves those properties.
