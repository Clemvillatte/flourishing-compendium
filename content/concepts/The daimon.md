The daimon (the daimonion, the divine sign) is the inner voice Socrates testifies to at his trial: a sign that had been with him since childhood, which only ever stops him from an action and never commands one. Plato gives the description in the Apology.

> "This sign I have had ever since I was a child. The sign is a voice which comes to me and always forbids me to do something which I am going to do, but never commands me to do anything, and this is what stands in the way of my being a politician." (Plato, Apology 31c-d, Jowett translation, [MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html))

### A voice that vetoes

The passage is careful about what the sign does. It never advises or instructs; it intervenes when an action would go wrong, and its silence is the only approval it gives. Socrates calls it a divinity, the one Meletus ridicules in the indictment, yet its working is ordinary: an inner check with a consistent direction, present from childhood, that a person can learn to trust. The principle that cites this concept pairs it with Stoic and Christian images of an inner supportive voice; Socrates' version is the stricter one, since the voice only ever says no.
