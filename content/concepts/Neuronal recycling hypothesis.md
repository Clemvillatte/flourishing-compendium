Dehaene and Cohen's 2007 paper in Neuron begins with a puzzle: "Part of human cortex is specialized for cultural domains such as reading and arithmetic, whose invention is too recent to have influenced the evolution of our species." To explain "this paradoxical cerebral invariance of cultural maps, we propose a neuronal recycling hypothesis, according to which cultural inventions invade evolutionarily older brain circuits and inherit many of their structural constraints" ([Dehaene and Cohen, Cultural Recycling of Cortical Maps, Neuron 2007, PDF](https://www.unicog.org/publications/DehaeneCohen_CulturalRecyclingCorticalMaps_Neuron2007.pdf)).

### The neuronal niche

The paper states that cultural acquisitions "must find their 'neuronal niche,' a set of circuits that are sufficiently close to the required function and sufficiently plastic as to reorient a significant fraction of their neural resources to this novel use". The term "neuronal recycling" was introduced in Dehaene (2005). Reading is the worked example: the visual word form area in the left occipito-temporal sulcus is described as one comprising "reproducible and specific neural mechanisms for recognizing written characters", though the label "should not be taken to imply that this cortical sector is entirely dedicated to reading" (same paper).

### Evidence

A Frontiers review reports that the area typically tied to word processing in literate adults responded to faces in illiterate adults (Dehaene et al., 2010), and that longitudinal fMRI in school-aged children observed word selectivity developing in areas previously selective for tools and limbs ([Frontiers in Neuroscience, unified model of cross-modal plasticity](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnins.2024.1334283/full)). A study of literacy found a cost: unlearning mirror invariance to read the Latin alphabet "has a cost for object identification", a side effect "expected under the neuronal recycling hypothesis" ([A cultural side effect, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01224/pdf)). A cross-cultural PNAS study reports its results as consistent with the idea that cultural effects "merely modulate a fixed set of invariant macroscopic brain circuits" ([PNAS, Universal brain systems for recognizing word shapes and handwriting gestures](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1217749109)).

### Related ideas and limits

The Neuroemergentism review distinguishes it from Anderson's neural reuse: recycling is about cultural acquisitions such as reading and arithmetic, whereas neural reuse extends to all novel skills ([Neuroemergentism, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6326375/)). The seed phrase "reading and culture" fits, but the hypothesis is narrower than a general claim that culture reshapes the brain: it stresses that old circuits constrain what culture can do. I did not open a published critique.
