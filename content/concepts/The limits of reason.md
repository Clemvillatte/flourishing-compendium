The limits of reason names the places where reasoning cannot take the last step: it cannot ground its own authority, it cannot choose its own ends, and it cannot inspect the body's contributions to the mind. The seed names Hume's Treatise of Human Nature, whose argument about passion and reason is the classic statement of one of these limits.

### Hume's ground

Hume's target is the ordinary talk of reason fighting the passions:

> "Nothing is more usual in philosophy, and even in common life, than to talk of the combat of passion and reason, to give the preference to reason, and assert that men are only so far virtuous as they conform themselves to its dictates." (Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature, [Project Gutenberg #4705](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/4705/pg4705.txt))

His conclusion inverts the picture:

> "Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them." (same work, "Of the influencing motives of the will")

Reason can work out how to get what is wanted; the wanting itself is not reasoned to. On Hume's account this is not reason's failure but its office: an instrument, and only an instrument, of ends it does not set.
