Assertiveness training is the structured teaching of direct, respectful self-expression. Since the 1970s, "assertiveness has essentially been defined as the capacity to express one's needs, rights, and opinions directly and respectfully, without infringing on the rights of others (Alberti and Emmons, 1970)" ([PMC, "The four pathways of assertiveness"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12379063/)). The training operationalizes that capacity: "appropriate social behaviors for self-presentation, expressing feelings, attitudes, wishes, ideas, and interests are taught to people so that they can easily express themselves without any fear of the public" ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9480929/)).

The evidence base is consistent. A randomized trial in college students found assertiveness training "resulted in significantly greater reductions in stress ... anxiety ... depression ... and total scores of DASS," alongside increased assertiveness scores ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11392326/)). Reviews converge: "social skills training programs incorporating assertiveness components are associated with reductions in anxiety symptoms, particularly social anxiety, as well as improvements in self-esteem and decreases in depressive symptoms (Speed et al., 2018)" ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12379063/)). In healthcare settings, a systematic review found "most demonstrated that assertiveness training improves 'speaking up' behaviors and confidence" ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12648174/)).

For the flourishing project, assertiveness training is a canonical case of skill acquisition: an interpersonal competence that can be learned, with measurable effects on anxiety, self-esteem and relationship quality.
