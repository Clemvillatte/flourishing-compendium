Effort-reward imbalance is Johannes Siegrist's model of occupational stress. In the author's own summary: "ERI (effort-reward imbalance) represents a theoretical model, rooted in sociological and social psychological research on social exchange, with explicit links to psychobiological stress theory" ([Siegrist, The ERI Model: Stress and Health](https://www.uniklinik-duesseldorf.de/fileadmin/Fuer-Patienten-und-Besucher/Kliniken-Zentren-Institute/Institute/Institut_fuer_Medizinische_Soziologie/Forschung/Siegrist/ERI.pdf)). The model aims "at explaining adverse health effects of costly transactions in core social roles... defined by a lack of reciprocity between efforts spent and rewards received in turn ('high cost/low gain' conditions)" (ibid.).

### Effort, reward, over-commitment

Effort means the job demands and obligations the employee faces; rewards come in three basic types: "salary or wage (financial reward), career promotion or job security (status-related reward), and esteem or recognition (socio-emotional reward)" (ibid.). The health claim follows:

> "Continued experience of 'high cost/low gain' at work frustrates basic reward expectations, compromising workers' self-esteem and causing sustained psychobiological stress reactions with adverse long-term effects on mental and physical health (Siegrist 1996)." — Siegrist, The ERI Model ([PDF](https://www.uniklinik-duesseldorf.de/fileadmin/Fuer-Patienten-und-Besucher/Kliniken-Zentren-Institute/Institute/Institut_fuer_Medizinische_Soziologie/Forschung/Siegrist/ERI.pdf))

The evidence base the author reports includes prospective studies of "effort-reward imbalance at work and risk of depressive disorders" and meta-analytic work on the model and "indices of cardiovascular health" (ibid.); associations of failed reciprocity "with reduced mental health, measured by depressive symptoms and poor self-rated health, were observed" (ibid.).
