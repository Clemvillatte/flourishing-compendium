Exemplarism is Linda Zagzebski's moral theory, which starts from admirable people instead of from rules or abstract definitions of the good. Her book "develops an original moral theory based on direct reference to exemplars of goodness, identified through the emotion of admiration" ([Oxford Academic, abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/6293)).

### What the theory does

The theory sets out to map "the main moral terms by reference to exemplars", and the abstract lists them: "good person," "good motive," "good end," "virtue," "good life," "right act," and "duty" ([abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/6293)). Because "a motivating emotion is at the root of the theory", Zagzebski says it is meant "to serve the practical purpose of making us want to act morally and showing us how to do so."

She illustrates the method with "three classes of exemplars: the hero, the saint, and the sage", and gives narratives "a key function in the theory" ([abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/6293)).

### Admiration and emulation

Two chapters carry the practical weight. In the chapter on admiration, Zagzebski defends "the basic trustworthiness of admiration" and proposes "a method for critiquing admiration by conscientious reflection on our own emotion over time and reflection on the emotions of others." She also describes how admiration "turns negative and can lead to envy and resentment", and proposes that "the suspicion of admirable people is a greater threat to moral development than moral disagreement" ([chapter 2 abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/6293/chapter/149968520)).

The chapter on emulation "turns to the practical side of exemplarism in the way exemplars can be used to cultivate virtue in children and in ourselves", and discusses "the way admiration leads to emulation" ([chapter 5 abstract](https://academic.oup.com/book/6293/chapter-abstract/149971625)). The book abstract adds that the theory "tracks a natural process of moral development in the emulation of exemplars" and so "connects with moral education."

### Scope

These are publisher abstracts of the book and two of its chapters, not the full text. The page therefore reports the shape of the theory and does not quote Zagzebski's arguments in detail. The phrase "moral knowledge through admired persons" is a loose summary. The abstracts speak of defining moral terms by reference to exemplars, and of moral development through emulation.
