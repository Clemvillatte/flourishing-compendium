Minority stress theory, set out for lesbian, gay and bisexual people by Ilan Meyer, explains higher rates of mental disorder through social conditions. His 2003 abstract says the framework explains the excess "in terms of minority stress--explaining that stigma, prejudice, and discrimination create a hostile and stressful social environment that causes mental health problems". The model "describes stress processes, including the experience of prejudice events, expectations of rejection, hiding and concealing, internalized homophobia, and ameliorative coping processes" ([Meyer, 2003, Psychological Bulletin 129(5), 674-697, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12956539/)). Meyer defined minority stress as "excess stress to which individuals from stigmatized social categories are exposed as a result of their social, often a minority, position" (Meyer, 2003, p. 675, quoted in [a Springer chapter on youth resilience](https://connect.springerpub.com/highwire_display/entity_view/node/134589/full)).

### Distal and proximal stressors

A later APA paper describes the processes "along a distal to proximal continuum, with distal stressors referring to events and experiences outside the person, and proximal stressors referring to stressors that are transmuted through socialization and experienced by the person through internalizing cognitive processes". Proximal stressors include "internalized negative social attitudes", "expectations of rejection and discrimination, or felt stigma, and concealment of sexual and gender identity" ([Resilience in the Study of Minority Stress, APA PDF](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/features/sgd-sgd0000132.pdf)). The same source says that, like general stress theory, the model shows that "coping and social support can buffer the effect of the stressors".

A public-health summary lists the assumptions underneath: stressors are "unique (not experienced by nonstigmatized populations), chronic (related to social and cultural structures) and socially based" ([Colorado Department of Public Health and Environment PDF](https://cdphe.colorado.gov/sites/cdphe/files/2021-08/the_minority_stress_perspective.pdf)).

### Evidence

The APA says a meta-analysis showed higher rates of stress-related psychiatric disorders in lesbian, gay and bisexual populations, and that "these differences are not large but are relatively consistent across studies" ([APA research summary](https://www.apa.org/about/policy/marriage)). A one-year follow-up study of physical health found that people who experienced an externally rated prejudice event had higher odds of a physical health problem, and the link "persisted after adjusting for experiences of general stressful life events". Self-appraised minority stress exposures "were not associated with poorer physical health", so effects "depended on the measure, type of stressor, and physical health outcome" ([NIH Public Access manuscript, Reginfo.gov](https://www.reginfo.gov/public/do/eoDownloadDocument?pubId=&eodoc=true&documentID=5604)).

### Critique

A PMC commentary calls the theory "the prevailing and dominant theoretical perspective" and notes that effect sizes are "generally modest rather than large". The coping and buffering part of the theory is "under-examined", and the author concludes that the hypotheses "have not received the degree of empirical support that one theoretically expects" ([Health Disparities by Sexual Orientation, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10175091/)). The APA summary notes that researchers use "minority stress" for stigmatized groups beyond sexual minorities, such as ethnic minorities, women, the elderly and the poor, though the evidence reviewed above concerns lesbian, gay and bisexual people. A Wiley abstract restates the claim for LGBQ individuals ([Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jftr.12246)).
