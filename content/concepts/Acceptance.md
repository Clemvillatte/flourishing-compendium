Acceptance, in the sense used by Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT), is the willing embrace of unwanted inner experience without trying to change its frequency or form. The working definition comes from Steven Hayes and colleagues' 2006 review of the ACT model, where acceptance is taught as the alternative to experiential avoidance. It is not resignation: the point is not to feel better about everything, but to free behavior from the struggle with private events so it can serve chosen values.

### The ACT definition

> "Acceptance is taught as an alternative to experiential avoidance. Acceptance involves the active and aware embrace of those private events occasioned by one's history without unnecessary attempts to change their frequency or form, especially when doing so would cause psychological harm." (Hayes, Luoma, Bond, Masuda & Lillis 2006, pp. 7-8, [full-text PDF](https://anxietyinstitute.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/12/Hayes-et-al.-2006.pdf))

The same paper is explicit that acceptance is instrumental: "Acceptance (and defusion) in ACT is not an end in itself. Rather acceptance is fostered as a method of increasing values-based action" (p. 8). Acceptance is one of the processes ACT trains toward psychological flexibility, "the ability to contact the present moment more fully as a conscious human being, and to change or persist in behavior when doing so serves valued ends" (p. 7).
