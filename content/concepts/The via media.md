Via media is Latin for a middle way, and the English noun is defined as "A third way; a middle course, a compromise" ([Wiktionary, "via media"](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/via_media)). The phrase names a course taken between two poles:

> "The quip highlighted the extent to which the philosophes were seeking a via media between what they saw as the morally and intellectually impoverished tradition of Christian revelation and its embattled polar opposite, a sceptical materialism [...]." (Colin Jones, The Great Nation, 2002, quoted in [Wiktionary, "via media"](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/via_media))

The account draws on Bernstein's Beyond Objectivism and Relativism, where the two poles are objectivism and relativism. The relativist side of the dilemma, as Bernstein states it: "There is no substantive overarching framework in which radically different and alternative schemes are commensurable" (Bernstein, Beyond Objectivism and Relativism, pp. 11-12, quoted in the [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://iep.utm.edu/cognitive-relativism-truth/)).

Bernstein's way across is not a compromise that splits the difference between the poles. He treats the Either/Or itself as the error, and looks for the shape of rationality that the shared assumption of both poles hides. A review of the book in Zygon states the route plainly:

> "What it takes to get us beyond the impasse of objectivism vs. relativism is the actual formation—and, perhaps, the simultaneous recognition—of human community. This is not 'just a theoretical problem but a practical task' (p. 230)." (Ted Peters, review of Bernstein, Beyond Objectivism and Relativism, [Zygon vol. 22, no. 4 (1987)](https://www.zygonjournal.org/article/12248/galley/24873/download/), quoting the book, p. 230)

The same review records Bernstein's own conclusion, that to realise the true nature of human rationality we must "dedicate ourselves to the practical task of furthering the type of solidarity, participation, and mutual recognition that is founded in dialogical communities" (Bernstein, Beyond Objectivism and Relativism, p. 231, quoted in the same review). The middle way, on this account, is worked out in dialogue rather than found in a new foundation: the review notes Bernstein's judgment that the hermeneutical philosophy of Hans-Georg Gadamer "holds the key to unlocking the chains of the Cartesian problematic" (p. 165). My principles use the concept in this spirit — the middle path is a practice of staying in conversation with both poles rather than an averaging of them.
