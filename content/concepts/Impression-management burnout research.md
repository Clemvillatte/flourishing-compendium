The research line behind this concept is emotional-labor research, which distinguishes surface acting (faking the required display) from deep acting (actually changing the feeling). The meta-analytic verdict, across 95 independent studies:

> "Results revealed substantial relationships of emotion–rule dissonance and surface acting with indicators of impaired well-being (ρs between .39 and .48) and job attitudes (ρs between −.24 and −.40) and a small negative relationship with performance outcomes (ρs between −.20 and −.05)." (Hülsheger and Schewe 2011, Journal of Occupational Health Psychology 16(3): 361-389, [open-access PDF](https://cris.maastrichtuniversity.nl/files/74901261/Hulsheger_2011_On_the_costs_and_benefits_of_emotional.pdf))

The same review found the contrast case weak rather than harmful: "Overall, deep acting displayed weak relationships with indicators of impaired well-being and job attitudes but positive relationships with emotional performance and customer satisfaction (ρs .18 and .37)." The paper's stated mechanism is effort: surface acting involves constant monitoring of actual and desired emotions, an effortful process expected to drain mental resources and thereby enhance strain, in the paper's own argument.

The meta-analysis was based on 494 individual correlations drawn from a final sample of 95 independent studies, and its regression and mediation analyses confirmed that surface acting uniquely predicts impaired well-being. The compendium's reading of this for self-presentation is direct: the pretend version of the display is the expensive one, and the price is paid in exhaustion.

**Plain words** Faking the feeling costs more energy than changing the feeling, and the bill arrives as burnout. Performing a self you do not actually feel is continuous work, hour after hour, and it is the kind of work that does not end when you finally go home.
