In Aristotle's account, responsibility begins with the voluntary: what has its moving principle in the agent. The Nicomachean Ethics opens Book III with the stakes:

> "Since virtue is concerned with passions and actions, and on voluntary passions and actions praise and blame are bestowed, on those that are involuntary pardon, and sometimes also pity, to distinguish the voluntary and the involuntary is presumably necessary for those who are studying the nature of virtue, and useful also for legislators with a view to the assigning both of honours and of punishments." (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics III.1, trans. W. D. Ross, [Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.3.iii.html))

### Where the buck stops

The inquiry stops where the person enters: "For every one ceases to inquire how he is to act when he has brought the moving principle back to himself and to the ruling part of himself; for this is what chooses." Choice is defined at the hinge between deliberation and desire: "The object of choice being one of the things in our own power which is desired after deliberation, choice will be deliberate desire of things in our own power; for when we have decided as a result of deliberation, we desire in accordance with our deliberation."

The framework turns on two contrasts. The voluntary and the involuntary mark where praise and blame are in order, and the passage's examples are institutional — honours and punishments, the legislator's tools. And within the voluntary, choice is narrower than mere wishing: choice is deliberate desire for things in one's own power, so what a person holds responsible is what they could deliberate about and then do. Choice is one route into responsibility on this account, not the whole of it; the text's own examples of the involuntary keep the notion wider than deliberation alone.
