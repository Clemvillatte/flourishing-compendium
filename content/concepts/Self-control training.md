"Self-control training" names the practice of doing small, regular self-control tasks — using the non-dominant hand, avoiding sweets, sitting up straight — in the hope of building a general capacity. The strongest test of the idea is a caution. Miles, Sheeran, Baird, Macdonald, Webb and Harris (2016) ran the six-week program with active controls: "In the present research, participants (N = 174) completed a 6-week training program of either cognitive or behavioral self-control tasks. We then tested the effects of practice on a range of measures of self-control, including lab-based and real-world tasks. Training was compared with both active and no-contact control conditions. Despite high levels of adherence to the training tasks, there was no effect of training on any measure of self-control." ([JEP: General abstract](https://www.ovid.com/journals/jepge/abstract/10.1037/xge0000185~does-self-control-improve-with-practice-evidence-from-a))

The authors' conclusion is stated without hedging: "The implication is that training self-control through repeated practice does not result in generalized improvements in self-control." A note on this page's seed: the original entry line claimed that small practice tasks produce modest trait improvement and cited Miles et al. (2016); the study in fact reports the null result quoted above, and the page follows the study.

### What the null does and does not settle

The null concerns the strength-model promise: that exercising control in one domain builds a general muscle usable in others. The study's trained participants "did not, for example, show reduced ego depletion effects, become better at overcoming their habits, or report exerting more self-control in everyday life." What the design does not rule out is improvement at the practiced tasks themselves, or gains that arrive by other routes — habit formation, situation selection, or practicing a specific behavior one actually wants.
