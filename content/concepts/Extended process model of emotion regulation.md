James Gross's extended process model describes emotion regulation as something you decide to do, in three steps: noticing that an emotion is worth changing, choosing a way to change it, and carrying that way out in the situation you are in. It keeps the earlier five families of strategies from his 1998 process model and adds the decisions that come before and after them ([Gross, 2015](http://www.johnnietfeld.com/uploads/2/2/6/0/22606800/gross_2015.pdf)).

### The core idea

Gross starts from the idea that emotions involve valuation, a "good for me" versus "bad for me" judgment. Regulation, in his words:

> Emotion regulation occurs when one valuation system (which I refer to as a second-level valuation system) takes another valuation system (one that is generating emotion, which I refer to as a first-level valuation system) as a target and evaluates it either negatively or positively, activating action impulses that are intended to modify the activity in the first-level valuation system.

(Gross, 2015, [Psychological Inquiry 26(1)](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/1047840X.2014.940781), section "An Overview of the Extended Process Model".) In plain terms, one part of you looks at the emotion another part is producing, judges it, and acts on it.

### The three stages

Gross's Figure 10 lays out the stages: identification, which entails deciding whether to regulate; selection, which entails deciding which strategy to select; and implementation, which entails implementing a tactic ([Gross, 2015](http://www.johnnietfeld.com/uploads/2/2/6/0/22606800/gross_2015.pdf)). Each stage has its own perceive, value and act substeps.

- Identification detects the emotion and asks whether it is bad or good enough to deserve a response. Gross notes that deficient emotional awareness at this point may lead to regulation failure.
- Selection represents the available strategies and weighs them against things like cognitive resources and how strong the impulse is. His example: people prefer reappraisal to distraction when emotion intensity is low, and distraction to reappraisal when it is high.
- Implementation turns a general strategy into a tactic that fits the moment. Gross's illustration is cognitive change becoming "think about this particular rebuff as an accidental oversight rather than a deliberate insult."

### The five families inside it

The selection stage is where the original five families sit. Gross says they act on the world (situation selection, situation modification), the perceptual input (attentional deployment), the valuation (cognitive change) or the action output (response modulation) ([Gross, 2015](http://www.johnnietfeld.com/uploads/2/2/6/0/22606800/gross_2015.pdf)). In his Handbook chapter he defines cognitive change as "modifying how one appraises a situation so as to alter its emotional significance", and response modulation as "directly influencing experiential, behavioral, or physiological components of the emotional response" ([Gross, Handbook of Emotion Regulation, 2nd ed.](https://dl.icdst.org/pdfs/files4/bd153eea518b5226e4fcbf13507c1b81.pdf), p. 10).

### Why the extension matters

The three stages give a place to locate what goes wrong. Gross separates regulation failure, "not regulating emotion when it would be useful to do so", from misregulation, "regulating in ways that are harmful rather than helpful". A person with few strategies in her repertoire, or with a habit of leaning on one, is stuck at selection. A person who picks a good strategy and bungles the tactic is stuck at implementation.
