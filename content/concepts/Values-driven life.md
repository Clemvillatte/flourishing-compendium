Values-driven life is Viktor Frankl's term for what a life runs on when it runs at all: not pleasure, not power, but the discovery and pursuit of what is personally meaningful. His formulation of the primary drive:

> "Man's search for meaning is the primary motivation in his life and not a 'secondary rationalization' of instinctual drives." (Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning, Logotherapy in a Nutshell, [full text](https://dn720006.ca.archive.org/0/items/english-collections-k-z/Man's%20Search%20for%20Meaning%20-%20Frankl,%20Viktor.pdf))

### The camp, and the how

The book's most famous borrowed line earned its fame in the concentration camp chapters, where Frankl reports what kept prisoners alive: "Nietzsche's words, 'He who has a why to live for can bear almost any how,' could be the guiding motto for all psychotherapeutic and psychohygienic efforts regarding prisoners. Whenever there was an opportunity for it, one had to give them a why, an aim, for their lives, in order to strengthen them to bear the terrible how of their existence" ([Frankl](https://dn720006.ca.archive.org/0/items/english-collections-k-z/Man's%20Search%20for%20Meaning%20-%20Frankl,%20Viktor.pdf)). The Nietzsche line is quoted with the source named, as it is throughout the book.

### Three ways meaning arrives

Logotherapy is deliberately concrete about where meaning comes from: "According to logotherapy, we can discover this meaning in life in three different ways: (1) by creating a work or doing a deed; (2) by experiencing something or encountering someone; and (3) by the attitude we take toward unavoidable suffering" ([Frankl](https://dn720006.ca.archive.org/0/items/english-collections-k-z/Man's%20Search%20for%20Meaning%20-%20Frankl,%20Viktor.pdf)). Work, love, and the stance taken toward what cannot be changed.
