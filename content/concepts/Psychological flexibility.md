Psychological flexibility is the capacity to adapt behavior to context: to contact the present moment as it is, accept or persist as values require, rather than being ruled by rigid rules or by the content of one's own thoughts. Two research traditions define it: the health-psychology review of Kashdan and Rottenberg (2010), and the ACT process definition of Hayes and colleagues.

> "Psychological flexibility spans a wide range of human abilities to: recognize and adapt to various situational demands; shift mindsets or behavioral repertoires when these strategies compromise personal or social functioning; maintain balance among important life domains; and be aware, open, and committed to behaviors that are congruent with deeply held values." (Kashdan and Rottenberg 2010, Clinical Psychology Review 30(7), p. 865, [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21151705/))

### The ACT definition

The therapy tradition's version: "Psychological flexibility can be defined as contacting the present moment as a conscious human being, fully and without defense, as it is and not as what it says it is, and persisting or changing in behavior in the service of chosen values" ([Hayes, ACT as a Unified Model of Behavior Change, APA](https://www.apa.org/education-career/ce/acceptance-commitment.pdf)). Its counter-construct is experiential avoidance, and the acceptance that answers it "is not passive in ACT—it is not a matter of tolerance or resignation... The goal is not to reduce arousal but to increase the flexibility of responding" ([APA PDF](https://www.apa.org/education-career/ce/acceptance-commitment.pdf)).

### What the review argues

Kashdan and Rottenberg present the construct "as a fundamental aspect of health," reviewing the literature to "offer evidence for the prominence of psychological flexibility in understanding psychological health" ([PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21151705/)). They also describe it dynamically, as processes that enable individuals to "adapt to fluctuating situational demands, reconfigure mental resources, shift perspectives, and balance competing desires, needs, and life domains" (p. 866). The seed page's stronger claims — outperforming rigid control-based coping on nearly every outcome, predicting mental health across hundreds of studies — are kept below as inherited formulations; this page asserts only what the opened sources support.

## Other formulations in this library

- the capacity to adapt behavior to context outperforms rigid control-based coping on nearly every outcome (Kashdan and Rottenberg, 2010)

- predicts mental health and performance across hundreds of studies (Kashdan and Rottenberg, 2010; Hayes et al., 2011 meta-analyses)

- inflexibility is trans-diagnostically linked to psychopathology (Kashdan and Rottenberg, 2010)
