"The chain of virtue" — Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.

The chain of virtue is Aristotle's account of how good character gets built: acts repeated become habits, and habits settled become character. Book II of the Nicomachean Ethics argues it from the crafts — the harpist and the builder — and states the principle in one line: virtue is produced or destroyed by the very same circumstances.

> "So too then is it with the Virtues: for by acting in the various relations in which we are thrown with our fellow men, we come to be, some just, some unjust: and by acting in dangerous positions and being habituated to feel fear or confidence, we come to be, some brave, others cowards." (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book II, D.P. Chase translation, [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt))

### What the chain is

The passage gives the compendium its sequence: act, habit, character. There is no step where virtue arrives directly; it comes by doing the acts first, in the relations one is actually thrown into. The same passage extends the chain to self-mastery and mildness, through the occasions of lust and anger, and to politics: law-givers make the individual members good men by habituation, and this is the intention of every law-giver, in which Aristotle locates the difference between a good constitution and a bad. The compendium's principle BE024 names the modern practice of chaining, linking behaviours into sequences, and Aristotle supplies the oldest version of the idea: each act is a link, and the links add up.
