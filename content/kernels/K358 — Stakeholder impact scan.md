> **Kernel K358** — Stakeholder impact scan

Before an institutional decision, write down every group the decision will touch, and one sentence per group describing the impact as that group would judge it. R. Edward Freeman's stakeholder definition offers a starting point for deciding who belongs on the list:

> "any group or individual who can affect or is affected by the achievement of the organization's objectives" (Freeman 1984, p. 46, quoted in [Bryson 2004](https://www.sog.unc.edu/sites/www.sog.unc.edu/files/course_materials/Bryson%20PMR%202004.pdf))

The routine below adapts stakeholder-analysis techniques reviewed by the public-management scholar John Bryson for public and nonprofit strategy work. It moves two dimensions at once: overt behaviour (a written list, not a vague sense of concern) and cognition (adopting each group's point of view before deciding).

### Protocol

1. Name the decision in one sentence, with the date it will be made.
2. Generate the list. Brainstorm every group that can affect the decision or is affected by it: employees, customers, suppliers, the surrounding community, the environment, future generations, the people the institution exists to serve. Do not filter yet; a list that looks embarrassingly long is doing its job.
3. For each group, write one sentence of the form "If we do this, this group experiences...", written from that group's side of the table. Bryson's basic technique makes the same move when it asks planners to list, for each stakeholder, the criteria that stakeholder would use to judge the organization's performance ([Bryson, Public Management Review 2004](https://www.sog.unc.edu/sites/www.sog.unc.edu/files/course_materials/Bryson%20PMR%202004.pdf)).
4. Select. Mark the two or three groups most affected, plus any group whose situation you cannot learn about any other way. These set the agenda for the decision conversation.
5. Retain and revise. For each marked group, note one thing that could be done quickly to address its interest and one longer-term issue. After the decision, check the predicted impacts with affected people where feasible, correct the assumptions that were wrong, and keep the questions and moves that proved useful for the next scan.

### Evidence

Bryson's review reports a blunt finding from Paul Nutt's studies of decisions in organizations: half of the decisions "failed", meaning they were not implemented, only partially implemented or otherwise produced poor results, "in large part because decision makers failed to attend to interests and information held by key stakeholders" ([Bryson 2004](https://www.sog.unc.edu/sites/www.sog.unc.edu/files/course_materials/Bryson%20PMR%202004.pdf)). Separately, Orlitzky, Schmidt and Rynes found a positive association between corporate social and financial performance across 52 studies comprising 33,878 observations, though the strength of the association varies with how social and financial performance are measured ([Orlitzky, Schmidt and Rynes, Organization Studies 2003](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0170840603024003910)). Their findings do not establish that this brief scan improves decisions.

### A fuller workshop version

Bryson's basic technique runs the scan with a group: one flip-chart sheet per stakeholder, that stakeholder's judgment criteria listed on it, and colored dots (green, yellow, red) marking how well the organization is doing from that stakeholder's point of view. The one-sentence scan above is the solo version of the same discipline.
