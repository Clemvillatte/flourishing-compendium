> **Kernel K395** — Exploring Difficulty meditation

Sit and settle with the breath for a few minutes. Then, if a difficulty is present (or you gently bring one to mind — something mild and unresolved), allow the thought or feeling to rest on the workbench of the mind. Do not try to fix, analyze, or solve it. Instead, drop your attention into the body and find the physical sensations that come with the difficulty. Then deliberately move your focus to the part of the body where the sensations are strongest. Breathe into that region on the in-breath, and breathe out from it on the out-breath. Explore the sensations with friendly curiosity.

### Protocol

Adapted from Williams and Penman, *Mindfulness*, 2011, ch. 9, printed pp. 164–167 (Week Five of the programme); the meditation track is Meditation 6 on the book's CD.

1. Sit quietly. Follow the breath for a few minutes; widen awareness to the body as a whole, then to sounds and thoughts. Settle before you begin.
2. Bring to mind something unresolved but mild, or allow whatever difficulty is already present to come forward. Rest the thought or feeling on the workbench of the mind. Do not fix it.
3. Drop attention into the body and notice the physical sensations that come with the difficulty: tightness, bracing, heat, contraction, anywhere in the body.
4. Deliberately move focus to the part of the body where the sensations are strongest. Breathe into that region on the in-breath; breathe out from it on the out-breath.
5. Explore the sensations with friendly curiosity. Notice their intensity changing from moment to moment. If they fade, return to the breath, or bring the same difficulty or a new one to mind.
6. If it gets overwhelming, widen awareness back to the whole body and the breath. If it is severe or trauma-linked, stop and seek support rather than pushing through.

### Evidence

The book's own summary of what Week Five introduces:

> "Week five introduces a meditation - Exploring difficulty - that helps you to face (rather than avoid) the difficulties that arise in your life from time to time. Many of life's problems can be left to resolve themselves, but some need to be faced with a spirit of openness, curiosity and compassion. If you don't embrace such difficulties, then they can increasingly blight your life."
> — [Williams & Penman, *Mindfulness*, programme summary p. 59 — quoted by [goodmedicine.org.uk, week-five blog post](https://goodmedicine.org.uk/blog/using-williams-penmans-book-mindfulness-a-practical-guide-as-a-self-help-resource-7th-post-fifth-weeks-practice/)]

The book's account of the acceptance the practice trains, carried by the same post:

> "Acceptance is a pause, a period of allowing, of letting be, of clear seeing."
> — [same source](https://goodmedicine.org.uk/blog/using-williams-penmans-book-mindfulness-a-practical-guide-as-a-self-help-resource-7th-post-fifth-weeks-practice/)

The mechanism, in the same passage's continuation:

> "[Acceptance] takes us off the hair trigger, so that we're less likely to make a knee-jerk reaction. It allows us to become fully aware of difficulties, with all of their painful nuances, and to respond to them in the most skilful way possible."
> — [same source](https://goodmedicine.org.uk/blog/using-williams-penmans-book-mindfulness-a-practical-guide-as-a-self-help-resource-7th-post-fifth-weeks-practice/)

The MBCT programme table in a peer-reviewed study lists the practice for Week 5 ("turning toward difficulties: ... 10-min exploring difficulty meditation") ([Zhang et al. 2019, Frontiers in Human Neuroscience](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/human-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnhum.2019.00208/full)); the practice is the same exercise, on the same week, in the programme's published form.

What the sources do not test: the meditation is a component of an eight-week programme whose overall effects on depression relapse and emotion regulation have been studied; the Exploring Difficulty exercise's standalone effect has not been isolated.
