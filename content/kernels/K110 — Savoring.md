> **Kernel K110** — Savoring

When a pleasant moment occurs, deliberately extend it. Notice the sensory details. Express it silently or aloud. Resist the urge to move on quickly. Savoring builds the capacity to receive good experience, which many people trained in vigilance find difficult.

### Protocol

1. When something pleasant arrives, a mouthful of food, a patch of sun, a good moment with a person, stop and give it your full attention instead of moving on.
2. Notice the sensory details slowly: what you see, hear, smell, taste, feel. Let the pleasantness register in the body as well as the head.
3. Name the good thing silently or say it aloud to yourself or someone else. Naming it keeps it from sliding past unnoticed.
4. Resist the reflex to dilute it with what comes next. Stay a few breaths longer than feels natural. If the mind drags you onward, return the way you would return to the breath in sitting practice.

### Evidence

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 20 randomised controlled trials with 4,805 participants found that savoring interventions produced a significant, moderate improvement in emotional outcomes overall (g = 0.51, 95% CI 0.26 to 0.77). The pooled effect was similar for reducing negative emotional symptoms (g = 0.61) and for increasing positive psychological states (g = 0.50), and the authors' publication-bias analyses suggested the effect was unlikely to be an artifact of selective reporting.

### A guided version

A well-specified guided version is the [Savoring Walk from Greater Good in Action](https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/savoring_walk), UC Berkeley's library of research-based well-being practices: a 20-minute daily walk devoted to consciously savoring positive sights, sounds, and sensations.
