> **Kernel K135** — Workability check

Ask: "Is holding this view working for me? Does it help me act in ways that align with my values?" The criterion is not whether the view is true but whether it is useful.

### Protocol

The check is a question applied to one held view; the experiment behind the underlying move is quoted below. Intended focus: Self and Motivation.

1. Name the view you are holding in one sentence, in your own words, as a belief about what is so.
2. Ask the workability question twice: does holding this view help me act in ways that align with my values, and what has it cost me so far? Answer with evidence from your own record, not with the view's plausibility.
3. Decide on the view's job, not its truth: keep it, hold it more lightly, or set it down for this situation. The check does not require abandoning views that work; it requires noticing when they do not.

### Evidence

From the study of the word-repeating defusion technique:

> "The cognitive defusion technique reduced both discomfort and believability more so than the comparison approaches." (Abstract, [Masuda, Hayes, Sackett & Twohig, 2004, Behaviour Research and Therapy 42(4), 477-485](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14998740/))

What this evidence does not test: the question itself. In eight single-case alternating treatment designs, rapidly repeating a single word reduced the discomfort and believability of self-relevant negative thoughts more than distraction or thought control, with control studies suggesting the effect was not just demand. The technique works on how thoughts function, not on whether they are true, which is the substrate of the workability check: the study shows believability can move while content stays fixed. But the abstract reports nothing about asking whether a view is working, about values alignment, or about decisions made after the check. What it supports is the kernel's premise: the grip of a thought is separable from its truth, and the grip is what the check interrogates.
