> **Kernel K113** — Transition pause

Between activities, pause for three breaths. Notice the ending of one thing and the beginning of the next. This micro-practice prevents attention residue and creates deliberate transitions in a day that might otherwise blur together.

### Protocol

1. When one activity ends, do not roll straight into the next. Stop for three slow breaths.
2. On the first breath, let the previous task end. Name it silently if that helps: "the meeting is over," "the writing is done."
3. On the second, notice what is still running: the unfinished thought, the mild irritation, the open loop. Do not chase it; just see it.
4. On the third, set the next thing: where the body goes, what the first step is. If the previous task is unfinished, write one line about where you will pick it up again. That single sentence is the studied version of the pause.

### Evidence

The construct behind this kernel is attention residue, defined by Sophie Leroy as the persistence of cognitive activity about a task after one has stopped working on it, which leaves fewer resources available for the next task and impairs its performance. A field study using daily diaries found that employees experience more attention residue and exhaustion on days with more intrusions, and that a brief transition intervention, writing a ready-to-resume plan about the interrupted task, reduced the residue and weakened its link to exhaustion. The pause is a small ritual around a real cognitive phenomenon: thoughts about Task A genuinely linger into Task B unless given a deliberate off-ramp.

### A guided version

No recording is needed; the three breaths are the guide. The studied written form is the one-line ready-to-resume note described in the protocol above.
