> **Kernel K186** — Don't-break-the-chain

A visible calendar and a red marker: mark each day you do the practice, and let the growing chain of marks become its own reason not to stop. The technique spread from a 2007 Lifehacker post by software developer Brad Isaac, recounting advice he said he received from Jerry Seinfeld as a young comic; Seinfeld has since said the idea was not his.

### Protocol

1. Get a big wall calendar that shows a whole year on one page and hang it somewhere prominent; get a big red marker ([Isaac, Lifehacker, 2007](https://lifehacker.com/jerry-seinfelds-productivity-secret-281626)).
2. Each day you do the chosen practice, put a big red X over that day. In Isaac's account: "After a few days you'll have a chain. Just keep at it and the chain will grow longer every day. You'll like seeing that chain, especially when you get a few weeks under your belt. Your only job next is to not break the chain" ([CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/a-productivity-secret-from-seinfeld-and-convicts/)).
3. *Editorial addition, not from the carriers:* when the chain does break, start a new chain the same day rather than abandoning the calendar; the habit research below suggests one missed day is not the catastrophe the method's absolutism implies.

### Evidence

The habit-formation evidence sits in tension with the technique's "never break it" framing, in a useful way. In the real-world habit study, 96 volunteers performed a self-chosen daily behaviour in a consistent context for 12 weeks, and "the time it took participants to reach 95% of their asymptote of automaticity ranged from 18 to 254 days" — the median was about 66 days. The same study found that "missing one opportunity to perform the behaviour did not materially affect the habit formation process," and that "with repetition of a behaviour in a consistent context, automaticity increases following an asymptotic curve" ([Lally et al., *European Journal of Social Psychology*, 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ejsp.674)). What that study measured was self-reported automaticity of simple daily behaviours, not the motivational effect of visible streaks, and not any well-being outcome; the chain's psychological pull (not wanting to lose an unbroken run) is plausible but not what these trials tested.

### A guided version

Not applicable: the practice is a calendar and a marker; there is nothing to be guided through.
