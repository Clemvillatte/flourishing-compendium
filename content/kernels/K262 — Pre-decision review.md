> **Kernel K262** — Pre-decision review

Before acting on a significant decision, write: What am I assuming? What would change if the assumption is wrong? What is the worst plausible outcome, and how would I handle it? What is the best plausible outcome? The review does not replace deliberation with a checklist. It slows the leap from judgement to action long enough for second thoughts to surface. Its closest named cousin is Gary Klein's premortem: "a premortem is the hypothetical opposite of a postmortem... a premortem in a business setting comes at the beginning of a project rather than the end, so that the project can be improved rather than autopsied" ([Klein, Harvard Business Review 2007](https://bankersadvocate.com/wp-content/themes/bankersadvocate/downloads/performing-project-pre-mortem.pdf)). Dimensions: cognition (the assumptions made explicit) and attention (held on the downside before the commitment).

### Protocol

1. Generate the four answers in writing: the assumption under the plan, what changes if it fails, the worst plausible outcome and its handling, the best plausible outcome. Writing is the point; a review done in the head folds back into the confidence it was meant to check.
2. Select the assumption that carries the most weight, and mark which of your four lines depend on it. A review that names three assumptions but cannot say which one matters has not reviewed anything.
3. Run the premortem frame on the worst line: assume the decision has already failed, and write the reasons as if explaining the past. Klein's instruction to teams is to "inform everyone that the project has failed spectacularly" and have each person "write down every reason they can think of for the failure", "especially the kinds of things they ordinarily wouldn't mention as potential problems" ([Klein 2007](https://bankersadvocate.com/wp-content/themes/bankersadvocate/downloads/performing-project-pre-mortem.pdf)).
4. Retain the review until the outcome is known, then score it: which failure reasons arrived, which were missed, which named assumptions held. The scoring is what turns a one-off review into judgment that improves.

### Evidence

The premise that failure is easier to explain backward than to foresee has a measured basis: research by Mitchell, Russo and Pennington "found that prospective hindsight, imagining that an event has already occurred, increases the ability to correctly identify reasons for future outcomes by 30%" ([Klein 2007](https://bankersadvocate.com/wp-content/themes/bankersadvocate/downloads/performing-project-pre-mortem.pdf)). The technique's test: "one hundred and seventy-eight people participated in one of five conditions to evaluate an H1N1 flu epidemic plan in a university context", and "the Premortem method, imagining that a plan has failed and then generating reasons to explain why, reliably reduced confidence more than each of the other conditions, and therefore can be a useful tool for combating overconfidence" ([Veinott, Klein and Wiggins, ISCRAM 2010](https://idl.iscram.org/files/veinott/2010/1049_Veinott_etal2010.pdf)). Scope limits: the measured effect is on confidence in one student scenario, not on outcomes, and later work found that "although premortems mute overconfidence, they introduce attribution bias, ironically focusing managers' attention away from the risks most within their control" ([Gandhi, Academy of Management Proceedings 2022](https://journals.aom.org/doi/10.5465/AMBPP.2022.12186abstract)). Scoring the review afterward keeps attention on controllable causes.
