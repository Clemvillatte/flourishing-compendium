> **Kernel K403** — Core and satellite values

Take the values you have already sorted by importance. Put the three most important in a pile at the center; scatter the remaining seven around them, roughly the way electrons circle a nucleus. Read each of the three core cards a few times.

Now step back three feet from the arrangement and look at it. Say what you see: these values matter to me; they are part of who I am; they do not exhaust who I am. Step back another three feet and look again.

The step backward is the exercise. A value laid out on the floor can be seen whole: something you carry, rather than something you must obey. Notice whether any of them has quietly become a rule that leaves no room for context.

### Protocol

Adapted (simplified) from Blonna, *Maximize Your Coaching Effectiveness with ACT* (2015), pp. 72–74, where the ranking is done over ten life-domain envelopes or slots before the values within them are ranked; this version assumes a values list you already have.

1. Take the values you have already sorted by importance. Put the three most important in a pile at the center; scatter the remaining seven around them, roughly the way electrons circle a nucleus.
2. Read each of the three core cards a few times.
3. Step back three feet from the arrangement and look at it. Say what you see: these values matter to me; they are part of who I am; they do not exhaust who I am.
4. Step back another three feet and look again. Allow yourself a little more room to breathe, and a little more distance between you and what you value.
5. Notice whether any value has quietly become a rule that leaves no room for context. If one has, write its name and one situation where the rule fails.

### Evidence

The core/satellite distinction, in a practitioner account that names Blonna as its source:

> "My experience (both personally and with clients) is that spotting patterns in values tends to lead to two sets of values – what Richard Blonna calls 'core' and 'satellite' values. The core values come up again and again, in nearly every context. The satellite values are also important but depend more on the situation."
> — [Dr Rob Archer, "Finding True North: How to Clarify Values", Working with ACT](https://workingwithact.com/2011/10/27/finding-true-north-how-to-clarify-values-part-1/)

The exercise's own spoken line, in the book's worksheet form (English translation of the German worksheets published with the book's German edition):

> "These are the values that matter most to me in my life, and I accept them as they are. Although they matter to me, I am made up of more than these values. They are a part of me and I carry them with me everywhere, but that does not mean they must control the actions I need to take to reach my goals."
> — [Blonna worksheets, "Values Ranking and Defusion: Core versus Satellite Values", German edition 2013, PDF](https://www.junfermann.de/_files_media/downloads/64399AE8B318CA64423C5B/arbeitsblaetter-zum-buch.pdf)

The step-back instruction, in the same worksheet:

> "Step back one or two more paces to gain more distance from your cards. Allow yourself a little more room to breathe and a little more space between yourself and what you value."
> — [same source](https://www.junfermann.de/_files_media/downloads/64399AE8B318CA64423C5B/arbeitsblaetter-zum-buch.pdf)

What the sources do not test: the exercise is a values-clarification and defusion technique inside ACT-based coaching; no trial isolates the core/satellite sort as its own intervention.

### A guided version

The exercise is written and arranged by the practicer; no recording is needed.
