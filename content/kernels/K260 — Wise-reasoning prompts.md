> **Kernel K260** — Wise-reasoning prompts

For a situation that is troubling, work through four questions: how the situation could unfold, what the different perspectives on it are, how one's own interests might be biasing the view, and what a wise friend would advise. The prompts translate the facets of wise reasoning from Igor Grossmann's research program into questions ([Grossmann, 2017](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~jhoey/teaching/cogsci600/papers/Grossmann_PPS_2017.pdf)). Reach for it when a conflict or decision keeps circling in the mind without moving.

### What it rests on

The framework of wise thinking as synthesized in the review:

> "They include (a) intellectual humility or recognition of limits of own knowledge, (b) appreciation of perspectives broader than the issue at hand, (c) sensitivity to the possibility of change in social relations, and (d) compromise or integration of different opinions (see Figure 1)." — [Grossmann, Wisdom in Context, Perspectives on Psychological Science 12(2), 2017](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~jhoey/teaching/cogsci600/papers/Grossmann_PPS_2017.pdf)

The four questions are the compendium's editorial translation of these facets into prompts, not a questionnaire from the studies.

### Protocol

1. Name the situation in one sentence.
2. Ask: what are the ways this situation could unfold?
3. Ask: what are the different perspectives on it?
4. Ask: how might my view be biased by my interests?
5. Ask: what would a wise friend advise?
6. Write the answers down; the written version is what later re-reading measures progress against.

### Evidence

The experiments behind the fourth prompt concern the self-other asymmetry: people reason more wisely about other people's problems than about their own. The review's summary of the ego-decentering experiments:

> "reflections on a personal problem from an ego-decentering perspective resulted in significantly greater wisdom than reflection on a person problem from an egocentric perspective, and they were statistically indistinguishable from reflections on a friend's problem. Further planned contrasts indicated that ego-decentering significantly attenuated the self–other asymmetry, enabling individuals to get closer to their actual potential in wise reasoning." — [Grossmann, 2017](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~jhoey/teaching/cogsci600/papers/Grossmann_PPS_2017.pdf)

The diary study found wise reasoning to be a state that varies by situation: "substantial and systematic intraindividual variability in WR, with wiser reasoning in the social versus nonsocial contexts" ([Grossmann, Gerlach & Denissen, 2016, Abstract](https://www.psych.uni-goettingen.de/de/biopers/publications_department/grossmannetal2016/@@download/pdf_file/grossmann_gerlach_denissen_2016_wise_reasoning_in_the_face_of_everyday_life_challenges_spps.pdf)).

What this evidence does not test: no study ran these four questions as a protocol, and the key experiments measured scored reasoning about scenarios, not the alleviation of suffering or the quality of later decisions.

### A guided version

Not applicable: the practice is self-questioning on paper, not a recording.
