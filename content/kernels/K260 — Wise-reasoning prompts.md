> **Kernel K260** — Wise-reasoning prompts

For a situation that is troubling, work through four questions: how the situation could unfold, what the different perspectives on it are, how one's own interests might be biasing the view, and what a wise friend would advise. The prompts are editorial questions informed by the facets of wise reasoning in Igor Grossmann's research program ([Grossmann, 2017](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~jhoey/teaching/cogsci600/papers/Grossmann_PPS_2017.pdf)). Reach for it when a conflict or decision keeps circling in the mind without moving.

### What it rests on

The framework of wise thinking as synthesized in the review:

> "They include (a) intellectual humility or recognition of limits of own knowledge, (b) appreciation of perspectives broader than the issue at hand, (c) sensitivity to the possibility of change in social relations, and (d) compromise or integration of different opinions (see Figure 1)." — [Grossmann, Wisdom in Context, Perspectives on Psychological Science 12(2), Contemporary scholarship section](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~jhoey/teaching/cogsci600/papers/Grossmann_PPS_2017.pdf)

The four questions are editorial prompts informed by this framework; they do not explicitly address the fourth facet, compromise and integration.

### Protocol

1. Name the situation in one sentence.
2. Ask: what are the ways this situation could unfold?
3. Ask: what are the different perspectives on it?
4. Ask: how might my view be biased by my interests?
5. Ask: what would a wise friend advise?
6. Write the answers down and keep them for later comparison.

### Evidence

The wise-friend question is an editorial prompt, not the tested instruction. The related experiments studied ego-decentred reflection, and they found that people reason more wisely about other people's problems than about their own; the review's summary:

> "reflections on a personal problem from an ego-decentering perspective resulted in significantly greater wisdom than reflection on a person problem from an egocentric perspective, and they were statistically indistinguishable from reflections on a friend's problem. Further planned contrasts indicated that ego-decentering significantly attenuated the self–other asymmetry, enabling individuals to get closer to their actual potential in wise reasoning." — [Grossmann, 2017, Ego-Decentering Cognitive Mind-Set section](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~jhoey/teaching/cogsci600/papers/Grossmann_PPS_2017.pdf)

The diary study found wise reasoning to be a state that varies by situation: "substantial and systematic intraindividual variability in WR, with wiser reasoning in the social versus nonsocial contexts" ([Grossmann, Gerlach & Denissen, 2016, Abstract](https://www.psych.uni-goettingen.de/de/biopers/publications_department/grossmannetal2016/@@download/pdf_file/grossmann_gerlach_denissen_2016_wise_reasoning_in_the_face_of_everyday_life_challenges_spps.pdf)).

What this evidence does not test: the cited research did not evaluate this editorial protocol, and the key experiments measured scored reasoning about scenarios, not the alleviation of suffering or the quality of later decisions.

### A guided version

Not applicable: the practice is self-questioning on paper, not a recording.
