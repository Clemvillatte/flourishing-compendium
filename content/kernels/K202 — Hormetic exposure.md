> **Kernel K202** — Hormetic exposure

In small, deliberate doses, expose the body to a mild stressor: cold water, heat, fasting, exertion. The heat side has the strongest single dataset: the Finnish sauna cohort, twenty years of follow-up on 2315 men.

### Protocol

*Steps are the compendium's editorial protocol; the evidence below is observational and establishes no dose.*

1. Start small and gradual: one stressor, one modest dose, and watch the response before increasing anything.
2. Keep the dose mild enough to be recovered from the same day; hormesis is the biphasic zone, not the punishing one.
3. Regularity over intensity: the sauna data associates frequency, more than duration, with outcomes.

### Evidence

The Finnish cohort prospectively followed 2315 middle-aged men for a median of 20.7 years, during which "190 SCDs, 281 fatal CHDs, 407 fatal CVDs, and 929 all-cause mortality events occurred." The finding: "After adjustment for CVD risk factors, compared with men with 1 sauna bathing session per week, the hazard ratio of SCD was 0.78 (95% CI, 0.57-1.07) for 2 to 3 sauna bathing sessions per week and 0.37 (95% CI, 0.18-0.75) for 4 to 7 sauna bathing sessions per week," with the conclusion that "increased frequency of sauna bathing is associated with a reduced risk of SCD, CHD, CVD, and all-cause mortality" ([Laukkanen et al., *JAMA Internal Medicine*, 2015](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamainternalmedicine/fullarticle/2130724)). What that study does not establish: causation. It is an observational cohort of Finnish men who chose their own sauna habits, and a published reply to the paper argued the link may be noncausal (JAMA Internal Medicine, 2015;175(10):1719-20). No randomised trial assigns cold showers or sauna doses, and no study tests hormetic exposure as a well-being practice; the cold-exposure side of the kernel rests on far thinner evidence than the heat side.

### A guided version

Not applicable: the exposure is a physical stressor administered to the body, not a recording.
