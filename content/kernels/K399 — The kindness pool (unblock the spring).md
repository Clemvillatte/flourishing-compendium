> **Kernel K399** — The kindness pool (unblock the spring)

Picture kindness as a clear pool fed by a small spring. You can ration the pool, giving each person you meet a thimbleful as they pass, and watch it shrink. Or you can unblock the spring that feeds it, so the pool is continuously replenished. The practice (Befriending meditation, K396) is the unblocking. When you notice yourself rationing kindness, holding back warmth because you are tired or drained, ask: is the pool actually low, or is the spring blocked?

### Protocol

Adapted from Williams and Penman, *Mindfulness*, 2011, ch. 11, printed p. 195; the underlying practice is the book's Befriending Meditation (Week Six, Track 7), and Mark Williams has spoken about it directly:

1. Begin with yourself. Say silently: "May I be safe and protected. May I be happy and healthy. May I have ease of being."
2. Extend the same three wishes to a loved one. Hold them in mind, and repeat the wishes with them as the subject.
3. Extend to a stranger: someone you see on the bus, on the subway, whose name you do not know, but who you assume has fears and hopes like every other member of humanity.
4. If you choose, extend to somebody you do not like very much: not the most difficult person in the world, but somebody you find somewhat difficult. Assume they too want to be happy, and wish them the same three things.
5. Ten minutes is a complete round. When you notice warmth returning toward yourself or others, that is the spring unblocking.

### Evidence

Mark Williams's own account of what the meditation does, from a spoken interview:

> "And so he took one 10-minute meditation from that programme, which is called the Kindness, or Befriending Meditation, which involves the meta-meditation from Buddhism. And it involves, first of all, bringing kindness to yourself. May I be safe and protected. May I be happy and healthy. May I have ease of being."
> — [Mark Williams, interview transcript, Beshara Magazine](https://besharamagazine.org/podcast/marc-williams/)

The extension to strangers and difficult people, from the same account:

> "Maybe you see them on the bus, on the subway, you don't know their name, but you assume they have fears and hopes like every other member of humanity. So you say, may they be safe and protected. Then lastly, if you choose, you extend that to somebody you don't like very much. Not the most difficult person in the world, but somebody you find somewhat difficult."
> — [same source](https://besharamagazine.org/podcast/marc-williams/)

The practice's effect has been tested on the polarization outcome in a peer-reviewed trial:

> "The results suggest that a brief befriending meditation can reduce affective polarization between Democrats and Republicans by increasing positive feelings relatively more for the political outgroup than the political ingroup."
> — [Simonsson et al., "Love thy (partisan) neighbor", Oxford Research Archive PDF](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:0d0bd042-cc53-494d-b71c-8e50e98b3fee/files/rzk51vh490)

What the sources do not test: the pool-and-spring metaphor is the book's own image for the practice's effect; the trial tested the meditation's effect on political-attitude outcomes, not on the metaphor's claim about the pool being replenished.
