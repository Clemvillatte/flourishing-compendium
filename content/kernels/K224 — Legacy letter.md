> **Kernel K224** — Legacy letter

Write a letter to be read after your death. What do you want to leave behind? What wisdom, what love, what unfinished business? The letter is for you, not for the recipient.

### Protocol

*Steps are the compendium's editorial protocol; the process they formalise is described in the life-review literature below.*

1. Write to one named person, living or future; a letter to everyone is a letter to no one.
2. Cover three things only: what you learned that they might use, what you love that you want them to know, and what you left unfinished that you are sorry for.
3. Seal it and set a date to reread it; the value is in the writing, and the rereading is the audit.

### Evidence

The practice is an instance of what Butler called the life review: "I conceive of the life review as a naturally occurring, universal mental process characterized by the progressive return to consciousness of past experiences, and, particularly, the resurgence of unresolved conflicts; simultaneously, and normally, these revived experiences and conflicts can be surveyed and reintegrated" ([Butler, 1963, quoted in a University of Victoria thesis on life review counselling](https://collectionscanada.ca/obj/thesescanada/vol2/BVAU/TC-BVAU-31106.pdf); original: *Psychiatry*, 26(1), 65–76). Butler held that the process is prompted by the realisation of approaching death and serves the putting of one's life in order. What this literature does not test: the specific letter-to-be-read-after-death format, or any effect on the writer's well-being; the outcomes studied were reminiscence, ego integrity, and life satisfaction in older adults, and the epistolary form is editorial.

Not applicable: the letter is itself the artefact; a recording would duplicate it.
