> **Kernel K364** — Cosmopolitan reflection

Once a month, spend time in a community not your own. Listen. Do not advise. The practice is the Stoic exercise of cosmopolitanism: the recognition that you are a citizen of the world, not just of your own group. [[T014 — Marcus Aurelius|Marcus Aurelius]] states the dual membership plainly: "My city and country as I am Antoninus, is Rome; as a man, the whole world" ([Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 6.44, Project Gutenberg ebook 2680](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/2680/2680-h/2680-h.htm)). The empirical companion is the contact literature: "with 713 independent samples from 515 studies, the meta-analysis finds that intergroup contact typically reduces intergroup prejudice" ([Pettigrew & Tropp 2006](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/pettigrew_tropp_2006_-_intergroup_contact_theory.pdf)). The monthly visit is a modern adaptation of the Stoic claim, and the contact research does not test this specific exercise: the other community's life heard from inside it, and your own group's habits seen from outside.

### Protocol

1. Generate the visit: choose a community whose life you do not share, a different congregation, neighbourhood, language, class or generation, and enter as a guest, with the standing of a guest, one who is received rather than received from.
2. Select the listening discipline: no advising, no explaining your own group, no correcting what you hear. You are there to be shown how the world looks from a place you do not stand, the whole-world citizenship Marcus names.
3. Retain the reflection after the visit: write down two things this community does that your own does not, and one thing your own does that you had never seen before because everyone around you did it too.
4. Repeat monthly, a different community each time where possible, until the boundary between "my group" and "people" thins on its own.

### Evidence

The contact evidence is quoted above: 515 studies, 713 independent samples, 250,089 individuals from 38 nations, mean r = −.21 between contact and prejudice, with the meta-analysis showing "greater intergroup contact is generally associated with lower levels of prejudice (mean r = −.215)" (Pettigrew & Tropp 2006, [Stanford SPARQ PDF](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/pettigrew_tropp_2006_-_intergroup_contact_theory.pdf)). The authors' own summary: "Allport's conditions are not essential for intergroup contact to achieve positive outcomes", though structuring the situation to meet them "generally enhances the positive effects" — which is why the practice asks for guest standing rather than waiting for ideal conditions. The Stoic ground is quoted above from Book 6.44 of the Gutenberg edition. Scope limits: most of the 515 studies are observational rather than experimental, so the causal reading rests on the stronger designs within the set rather than on randomisation throughout; and the sources cited here do not test the monthly-visit practice itself, as distinct from contact generally. The listening without advising is the practice's own addition, drawn from the Stoic framing rather than the meta-analysis.
