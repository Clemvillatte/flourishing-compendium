> **Kernel K139** — Self-as-context

When caught in a self-story ("I am a failure", "I am an anxious person"), shift attention from the story to the one who is noticing the story. The noticing position is not the content it notices. This is Acceptance and Commitment Therapy's self-as-context: the observing position rather than the evaluated contents. Reach for it when a self-judgment has hardened into a verdict and the verdict has started to run the day.

### Protocol

Suggested adaptation of the ACT tradition cited below. The practice moves the Self and Attention dimensions: the vantage point, and where attention rests.

1. Name the story as a story. In the ACT model the work begins by "undermin[ing] an attachment to a conceptualized self (our fused, evaluative stories about who we are)" ([Hayes et al. 2013, epub 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3635495/)). Editorial addition, not from the carriers: say it to yourself in the form "I am noticing the thought that I am a failure", which puts the noticing on the record.
2. Ask who is doing the noticing, and let attention rest there. The model's term for this position is "a sense of self based on the 'I/here/nowness' of conscious experience".
3. From the observing position, let the story be one event among others in the field of consciousness. The clinical rationale in the review is that "self-as-context interventions help clients see inner experiences as distinct from consciousness as such, and thus not necessarily a threat". A few minutes is a complete round.

### Evidence

The definition, from the model's own progress review:

> ACT seeks to undermine an attachment to a conceptualized self (our fused, evaluative stories about who we are) and to promote contact with a sense of self based on the "I/here/nowness" of conscious experience. It is usually termed "self as context" although other terms ("noticing self," "observer perspective," "transcendent sense of self") have been used.
> — [Hayes, Levin, Plumb-Vilardaga, Villatte & Pistorello (2013), *Behavior Therapy* 44(2), 180–198 (epub 2011)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3635495/)

The component evidence is honestly thin, and the review that canvassed it says so:

> "On the basis of four identified studies, there is only provisional but very limited evidence to suggest that SAC can be effectively taught and implemented as a stand-alone process to manage emotional well-being. Mixed findings emerged in relation to SAC improving emotional well-being. The methodological quality of the studies was variable, which contributed to the mixed outcomes. There is limited evidence to support the use of the SAC component as a stand-alone process in ACT-based interventions, and research recommendations are discussed."
> — [Godbee & Kangas (2020), *Behavior Therapy* 51(6), 917–932, PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33051034/)

What the studies do not test: the review covers single-session laboratory trials, intervention trials and cross-sectional designs, 20 studies in 14 papers, not a self-administered noticing round; the mixed outcomes are a real caution, and the page does not claim a symptom effect for the exercise.
