> **Kernel K274** — Evening examination

Each evening, review the day through three questions: What did I do well? Where did I drift from my principles? What will I do differently tomorrow? The practice is the Stoic evening meditation (Seneca's evening review). It functions as a daily feedback loop, not as self-criticism. Research on self-reflection shows that structured review improves performance and well-being when it is specific and forward-looking rather than ruminative.

### Protocol

The three questions are a loop, not a court; the trial of the daily questioning is quoted below. Intended focus: Self and Motivation.

1. At the end of the day, take three questions in order: what did I do well, where did I drift from my principles, what will I do differently tomorrow. Each gets one honest sentence.
2. Keep the third question concrete: a differently that names a situation and an action, not a character verdict. The moment the answers turn into a case against yourself, the review has become rumination in the practice's clothes, and it is time to close the notebook.
3. Close the loop at the next day's first drift-point, where the differently is meant to land; a review that never feeds a next action is only a mood. One line per question is the whole record.

### Evidence

From the randomized trial of brief daily reflection:

> "Stress and negative affect decreased in the intervention group relative to the control group, but no interaction was observed for happiness and positive affect." (Abstract, [Fiodorova & Farb, 2022, Anxiety, Stress and Coping 35(2), 158-170](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34313502/))

What this evidence does not test: the Stoic form. In the trial, 95 undergraduates were randomized so that 52 received a daily email prompt with questions about self-care habits and plans for three weeks while 43 served as passive controls; the prompted group's stress and negative affect fell relative to controls, with happiness and positive affect unmoved. The prompts were about self-care, not the three questions of the evening examination, and the trial says nothing about performance, Seneca, or the drift the review is meant to catch. What it supports is the practice's minimal claim: a brief, structured, forward-looking daily reflection, small enough to arrive by email, measurably moved the negative side of daily experience.
