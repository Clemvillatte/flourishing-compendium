> **Kernel K238** — Kill-the-Buddha drill

For one belief you hold, argue from your own experience against your favorite authority. The exercise tests whether the belief survives contact with lived evidence or whether it is held on authority alone.

### Where the drill comes from

The name is a ninth-century Zen instruction. The Chan master Linji told his followers:

> "If you meet a buddha, kill the buddha. If you meet a patriarch, kill the patriarch. If you meet an arhat, kill the arhat. If you meet your parents, kill your parents. If you meet your kinfolk, kill your kinfolk." (Linji yulu, in [Park 2019](https://terebess.hu/zen/mesterek/kyong.pdf), pp. 258–259, quoting Watson 1993, p. 52)

Park reads the passage as a teaching about detachment from names: Buddha and the patriarchs are, in her words, "only names", and she describes breaking one's attachment to names as the first step toward awakening. The killing in the instruction is aimed at names, not at people.

The drill is a modern writing exercise inspired by that passage, and the adaptation is this practice's own: it asks whether a belief you hold on the word of an admired authority can stand on your own evidence, or whether the authority's name is doing the work your evidence should be doing.

### Protocol

The steps are this practice's own exercise, not Linji's instructions. The practice moves the Cognition dimension: what you think and how you test it.

1. Choose one belief you hold mainly because an authority you admire holds it: a teacher, an author, a tradition.
2. Write the authority's claim in one or two sentences, as the authority stated it, with where it appears.
3. Write what your own experience shows about the claim, separating what you directly observed, what you inferred, and what the question would need beyond your own experience.
4. Argue the case against the authority from that evidence, as if to a reader who has never heard of the authority and is not impressed by names.
5. Record the verdict honestly: the belief survives on its own evidence, it needs restating in your terms, it is unresolved, or it was held on the name alone.

### A guided version

Not applicable. The drill is an argument written for oneself, not a recorded exercise.

### Evidence

Relevant background, not a validation of this drill: the research below measures how much a message's acceptance depends on who delivers it. In the classic Yale experiments:

> "Opinions were changed immediately after the communication in the direction advocated by the communicator to a significantly greater degree when the material was presented by a trustworthy source than when presented by an untrustworthy source." (Hovland & Weiss 1951, p. 650, quoted verbatim in [Hellmueller & Trilling 2012](https://pure.uva.nl/ws/files/1362753/164537_vd_3.pdf))

The same study's abstract records that over time people "disassociate the content and the source with the result that the original scepticism faded and the 'untrustworthy' material was accepted" ([Hovland & Weiss 1951](https://academic.oup.com/poq/article-abstract/15/4/635/1923117)). A review of five decades concluded that "The main effect studies of source credibility on persuasion seem to indicate the superiority of a high-credibility source over a low-credibility one", while its abstract also reports that interaction studies show source credibility to be a liability under certain conditions ([Pornpitakpan 2004](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1559-1816.2004.tb02547.x)).

Not covered: these are experiments on opinion change after messages attributed to sources of differing credibility, not tests of a written self-examination drill, and Linji's instruction is a religious teaching, not a studied intervention.
