> **Kernel K179** — Deliberate practice

For a skill you want to develop, structure practice with a clear goal, immediate feedback, and repetition at the edge of your ability. Expertise is built in doing, with feedback, not in passive study. The term comes from Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer's studies of expert performers: "deliberate practice includes activities that have been specially designed to improve the current level of performance", and "in contrast to play, deliberate practice is a highly structured activity, the explicit goal of which is to improve performance" ([Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Römer, Psychological Review 1993](https://clinica.ispa.pt/sites/default/files/1_-_the_role_of_deliberate_practice_in_the_acquisition_of_expert_performance_3.pdf)). Dimensions: overt behaviour (the structured practice session) and cognition (the attention that spots the error the feedback confirms).

### Protocol

1. Name one skill and one measurable sub-goal for the month. "Play the piano" is not a goal; "play the left hand of these eight bars at tempo with no missed notes" is.
2. Generate the task at the edge: design (or borrow from a teacher) a task you fail roughly half the time. Comfortable repetition is play; deliberate practice lives just past reliable performance.
3. Arrange feedback before practicing, not after: a recording, a metronome, a coach, a test. Ericsson's conditions are explicit: "the subjects should receive immediate informative feedback and knowledge of results of their performance. The subjects should repeatedly perform the same or similar tasks" ([Ericsson et al. 1993](https://clinica.ispa.pt/sites/default/files/1_-_the_role_of_deliberate_practice_in_the_acquisition_of_expert_performance_3.pdf)).
4. Select the error. Each session, work one named flaw at a time, and stop when attention fades; the concentration is the active ingredient, and it is a depletable resource.
5. Retain the log: date, task, errors, minutes. The plateau is broken by reading the log, finding the flaw that persists, and designing next month's task against exactly that.

### Evidence

The original studies of violinists and pianists concluded that "individual differences, even among elite performers, are closely related to assessed amounts of deliberate practice" ([Ericsson et al. 1993](https://clinica.ispa.pt/sites/default/files/1_-_the_role_of_deliberate_practice_in_the_acquisition_of_expert_performance_3.pdf)). The later quantitative picture is more modest: a meta-analysis of 88 studies totalling 11,135 participants found that "deliberate practice explained 26% of the variance in performance for games, 21% for music, 18% for sports, 4% for education, and less than 1% for professions", concluding that practice is "not as important as Ericsson and his colleagues have argued" ([Macnamara, Hambrick and Oswald, Psychological Science 2014](https://www.psychologie.uzh.ch/dam/jcr:658d39fe-2f4b-467f-9ae2-ffeb25caf2b6/McNamara.PsychSci.25.2014.pdf)). Scope limits: variance explained differs sharply by domain, the 2018 corrigendum adjusted some figures upward, and Ericsson's later reanalyses argue the meta-analysis diluted his original definition. Practice hours are one real input among several, which is a reason to practice well, not a reason to skip practice.
