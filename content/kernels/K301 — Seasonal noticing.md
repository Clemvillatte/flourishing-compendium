> **Kernel K301** — Seasonal noticing

An hour outdoors at each change of season, noticing what has changed: light, temperature, growth, decay, animal behavior. The page leans on two findings from the nature-wellbeing literature, both quoted below, and claims nothing the season's turn itself guarantees. It suits anyone whose sense of time has collapsed into the phone's endless present.

### Protocol

The steps are my practice form; the findings the form leans on are quoted in the Evidence section.

1. Pick the same route or patch for each visit, so the comparison is honest: the same hedgerow, park, or shoreline seen in different light.
2. Notice the changes by category: light, temperature, growth, decay, animal behavior. Write them down or say them aloud; the noticing is the point, not the walk's length.
3. Treat the noticing itself as the practice, not the time spent. The study quoted below found engagement and connectedness, not duration, were the significant factors.

### Evidence

From the meta-analysis of nature connectedness and happiness:

> "Based on 30 samples (n = 8523), a fixed-effect meta-analysis found a small but significant effect size (r = 0.19). Those who are more connected to nature tended to experience more positive affect, vitality, and life satisfaction compared to those less connected to nature." (Abstract, [Capaldi, Dopko & Zelenski, 2014, Frontiers in Psychology 5, 976](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25249992/))

From the study that examined several nature-related factors at once:

> "A consistent pattern of results emerged across multiple analytical approaches (i.e., correlations, linear regression, dominance analyses, commonality analysis), wherein time in nature was not the main (or significant) predictive nature-related factor for wellbeing. Rather, nature connectedness and engaging with nature through simple activities (e.g., smelling flowers) consistently emerged as being the significant and prominent factors in predicting and explaining variance in mental health and wellbeing." (Abstract, [Richardson, Passmore, Lumber, Thomas & Hunt, 2021, International Journal of Wellbeing 11(1), 8-33](https://doi.org/10.5502/ijw.v11i1.1267))

What this evidence does not test: the practice itself. The meta-analysis is correlational, and its connectedness measure is described there as a stable individual difference; the 2021 study surveyed a UK national sample. Neither study tested a seasonal noticing practice, and neither licenses a claim that noticing seasons causes well-being.
