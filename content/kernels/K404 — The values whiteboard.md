> **Kernel K404** — The values whiteboard

When thinking about a values conflict keeps going in circles, honesty against kindness, ambition against family, write the conflict on a whiteboard or a large sheet of paper. Write what your mind says about it: the arguments, the scripts, the key words. Write the images you see when you close your eyes, and the emotions and body sensations that come with them. Fill the board.

Now put six feet between you and the board. Look at it from there and say: my mind has a lot to say about this, how interesting. From that distance, ask which parts of what you wrote are useful for choosing your next action, and write your answer on a different part of the board.

The exercise is not a verdict on the conflict. It is a way of seeing everything the conflict consists of at once, from a position where you are not inside it.

### Protocol

Adapted from Blonna, *Stress Less, Live More* (2010), as presented in Blonna, *Maximize Your Coaching Effectiveness with ACT* (2015), pp. 80–81; the underlying worksheets (Whiteboard defusion and Values-Whiteboard defusion) are published with the book's German edition.

1. When you catch your mind stuck in a thinking trap, get a whiteboard or a large pad and pens.
2. Write the heading: "Unhelpful thoughts my mind gives me about ____", filling in whatever you are stuck on.
3. List every thought by which your mind is driving you into a dead end. List them all, no matter how crazy, stupid or unimportant they seem.
4. Put the pen down when you are done, and step back a few paces. Look at the board from that distance and say: these are just my thoughts. They are not me. I am much more than these thoughts.
5. Feel the distance between yourself and the unhelpful thoughts. Then try to widen it further, step by step, until you feel your connection to the thoughts loosen.
6. Ask how the thoughts feel now, and which parts of what you wrote are useful for choosing your next action. Write your answer on a different part of the board.

### Evidence

The worksheet's own heading and the list-everything instruction, in the book's published worksheet form (English translation of the German worksheets):

> "Write the following heading: 'Unhelpful thoughts my mind gives me about ____' (whatever you are stuck on). List every thought by which your mind drives you into a dead end."
> — [Blonna worksheets, "Defusion Exercise: The Whiteboard", *Effektiver Coachen mit ACT* (German edition), Junfermann 2013, PDF](https://www.junfermann.de/_files_media/downloads/64399AE8B318CA64423C5B/arbeitsblaetter-zum-buch.pdf)

The list-all rule:

> "It is important that you list all your thoughts, no matter how crazy, stupid or unimportant they may seem to you."
> — [same source](https://www.junfermann.de/_files_media/downloads/64399AE8B318CA64423C5B/arbeitsblaetter-zum-buch.pdf)

The step-back close:

> "Put the pen aside when you are finished, and step back a few paces. Look at the board or pad from some distance and say to yourself: These are just my thoughts. They are not me. I am much more than these thoughts. Feel the distance between yourself and the unhelpful thoughts. Then try to widen it even further."
> — [same source](https://www.junfermann.de/_files_media/downloads/64399AE8B318CA64423C5B/arbeitsblaetter-zum-buch.pdf)

The exercise belongs to the defusion family in ACT-based coaching. The values-conflict version is not this wiki's invention: the same German worksheet set carries a Values-Whiteboard defusion worksheet (Arbeitsblatt 4.5) that applies the move to a values conflict, down to the spoken line ("My mind really has a lot to say about this values conflict – how interesting") and the two-metre step-back. The specific conflicts named above (honesty against kindness, ambition against family) are this wiki's examples.

What the sources do not test: the worksheet is a defusion technique inside ACT-based coaching; no trial isolates the whiteboard form as its own intervention.

### A guided version

The exercise is written and read at a distance; no recording is needed.
