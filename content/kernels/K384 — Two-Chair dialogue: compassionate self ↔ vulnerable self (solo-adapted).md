> **Kernel K384** — Two-Chair dialogue: compassionate self ↔ vulnerable self (solo-adapted)

Set up two chairs facing each other. One is the "vulnerable chair": the anxious, angry, sad, or ashamed part of you. The other is the "compassionate chair": your Compassionate Self (see K378).

Sit in the vulnerable chair first. Let that part have its say. What is it afraid of? What is it angry about? What does it want? Don't edit it.

When that part has had its say, physically move to the compassionate chair. Take a breath, settle into the compassionate persona: wise, warm, courageous. From here, speak *to* the vulnerable part. Validate what you heard, then offer kindness, understanding, encouragement.

If you slip back into critical or dismissive language, stand up, return to the vulnerable chair, let it respond, then come back to the compassionate chair.

Practice 10 to 15 minutes. You're building the capacity to hear your own fear or anger without being run by it.

**Note:** This is a solo adaptation of a therapist-facilitated practice. If a difficult emotion (trauma, panic, intense shame) feels unworkable alone, stop and consider professional support: this practice is for building the everyday capacity, not for processing severe distress.

Adapted from Kolts, *CFT Made Simple*, New Harbinger, 2016, ch. 12, "Embodying Compassion: Chair Work in CFT", two-chair work, printed pp. 159 to 161 ([owner's Drive copy](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1uN4kJ9GGbWVIlzNAxeOPsO9w6VYJr-iP)); for several emotions at once Kolts points to the Multiple Selves exercise in ch. 14.

> "In this practice, two chairs are set up, and the therapist assists the client in having a dialogue between a version of herself that represents the emotion with which she is struggling (such as anxiety, depression, or anger) and her wise, kind, confident compassionate self." — Kolts, *CFT Made Simple*, ch. 12, p. 159 ([owner's Drive copy](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1uN4kJ9GGbWVIlzNAxeOPsO9w6VYJr-iP))

### Protocol

1. Two chairs facing each other. Sit in the vulnerable chair first; the book's order is deliberate: "Typically, we’ll have the client begin in the vulnerable chair, inviting that version of the self to speak its piece—to really let the fear, sadness, or anger flow." Target dimensions: Overt behaviour (physically switching chairs) and Affect (letting the emotion speak, then addressing it warmly).
2. Speak the fear, sadness or anger without editing. If more than one emotion shows up, either take them in turn or pause and try the Multiple Selves variant (ch. 14).
3. Move to the compassionate chair and shift fully into the perspective before speaking. From there, address the vulnerable self, "offering validation, understanding, kindness, and encouragement" (p. 160).
4. If you slip back into critical or dismissive language, that is expected and repairable: "it’s no problem—the therapist just prompts her to switch chairs until the vulnerable self has had her say" (p. 160). Solo, you are the one doing the prompting: stand up, return to the vulnerable chair, let it finish, then switch again.
5. Ten to fifteen minutes. What you are training is the capacity to hear your own fear or anger without being run by it.

### Evidence

Kolts situates chair work in the emotion-focused therapy tradition ("CFT draws heavily from the work of a pioneer of modern chair work, Leslie Greenberg", p. 155) and reports a pilot study: chair work "demonstrated the usefulness of such chair work for increasing self-compassion and self-reassurance in self-critical clients, in addition to decreasing self-criticism and symptoms of anxiety and depression (Shahar et al., 2012)" (p. 155). That study and Greenberg's originals were not opened; the solo form above is an adaptation of a therapist-facilitated exercise, and the safety note stands.
