> **Kernel K418** — Six Healing Sounds (liuzi jue)

The liuzi jue (六子訣, as Kohn prints the term), the Six Healing Sounds, is a set of six exhalation patterns from the daoyin tradition. Each sound is made on the out-breath and matched to one organ: he for the heart, hu for the spleen, xu for the liver, chui for the kidneys, si for the lungs, and xi for the Triple Heater, the organ of digestion and energy processing. The practice appears in early texts such as the Daoyin jing and, in Kohn's assessment, "still forms an important part of qigong practice today" ([Kohn](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1WPlkEDK5FTR9LkFGrutj1YCqIDZ9dMx_)).

### Protocol

The sources below are layered: Kohn's historical descriptions, the standardized modern form as studies describe it, and one general framework. The Association's manual itself was not opened; each standardized-form passage cites the study describing it.

#### Historical breathing descriptions

From Kohn's account of the early texts, the six breaths differ in sound quality: chui, a sharp expulsion with the lips almost closed, warming and relaxing; xu, gentle with the mouth wide open, air from the bottom of the lungs, cooling; hu, the mildest, blown out with rounded lips; si, a gentle release between slightly opened lips; he, a strong breath through the open mouth with a rasp in the throat; xi, a sigh, soft and from deep in the body. The Yinshu's seasonal selection: spring, xu; summer, hu; fall and winter, chui — each several times, plus once each with the other two (Kohn, printed p. 57).

#### General practice framework

From a peer-reviewed description of the modern practice: "one can either stand or kneel and calm the mind, breathing peacefully into the cinnabar field (Dan Tian; the navel), and then apply one of the six sounds, or all of them in a specific sequence" ([Shi et al. 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2158244020927024)). The sound is voiced softly or silently; exhalation "is either equal to or longer than that of the inhalation... not prolonged by force."

#### The standardized Health Qigong form

The modern form was standardized under China's General Administration of Sport; its structure, from a COPD trial protocol:

> "The routine consists of eight components: a preparatory posture, six-character vocalizations ("xu," "he," "hu," "si," "chui," and "xi"), and a closing posture." — [Ni et al. 2026, JMIR Research Protocols](https://www.researchprotocols.org/2026/1/e91283)

The same protocol gives the coordination: "inhaling during the upward movement of the upper limbs and exhaling during their descent", movements executed "slowly, continuously, smoothly, and with lightness"; a round "requires approximately 15 minutes". The EEG study of the form:

> "Each sound is performed within a specific movement routine without moving the feet, with each sound and movement routine repeated six times. Inverse abdominal respiration is required while performing the practice, as well as producing the six sounds respectively during exhaling. While inhaling with lips closed and tongue reaching the palate, one should breath naturally through the nose and make the abdomen uplifted (Chinese Health Qigong Association, 2007)." — [Henz & Schöllhorn 2018, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.02291/full)

A randomized trial using the 2003 reorganized version spells out the modules:

> "(a) Stand with feet open at the same width as the shoulders, put your hands on the sides of the waist with palms up, stretch out the right hand toward the left front by 45°, say "Xu," and then return. Complete the mirror action with the other hand. (b) Bend your knees, raise your forearms, fold your palm down, press down, say "He," and then return. (c) Place your hands in front of the abdomen, rotate the palms inward, stretch forward with a circular trajectory, say "Hu," and then return. (4) Lift your hands naturally to your chest, raise your palms to push forward, say "Si," and finally return. (5) Place your hands in front of the abdomen, wrap your hands around your sides, press down slowly, say "Chui," and finally return. (6) Place your hands in front of your abdomen, lift your hands up to the top of your head, open your arms, pull back, press down, say "Xi," and finally return. Each set of actions was repeated three times before the next set of actions was performed." — [Zhu et al. 2022, Journal of Traditional Chinese Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9924758/)

Repetition counts differ: three per sound in that trial, six in the EEG study and a manual-based Parkinson's RCT ([Yin et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11843974/)).

### Evidence

Kohn presents the organ associations as traditional Chinese medicine correspondence: a "detailed medical method to treat various imbalances of qi associated with the five inner organs and the Triple Heater". The standardized form has since served as the intervention in randomized trials and protocols (COVID-19, Parkinson's, COPD; citations above); the studies report their own outcomes. Henz & Schöllhorn report EEG findings: alpha activity decreased after 15 minutes and increased after 30 minutes of practice. The classical closing promise:

> "Each of the six breaths—he, si, hu, xu, chui, and xi—is ruled by one of the five organs. In case of extreme fatigue, one can use them easily to regulate the qi and improve one’s condition." — Kohn, Chinese Healing Exercises, printed p. 102, quoting Daoyin jing 16b ([owner's Drive copy](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1WPlkEDK5FTR9LkFGrutj1YCqIDZ9dMx_))
