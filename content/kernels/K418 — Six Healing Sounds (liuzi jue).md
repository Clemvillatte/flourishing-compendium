> **Kernel K418** — Six Healing Sounds (liuzi jue)

The liuzi jue (六子訣, as Kohn prints the term), the Six Healing Sounds, is a set of six exhalation patterns from the daoyin tradition. Each sound is made on the out-breath and, in the traditional correspondences, matched to one organ: he for the heart, hu for the spleen, xu for the liver, chui for the kidneys, si for the lungs, and xi for the Triple Heater, the organ of digestion and energy processing in that system. It appears in early texts such as the Daoyin jing; in Kohn's assessment, "still forms an important part of qigong practice today" ([Kohn](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1WPlkEDK5FTR9LkFGrutj1YCqIDZ9dMx_)).

### Protocol

The sources below are layered: Kohn's historical descriptions, the modern form as separate studies describe it, and one general framework. The Association's manual was not opened; each passage cites its carrier.

#### Historical breathing descriptions

From Kohn's account of the early texts, the six breaths differ in sound quality: chui, a sharp expulsion with the lips almost closed, warming and relaxing; xu, gentle with the mouth wide open, air from the bottom of the lungs, cooling; hu, the mildest, with rounded lips; si, a gentle release between slightly opened lips; he, a strong breath through the open mouth with a rasp in the throat; xi, a soft sigh from deep in the body. The Yinshu's seasonal selection: spring, xu; summer, hu; fall and winter, chui — each several times, plus once with the other two (Kohn, printed p. 57).

#### General practice framework

A peer-reviewed description: "one can either stand or kneel and calm the mind, breathing peacefully into the cinnabar field (Dan Tian; the navel), and then apply one of the six sounds, or all of them in a specific sequence" ([Shi 2020](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/2158244020927024)). The sound is voiced softly or silently; exhalation "is either equal to or longer than that of the inhalation... not prolonged by force."

#### Published study versions

The modern form was standardized under China's General Administration of Sport. The passages below are separate study descriptions, not one prescription; the structure, from a COPD protocol:

> "The routine consists of eight components: a preparatory posture, six-character vocalizations ("xu," "he," "hu," "si," "chui," and "xi"), and a closing posture." — [Ni et al. 2026, JMIR Research Protocols](https://www.researchprotocols.org/2026/1/e91283)

The protocol gives the coordination: "inhaling during the upward movement of the upper limbs and exhaling during their descent", movements "slowly, continuously, smoothly, and with lightness"; a round takes "approximately 15 minutes". An EEG study's description, citing the 2007 manual:

> "Each sound is performed within a specific movement routine without moving the feet, with each sound and movement routine repeated six times. Inverse abdominal respiration is required while performing the practice, as well as producing the six sounds respectively during exhaling. While inhaling with lips closed and tongue reaching the palate, one should breath naturally through the nose and make the abdomen uplifted (Chinese Health Qigong Association, 2007)." — [Henz & Schöllhorn 2018, Frontiers in Psychology](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2017.02291/full)

A COVID-19 trial's implementation of that version spells out the modules:

> "(a) Stand with feet open at the same width as the shoulders, put your hands on the sides of the waist with palms up, stretch out the right hand toward the left front by 45°, say "Xu," and then return. Complete the mirror action with the other hand. (b) Bend your knees, raise your forearms, fold your palm down, press down, say "He," and then return. (c)Place your hands in front of the abdomen, rotate the palms inward, stretch forward with a circular trajectory, say "Hu," and then return. (4) Lift your hands naturally to your chest, raise your palms to push forward, say "Si," and finally return. (5) Place your hands in front of the abdomen, wrap your hands around your sides, press down slowly, say "Chui," and finally return. (6) Place your hands in front of your abdomen, lift your hands up to the top of your head, open your arms, pull back, press down, say "Xi," and finally return. Each set of actions was repeated three times before the next set of actions was performed." — [Zhu et al. 2022, Journal of Traditional Chinese Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9924758/)

Repetitions differ: three per sound in that trial, six in the EEG study and a Parkinson's RCT ([Yin 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11843974/)).

### Evidence

Kohn presents the organ associations as traditional Chinese medicine correspondence: a "detailed medical method to treat various imbalances of qi associated with the five inner organs and the Triple Heater". The modern form has since served as the intervention in randomized trials and protocols (COVID-19, Parkinson's, COPD; citations above), which report their own outcomes. Henz & Schöllhorn report EEG findings: alpha activity decreased after 15 minutes and increased after 30 minutes of practice. Effect-size reporting for those trials is unfinished here. The classical closing promise:

> "Each of the six breaths—he, si, hu, xu, chui, and xi—is ruled by one of the five organs. In case of extreme fatigue, one can use them easily to regulate the qi and improve one’s condition." — Kohn, Chinese Healing Exercises, printed p. 102, quoting Daoyin jing 16b ([owner's Drive copy](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1WPlkEDK5FTR9LkFGrutj1YCqIDZ9dMx_))
