> **Kernel K134** — Two-truths drill

Argue the strongest case for your position. Then argue the strongest case against it. The exercise reveals that both positions have something to say, which loosens the grip of either.

### Protocol

*Steps are the compendium's editorial protocol; the corrective effect is documented in the consider-the-opposite research below.*

1. Write the strongest case for your current position, in its own best voice, as though you believed it.
2. Write the strongest case against it, with the same effort; a strawman opposite is a wasted repetition of what you already think.
3. Ask what each side sees that the other misses; the answer is usually the useful output of the drill.

### Evidence

The empirical ground is the consider-the-opposite strategy: "individuals who are induced to consider the opposite, therefore, should display less bias in social judgment," and in two experiments "the induction of a consider-the-opposite strategy had greater corrective effect than more demand-laden alternative instructions to be as fair and unbiased as possible" ([Lord, Lepper & Preston, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1984, abstract via PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6527215/)). Notably, being told to be objective did not reduce the bias; the structural act of constructing the opposing case did. What this study does not test: any effect on belief strength or well-being over time; the outcomes were evidence evaluation and attitude polarisation in a single session, and the two-truths framing is an editorial extension of the debiasing findings.
