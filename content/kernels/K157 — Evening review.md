> **Kernel K157** — Evening review

Three questions: "What did I do well? Where did I drift? What is tomorrow's one thing?" The review is not self-judgment but calibration.

### Protocol

*Steps are my own protocol; the effect of written end-of-day reflection is measured in the study below.*

1. Take the last quarter hour of the day; the timing at the end of the day is part of what makes the reflection work.
2. Answer the three questions in writing; the written form outperformed more practice in the field experiment.
3. Close with tomorrow's one thing, stated as a single sentence; a review that ends without a next action is an inventory.

### Evidence

The empirical ground is the reflection research: "employees who spent the last 15 minutes of each day of their training period writing and reflecting on what they had learned did 23% better in the final training test than other employees," and "'learning by doing' is more effective when coupled with deliberate reflection, or 'learning by thinking'" ([Di Stefano, Gino, Pisano & Staats, reported in *Harvard Business Review*, 2014](https://hbr.org/2014/07/dear-diary); working paper 14-093, Harvard Business School). The authors define reflection as "the attempt to synthesize, abstract, and articulate the key lessons taught by an experience," and found the performance effect mediated by increased self-efficacy. What this study does not test: the three-question format, or any effect on well-being; the outcome was a training test score, and the three questions are editorial.

Not applicable: the review is a written reflection, which is its own record.
