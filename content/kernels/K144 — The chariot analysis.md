> **Kernel K144** — The chariot analysis

The chariot analysis is a way of examining a self-image by taking it apart. It comes from the Questions of Milinda, where the monk Nāgasena asks King Milinda to point to the chariot among its parts, the pole, the axle, the wheels, the framework, and Milinda cannot. Reach for it when a self-image starts speaking as the whole self, the wounded child, the achiever, the critic.

### Protocol

Steps 1 to 3 follow the examination Nāgasena runs in the opened passage; step 4 is the compendium's entry point for daily use.

1. Name the self-image that is speaking: the wounded child, the achiever, the protector, the critic. Name it as a voice, not as yourself.
2. Run the chariot examination on it. Ask of each part: is this feeling the "I" that is speaking? Is this thought? This memory? This role? Answer each as Milinda answered, no.
3. Ask the two closing questions: is the "I" all these parts together? Is it something outside them? The honest answer to both, in the dialogue as in the practice, is no.
4. Land where Milinda landed. The self-image is a working label, a designation in common use, useful for ordering a life and not findable in any single part. The voice loses its claim to speak as the whole self because the whole self was never in the voice.

### Evidence

Milinda's own conclusion, from the opened translation:

> "I have spoken no untruth, reverend Sir. It is on account of its having all these things—the pole, and the axle, the wheels, and the framework, the ropes, the yoke, the spokes, and the goad—that it comes under the generally understood term, the designation in common use, of 'chariot.'" (Milindapanha 3.1.1, [Rhys Davids translation, SuttaCentral](https://suttacentral.net/mil3.1.1/en/tw_rhysdavids))

Nāgasena then applies the same reasoning to the person:

> "And just even so it is on account of all those things you questioned me about—The thirty-two kinds of organic matter in a human body, and the five constituent elements of being—that I come under the generally understood term, the designation in common use, of 'Nāgasena.'" (Milindapanha 3.1.1)

The nearest experimental literature is on self-distanced reasoning rather than on the chariot examination itself. In two experiments, cueing people to reason about personally meaningful issues from a distanced perspective "enhances wise reasoning (dialecticism; intellectual humility), attitudes (cooperation-related attitude assimilation), and behavior (willingness to join a bipartisan group)" (Abstract, [Kross & Grossmann, 2012, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General 141(1), 43-48](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21728454/)). What these studies do not test: the part-by-part examination of a self-image. They test perspective on an issue, not the chariot questions, and the dialogue itself is a philosophical text, not a trial.

### A guided version

Not applicable: the practice is an examination run on one's own experience, not a recording.
