> **Kernel K144** — The chariot analysis

The chariot analysis is a way of examining a self-image by taking it apart. It comes from the Questions of Milinda, where the monk Nāgasena asks King Milinda to point to the chariot among its parts, the pole, the axle, the wheels, the framework, and Milinda cannot. This page is an adaptation of that examination for everyday use: when a self-image starts speaking as the whole self, the wounded child, the achiever, the critic, the analysis gives it the chariot treatment.

### Protocol

The steps are my everyday adaptation of the dialogue; the dialogue's own moves are quoted in the Evidence section.

1. Name the self-image that is speaking, and name it as a voice rather than as yourself.
2. Take it apart as Nāgasena takes the chariot apart: ask whether the "I" now speaking is this feeling, this thought, this memory, this role. The dialogue asks such questions one part at a time and does not tell the questioner what to answer.
3. Ask the two closing questions the dialogue asks: is the "I" all these parts together, and is it something outside them? In the dialogue, Milinda answers no to both.
4. Close where the dialogue closes. The person, like the chariot, comes under "the generally understood term, the designation in common use": a working label for the parts working together, not an essence hiding in any single part.

### Evidence

Milinda's own conclusion, from the Rhys Davids translation:

> "I have spoken no untruth, reverend Sir. It is on account of its having all these things—the pole, and the axle, the wheels, and the framework, the ropes, the yoke, the spokes, and the goad—that it comes under the generally understood term, the designation in common use, of 'chariot.'" (Milindapanha 3.1.1, [Rhys Davids translation, SuttaCentral](https://suttacentral.net/mil3.1.1/en/tw_rhysdavids))

Nāgasena then applies the same reasoning to the person:

> "And just even so it is on account of all those things you questioned me about—The thirty-two kinds of organic matter in a human body, and the five constituent elements of being—that I come under the generally understood term, the designation in common use, of 'Nāgasena.'" (Milindapanha 3.1.1)

A separate comparison is research on self-distanced reasoning rather than on the chariot examination itself. In two experiments, cueing people to reason about personally meaningful issues from a distanced perspective "enhances wise reasoning (dialecticism; intellectual humility), attitudes (cooperation-related attitude assimilation), and behavior (willingness to join a bipartisan group)" (Abstract, [Kross & Grossmann, 2012, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General 141(1), 43-48](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21728454/)). What these studies do not test: the part-by-part examination of a self-image. They test perspective on an issue, not the chariot questions, and the dialogue itself is a philosophical text, not a trial.
