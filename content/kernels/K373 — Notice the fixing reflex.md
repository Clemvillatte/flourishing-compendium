> **Kernel K373** — Notice the fixing reflex

When someone tells you about a problem, notice the urge that arrives first: the urge to *fix it*. Give advice, reassure, solve, say "Have you tried…?" The third edition of Miller & Rollnick's book calls this the righting reflex — well-meant, and a common reason conversations go in circles.

### Protocol

The practice: when you feel the urge to fix, pause and check — did they ask for a solution, or are they telling you how it is? If you're not sure, you can ask: "Do you want ideas, or do you want me to just listen?"

The passage quoted below is the third edition's account of the reflex; the explicit check-in question is an addition, not Miller & Rollnick's own.

### Evidence

The reflex, in the book's own description:

> "these very same motives can lead to the overuse of a directing style in an ineffective or even counterproductive way when the task is helping people to change. Helpers want to help, to set things right, to get people on the road to health and wellness. Seeing people head down a wrong path stimulates a natural desire to get out in front of them and say, “Stop! Go back! Don’t you see? There is a better way over there!,” and it is done with the best of intentions, with one’s heart in the right place."
> — [Miller and Rollnick, third edition, PDF](https://ia800603.us.archive.org/29/items/applications-of-motivational-interviewing-william-r.-miller-stephen-rollnick-mot/%28Applications%20of%20Motivational%20Interviewing%29%20William%20R.%20Miller,%20Stephen%20Rollnick%20-%20Motivational%20Interviewing,%20Third%20Edition_%20Helping%20People%20Change-The%20Guilford%20Press%20%282012%29.pdf)

The kernel title says "fixing"; the opened third edition prints "righting reflex" for the same pattern.

What the sources do not test: the passage describes the reflex; it does not evaluate the noticing practice on this page.
