> **Kernel K237** — See-the-small practice

A weekly scan for the earliest signal of an emerging problem. The name comes from the Daodejing's fifty-second chapter, where seeing the small is named the secret of clear-sightedness. It suits the weeks when everything is technically fine and a few small brittles can still be felt: a first skipped session, a task that has slipped twice, a slight stiffness in a conversation.

### Protocol

The steps are my weekly adaptation; the lines they lean on are quoted in the Evidence section.

1. At the end of a week, list what is not yet a problem: the things that are brittle, small, or resting. The text's own examples of what is easy to act on are exactly these.
2. For each, ask whether it can still be perceived while it is small. Name it plainly, at its current size rather than its feared size.
3. Choose one and take the small measure now, before the thing has made its appearance as a problem. The order of operations is the text's: act first, secure order before disorder begins.
4. The weekly rhythm and the framing of the scan are my. The text's claim is observational, that difficulties arise from a previous state in which they were easy, not a result from a trial.

### Evidence

The lines behind the practice, from James Legge's translation:

> "The perception of what is small is (the secret of) clear-sightedness; the guarding of what is soft and tender is (the secret of) strength." (Daodejing 52, [Legge translation, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/216/pg216.txt))

> "That which is at rest is easily kept hold of; before a thing has given indications of its presence, it is easy to take measures against it; that which is brittle is easily broken; that which is very small is easily dispersed. Action should be taken before a thing has made its appearance; order should be secured before disorder has begun." (Daodejing 64, same translation)

> "(The master of it) anticipates things that are difficult while they are easy, and does things that would become great while they are small. All difficult things in the world are sure to arise from a previous state in which they were easy, and all great things from one in which they were small." (Daodejing 63, same translation)

What this evidence does not test: the weekly scan as an intervention. The Daodejing is a classical wisdom text, and the page's claim that intervention is cheapest at the weak signal is editorial framing, not a finding from any study opened here.
