> **Kernel K332** — Life chapter mapping

Divide your life into chapters: childhood, adolescence, early adulthood, and so on. For each chapter, write one sentence about the turning point and one about what it taught. The map builds narrative coherence, which research (McAdams) shows predicts well-being and generativity. The exercise is not therapy. It is the structured attention to the shape of one's life.

### Protocol

The map is one sentence per chapter, twice; the experiment on shaping a life into story is quoted below. Intended focus: Meaning and Self.

1. Divide your life into chapters with the titles you would actually use: childhood, adolescence, early adulthood, or your own names for them; the divisions should fit your life, not the template's.
2. For each chapter write exactly two sentences: one on the turning point, one on what it taught. Keep both concrete; a chapter whose turning point cannot be named is a chapter still waiting for its title.
3. Step back and read the whole map once, as a map; notice the shape, the recurrences, the direction of travel. Revise a chapter title if the reading shows one that does not hold, and then leave the map alone; it is a document to revisit, not to polish nightly.

### Evidence

From the eight studies on the Hero's Journey and meaning:

> "Eight studies reveal that the Hero's Journey predicts and can causally increase people's experience of meaning in life." (Abstract, [Rogers, Chicas, Kelly, Kubin, Christian, Kachanoff, Berger, Puryear, McAdams & Gray, 2023, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 125(4), 752-778](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36972106/))

What this evidence does not test: the chapter map. The research distilled a seven-element story template, developed a measure of it, and found that seeing one's life through it predicted felt meaning, and that an intervention prompting people to reflect on the important elements of their lives and connect them into a coherent narrative causally increased meaning. The template studied was the Hero's Journey, not childhood-to-now chapters, and the outcome was meaning in life, not well-being or generativity, the outcomes the kernel's McAdams citation gestures at; the abstract reports nothing about turning points or the two-sentence form. What it supports is the kernel's core mechanism: the connection of life's elements into a coherent narrative can be trained, and the training moves the felt meaning of the life it describes.
