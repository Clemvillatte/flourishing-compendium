> **Kernel K339** — The third story

In a conflict, describe the situation from the perspective of a neutral third party who wants the best for everyone involved. What would they see? What would they say? The exercise is not about finding a compromise. It is about generating a perspective that neither party can see from their own position.

### Protocol

The story is written from a chair nobody is sitting in; the experiment behind the chair is quoted below. Intended focus: Self and Connection.

1. Take one live conflict and write the situation as a neutral third party would see it, a narrator who wants the best for everyone involved and is standing in neither position.
2. Give the third party a voice in the story: what they would notice that neither of you can see from inside, and what they would say to both of you. The voice is not a referee and its job is not compromise; it is the wider view neither position contains.
3. Read the story back once, then return to your own position and decide from there, carrying what the third party saw. The story is written, not sent; it is a lens, not a mediation.

### Evidence

From the experiments on self-distancing and wise reasoning:

> "Across Studies 2 and 3, instructing individuals to self-distance (rather than self-immerse) eliminated this asymmetry." (Abstract, [Grossmann & Kross, 2014, Psychological Science 25(8), 1571-1580](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24916084/))

What this evidence does not test: the story. In three experiments with 693 participants, people reasoned more wisely about other people's conflicts than about their own, recognizing limits of knowledge, the importance of compromise and future change, and other people's perspectives; instructing them to self-distance while reflecting on their own problem closed the gap, with the effect holding for younger and older adults alike. The instruction was a shift in perspective during reflection, not the writing of a third-party narrative, and the abstract reports nothing about conflicts between actual parties, about compromise, or about what a narrator would see. What it supports is the kernel's premise: the view from neither position is available on purpose, and taking it measurably changes the quality of the reasoning.
