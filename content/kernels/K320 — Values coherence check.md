> **Kernel K320** — Values coherence check

Monthly, review whether the way you are spending your time aligns with what you say you value. Where does your calendar contradict your stated priorities? The check is distinct from the Volume I meaning audit in that it targets the gap between stated values and lived values, not the felt quality of activities. Research on value congruence shows that alignment between stated and lived values predicts well-being more strongly than the content of the values themselves. The research version is the self-concordance model: goals are weighed by "their consistency with the person's developing interests and core values", and "those who attain self-concordant goals reap greater well-being benefits from their attainment" ([Sheldon and Elliot, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 1999](https://www.scribd.com/document/904038670/sheldon-and-elliot-1999)). Dimensions: cognition (the calendar read against the list) and motivation (the energy that follows alignment).

### Protocol

1. Generate the two columns: your three stated top values on the left, and last month's actual hours on the right. The calendar, the phone's screen-time report, and the bank statement are the honest witnesses; memory is not.
2. Select the contradictions: circle each place where the hours say something other than the values do. Two or three circles are enough; a full audit becomes a project of its own.
3. For each circle, name which side is wrong: the value is stated and not held, or it is held and not scheduled. The two corrections are different, one requiring honesty about the value, the other requiring a decision about the time.
4. Retain one change per month: a rescheduled block that puts a value into the calendar, or a struck-out value that was decoration. Seneca's warning stands behind the monthly repetition: "the greatest waste of life lies in postponement: it robs us of each day in turn, and snatches away the present by promising the future" ([Seneca, On the Shortness of Life](https://archive.org/stream/SenecaOnTheShortnessOfLife/Seneca%20on%20the%20Shortness%20of%20Life_djvu.txt)).

### Evidence

Seneca's essay is the oldest form of the check: "it's not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste much of it", and "the life we are given isn't short but we make it so; we're not ill provided but we are wasteful of life" ([Seneca, De Brevitate Vitae, Williams trans.](https://archive.org/stream/SenecaOnTheShortnessOfLife/Seneca%20on%20the%20Shortness%20of%20Life_djvu.txt)). The modern evidence is Sheldon and Elliot's: "the self-concordance of goals (i.e., their consistency with the person's developing interests and core values) plays a dual role", since "those pursuing self-concordant goals put more sustained effort into achieving those goals and thus are more likely to attain them", and attainment pays better in well-being when the goal was yours ([Sheldon and Elliot 1999](https://www.scribd.com/document/904038670/sheldon-and-elliot-1999)). Scope limits: the model was built on personal-goal lists in longitudinal student samples, it concerns goals rather than stated values in general, and no study tests the monthly calendar procedure itself; the claim the evidence supports is that alignment pays, which the check assumes rather than proves.
