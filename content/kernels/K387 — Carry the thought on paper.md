> **Kernel K387** — Carry the thought on paper

Carry the thought on paper is a self-led acceptance exercise I attribute to David F. Tolin's Doing CBT (2nd ed., 2024), chapter 15, "Leaving Thoughts Alone" ([publisher's contents page](https://www.guilford.com/books/Doing-CBT/David-Tolin/9781462554126/contents)). You write down a negative recurring thought, fold the paper, and keep it in your pocket for the rest of the day. The slip stands in for the thought: something you can notice, touch, and carry while the day goes on. You reach for this when a thought keeps returning and the pull is to argue with it or push it away.

### Protocol

The exercise passage attributed to the book's printed pp. 401-402 has not yet been verified; the steps below are my adaptation, and the example thought is illustrative.

1. Write the recurring negative thought on a slip of paper, word for word as it shows up (an illustrative example: "I'm going to fail miserably").
2. Fold the paper and put it in your pocket. Carry it with you for the rest of the day.
3. When you notice the thought again, you can spend your energy arguing with the slip, trying to forget it is there, or blaming yourself for thinking it. Or you can take the second option: OK, I have this thought. No big deal. Let me get on with my day.

The point is the second half: the thought gets to exist, folded in your pocket, while you get on with the day.

### Evidence

The studies below did not test this pocket exercise. They compare acceptance and suppression strategies in laboratory tasks, and their findings do not establish the effectiveness of a paper-and-pocket exercise. In a controlled comparison of thought-management strategies, participants who suppressed their personal intrusive thoughts "were unable to do so and experienced an increased level of distress after suppression, whereas those instructed to use an acceptance-based strategy experienced a decrease in discomfort level (but not thought frequency)" ([Marcks & Woods, 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15701355/), abstract).

In a study with twenty OCD patients and twenty healthy controls, "Distress after suppression (M = 4.60) was greater than after focused distraction (M = 2.30), t(19) = 5.51, p < .001, and after acceptance (M = 1.85), t(19) = 5.93, p < .001" in the OCD group, and the authors report support for the claim that "suppression is a counterproductive technique for dealing with unwanted, intrusive thoughts in OCD" ([Najmi, Riemann & Wegner, 2009](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/DANWEGNER/pub/Najmi,%20Riemann,%20&%20Wegner%20(2009).pdf), abstract and results, printed pp. 499-500). The reports quoted here describe distress and thought frequency during mental-control tasks; they do not describe any written or carried-thought component.

### A guided version

Self-led. The exercise needs only a pen, a slip of paper, and a pocket.
