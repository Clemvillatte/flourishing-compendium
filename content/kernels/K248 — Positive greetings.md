> **Kernel K248** — Positive greetings

Greet the people in your daily environment warmly: neighbors, colleagues, the person at the counter, strangers on the platform. The practice is small on purpose. The research behind it comes from the study of weak ties, and from experiments on talking to strangers on transit.

## Protocol

1. Pick the targets. People you pass daily and greet rarely: the neighbor, the barista, the colleague from the other floor.
2. Greet with the study's three parts. In the barista experiment, the social condition instructed participants to "have a genuine interaction with the cashier – smile, make eye contact to establish a connection, and have a brief conversation" ([Sandstrom, 2013](https://open.library.ubc.ca/media/stream/pdf/24/1.0074024/1), Study 5).
3. For a longer stop, use the commuters' instruction: "Please have a conversation with a new person on the train today. Try to make a connection. Find out something interesting about him or her and tell them something about you. The longer the conversation, the better. Your goal is to try to get to know your community neighbor this morning." ([Epley and Schroeder, 2014](https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/5c484e0f4aa6f839dc553c45/5c9540a7e291048443c0c276_EpleySchroederJEPG2014.pdf))
4. Keep it genuine, not performed. The instruction is "genuine interaction"; the greeting should be one you mean, which is why the targets are people you truly encounter.
5. Expect the first attempts to feel odd. The commuters' expectations pointed the wrong way, and yours may too.

## A guided version

The greeting happens with the other person, face to face; no guided recording is cited for it here.

## Evidence

The barista experiment randomized customers to be social or efficient: "Compared to those in the efficient condition, participants in the social condition reported significantly higher positive affect, F(1, 56) = 9.28, p = .004, ηp² = .14, significantly lower negative affect, F(1, 56) = 4.45, p = .04, ηp² = .07" ([Sandstrom, 2013](https://open.library.ubc.ca/media/stream/pdf/24/1.0074024/1), Study 5). The commuter experiments found that talking to a stranger beat sitting alone: "participants in the connection condition reported having a significantly more positive experience than participants in the solitude condition, t(94) = 2.49, p = .02, d = 0.63" (Experiment 1a, train), and on the bus "a significantly more positive experience than participants in the solitude condition, t(72) = 2.14, p = .03, d = 0.56" (Experiment 2a) ([Epley and Schroeder, 2014](https://uploads-ssl.webflow.com/5c484e0f4aa6f839dc553c45/5c9540a7e291048443c0c276_EpleySchroederJEPG2014.pdf)). In the authors' words, "Human beings are social animals. Those who misunderstand the consequences of social interactions may not, in at least some contexts, be social enough for their own well-being" (same article).

What the research does not cover: neither study tested greetings as such. The barista study tested brief conversations at a counter; the commuter studies tested whole conversations. A ten-day instruction to increase weak-tie interactions produced "increased flourishing over time" and a marginal drop in loneliness, but "Neither people in the strong tie condition nor people in the weak tie condition showed broad, sustained changes in subjective well-being or belonging" (Sandstrom, 2013, Study 6). Greeting neighbors on purpose sits between them.
