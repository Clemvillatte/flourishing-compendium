> **Kernel K280** — Gratitude for sufficiency

Each evening, name one thing you already have that is enough. Not a new thing you are grateful for, but a thing you already possess whose sufficiency you normally overlook. The practice is not the same as gratitude journaling (which has mixed evidence). It targets the specific mechanism of desire: the tendency to take what is sufficient for granted while pursuing what is not. The classic journaling study itself reported the pattern this kernel leans on: participants who listed blessings "exhibited heightened well-being across several, though not all, of the outcome measures across the 3 studies, relative to the comparison groups", with "the effect on positive affect" as "the most robust finding" ([Emmons and McCullough, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 2003](https://emmons.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/90/2015/08/2003_2-Emmons_McCullough_2003_JPSP.pdf)). Dimensions: attention (turned toward what is already present) and affect (the small lift the naming gives the evening).

### Protocol

1. Generate the naming: each evening, one thing you already have that is enough today. The bed, the working kettle, the friend who answered, the body that carried you up the stairs. Already-owned only; the rule is what makes this different from a wish list read backwards.
2. Select for overlookedness: prefer things whose sufficiency you have not noticed in weeks. The point is not to feel gratitude for a favor but to see a standing condition.
3. Name it in one sentence with the reason it is enough: "the kettle is enough because it boils water for two without complaint." The reason is the practice; the noun alone is a list item.
4. Retain the record loosely: a line in a notebook, no review required. Reread it when a purchase or a pursuit starts to feel urgent; the record is the counterweight, not the cure.

### Evidence

The caution about mixed evidence is documented. The original experiment: "in Studies 1 and 2, participants were randomly assigned to 1 of 3 experimental conditions (hassles, gratitude listing, and either neutral life events or social comparison)", and "results suggest that a conscious focus on blessings may have emotional and interpersonal benefits" ([Emmons and McCullough 2003](https://emmons.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/90/2015/08/2003_2-Emmons_McCullough_2003_JPSP.pdf)). The later quantitative picture is smaller: a meta-analysis of 27 studies with 3,675 participants found that "gratitude interventions had a small effect on symptoms of depression and anxiety at both post-test (g = − 0.29, SE = 0.06, p < .01) and follow-up (g = − 0.23, SE = 0.06, p < .01)", concluding the effects "are relatively modest" and recommending "interventions with stronger evidence of efficacy for these symptoms" ([Cregg and Cheavens, Journal of Happiness Studies 2021](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10902-020-00236-6.pdf)). Scope limits: the sources cited here do not test this kernel's sufficiency variant separately from gratitude journaling, and neither study speaks to desire or consumption outcomes; the claim here is only that the attention is cheap, that the studied gratitude interventions show small real effects on mood, and that the aim is calibration rather than therapy.
