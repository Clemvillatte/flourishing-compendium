> **Kernel K336** — Contamination sequence audit

Review a recent disappointment and check how you have been telling it. If the telling is a contamination sequence, a good beginning ruined by a bad ending that spreads backward over everything before it, re-audit the facts: what was good in it that the bad ending did not erase? The audit tests whether your narrative habit is amplifying loss. Reach for it when a disappointment has started to eat its own past.

### Protocol

The practice moves the Cognition and Self dimensions: the story's structure is inspected, and the self that lives inside the story gets a fairer one.

1. Write the disappointment as you have been telling it, in three sentences, without editing the gloom. The current version must be on the table before it can be audited.
2. Mark the structure: does the bad ending contaminate the good beginning, or merely follow it? Editorial addition, not from the carriers: contamination is a narrative structure, not a moral one; the audit asks what the story does, not whether you are allowed to grieve.
3. Write one sentence of what was good that remains fact: the good months before the layoff, the years of the friendship before the betrayal. Keep the loss; withdraw the story's license to erase it.

### Evidence

The finding behind the audit, from a two-study longitudinal investigation of narrative identity and mental health over several years:

> "Concurrent analyses indicated that the themes of agency, redemption, and contamination were significantly associated with mental health. Longitudinal analyses indicated that these same 3 themes were significantly associated with participants' trajectories of mental health over the course of 4 years."
> — [Adler et al. 2015, *JPSP* 108(3), PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25751718/)

What the cited study does not test: these are associations between narrated themes and mental-health trajectories, not evidence that rewriting a story improves anything; no study tests this page's three-sentence audit, and the page claims no measured effect for it.

### A guided version

No official recording of the audit exists. A different, optional exercise sits one step beyond it: the [narrative identity revision practice](https://www.ixcoach.com/practices/the-life-crafting-intervention/narrative-identity-revision) has you identify one limiting story ("I'm not the kind of person who ___") and rewrite the same events with a redemption frame ("This happened, and what it eventually gave me was ___"), locating one piece of genuine evidence so the revision stays honest. It is not this audit: recovering what was good before a bad ending makes no claim that the ending gave you anything.
