> **Kernel K406** — Compassionate letter to yourself

Write a letter to yourself about a difficulty you are carrying — written from the perspective of someone who understands your circumstances and wants to help: someone with kindness, patience, and courage to spare. Acknowledge what hurts. Include why your response makes sense given what you have lived through. Include, plainly, what would help.

Take days if you need them; the letter improves with a few sittings. Write about the difficulty without dramatising or minimising it.

Then keep the letter and reread it in the moments you would normally turn on yourself. The book suggests writing multiple letters to support yourself in different situations, and keeping them where the hardest moments find them.

Adapted from Kolts, CFT Made Simple, ch. 9, pp. 124–126 ([owner's Drive copy](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1uN4kJ9GGbWVIlzNAxeOPsO9w6VYJr-iP)); Kolts credits the exercise to Gilbert (2009, 2010).

### Protocol

1. Pen and paper, then a minute of soothing rhythm breathing. Shift into the compassionate-self perspective before writing; the book's entering image:

> "Now try to shift into the perspective of your compassionate self. Connect with your compassionate self, imagining yourself at your best—your calmest, your wisest, your most caring, your most confident and courageous." — Kolts, CFT Made Simple, ch. 9, p. 125 ([owner's Drive copy](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1uN4kJ9GGbWVIlzNAxeOPsO9w6VYJr-iP))

2. Write about one difficulty from that perspective, starting from what is true. The book's sentence starters: "I am sad, and I feel distress. My distress is understandable because…" and then "I would like myself to know that…" ([Kolts, ch. 9, p. 125](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1uN4kJ9GGbWVIlzNAxeOPsO9w6VYJr-iP)). Target dimensions: Self (who is writing) and Affect (warmth toward your own distress).
3. Treat self-doubt (Am I doing it right? I'm not really feeling it) as normal comments the mind makes, and keep writing; "There is no right or wrong…you're just practicing, working with your compassionate self." ([Kolts, ch. 9, p. 125](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1uN4kJ9GGbWVIlzNAxeOPsO9w6VYJr-iP))
4. After a few sittings, reread and check the letter against the book's qualities: concern and encouragement, sensitivity to your distress, help facing feelings, understanding of your difficulties, non-judgment, genuine warmth.
5. Keep the letter where hard moments find it; write others for different self-critical voices, and reread them in the moments you would normally turn on yourself.

### Evidence

The book's rationale for the format:

> "this exercise produces a product—the compassionate letter—that can be read again and again in those moments when the client needs compassionate support, encouragement, and coaching." — Kolts, CFT Made Simple, ch. 9, p. 124 ([owner's Drive copy](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1uN4kJ9GGbWVIlzNAxeOPsO9w6VYJr-iP))

Kolts grounds the exercise in clinical rationale and the Gilbert (2009, 2010) works he names; he cites no trial of the letter exercise itself.
