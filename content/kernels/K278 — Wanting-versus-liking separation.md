> **Kernel K278** — Wanting-versus-liking separation

When you want something, ask: will I actually enjoy this, or do I just want it? The distinction between wanting (dopaminergic motivation) and liking (hedonic pleasure) is one of the most robust findings in affective neuroscience. Many things we want intensely give little pleasure once obtained. The question interrupts the automatic link between desire and pursuit. The neuroscience behind it, in Berridge and Robinson's summary: "the brain circuitry that mediates the psychological process of 'wanting' a particular reward is dissociable from circuitry that mediates the degree to which it is 'liked'" ([Berridge and Robinson, American Psychologist 2016](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/berridge-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/743/2019/10/2016-Berridge-Robinson-Liking-wanting-IS-theory-of-addiction-Am-Psychol.pdf)). Dimensions: affect (the pull of the want and the pleasure of the liking) and cognition (the comparison the question forces between them).

### Protocol

1. Generate the question at the moment of pull: when reaching for the phone, the drink, the purchase, the extra helping, pause and ask which of the two is present, the wanting or the liking.
2. Select the split. Name, honestly, how much you want it now (the pull), and how much you expect to like it when you have it (the pleasure), on a scale of ten each. The gap between the two numbers is the working data.
3. Retain the record. After obtaining it, rate the actual liking. Over a few weeks the log shows which wants reliably convert to liking and which only promised to.
4. Use the record alongside your values and longer-term aims: for wants that never convert to liking, either skip the pursuit or take the smallest version that satisfies the genuine part of the pull.

### Evidence

The neural claim is quoted directly: "incentive salience or 'wanting', a form of motivation, is generated by large and robust neural systems that include mesolimbic dopamine. By comparison, 'liking', or the actual pleasurable impact of reward consumption, is mediated by smaller and fragile neural systems, and is not dependent on dopamine" ([Berridge and Robinson 2016](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/berridge-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/743/2019/10/2016-Berridge-Robinson-Liking-wanting-IS-theory-of-addiction-Am-Psychol.pdf)). The clinical extreme makes the practical point vivid: in addiction, "sensitized 'wanting' can persist for years, even if the person cognitively does not want to take drugs, does not expect the drugs to be very pleasant, and even long after withdrawal symptoms have subsided" ([Berridge and Robinson 2016](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/berridge-lab/wp-content/uploads/sites/743/2019/10/2016-Berridge-Robinson-Liking-wanting-IS-theory-of-addiction-Am-Psychol.pdf)). Scope limits: the dissociation is best documented for cue-triggered, dopaminergic wanting, and the ten-point log is a reflection aid, not a measure of dopamine activity, nor a protocol evaluated in this research.
