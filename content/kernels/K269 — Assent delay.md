> **Kernel K269** — Assent delay

When a strong impression hits, an insult, a provocation, a piece of news, pause before giving it assent. The pause is the Stoic one: the impression prints itself on the mind, but the assent remains in our power ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Stoicism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/)). Reach for it in the moments when the response is already forming.

### What it rests on

Chrysippus's own image, as reported in the encyclopedia's account:

> "although the impression encountered will print and, as it were, emblazon its appearance on the mind, assent will be in our power." — [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Stoicism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/)

And the faculty that makes the pause possible:

> "Arguably, the most important difference is the power of assent (sunkatathesis). Assent is a faculty found only in the rational soul – in particular, only in the ruling part or commanding faculty (hêgemonikon) of the rational soul – which enables us to adopt a critical stance towards our own impressions and pass judgment" — [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Stoicism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/)

The three-breath count is the compendium's editorial timing; the withholding it buys is the Stoic act.

### Protocol

The questions are the Stoic ones; the breath count is editorial.

1. When a strong impression arrives, count three breaths before responding to it.
2. In that gap, ask: is this impression accurate?
3. Ask: is the interpretation about to be given to it the only one possible?
4. Give or withhold assent on the answers, then respond.

### Evidence

The psychology nearest to the practice is response inhibition, as framed in the stop-signal literature:

> "Response inhibition is a hallmark of executive control. The concept refers to the suppression of actions that are no longer required or that are inappropriate, which supports flexible and goal-directed behavior in ever-changing environments." — [Verbruggen & Logan, 2008, Trends in Cognitive Sciences 12(11), Abstract](http://www.psy.vanderbilt.edu/faculty/logan/verbruggen_logan_2008_tics.pdf)

What this evidence does not test: the cited research studies laboratory stop-signal tasks, millisecond-scale suppression of button presses, not a three-breath assent delay; and the claim that brief deliberate delays reduce impulsive behaviour in daily life is not carried, because the consulted sources did not establish it.

### A guided version

Not applicable: the practice is a pause inside a moment, not a recording.
