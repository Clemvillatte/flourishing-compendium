> **Kernel K176** — Habit stacking

Anchor a new habit to an existing cue: "After I brush my teeth, I will sit for two minutes." The existing routine does the reminding, and the new habit rides on it. The recipe format comes from BJ Fogg's behavior design work, and the evidence base under if-then planning is the implementation-intention literature.

## Protocol

1. Pick the anchor. A routine you already do without fail, ideally at the same point each day: brushing teeth, pouring coffee, sitting down at the desk.
2. Write the recipe. Fogg's format, verbatim: "After I [existing anchor] , I will [new tiny behavior]". Pair the two: "In this process you will pair a tiny behavior with a good anchor" (tinyhabits.com, quoted verbatim).
3. Keep the new behavior tiny. Small enough that it survives your worst day. The two-minute sit in the example above is already near the upper limit.
4. Revise what fails. Fogg's page is blunt about the hit rate: "Some pairings work well, and the habit forms quickly and naturally. Some pairings don't work at all. But don't worry. If the pairing doesn't work, you revise. In other words, you just redesign the recipe" (quoted verbatim).
5. One new habit per anchor. Give the link a week or two of boring reliability before you stack anything else onto the same cue.

## A guided version

The recipe is written and revised, not listened to; no guided recording is cited for it here.

## Evidence

The if-then plan at the bottom of the recipe has a long research history. A meta-analysis of the implementation-intention literature reported that "Findings from 94 independent tests showed that implementation intentions had a positive effect of medium-to-large magnitude (d= .65) on goal attainment" (Gollwitzer and Sheeran, 2006, quoted verbatim). The plan works by spelling out "the when, where, and how of goal striving in advance ('If situation Y is encountered, then I will initiate goal-directed behavior X!')" (quoted verbatim), and the meta-analysis found the plans "were effective in promoting the initiation of goal striving, the shielding of ongoing goal pursuit from unwanted influences, disengagement from failing courses of action, and conservation of capability for future goal striving" (quoted verbatim).

What the research does not cover: the 94 tests measured if-then plans against goal attainment, not Fogg's anchor-recipe specifically and not long-run habit formation. The two-minute sit example is this practice's own; the meta-analysis is about goals from exercise to voting, not about stacking habits onto tooth-brushing.
