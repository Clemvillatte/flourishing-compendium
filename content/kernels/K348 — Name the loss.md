> **Kernel K348** — Name the loss

When you notice grief about environmental change, name it. "I am grieving the river I grew up near, which now runs dry in summer." The example sentence is this practice's own; the grief it names is documented in the ecological-grief literature, where mourning was a recurring theme in communities living through environmental change. What the naming itself does is less documented, and the page below stays inside that line.

## Protocol

1. Notice the grief where it sits: in the stomach at the dry creek, in the flatness at the felled street trees.
2. Write the sentence. One line, first person, specific: what is lost, and where. The river example above is the shape.
3. Say it to someone, or write it where it can be seen. The research draws on Judith Butler's reading of mourning: grief and mourning have "'we-creating' capacities, exposing our known, unknown and unacknowledged connections to others, and allowing for opportunities to reach across differences to connect with others" ([Cunsolo and Ellis, 2018](https://www.uwosh.edu/sirt/wp-content/uploads/sites/86/2020/04/Cunsolo-and-Ellis-2018.pdf), p. 276).
4. Let the questioning run. In the article's words, "grief and mourning can also question fundamental assumptions about what we choose to value — and what we choose to grieve and mourn — including climate change-induced ecological loss and degradation" (p. 276).
5. Keep the love in view. The article closes on Parkes and Prigerson's line: "The pain of grief is just as much a part of life as the joy of love; it is, perhaps, the price we pay for love, the cost of commitment" (p. 279).

## A guided version

The naming happens in your own words about your own places; no guided recording is cited for it here.

## Evidence

The grief is real and documented: in the communities the authors studied, "grief and mourning were prominent and recurring themes as people struggled to make sense of the environmental changes wrought upon their loved home environments" (p. 276). In Nunatsiavut, "This grief emerged from the loss of the ability to travel on the land and ice for food and for participating in health-sustaining land-based activities such as hunting and fishing, and deep concern around how to feed one's family and continue to live a traditional Inuit life" (p. 277). And the witnessing matters: "To bear witness to ecological losses personally, or to the suffering encountered by others as they bear their own losses, is to be reminded that climate change is not just an abstract scientific concept" (p. 279).

What the research does not cover: the article documents grief in affected communities; it does not test the effects of naming that grief, and the claim that naming motivates stewardship is not established by this source. The practice here rests on what the source does say: the grief is real, mourning connects, and it questions what we value.
