> **Kernel K173** — Creative hopelessness

When a coping strategy is not working, say so plainly: "This approach has cost me more than it has given me." The wording of that sentence is this practice's own; the move behind it comes from Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, which opens treatment by auditing the strategies you have been using and asking whether they work. The hopelessness is aimed at the strategy, not at your life.

## Protocol

1. List what you have tried. Write the strategies you have used to get rid of the unwanted feeling or thought: suppression, distraction, reassurance, avoidance, substances, rituals.
2. Ask the trial's question for each one. In a randomized trial of ACT for obsessive-compulsive disorder, participants were asked "what he or she had done to decrease the obsession, with particular attention paid to which strategies had worked to reduce obsessions and which had not" (Twohig et al., 2010, quoted verbatim).
3. Weigh short-term relief against long-term cost. The same trial noted that a compulsion "only worked for brief periods of time, and this process generally pulled the participant into a greater struggle with the obsessions" (quoted verbatim). Look for that shape in your own list: relief now, a bigger struggle later.
4. Say the plain sentence. "This approach has cost me more than it has given me" is the practice's own wording; adapt it until it is true and yours.
5. Leave the strategy on the table. The point is not despair; it is that a slot opens where a new approach can be tried.

## A guided version

The audit runs on your own list of strategies, in your own words; no guided recording is cited for it here.

## Evidence

In the trial above, ACT was compared with progressive relaxation training over eight sessions. ACT "produced greater changes at posttreatment and follow-up over PRT on OCD severity (Y-BOCS: ACT pre=24.22, post=12.76, follow-up=11.79; PRT pre=25.4, post=18.67, follow-up=16.23)," and clinically significant change "occurred more in the ACT condition than PRT using multiple criteria (clinical response rates: ACT post=46-56%, follow-up 46-66%; PRT post=13%-18%, follow-up 16-18%)" (both quotes verbatim). The treatment's opening move is described in the clinical literature this way: "The beginning stage of ACT typically involves attempts to draw out a sense of 'creative hopelessness' from the client, where the client's specific experiential avoidance strategies are articulated, and these strategies' short- and long-term effectiveness and effects on movement toward personal values are assessed" (Blackledge and Barnes-Holmes, quoted verbatim).

What the research does not cover: the trial tested an eight-session package delivered by therapists, of which this audit was one phase; it did not test a standalone self-administered dissection. The response rates describe OCD patients in treatment, not people doing this exercise on their own, and the sentence in step 4 is this practice's wording, not study material.
