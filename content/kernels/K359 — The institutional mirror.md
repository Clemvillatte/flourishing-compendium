> **Kernel K359** — The institutional mirror

Ask whether the institution you are part of practices what it preaches. Where does the gap between stated values and actual practice lie? The mirror is not about cynicism. It is about the Aristotelian insight that institutions, like persons, have characters, and that character is shown in action, not in mission statements. Organizational sociology gives the gap a dry name, decoupling, and a mechanism: "to maintain ceremonial conformity, organizations that reflect institutional rules tend to buffer their formal structures from the uncertainties of technical activities by becoming loosely coupled, building gaps between their formal structures and actual work activities" ([Meyer and Rowan 1977](http://www.iot.ntnu.no/innovation/norsi-pims-courses/harrison/Meyer%20&%20Rowan%20(1977).PDF)). Dimensions: cognition (the comparison made explicit) and overt behaviour (the question asked in the institution's own meetings).

### Protocol

1. Name the institution's three loudest stated values, in the words of its own mission statement or code of conduct.
2. Generate the evidence. For each stated value, write two concrete recent actions of the institution: what the last budget rewarded, who was promoted, what got punished, what a newcomer is told to do first.
3. Hold up the mirror. Place each value beside its actions and write one sentence on the gap or the match. Character is what recurs, not what is proclaimed.
4. Select the largest gap you can influence, and name one act that tests or narrows it: a question in a meeting, a line in a proposal, an item for the agenda. The mirror is finished when it changes something.
5. Retain the record. Repeat quarterly and keep the sentences; the drift of an institution's character shows only across repetitions.

### Evidence

The gap the mirror looks for is well documented. In Meyer and Rowan's account, formal structures function as myths that "gain legitimacy, resources, stability, and enhanced survival prospects" while the work goes on elsewhere; their Proposition 4 states that "elements of structure are decoupled from activities and from each other" ([Meyer and Rowan 1977](http://www.iot.ntnu.no/innovation/norsi-pims-courses/harrison/Meyer%20&%20Rowan%20(1977).PDF)). The Aristotelian side also has empirical company: Cameron's studies of organizational virtuousness scored institutions on "measures of compassion, integrity, forgiveness, trust, and optimism", and found that "organizations with higher virtuousness scores also had significantly higher productivity, quality outputs, and lower employee turnover", with virtuousness "mitigat[ing] the negative effects of downsizing" ([Cameron 2003](https://webuser.bus.umich.edu/cameronk/PDFs/POS/POS%20-%20CHAPTER%204.pdf)). The author states the caveats himself: "these data do not provide unequivocal evidence of an association", samples were small, and "causality is not implied". Virtuousness is also "not an all or nothing condition": neither persons nor institutions are fully virtuous or fully not, which is why the mirror compares values with actions rather than issuing verdicts.
