> **Kernel K347** — Story-before-statistic

When encountering a group different from your own, seek stories before statistics. Statistics describe; stories let you run the simulation. The page leans on a theoretical account of fiction, quoted below; it is a rationale, not a measured finding. Reach for it when a group has become a demographic in your mind.

### Protocol

The steps are my practice form; the finding the form leans on is quoted in the Evidence section.

1. Before reading another summary of a group's numbers, find one member's told story: an interview, a memoir, a documentary, a conversation.
2. Read or listen for the specifics the statistics abstract away: names, costs, choices, what a day weighs.
3. Return to the statistics afterward and notice what the story changed about how the numbers land. The pairing is the practice; neither element alone is.

### Evidence

From Mar and Oatley's account of the function of fiction:

> "Narrative fiction also creates a deep and immersive simulative experience of social interactions for readers. This simulation facilitates the communication and understanding of social information and makes it more compelling, achieving a form of learning through experience." (Abstract, [Mar & Oatley, 2008, Perspectives on Psychological Science 3(3), 173-192](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00073.x))

> "Engaging in the simulative experiences of fiction literature can facilitate the understanding of others who are different from ourselves and can augment our capacity for empathy and social inference." (same abstract)

What this evidence does not test: the practice. The paper is a theoretical account of literary fiction's function, not an intervention study; it does not show that seeking stories before statistics improves social cognition, and the stories-versus-data contrast is carried as the practice's rationale, not as a measured difference.
