> **Kernel K231** — Living the question

Keep the question "What is this life for?" alive as an ongoing inquiry rather than settling it with a fixed answer. The phrase comes from Rilke's fourth letter to the young poet Franz Xaver Kappus, Worpswede, 16 July 1903: "to be patient toward all that is unsolved in your heart and to try to love the questions themselves like locked rooms and like books that are written in a very foreign tongue. Do not now seek the answers, which cannot be given you because you would not be able to live them. And the point is, to live everything. Live the questions now. Perhaps you will then gradually, without noticing it, live along some distant day into the answer" ([Rilke, Letters to a Young Poet](https://www.alyve.org/english/docs/9.1/Rilke-Letters_to_a_Young_Poet.pdf)). The kernel turns the instruction into a habit. Dimensions: cognition (the question kept open rather than closed) and motivation (the search sustained across years).

### Protocol

1. Name your version of the question in one sentence. "What is this life for?" is one possible prompt; yours may be "what am I for?", "what is worth loving?", "what does this suffering want to teach?" Write it where you will see it.
2. Generate experiences that carry the question: conversations, books, works of your hands, days spent in ways that put the question to the test. The point is to live everything, not to think about everything.
3. When an answer presents itself, hold it as an experiment rather than a verdict. Try living as if it were true for a month, and note what the month showed.
4. Select: once a season, read your notes and mark which experiences deepened the question and which merely distracted from it. Keep the deepening ones in the rotation.
5. Retain the question itself. Answers get recorded and dated; the question gets carried forward unchanged. An answer that survives several rounds of living is worth keeping; revise the others when experience gives you reason to.

### Evidence

Rilke's letter is the source of the instruction, and its psychological cousin is measurable: the Meaning in Life Questionnaire treats the search for meaning as a distinct dimension of life alongside the presence of meaning, with the search measurable reliably over time. In the validation paper's own summary, "in 3 studies, evidence is provided for the internal consistency, temporal stability, factor structure, and validity of the Meaning in Life Questionnaire (MLQ), a new 10-item measure of the presence of, and the search for, meaning in life" ([Steger, Frazier, Oishi and Kaler, Journal of Counseling Psychology 2006](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ734394)). The two dimensions are not mutually exclusive in the scale's design: a person can report both having meaning and searching for it. Scope limits: the questionnaire measures self-reported search, not Rilke's patient, lived inquiry, and it cannot tell you whether holding the question is more generative than any particular answer.
