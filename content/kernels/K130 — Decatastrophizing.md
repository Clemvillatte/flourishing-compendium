> **Kernel K130** — Decatastrophizing

When a feared outcome looms, ask: "What is the worst that could happen? If that happened, how would I cope? What is the most likely outcome? What is the best that could happen?" The exercise restores proportion.

### Protocol

*Steps are the compendium's editorial protocol; the forecasting error they correct is documented below.*

1. Write the feared outcome as a single sentence; vagueness is what makes catastrophes feel unbounded.
2. Run the four questions in order, in writing, and answer each in a sentence; the writing slows the forecast down to the speed of evidence.
3. Give the most likely outcome the last word; the worst case has had the first word for long enough.

### Evidence

The bias the exercise counters is the impact bias: "people base many decisions on affective forecasts, predictions about their emotional reactions to future events. They often display an impact bias, overestimating the intensity and duration of their emotional reactions to such events," and "this is especially true when predicting reactions to negative events: people fail to anticipate how quickly they will cope psychologically with such events in ways that speed their recovery from them" ([Wilson & Gilbert, *Current Directions in Psychological Science*, 2005, abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.0963-7214.2005.00355.x)). The classic studies found participants overestimated the duration of distress after relationship breakups, tenure denials, electoral defeats, and job rejections. What this research does not test: the four-question decatastrophizing format as a clinical technique; the findings establish the forecasting error, and the question sequence is the editorial protocol built on it.

Not applicable: the practice is a set of questions answered in writing, not a recording.
