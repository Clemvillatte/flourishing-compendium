> **Kernel K284** — The "what does this role require?" practice

Before entering a role-specific situation (a meeting, a conversation with your child, a community event), pause and ask: what does this role require of me right now? The question shifts attention from your preferences to your obligations. It is the Stoic practice of role ethics, which holds that flourishing comes from [[Fulfil your roles well|performing one's roles well]], not from escaping them. Epictetus's version is the actor: "Remember that you are an actor in a drama, of such a kind as the author pleases to make it... For this is your business, to act well the character assigned you; to choose it is another's" ([Epictetus, Enchiridion 17](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html)). Dimensions: attention (turned from preference to obligation at the door) and motivation (the duty, not the mood, as the engine of the act).

### Protocol

1. Generate the pause at the threshold: one breath before entering, and the question asked once, silently: what does this role require here? The question is asked in the role's own grammar, parent, chair, neighbour, not in the grammar of what you feel like doing.
2. Select the one requirement that matters in this situation, not the whole duty of the role: what the meeting needs from the chair, what the moment needs from the parent. One requirement, held plainly, survives the situation's noise.
3. Act the part naturally, in Epictetus's sense: without resenting the casting or overplaying the role. The cripple and the governor have the same job, to act the assigned character well.
4. Retain the question across situations until it fires on its own. The practice is finished when the pause and the question arrive together, without being summoned.

### Evidence

The Stoic ground is quoted above, and the Confucian tradition addresses the same matter in its own words: "the duke Jing, of Qi, asked Confucius about government. Confucius replied, 'There is government, when the prince is prince, and the minister is minister; when the father is father, and the son is son'" ([Analects 12.11, Legge trans.](https://ctext.org/analects/yan-yuan)), the rectification of names that treats role fit as the condition of order. The practice is a pre-situation application of what the role inventory (K282) maps monthly and the role-conflict deliberation (K283) resolves when two roles collide. Scope limits: both sources are normative philosophies rather than measured interventions, and the sources cited here do not test the threshold question itself; what the sources offer are two philosophical starting points for this modern role-reflection exercise, which is a reason to take it seriously, not a measured effect size.
