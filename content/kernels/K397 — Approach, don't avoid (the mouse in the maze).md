> **Kernel K397** — Approach, don't avoid (the mouse in the maze)

When you notice yourself bracing against a task, a conversation, or a feeling, ask which direction you are facing. Are you moving toward something (helping the mouse find the cheese) or away from something (fleeing the owl)? In a real experiment, students who solved a maze by avoiding an owl did 50% worse on a later creativity test than students who solved the same maze by helping a mouse find cheese. The same act, different spirit, different aftermath: avoidance closes the mind; approach opens it.

### Protocol

Adapted from Williams and Penman, *Mindfulness*, 2011, ch. 7, printed pp. 110–111, which reports Friedman & Förster (2001); the kernel turns the experiment's finding into a self-check.

1. Name the thing you are bracing against: a task, a conversation, a feeling, a decision you keep postponing.
2. Ask the one question: which direction am I facing? Toward a goal I want, or away from something I fear?
3. If the answer is away, rewrite the same act in the direction of what you want. Not "avoid the failure" but "produce the draft"; not "escape the conversation" but "say the true thing kindly".
4. Do the act once in its approach form. Notice whether the mind feels wider or narrower after it.

### Evidence

The experiment the book retells, as described by a university mindfulness programme page:

> "Both groups were given a sheet of paper which showed a cartoon mouse trapped inside the picture of a maze. The task was to help the mouse find a way out of the maze. But there was one slight difference in the pictures the groups received. The 'approach' version of the picture showed a piece of Swiss cheese lying outside the maze in front of a mouse hole. The 'avoidance' version showed an identical maze except that, instead of a satisfying meal of cheese at the end, an owl hovered over the maze – ready to swoop and catch the mouse at any moment."
> — [Trinity College Dublin, Mindfulness Week 3, reporting Williams & Penman](https://www.tcd.ie/collegehealth/promotion/mindfulness/week3.php)

The aftermath:

> "When the participants took a test of creativity soon afterwards, those who had helped the mouse avoid the owl came out with scores 50% lower than those who had helped the mouse find the cheese. The state of mind that was brought about by attending to the owl had produced a lingering sense of caution, avoidance and vigilance for things going wrong."
> — [same source](https://www.tcd.ie/collegehealth/promotion/mindfulness/week3.php)

The primary paper's own framing, in a peer-reviewed review of the paradigm:

> "Notably, as with manipulations of approach and avoidance motor actions, completion of the cheese and owl mazes has also been found to influence creativity, with approach, relative to avoidance, maze completion promoting the ability to generate innovative uses for a common object, controlling for sheer response fluency (Friedman & Förster, 2001)."
> — [Friedman & Förster (2010), "Implicit Affective Cues and Attentional Tuning", PMC2933078](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2933078/)

The maze has been reused since as a standard procedural prime for promotion vs. prevention focus in later studies, with the same operationalisation of "seeking reward" and "avoiding punishment" ([Frontiers in Psychology 2018](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02121/full)). The original is Friedman, R. S., & Förster, J. (2001), "The effects of promotion and prevention cues on creativity", *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 81(6), 1001–1013 ([PubMed 11761303](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11761303/)).

What the sources do not test: the kernel's self-check is an editorial application of the experimental finding; the experiment tested a single creativity outcome after a maze prime, not the day-to-day reframing practice this page describes.

### A guided version

The practice is a one-question self-check; no recording is needed.
