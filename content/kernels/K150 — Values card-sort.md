> **Kernel K150** — Values card-sort

A deck of values written on cards, sorted into piles by importance, until the top ten stand out. The instrument is the Personal Values Card Sort of William R. Miller and colleagues at the University of New Mexico (2001), a free tool from the motivational interviewing tradition. It suits the seasons when the day's allocations and the stated priorities have drifted apart and the person sorting cannot say exactly when.

### Protocol

The deck and the piles come from the original instrument; the ranking and discrepancy steps come from the treatment guidance that describes its use.

1. Print or hand-write the deck. The instrument's pile headers read "IMPORTANT TO ME", "VERY IMPORTANT TO ME" and "NOT IMPORTANT TO ME", and its cards carry one value each with a plain gloss, such as "AUTONOMY, to be self-determined and independent" or "FAMILY, to have a happy, loving family".
2. Sort the whole deck into the three piles by importance, first pass without long deliberation.
3. Take up to ten cards from the most important pile. In clinical use the counselor converses about each one; in solo use, write one honest sentence on why each card earned its place.
4. Watch for discrepancy. Where a top value and the week's actual behavior disagree, note the disagreement plainly; in the clinical tradition these discrepancy statements are the working material, not the sorted piles.

### Evidence

From the treatment guidance describing the tool:

> "Invite clients to sort the cards into piles by importance; those that are most important are place in one pile, and those that are least important are in another pile. Ask clients to pick up to 10 cards from the most important pile; converse about each one." ([SAMHSA TIP 39 update, Chapter 3, NBK571068](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK571068/))

And the outcome line attached to values work in that tradition:

> "Client experience of discrepancy between values and substance use behavior is related to better client outcomes (Apodaca & Longabaugh, 2009)." (same chapter)

What this evidence does not test: the card sort as a standalone intervention. The outcome line concerns discrepancy between values and behavior in counseling sessions, not the sorting exercise alone, and the instrument is a clinical tool whose direct trial evidence is not opened here.
