> **Kernel K263** — Post-action phronesis review

After a significant action, review four questions: what did I intend? what actually happened? where did the gap between intention and outcome come from? what would I do differently? The review is not self-criticism; it is the feedback loop through which practical wisdom is supposed to grow, and without it experience becomes repetition rather than learning. Reach for it after actions that matter, not after every errand.

### Protocol

The practice moves the Cognition and Motivation dimensions: the action is re-described, and the next one is amended.

1. Choose one significant action from the past day or week, and answer the four questions in writing. The writing is the review; a vague memory is not.
2. For the fourth question, Aristotle's definition marks the target: practical wisdom is "a true and reasoned state of capacity to act with regard to the things that are good or bad for man", and it "must also recognize the particulars; for it is practical, and practice is concerned with particulars" ([Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* VI.5, Ross trans., full text](http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.6.vi.html)). The review's value lies in the particulars it recovers.
3. Editorial addition, not from the carriers: end with one concrete amendment for the next similar action. A review that changes nothing is a diary entry.

### Evidence

The empirical grounding for reviewing after acting, from a meta-analysis of the after-action review across 61 studies:

> "Based on a bare-bones meta-analysis of the results from 61 studies (107 ds [915 teams and 3,499 individuals]), the AAR leads to an overall d of 0.79 improvement in multiple training evaluation criteria."
> — [Keiser & Arthur 2021, *Journal of Applied Psychology* 106(7), PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32852990/)]

What the cited study does not test: the effect is measured on structured training evaluations with review media and facilitation, not on a personal four-question written review; the philosophical claim about phronesis is Aristotle's thesis, not a measured outcome; and the page claims no wisdom gain for the exercise.
