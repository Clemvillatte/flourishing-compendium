> **Kernel K417** — Shouyi (guarding the One)

Shouyi 守一, "guarding the One", is one of the oldest named Daoist meditations. A chapter in the [Routledge Handbook of Yoga and Meditation Studies](https://ebrary.net/322857/health/_guarding_one_) calls it "a key Daoist technical term for apophatic meditation, that is, emptiness- and stillness-based meditation", adds that it "was the central practice of the inner cultivation lineages of classical Daoism", and locates its two earliest references in the Neiye (Inward Training) and in the Zhuangzi, chapter 11. In that [chapter's rendering](https://ebrary.net/322857/health/_guarding_one_), the One is the Dao itself, and guarding it means "returning to the original and inherent stillness of innate nature". The Neiye verse reads:

> Expand your heart-mind and release it.  
> Relax your qi and allow it to extend.  
> When your body is calm and unmoving,  
> Guard the One and discard myriad disturbances.  
> You will see profit and not be enticed by it,  
> You will see harm and not be frightened by it.  
> — Neiye 24, as translated in the [Handbook chapter](https://ebrary.net/322857/health/_guarding_one_)

### Protocol

The steps below are this compendium's adaptation, assembled from the Neiye verses and the Handbook chapter's account of the classical technique.

1. Align the body. Sit steady and unmoving, limbs squared up, in the manner of the verse that speaks of eyes and ears acute and clear and the four limbs firm and fixed ([Neiye article, Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neiye)). The [Handbook chapter](https://ebrary.net/322857/health/_guarding_one_) says the Inward Training emphasizes a four-fold process of alignment: body, four limbs, qi, and heart-mind.
2. Relax the breath. Follow the verse's instruction: "Relax your qi and allow it to extend" ([Handbook chapter](https://ebrary.net/322857/health/_guarding_one_)).
3. Guard the One and discard the myriad disturbances. Roth's account of the verse, quoted in [Wikipedia's Taoist meditation article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoist_meditation), is of a technique in which the adept "concentrates on nothing but the Way, or some representation of it", undertaken "sitting in a calm and unmoving position", setting aside "the disturbances of perceptions, thoughts, emotions, and desires that normally fill your conscious mind".
4. Meet gain and loss evenly. When profit appears, see it without enticement; when harm appears, see it without fright. Return to the One ([Neiye 24, Handbook chapter](https://ebrary.net/322857/health/_guarding_one_)).
5. Sit in silence. The [Handbook chapter](https://ebrary.net/322857/health/_guarding_one_) describes the deepening this way: "One simply sits in silence, which is the ground of one's being", and as habituated patterns decrease, "one gradually enters a state of deep stillness."

### A later variant

A Han-dynasty scripture, the Taiping jing, turns guarding into watching a light: "First of all, it will be red, after a long time it will change to be white, later again it will be green, and then it will pervade all of you completely" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taoist_meditation), quoting Livia Kohn's translation). In the medieval Taiqing movement, the [Handbook chapter](https://ebrary.net/322857/health/_guarding_one_) notes, shouyi "appears in discussions of visualisation practice", a different stream from the classical apophatic form.

### Evidence

The passages cited here describe historical practice; they do not evaluate the effectiveness of this compendium's adaptation.
