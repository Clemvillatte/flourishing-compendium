> **Kernel K417** — Shouyi (guarding the One)

Shouyi 守一, "guarding the One", is one of the oldest named Daoist meditations. A chapter in the Routledge Handbook of Yoga and Meditation Studies calls it "a key Daoist technical term for apophatic meditation, that is, emptiness- and stillness-based meditation", adds that it "was the central practice of the inner cultivation lineages of classical Daoism", and locates its two earliest references in the *Neiye* (Inward Training) and in the *Zhuangzi*, chapter 11. In that chapter's rendering, the One is the Dao itself, and guarding it means "returning to the original and inherent stillness of innate nature". The *Neiye* verse reads:

> Expand your heart-mind and release it.
> Relax your *qi* and allow it to extend.
> When your body is calm and unmoving,
> Guard the One and discard myriad disturbances.
> You will see profit and not be enticed by it.
> You will see harm and not be frightened by it.
> — *Neiye* 24, as translated in the Handbook chapter

### Protocol

The steps below are this compendium's adaptation, assembled from the *Neiye* verses and the Handbook chapter's account of the classical technique.

1. Align the body. Sit steady and unmoving, limbs squared up. The Handbook chapter notes that the *Inward Training* "emphasises a four-fold process of alignment": aligning the body, the four limbs, the *qi*, and the heart-mind.
2. Relax the breath. The *Neiye* describes breathing that coils and contracts, uncoils and expands, and says of the Way that it "cannot be halted by force, yet can be secured by inner power". Nothing in the breath is forced.
3. Guard the One and discard the myriad disturbances. Hold the One, the Way, as the single content of attention, and let perceptions, thoughts, emotions, and desires fall away. The Wikipedia article on the *Neiye* summarizes Roth's account of the technique as one in which a person "sits calmly and exclusively concentrates on the Way", setting aside all the "disturbances of perceptions, thoughts, emotions, and desires that normally fill your conscious mind".
4. Meet gain and loss evenly. When profit appears, see it without enticement; when harm appears, see it without fright. Return to the One.
5. Sit in silence. The Handbook chapter describes the deepening this way: "One simply sits in silence, which is the ground of one's being", and as habituated patterns decrease, "one gradually enters a state of deep stillness."

### A later variant

The *Zhuangzi* passage has Laozi say, "You only have to take care and guard your own body... As for myself, I guard this oneness and abide in this harmony." A Han-dynasty scripture, the *Taiping jing*, turns guarding into watching a light: "First of all, it will be red, after a long time it will change to be white, later again it will be green, and then it will pervade all of you completely." In the medieval Taiqing movement, the Handbook chapter notes, shouyi "appears in discussions of visualisation practice", a different stream from the classical apophatic form.

### Evidence

No randomized trial has tested shouyi by name. The passages cited here describe historical practice; they do not evaluate the effectiveness of the adaptation presented on this page. A search of the trial literature in October 2026 found no shouyi study; the nearest studied relatives are concentrative practices with a single anchor.
