> **Kernel K132** — The pragmatic maxim

Take the belief or concept currently doing the most work in your thinking and ask Charles Sanders Peirce's question: what conceivable practical difference does it make? If you held it, and if you held its rival, what would you do, expect, or feel differently in some possible situation? If no difference can be found, the dispute is about a word, not a world. Peirce published the rule in 1878, and it remains philosophy's cleanest broom.

### Protocol

The maxim, in the author's words. The practice moves the Cognition dimension above all, the meaning of a belief, with Motivation close behind, since the difference sought is a difference in action.

1. Name the concept or belief precisely: free will, ego, innate talent, being a "failure".
2. Run Peirce's test on it: "Consider what effects, that might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then, our conception of these effects is the whole of our conception of the object" ([Peirce 1878, full text](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p119.html)). Write down what each version of the belief would have you expect, do, or refuse to do.
3. If the two columns differ, the belief has content; act on the differences. If they do not, Peirce's verdict: "It is foolish... to fancy themselves in disagreement about the elements of the sacrament, if they agree in regard to all their sensible effects, here and hereafter." Drop the quarrel, keep the word if it is useful.

### Evidence

The maxim's foundation, in Peirce's own sentences:

> "The whole function of thought is to produce habits of action; and that whatever there is connected with a thought, but irrelevant to its purpose, is an accretion to it, but no part of it."
> — [Peirce (1878), "How to Make Our Ideas Clear", *Popular Science Monthly* 12, 286–302 — full text](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p119.html)

And the ground rule for beliefs:

> "The essence of belief is the establishment of a habit; and different beliefs are distinguished by the different modes of action to which they give rise."

Peirce's own worked example, on hardness: "There is absolutely no difference between a hard thing and a soft thing so long as they are not brought to the test" — a diamond never scratched is hard only in what would happen if it were. What the essay does not test: this is a philosophical method, not an experimental finding; the cited studies do not measure whether applying the maxim improves decisions or wellbeing, and the page claims none.

### A guided version

No public guided recording of the maxim exists, and the bounded search confirms that. The nearest worked guide is the [Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy's walkthrough of Peirce's method](https://iep.utm.edu/peircepr/): take a concept, derive its conditional expectations in the form "if X, then doing A will yield experience B" (their example: "if vinegar is acetic acid, then if I dip litmus paper into it, it will turn red"), and keep listing conditionals until the concept's practical content is exhausted. The list is the guide; there is nothing to listen to.
