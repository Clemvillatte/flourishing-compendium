> **Kernel K249** — Half-smile

Allow a faint smile to form on your face, particularly during difficult moments. The skill comes from dialectical behaviour therapy's radical-acceptance module; the mechanism usually cited for it, facial feedback, has a replication history the practice must be honest about.

### Protocol

*Steps follow the DBT skill as taught; the framing is the compendium's.*

1. Turn the corners of the lips up slightly, with a relaxed face; a half-smile, not a grin.
2. Hold it while accepting what is happening, in the difficult moment itself rather than after it.
3. Let the posture follow: a relaxed body, an unclenched jaw, the smile faint enough to keep.

### Evidence

The skill itself is a DBT distress-tolerance and acceptance technique (Linehan's skills training manual); direct trials of the half-smile as a standalone practice are not what the literature provides. The putative mechanism, facial feedback, rests on the classic Strack pen-in-mouth study, whose registered replication across 17 labs and 1894 participants failed: "The original Strack et al. (1988) study reported a rating difference of 0.82 units on a 10-point Likert scale," but "our meta-analysis revealed a rating difference of 0.03 units with a 95% confidence interval ranging from −0.11 to 0.16," and "0 out of 17 outcomes (0%) showed a difference in mean Likert scores (smile minus pout) that was at least as high as 0.82," leading to the conclusion that "this RRR did not replicate the SMS result and failed to do so in a statistically compelling fashion" ([Wagenmakers et al., *Perspectives on Psychological Science*, 2016](https://www.ejwagenmakers.com/2016/WagenmakersEtAl2016Strack.pdf)). The hypothesis is not dead: a later 19-country collaboration of 3878 participants found that voluntarily posing a happy expression raised self-reported happiness (2.51 vs 2.16) while the classic pen-in-mouth manipulation still showed no reliable effect (Coles et al., 2024, the Many Smiles Collaboration). What no study tests: the half-smile as a DBT acceptance skill during difficult moments, or any effect on suffering as such.

### A guided version

Not applicable: the practice is a facial posture held in life, not a recording.
