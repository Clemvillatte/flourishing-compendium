> **Kernel K166** — Sympathetic joy

When you see someone doing well, especially someone you might compare yourself to, deliberately rejoice. "Good for them." The practice dissolves envy and trains the mind to find joy in others' happiness.

### Protocol

*Steps are my own protocol; the concept is defined in the Buddhist ethics below.*

1. When you notice another's success, especially where comparison bites, say the two words to yourself: good for them.
2. If envy arrives first, name the envy, then say the words again; the practice is the repetition, not the absence of envy.
3. Extend it past the easy cases: a stranger's promotion, a rival's win, the person who got what you wanted.

### Evidence

The practice is the Buddhist brahmavihāra of muditā, defined as "sympathetic joy at the good fortune or success of others": "the virtue of mudita, i.e., finding joy in the happiness and success of others, has not received sufficient attention either in expositions of Buddhist ethics, or in the meditative development of the four sublime states (brahma-vihara), of which mudita is one" ([Nyanaponika Thera et al., *Wheel* 170, Access to Insight](https://accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/various/wheel170.html)). The tradition names envy as the state muditā counters, and asks "should not, therefore, all effort be made to cultivate their antidote, that is mudita?" What this source does not test: any effect on well-being or envy reduction in a controlled trial; the text is expository ethics, and the envy-dissolution claim is the tradition's own framing, not a measured outcome.

### A guided version

Not applicable: the practice is a sentence said to oneself on seeing another's success, not a recording.
