> **Kernel K117** — Graded exposure

Create a list of avoided situations, ordered from least to most difficult. Enter the easiest one, stay, and move up the list gradually. The method comes from Joseph Wolpe's systematic desensitisation, and its modern descendants treat phobias, panic and post-traumatic stress. Reach for it when avoidance has fenced off a piece of ordinary life.

### Protocol

Suggested adaptation of the clinical procedure; the carriers below describe the clinical form, and this page scales it to self-help size. The practice moves the Overt behaviour, Affect and Cognition dimensions: entering the situation, staying through the fear, and letting the expectation be tested.

1. Write the hierarchy: a list of situations you avoid, ordered by difficulty. In Wolpe's clinical form this is "an anxiety hierarchy... containing a list of stimuli to which the patient reacts with graded degrees of anxiety (the most disturbing item is placed at the top, and the least disturbing at the bottom of the list)" ([Carona, Ramos & Salvador, BJPsych Advances](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-advances/article/psychotherapy-by-reciprocal-inhibition-wolpes-unique-legacy-to-the-evolution-of-cognitivebehavioural-therapy/A34DE39AC4C20EA598209C8150F9117F)). Wolpe's original principle was that "if a response antagonistic to anxiety can be made to occur in the presence of anxiety-evoking stimuli so that it is accompanied by a complete or partial suppression of the anxiety responses, the bond between these stimuli and the anxiety responses will be weakened"; his antagonistic response was usually deep muscle relaxation, and the desensitisation was mostly conducted in imagination.
2. Enter the easiest situation in real life and stay. The classic instruction, not from the carriers below: remain until the anxiety drops by at least half before moving up.
3. Editorial addition, not from the carriers: if a step proves too large, split it into two smaller steps and re-enter. Moving up the list is the compendium's own schedule; the carriers describe therapy conducted over sessions.

### Evidence

The modern refinement is worth knowing, because it revises step 2. The inhibitory-learning review states that exposure optimization strategies include "expectancy violation" and that the model distinguishes itself "from a 'fear habituation' approach and 'belief disconfirmation' approach within standard cognitive-behavior therapy" ([Craske et al. 2014, PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864005/)). On that view, the working ingredient is the moment the feared outcome fails to occur, not the waiting-out of anxiety itself; the drop-by-half instruction is the older habituation framing, and both live in current practice.

The same review's honest limits: "Exposure therapy is an effective approach for treating anxiety disorders, although a substantial number of individuals fail to benefit or experience a return of fear after treatment. Research suggests that anxious individuals show deficits in the mechanisms believed to underlie exposure therapy, such as inhibitory learning." What the carriers do not test: the clinical trials concern anxiety disorders under clinician guidance, not a self-administered list of avoided situations; and neither carrier tests this page's schedule.
