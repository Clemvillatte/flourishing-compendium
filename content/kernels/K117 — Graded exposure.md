> **Kernel K117** — Graded exposure

Create a list of avoided situations, ordered from least to most difficult. Enter the easiest, stay, move up gradually. The method descends from Joseph Wolpe's systematic desensitisation. Reach for it when avoidance has fenced off a piece of ordinary life. The self-help form suits ordinary fears; trauma or severe symptoms belong with a clinician.

### Protocol

Suggested adaptation of the clinical procedure, scaled to self-help size. The practice moves the Overt behaviour, Affect and Cognition dimensions: entering, staying through the fear, and letting the expectation be tested.

1. Write the hierarchy: a list of situations you avoid, ordered by difficulty. In Wolpe's clinical form this is "an anxiety hierarchy... containing a list of stimuli to which the patient reacts with graded degrees of anxiety (the most disturbing item is placed at the top, and the least disturbing at the bottom of the list)" ([Carona, Ramos & Salvador, BJPsych Advances](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/bjpsych-advances/article/psychotherapy-by-reciprocal-inhibition-wolpes-unique-legacy-to-the-evolution-of-cognitivebehavioural-therapy/A34DE39AC4C20EA598209C8150F9117F)). Wolpe's original principle was that "if a response antagonistic to anxiety can be made to occur in the presence of anxiety-evoking stimuli so that it is accompanied by a complete or partial suppression of the anxiety responses, the bond between these stimuli and the anxiety responses will be weakened"; his antagonistic response was usually deep muscle relaxation, done in imagination.
2. Enter the easiest situation in real life and stay. The rule, from NHS phobias guidance: "You need to stay in the situation until your anxiety has dropped by at least 50% from its level at the start of the exercise. This can take up to 40 minutes" ([NHS inform phobias self-help guide](https://www.nhsinform.scot/illnesses-and-conditions/mental-health/mental-health-self-help-guides/phobias-self-help-guide/)).
3. Editorial addition: if a step proves too large, split it and re-enter.

### Evidence

A modern refinement revises the old habituation framing. The inhibitory-learning review proposes that exposure optimization strategies include "expectancy violation" and that the model distinguishes itself "from a 'fear habituation' approach and 'belief disconfirmation' approach within standard cognitive-behavior therapy" ([Craske et al. 2014, PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24864005/)). On that view, the proposed working ingredient is the moment the feared outcome fails to occur, not the waiting-out of anxiety itself; that is the review's proposal for clinical practice, not an established fact about a self-help list.

"Exposure therapy is an effective approach for treating anxiety disorders, although a substantial number of individuals fail to benefit or experience a return of fear after treatment. Research suggests that anxious individuals show deficits in the mechanisms believed to underlie exposure therapy, such as inhibitory learning." What the carriers do not test: the trials concern anxiety disorders under clinician guidance, not a self-administered list; neither carrier tests this page's schedule.

### A guided version

A guided module exists: the [TalkPlus Graded Exposure guide](https://www.talkplus.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Graded-Exposure.pdf) is a NHS Talking Therapies worksheet with its own [video module](https://www.talkplus.org.uk/graded-exposure-video-module) walking the steps: identify avoided activities, rank them by anxiety level, plan exposures on the four conditions (graded, prolonged, without distraction, repeated), and move up only when a step is comfortable.
