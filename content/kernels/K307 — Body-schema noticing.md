> **Kernel K307** — Body-schema noticing

Throughout the day, notice your body schema: the implicit sense of where your body ends and the world begins. Notice how it shifts in different contexts: larger when you are confident, smaller when you are anxious, blurred when you are absorbed. The body schema is not the body as seen but the body as lived, the pre-reflective sense that structures action.

### Protocol

Steps are a practice form; the study behind it is quoted below. Intended focus: Attention and Embodiment.

1. Three times a day, take ten seconds to sense the body's outline as you act: where does the body seem to end right now?
2. Notice the outline in different situations and moods, and let the differences be data rather than problems: larger in some rooms, smaller in some meetings, blurred in absorbed work.
3. Keep a light weekly note of which situations shrink or blur the outline; the aim is the noticing itself, not a fix.

### Evidence

From the research on the body's edges:

> "Among all patients, the regular use of a wheelchair induced the perception that the body's edges are not fixed, but are instead plastic and flexible to include the wheelchair." (Abstract, [Pazzaglia, Galli, Scivoletto & Molinari, 2013, PLoS ONE 8(3), e58312](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23484015/))

What this evidence does not test: the noticing practice. The study analysed structured reports from 55 wheelchair users with spinal cord injury; the wheelchair became part of the body's boundaries in function, varying with which body segments were disconnected, and the authors read this as the brain updating bodily signals rather than the tool standing in for the limbs. The day-long noticing of an able-bodied walker's outline is not what was measured, and the confident/anxious sizing in the opening paragraph is the practice's own phenomenology, not a finding. What the study supports is the core premise: the felt boundary of the body is not fixed.
