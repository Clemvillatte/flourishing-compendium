> **Kernel K402** — Listen to the storyteller for thirty seconds

Close your eyes and, for thirty seconds, do nothing except listen to what your mind is saying. Do not argue with it, do not follow it anywhere. Just listen, the way you would listen to a radio playing in another room.

Notice what the stream contains: predictions, judgments, memories, instructions, warnings. Then ask one question: is following the current story useful for what you need to do next? Some of what the mind produces is fact; much of it is commentary on fact. The thirty seconds is spent learning to tell the difference from the outside.

This is not a silence exercise — the mind will keep talking. The practice is the listening.

### Protocol

Adapted from Harris (2009, p. 119), retold in Stoddard & Afari, *The Big Book of ACT Metaphors* (2014), printed pp. 80–81; the original is the Master Storyteller exercise.

1. Sit and close your eyes. Say nothing for about thirty seconds.
2. Just listen to the story your mind is telling you right now. If your thoughts stop, keep listening until they start again.
3. Do not argue with the story, do not follow it anywhere, and do not try to stop it. Just listen, the way you would listen to a radio playing in another room.
4. After the thirty seconds, name what the stream contained: predictions, judgments, memories, instructions, warnings.
5. Ask the one question: is following the current story useful for what you need to do next? Some of what the mind produces is fact; much of it is commentary on fact. The thirty seconds is spent learning to tell the difference from the outside.

### Evidence

The metaphor the exercise belongs to, in the original:

> "Our mind is like the world's greatest storyteller. It never shuts up. It's always got a story to tell and guess what it wants more than anything else? ... It wants what any good storyteller wants. It wants us to listen. It wants our full attention. And it'll say whatever it can to get our attention. Even if it's painful or nasty or scary."
> — [Russ Harris, *ACT Made Simple* (2009), the Master Storyteller Metaphor, PDF](https://www.pensierocritico.eu/files/ACT_Made_Simple_Dr._Russ_Harris_preface.pdf)

The distinction the listening trains:

> "And some of the stories it tells us are true. We call those 'facts.' But most of the stories it tells us, we can't really call 'facts.' They're more like opinions, beliefs, ideas, attitudes, assumptions, judgments, predictions, and so forth. They're stories about how we see the world, and what we want to do, and what we think is right and wrong or fair and unfair, and so on."
> — [same source](https://www.pensierocritico.eu/files/ACT_Made_Simple_Dr._Russ_Harris_preface.pdf)

The exercise's own closing, in the Big Book's retelling:

> "For the next thirty seconds, silently listen in to what your mind is saying. And if your thoughts stop, just keep listening until they start again. (Pause for thirty seconds.) So there you have it: there's a part of your mind that talks — the thinking self — and a part of your mind that listens — the observing self."
> — [Harris (2009), "Talking and Listening", as retold in Stoddard & Afari, *The Big Book of ACT Metaphors* (2014), printed pp. 177–178, PDF](https://mimhtraining.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/The-Big-Book-of-Act-Metaphors.pdf)

The Big Book's own framing of what the metaphor names:

> "The Master Storyteller is a metaphor for the mind's ceaseless narrative, including its evaluations, rules, predictions, and so on."
> — [same source](https://mimhtraining.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/The-Big-Book-of-Act-Metaphors.pdf)

What the sources do not test: the exercise is a defusion technique inside ACT, whose programme-level outcomes are studied; no trial isolates the thirty-second listening form as its own intervention.

### A guided version

The exercise is silent self-directed listening; no recording is needed.
