> **Kernel K129** — Prediction log

Before acting on a belief or fear, write down what you predict will happen. After the event, check the prediction against what actually occurred. The research behind the habit comes from forecasting tournaments, where thousands of people made hundreds of thousands of probability estimates and the analysts could see what separated the accurate from the rest.

## Protocol

1. Write the prediction before acting. One sentence, dated, with a probability if you can give one honestly: "70% the conversation goes badly."
2. Name the reference class. The tournament training pushed forecasters toward comparison cases; in the personal version, ask "how often does this kind of thing go this way?"
3. Update as the picture changes. Tournament participants "were encouraged to update their beliefs as often as they wished before the close of each question" ([Mellers et al., 2014](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4rg4n9vr/qt4rg4n9vr.pdf)); your log takes the same permission. The strongest behavioral correlate of accuracy in the tournament data was updating frequency: "Correlations between standardized Brier score accuracy and effort were statistically significant for belief updating, −.49, t(740) = −15.29, p < .001, and deliberation time, −.30, t(694) = −8.28, p < .001, but not for number of forecasting questions attempted" ([Mellers et al. 2015](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/xap-0000040.pdf)).
4. Score it when it closes. What happened, and what you had said. No curve-fitting; the log entry stays as written.
5. Read the log quarterly. The pattern you are looking for is calibration: where you are overconfident, where you are flat-out wrong in a repeatable direction.

## A guided version

The log is your own predictions on your own page; no guided recording is cited for it here.

## Evidence

The tournament results: five research groups competed over two years, and "Results showed that probability training, team collaboration, and tracking improved both calibration and resolution"; "All three manipulations significantly reduced forecasting errors" ([Mellers et al., 2014](https://escholarship.org/content/qt4rg4n9vr/qt4rg4n9vr.pdf)). The training itself worked on the same muscles this log works: "Probability training guided forecasters to consider reference classes; average multiple estimates from existing models, polls, and expert panels; extrapolate over time when variables were continuous; and avoid judgmental traps such as overconfidence, the confirmation bias, and base-rate neglect" (same article).

What the research does not cover: the tournaments scored geopolitical forecasts by trained volunteers on a scoring website, not personal beliefs and fears in a notebook. The calibration improvements came with the tournament's training, teaming, and tracking manipulations; a solo notebook is an adaptation with none of that machinery. The habit borrows the tournament's logic, not its results.
