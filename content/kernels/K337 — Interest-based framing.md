> **Kernel K337** — Interest-based framing

In a conflict, separate the position from the interest. The position is what someone says they want; the interest is the need, fear or hope underneath it. Ask what the concern behind the demand is, and the conversation moves from competing claims to the needs both sides are trying to protect.

### Protocol

1. State the conflict as two positions on one line: "She wants X, I want Y."
2. Under each position, list that side's interests: the needs, worries and hopes the position is standing in for. Ask "why is that important to you?" until the answers stop moving.
3. Mark the interests that overlap and the ones that can be traded against each other; most conflicts contain both.
4. Invent at least three options that meet the overlapping interests before evaluating any of them, and put your BATNA in writing so you know your walk-away price.
5. Evaluate the options against the interests of both sides, not against the original positions, and pick the one that meets the most interests at the least cost.

### Evidence

The method is Fisher and Ury's, and their formulation is exact: "Interests motivate people; they are the silent movers behind the hubbub of positions. Your position is something you have decided upon. Your interests are what caused you to so decide" (Fisher & Ury, Getting to Yes, ch. 3). Their two sisters who split an orange in half, one squeezing her half for juice and discarding the peel, the other using the peel for her baking and discarding the fruit, are the standing proof: "for every interest there usually exist several possible positions that could satisfy it", so positional bargaining settles for half an orange when interest-based framing could have given each sister the whole of what she wanted (Fisher & Ury, ch. 4). The Harvard Program on Negotiation teaches the same reframe as the core of principled negotiation (PON, Principled Negotiation).

### A guided version

The [Interests vs. Positions sheet from the University of Utah S.J. Quinney College of Law](https://www.law.utah.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Interests_vs_Positions.pdf) is a worked guide with the question prompts that do the reframing: "Why is that important to you?", "What would that solution accomplish?", "What if that did happen?", "How would that affect you?", and "What changes would that solution make in your life?"

## Related concepts

- [[Restorative justice]] — the process this reframe feeds into: harms named, needs met, agreements built on them.
- [[Face negotiation theory]] — conflicts about face are interests wearing a positional mask; the reframe is how you take the mask off.
- [[Dialogue]] — interest-based framing is a structure a hard dialogue can survive.
