> **Kernel K187** — Cue engineering

Make the cue for a desired behaviour obvious, and the cue for an undesired behaviour invisible. The ground for this is Wendy Wood's habit research: a large share of daily behaviour is not decided but cued, and habits follow their cues rather than intentions.

### Protocol

*Steps follow the logic of the habit research; the steps themselves are the compendium's editorial protocol.*

1. Inventory the cues that already trigger the behaviour you want: place, time of day, people, preceding actions. Keep the ones that work.
2. Make the desired cue obvious and frictionless: the object in sight, the gear by the door, the practice tied to an existing routine.
3. Make the undesired cue invisible: the object out of sight, the app off the phone, the route that avoids the shop.
4. When a context change is coming, expect habits to reset; the transfer research says habits survive a move only when their cues survive it.

### Evidence

The base rate finding: in the diary studies, "between a third and a half of all behaviors listed were classified as habits, given that they were performed just about every day and usually in the same location. This estimate was greater in Study 2 (43%) than in Study 1 (35%)" ([Wood, Quinn & Kashy, 2002](https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/Wood.Quinn_.Kashy_.2002_Habits_in_everyday_life.pdf)). In Wood's own summary: "about 43 percent of what people do every day is repeated in the same context, usually while they are thinking about something else" ([Behavioral Scientist interview](https://behavioralscientist.org/good-habits-bad-habits-a-conversation-with-wendy-wood/)). The cue-dependence claim was tested directly in the university-transfer study of 115 students (Wood, Tam & Guerrero Witt, 2005): habits of exercising, newspaper reading and television watching survived the transfer when performance contexts stayed similar and dissolved when the cues changed ([APA Monitor summary](https://www.apa.org/monitor/jun05/habits)). What this research does not test: the deliberate redesign of cues as a self-directed practice, or any well-being outcome; the measured outcomes were habit persistence and behaviour frequency.
