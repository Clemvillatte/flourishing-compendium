> **Kernel K290** — Prosocial courage drill

Once a week, act on a situation where you see unfairness but would normally stay silent: speak up in a meeting when someone is talked over, challenge a biased comment, report a safety concern. The drill trains courage in its social form, and it is designed against a specific enemy: the presence of other people. In the classic smoke-filled-room experiment, "Ss were less likely to report the smoke when in the presence of passive others (10%) or in groups of 3 (38% of groups) than when alone (75%)" ([Latané and Darley 1968](https://ia800108.us.archive.org/view_archive.php?archive=/24/items/wikipedia-scholarly-sources-corpus/10.1037%252Fa0017364.zip&file=10.1037%252Fh0026570.pdf)). A standing weekly slot is a working response to that finding: it converts speaking up from a decision made under pressure into a decision already made. Dimensions moved: overt behaviour (the weekly act) and motivation (the sense of responsibility the act feeds).

### Protocol

1. Choose the weekly slot in advance. Use it for review and rehearsal; a safety concern is reported when it arises, not held for the slot. A standing slot gives the decision a home before the pressured moment arrives.
2. During the week, note one situation where you saw unfairness or risk and stayed silent. Write it down the same day. This is the generate step: the raw material is what you would otherwise have explained away.
3. Select one situation and act on it, or on its next occurrence: the name of the talked-over colleague repeated in the meeting, the one-line disagreement with the biased joke, the email reporting the safety concern.
4. Retain. After acting, note what you did, what it cost, and what it accomplished. Acts that went well become the template for the next week; acts that misfired get adjusted, not abandoned.
5. If no opportunity arises in a given week, rehearse one aloud: what you would say, to whom, in which words. Rehearsal keeps the habit warm for the week it is needed.

### Evidence

The bystander research explains why the drill schedules the act rather than leaving it to the moment. In the smoke-filled-room study, "seeing other people remain passive led Ss to decide the smoke was not dangerous" ([Latané and Darley 1968](https://ia800108.us.archive.org/view_archive.php?archive=/24/items/wikipedia-scholarly-sources-corpus/10.1037%252Fa0017364.zip&file=10.1037%252Fh0026570.pdf)). Training can move the numbers: in an experimental evaluation of the Bringing in the Bystander program, "three hundred and eighty-nine undergraduates participated and were randomly assigned to one of two treatment groups or a control group", and "participants in the treatment conditions showed improvements across measures of attitudes, knowledge, and behavior while the control group did not", with "most program effects" persisting "at 4- and 12-month follow-ups" ([Banyard, Moynihan and Plante, Journal of Community Psychology 2007](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ958176)). Scope limit: that trial measured attitudes, knowledge and self-reported bystander behavior in a campus sexual-violence-prevention context, not weekly drills of speaking up at work; this drill is a practice informed by that research, not a tested countermeasure.
