> **Kernel K268** — Preferred-indifferent hierarchy

List the things being pursued, wealth, reputation, health, comfort, achievement, rank them, and then ask which have quietly become conditions of worth. The tool is the Stoic doctrine of preferred indifferents: externals are neither good nor bad for happiness, but some are naturally preferred and worth selecting ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Stoicism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/)). Reach for it when the loss of a pursuit feels like the loss of oneself.

### What it rests on

The Stoic position, as the encyclopedia states it:

> "In sharp contrast with eudaimonist theories which regard health, wealth, reputation, etc. as “external goods”, the Stoics maintain that such items are neither good nor bad and, thus, “indifferent” (adiaphora/indifferentia) to human happiness." — [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Stoicism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/)

Indifference is not equality, though; the mainstream view assigns value by nature:

> "the mainstream Stoic view is that some indifferents – such as health, wealth, and reputation – are of a promoted or preferred type (proêgmena/praeposita), have value (axia/aestimatio), and are according to nature (kata phusin/secundum naturam), such that is appropriate or dutiful or one's proper function (kathêkon/officium) to select them over their opposites in normal circumstances" — [Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Stoicism](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/stoicism/)

The virtuous agent pursues health and takes the steps needed to obtain it, "even though her successful acquisition of health – her actually being healthy rather than unhealthy – makes no difference to her happiness and is neither good nor bad" (same entry). The ranking exercise is my editorial tool for finding where that boundary has been lost.

### Protocol

The ranking form is editorial; the test it applies is the Stoic one.

1. List the things being pursued: wealth, reputation, health, comfort, achievement, and whatever else competes for the week's effort.
2. Rank them by the actual share of time and worry they receive, not by professed priority.
3. For each, ask the Stoic question: preferred, or treated as necessary? A pursuit answered necessary is a candidate for the hierarchy's demotion.
4. Re-rank on the answer, and let virtue, the one thing the Stoics call good, sit above the list rather than on it.

### Evidence

The psychology nearest to the exercise concerns the centrality of financial aspirations:

> "Studies 1 and 2 use 2 methods to show that the relative centrality of money-related values and expectancies is negatively related to college students' well-being and mental health. Study 3, using a heterogeneous noncollege sample, extends these findings by showing that a high centrality of aspirations for financial success is associated with interview ratings of lower global adjustment and social productivity and more behavioral disorders." — [Kasser & Ryan, 1993, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 65(2), Abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8366427/)

What this evidence does not test: the cited research did not evaluate a ranking exercise, the associations are correlational, and the studies measure aspiration centrality and self-reported or rated adjustment, not Stoic hierarchy-building.
