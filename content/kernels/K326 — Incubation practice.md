> **Kernel K326** — Incubation practice

When stuck on a problem, step away from it: walk, shower, garden, sleep. The meta-analysis quoted below found a positive incubation effect overall, with the size of the effect depending on the task and on how the away-time is spent. Reach for it when more effort has stopped paying.

### Protocol

The steps are the compendium's practice form; the moderators the form leans on are quoted in the Evidence section.

1. Work the problem long enough to load it: the study below found longer preparation periods gave a greater incubation effect.
2. Step away to something with low cognitive demand, not to something demanding: high-demand fillers gave a smaller effect.
3. Return to the problem and give the fresh attempt a real chance before judging whether the break helped.

### Evidence

From the meta-analytic review:

> "The authors identified a positive incubation effect, with divergent thinking tasks benefiting more than linguistic and visual insight tasks from incubation. Longer preparation periods gave a greater incubation effect, whereas filling an incubation period with high cognitive demand tasks gave a smaller incubation effect." (Abstract, [Sio & Ormerod, 2009, Psychological Bulletin 135(1), 94-120](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19210055/))

> "Surprisingly, low cognitive demand tasks yielded a stronger incubation effect than did rest during an incubation period when solving linguistic insight problems." (same abstract)

What this evidence does not test: the mechanism. The review's authors offer differential invocation of knowledge-based versus strategic solution processes as an interpretation, not a settled mechanism, and the inherited framing that unconscious processing continues during the break is carried here as the practice's rationale rather than as an established explanation. The effect also varies by task type, and the review cautions that incubation "must be designed with care."
