> **Kernel K258** — Stakeholder mapping

Before a significant decision, list every person the decision lands on, and write one sentence per person about how they would be affected and what they would care about. The exercise is a tool for the perspective-taking facet of wise reasoning, as studied by Igor Grossmann's research program: reasoning about social conflicts that takes other people's viewpoints into account ([Grossmann, Gerlach & Denissen, 2016](https://www.psych.uni-goettingen.de/de/biopers/publications_department/grossmannetal2016/@@download/pdf_file/grossmann_gerlach_denissen_2016_wise_reasoning_in_the_face_of_everyday_life_challenges_spps.pdf)). Reach for it before decisions that other people will live with.

### What it rests on

The wise-reasoning research program measures how people reason about social conflicts, with three facets. From the diary study that tracked wise reasoning in daily life:

> "We conducted a daily diary study of wise reasoning (WR) by recording people's reflections on daily challenges in terms of three facets: intellectual humility, self-transcendence, and consideration of others' perspectives/compromise." — [Grossmann, Gerlach & Denissen, 2016, Social Psychological and Personality Science 7(7), Abstract](https://www.psych.uni-goettingen.de/de/biopers/publications_department/grossmannetal2016/@@download/pdf_file/grossmann_gerlach_denissen_2016_wise_reasoning_in_the_face_of_everyday_life_challenges_spps.pdf)

A stakeholder map is a concrete way of prompting the third facet before a decision rather than after it. The one-sentence-per-person format is editorial, not from the studies.

### Protocol

The steps are my own design; the underlying construct is measured, not prescribed, by the research.

1. Before the decision, list every person affected by it, including the people easy to forget: the absent, the distant, the ones who will not be in the room.
2. For each person, write one sentence about how they would be affected, and one about what they would care about in that effect.
3. Read the map back and check whose interests the first draft silently omitted, then decide with the map in view.

### Evidence

The association study, a random sample of Americans reasoning about social conflicts:

> "wise reasoning is associated with greater life satisfaction, less negative affect, better social relationships, less depressive rumination, more positive vs. negative words used in speech, and greater longevity." — [Grossmann, Na, Varnum, Kitayama & Nisbett, 2013, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General 142(3), Abstract](https://uwaterloo.ca/wisdom-and-culture-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/Grossmann_wellbeing.FINAL_.f.pdf)

That evidence is correlational: it does not show that a mapping practice produces those outcomes. The diary study measured state-level variation, finding "substantial and systematic intraindividual variability in WR, with wiser reasoning in the social versus nonsocial contexts", and that state-level wise reasoning predicted "a bigger-picture construal of the event, more positive (vs. negative) emotions, greater emotional complexity, lower emotional reactivity, less thought suppression, and more reappraisal and forgiveness" ([Grossmann et al., 2016, Abstract](https://www.psych.uni-goettingen.de/de/biopers/publications_department/grossmannetal2016/@@download/pdf_file/grossmann_gerlach_denissen_2016_wise_reasoning_in_the_face_of_everyday_life_challenges_spps.pdf)).

What this evidence does not test: neither study ran a stakeholder-mapping protocol, and neither measured the quality of the decisions that followed.

### A guided version

Not applicable: the practice is a list on paper made before a decision, not a recording.
