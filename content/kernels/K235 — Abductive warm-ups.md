> **Kernel K235** — Abductive warm-ups

When surprised by something, generate three possible explanations before settling on one. The practice counteracts the rush to certainty and trains the capacity to hold multiple hypotheses.

### Protocol

*Steps are the compendium's editorial protocol; the multiple-hypothesis discipline they train is Platt's first step below.*

1. On noticing the surprise, write down the surprise in one sentence; a surprise not named cannot be explained.
2. Generate three explanations before testing any of them; the number is a floor, and the explanations need not be good, only distinct.
3. Only then ask what evidence would distinguish among them; a hypothesis generated after the test is a rationalisation.

### Evidence

The discipline is Platt's strong inference, which he defines as applying certain steps "to every problem in science, formally and explicitly and regularly," beginning with "1) devising alternative hypotheses," followed by experiments whose possible outcomes "will, as nearly as possible, exclude one or more of the hypotheses," and ending with "carrying out the experiment so as to get a clean result" ([Platt, *Science*, 1964, PDF at WHOI](https://www.whoi.edu/cms/files/platt64sci_72743.pdf)). He adds that "in every field and in every laboratory we need to try to formulate multiple alternative hypotheses sharp enough to be capable of disproof." What this paper does not test: the three-explanation warm-up as an intervention, or any effect on everyday judgment; the argument is methodological and illustrated with historical cases in science, and the warm-up is an editorial adaptation.
