> **Kernel K329** — Revision loop

Take something you have made and revise it three times. Each pass asks a different question: Is it clearer? Is it more honest? Is it more alive? The three questions, and the order of the passes, are this practice's own. The craft research behind the order is described below.

## Protocol

1. Pick one piece and name its job. A paragraph, an email, a chapter. What is it for?
2. First pass, the shape. Ask: is it clearer? Move sections, cut whole ideas, find the argument. In Nancy Sommers' study, "The experienced writers describe their primary objective when revising as finding the form or shape of their argument" ([Sommers, 1980](https://openlab.citytech.cuny.edu/fywpd/files/2019/01/sommors-revision-strategies.pdf), p. 383). One experienced writer put it as "I am constantly chiseling and changing as I revise" (p. 383).
3. Second pass, the truth. Ask: is it more honest? Check each claim against what you actually know, and cut what you cannot stand behind.
4. Third pass, the words. Ask: is it more alive? Now the rewording, at the end, where the students in Sommers' study put it first: "They approach the revision process with what could be labeled as a 'thesaurus philosophy of writing'; the students consider the thesaurus a harvest of lexical substitutions and believe that most problems in their essays can be solved by rewording" (pp. 380-381).
5. Stop on purpose. Three passes is a loop, not a life sentence. Ship it, note one lesson, and apply that lesson to the next piece.

## A guided version

The revising happens on your own draft; no guided recording is cited for it here.

## Evidence

The loop borrows its logic from deliberate practice. In Ericsson's framework, "Deliberate practice includes activities that have been specially designed to improve the current level of performance," it is "a highly structured activity, the explicit goal of which is to improve performance," where "Specific tasks are invented to overcome weaknesses, and performance is carefully monitored to provide cues for ways to improve it further" ([Ericsson, Krampe and Tesch-Romer, 1993](https://review.firstround.com/content/files/images/blogs/freakonomics/pdf/deliberatepractice-psychologicalreview.pdf), p. 368). The revision research gives the loop its sequence. Sommers redefined revision "as a sequence of changes in a composition-changes which are initiated by cues and occur continually throughout the writing of a work" (p. 379), and found that "Lexical changes are the major revision activities of the students because economy is their goal" (pp. 380-381), while the experienced writers worked structure first.

What the research does not cover: neither study tested a three-pass loop. Ericsson's deliberate practice was defined for skill domains with teachers, full concentration, and immediate feedback, and his claims concern expert performance "not reached with less than 10 years of deliberate practice" (p. 372); a solo revision loop has no independent feedback and makes no such claim. Sommers' findings come from twenty freshmen and twenty experienced writers, a case study, not an experiment.
