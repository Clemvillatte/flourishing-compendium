> **Kernel K415** — Mindful eating

Mindful eating brings the full attention of a formal practice to the act of eating: the look, smell, and feel of food before it reaches the mouth, the changing tastes while chewing, and the body's hunger and fullness signals as they shift across a meal. It is the general form of the classic raisin exercise (see [[K390 — The Raisin meditation]]), extended from one surprising bite to ordinary meals.

### Protocol

1. Begin once with the raisin exercise, done as a discovery practice rather than a rule.
2. For the daily practice, pick one meal or snack. Before the first bite, pause: look at the food, take one conscious breath, and notice how hungry the body actually is, on a scale of one to ten.
3. Take the first three bites slowly, putting the utensil down between them, naming the taste and texture as they change.
4. Midway through the meal, pause again and re-rate hunger. Notice whether the urge to keep eating comes from the stomach, the eyes, the emotion of the moment, or plain habit.
5. Finish with thirty seconds of noticing the aftertaste and the change in the body's state. One mindful meal a day is the full dose.

### Evidence

The strongest single trial is the "9 Hunger" online RCT (Peitz & Warschburger, 2023, *Mindfulness*): adults practiced a brief daily mindful moment before eating, rating their different motivations to eat for two weeks. Emotional eating, external eating, and loss-of-control eating all improved relative to waitlist with moderate effects (d = 0.38 to 0.61), most secondary outcomes improved (d = 0.29 to 1.16), and the gains held at three months. A meta-analysis in youth aged 7 to 18 (Lai & Chang, 2026, *Mindfulness*; five trials) found reduced calorie intake, reduced total food intake, and reduced emotional eating, with no improvement in self-reported awareness or distraction during eating. For weight, a meta-analysis of ten mindful and intuitive eating RCTs (Fuentes Artiles et al., 2019, *Obesity Reviews*) found a small weight reduction versus nonintervention controls (−0.348 kg), no difference versus conventional diet programs, and no significant change in BMI or waist circumference. Tapper's 2022 review in *Nutrition Bulletin* is the honest cap: multi-component mindfulness-based interventions may help with disordered eating, whether their benefits exceed other treatments is unclear, and evidence for individual mindful-eating strategies alone is insufficient, with decentering and attention to food's sensory properties the most promising ingredients. For binge eating specifically, a 2025 meta-analysis found mindfulness-based interventions better than non-psychological controls at post-treatment (g = −0.65) and follow-up (g = −0.71), but negligible against active psychological controls (g = −0.05 and 0.13). Read together: the trials support a real shift in eating behaviors, while evidence for isolated mindful-eating strategies is insufficient and weight effects are small.
