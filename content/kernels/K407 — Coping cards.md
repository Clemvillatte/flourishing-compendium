Practice: **Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)**

> **Kernel K407** — Coping cards

When you have worked out a balanced response to a recurring distorted thought, in a journal, in a calm hour, with your full attention, write that response on a card small enough to carry. One thought, one card, in your own words.

Carry it in your wallet. Put copies where the trouble happens: the car, the fridge, the desk drawer. Read it when the original thought shows up hot. Stress can make a balanced response harder to recall exactly when you need it; the card keeps it available.

A card can hold a response ("one unproductive day does not make me a failure") or an action plan ("when I feel like hiding: 1. walk the block, 2. call someone, 3. ten minutes of cleaning"), or both sides, response on one side, plan on the other.

### Protocol

Adapted from Tolin, *Doing CBT* (1st ed., 2016), cognitive restructuring chapters, printed pp. 351–353; and Riggenbach, *The CBT Toolbox* (2nd ed., 2021), tools 5.24–5.25. The technique is standard CBT practice, commonly described in Beck-tradition clinical materials.

1. Identify the recurring situations, thoughts, or urges where support is most needed.
2. Write a concise, personally meaningful prompt for each: a balanced thought, a coping step, or a reminder. Use your own wording, and keep each card short enough to read at a glance.
3. One thought, one card. The card targets a specific hot thought, not a general category of worry.
4. Put the cards where they will be reached for: wallet, phone lock screen, the car, the fridge, the desk drawer.
5. Rehearse using a card when calm, so reaching for it becomes an automatic response to a known trigger.
6. Review after real use and revise wording that did not land. Retire cards that are no longer needed and add new ones as challenges change.

### Evidence

The technique's mechanism, in a clinical-education account:

> "When anxiety, low mood, or craving peaks, the thinking that fuels the problem crowds out the calmer, more balanced perspective a person can access when settled. A card externalizes that perspective so it is available at the exact moment recall is hardest."
> — [BehavioralTherapyTechniques.com, "Coping Cards"](https://behavioraltherapytechniques.com/techniques/coping-cards)

The Beck-tradition framing, from a resource that cites Beck's own clinical text:

> "Cognitive restructuring cards ... support CBT interventions by pairing negative automatic thoughts or core beliefs with more balanced alternatives, helping clients 'overlearn' adaptive responses so they remain accessible under stress (Beck, 2011)."
> — [PositivePsychology.com, "Coping Cards"](https://positivepsychology.com/coping-cards/)

The honest evidence scope:

> "Coping cards are a practical, widely used CBT tool and a common element of safety planning and relapse-prevention work. Direct standalone trial evidence is limited: they are best understood as a component skill that reinforces the techniques they summarize."
> — [BehavioralTherapyTechniques.com, "Coping Cards"](https://behavioraltherapytechniques.com/techniques/coping-cards)

What the sources do not test: the card is a delivery format for cognitive restructuring and coping-skills work; the restructuring itself, not the card, is the tested intervention.

### A guided version

The cards are written and carried by the practicer; free printable templates are carried at [BehavioralTherapyTechniques.com](https://behavioraltherapytechniques.com/worksheets/coping-cards) and [Pabau](https://pabau.com/templates/coping-cards/).
