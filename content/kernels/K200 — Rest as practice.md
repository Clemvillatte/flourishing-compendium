> **Kernel K200** — Rest as practice

Schedule one deliberate do-nothing period daily, 10 minutes, guilt-free. Rest is not laziness; it is the root of activity.

### Protocol

1. Choose the rest window after a work block or at a fixed daily slot; ten minutes, horizontal or reclined where possible. Target dimensions: Body (recovery) and Emotion (the guilt that rest must earn its keep).
2. Set the terms in advance: screens off, eyes closed or resting on something blank. The body is doing the work; you are letting the repair run rather than doing nothing.
3. If guilt shows up, treat it as part of the practice: notice it, name it, and let it sit alongside the rest without obeying it.
4. End by noting body tension before and after. Retain the time of day that produced the clearest drop; vary the length around that.

### Evidence

The nearest studied relatives are the micro-break and recovery literatures, not trials of a scheduled daily rest. A meta-analysis of experimental and quasi-experimental studies (22 independent samples, N = 2,335) found "statistically significant but small effects of micro-breaks in boosting vigor (d = .36, p < .001; k = 9, n = 913), reducing fatigue (d = .35, p < .001; k = 9, n = 803), and a non-significant effect on increasing overall performance" ([Albulescu et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36044424/)). The breaks bought energy and lower fatigue, not measurable output.

The psychological side of stepping away has its own meta-analysis: across 30 studies with 34 interventions (N = 3,725), "interventions showed a significant positive effect on detachment from work (d = 0.36) on average" ([Karabinski et al., 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34096763/)). Detachment is the off-duty mind that a protected rest block trains.

The guilt this kernel names has a philosophical witness. Bertrand Russell, in the essay that argued for shorter working hours: "I think that there is far too much work done in the world, that immense harm is caused by the belief that work is virtuous" ([Russell, 1932](https://harpers.org/archive/1932/10/in-praise-of-idleness/)).
