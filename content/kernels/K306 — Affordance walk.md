> **Kernel K306** — Affordance walk

Walk through an environment and notice what each object, surface, and space invites you to do: a chair invites sitting, a path invites walking, a low wall invites leaning. The exercise trains attention to the relational structure of perception: we perceive the world in terms of what it offers for action. This is the core insight of Gibson's ecological psychology and Merleau-Ponty's phenomenology.

### Protocol

The walk is a slow reading of a familiar route; the study behind the insight is quoted below. Intended focus: Emotion, Perception and Environment.

1. Walk through an ordinary environment and, for each object, surface and space, ask what it invites: a chair invites sitting, a path invites walking, a low wall invites leaning. Name the invitation in a word or two.
2. Notice the invitations that come with your body: the step that invites your stride, the gap that invites your hand. The invitation is a relation between the thing and you, not a property of the thing alone.
3. Pick three invitations you usually obey without noticing, and three you decline, and ask what the declining says about you as much as about the place. No notes needed; the walk itself is the record.

### Evidence

From the study of tools and perceived distance:

> "These results suggest that reachability serves as a metric for perception." (Abstract, [Witt, Proffitt & Epstein, 2005, Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance 31(5), 880-888](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16262485/))

What this evidence does not test: the walk. Participants judged distances to targets while holding or not holding a reaching tool; the same targets looked closer when they were within reach with the tool, and a third experiment found the effect only appeared when the perceiver intended to reach. The finding is the kernel's insight compressed: perception is scaled to what the environment offers your possible action. But the abstract reports nothing about walking, about naming invitations, or about any change from practicing the exercise. What it supports is the premise the walk trains: the world you see is already a world in terms of action.
