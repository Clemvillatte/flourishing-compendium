> **Kernel K361** — Institutional feedback loop

In an institution you are part of, create a channel for honest feedback about decisions. The channel does not need to be formal. It needs to be safe enough that people will tell you what you do not want to hear. Research on organisational learning (Argyris) shows that institutions fail not from lack of information but from the suppression of information that challenges existing assumptions. Argyris's own summary of the failure: "the inability to uncover errors and other unpleasant truths arises from faulty organizational learning" ([Argyris, Harvard Business Review 1977](https://cmapspublic3.ihmc.us/rid=1NT8Y36L7-185BXTZ-1GHR/double-loop-learning-in-organizations%20Argylis.pdf)). Dimensions: overt behaviour (the channel built and kept open) and self (the willingness to receive what comes through it).

### Protocol

1. Generate the channel: a standing question attached to each decision, asked the same way each time. "What did this decision get wrong, and what would you change before we do it again?" A fixed question asked regularly beats an open invitation asked once.
2. Select the safety: answer, visibly, the first piece of bad news that arrives through the channel, with thanks and without defense. The channel's safety is set less by policy than by what happens to the first person who uses it.
3. Retain the record: what came in, what changed because of it, and what was heard but not acted on, with the reason. The unacted-on list, kept honestly, is what keeps the channel from becoming decoration.
4. Escalate to the second loop when the feedback keeps returning the same theme: the problem is the assumption under the decision, and the inquiry must question "the underlying policies and goals as well as its own program", the second and more comprehensive inquiry Argyris calls double loop learning ([Argyris 1977](https://cmapspublic3.ihmc.us/rid=1NT8Y36L7-185BXTZ-1GHR/double-loop-learning-in-organizations%20Argylis.pdf)).

### Evidence

The suppression mechanism is documented in Argyris's case material: middle managers "knew that, in their company, bad news would not be well received at the upper levels if it was not accompanied by suggestions for positive action", so they "spent much time in composing memos that communicated the realities yet would not be too shocking to top managers" ([Argyris 1977](https://cmapspublic3.ihmc.us/rid=1NT8Y36L7-185BXTZ-1GHR/double-loop-learning-in-organizations%20Argylis.pdf)). The safety side is measured: psychological safety is "a shared belief held by members of a team that the team is safe for interpersonal risk taking", and "results of a study of 51 work teams in a manufacturing company... show that team psychological safety is associated with learning behavior, but team efficacy is not, when controlling for team psychological safety", with "learning behavior mediat[ing] between team psychological safety and team performance" ([Edmondson, Administrative Science Quarterly 1999](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/15341_Readings/Organizational_Learning_and_Change/Edmondson_1999_Psychological_safety.pdf)). Scope limits: Edmondson's study is one manufacturing company, the Argyris cases are illustrative rather than experimental, and neither study tests this kernel's standing-question procedure; what the evidence supports is the mechanism, safety enabling learning, not any particular channel design.
