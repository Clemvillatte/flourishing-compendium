> **Kernel K183** — Behavioral experiment

Test a belief through action. If you believe "I cannot speak up in meetings," speak up once and observe what happens. The technique comes from cognitive therapy, where it is a core tool: "Behavioural experiments are experiential exercises used in Cognitive Behavioural Therapy to drive cognitive change by testing patients' idiosyncratic, emotionally linked beliefs" ([Warnock-Parkes et al., 2025](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/179644/2/Driving%20cognitive%20change%20a%20guide%20to%20behavioural%20experiments%20in%20cognitive%20therapy%20for%20anxiety%20disorders%20and%20PTSD.pdf)).

## Protocol

The clinical guide's seven steps, quoted:

1. "Identify the beliefs to test;"
2. "Identify the best situation to test the belief;"
3. "Identify observable predictions;"
4. "Identify safety behaviours and other maintaining processes to drop;"
5. "Carry out the experiment;"
6. "Review the outcome and generalized learning;"
7. "Build on the learning by planning follow-up experiments."

The step most people skip is step 3, and the guide is blunt about it: "it is vital that patients make specific observable predictions about what they think will happen in a given situation (e.g. the prediction 'I will be boring' could be made more specific and observable by adding 'Other people will look away and stop speaking to me')" (same article). Write the prediction down before you act; the sentence "I cannot speak up" predicts something observable or it predicts nothing. The meetings example is this practice's own.

## A guided version

The experiment happens in the meeting, not on the page; no guided recording is cited for it here.

## Evidence

Why action rather than argument: in the clinical model, "this experiential approach is often more powerful than simply providing psychoeducation or discussion," because "This allows patients to learn directly through experience" (same article). The safety-behaviour step has a theory behind it: "in all cognitive models of anxiety, safety behaviours and other key processes (such as self-focused attention or hypervigilance to threat) are proposed to maintain negative beliefs" (same article). The belief you are testing may be maintained by the very precautions you take while testing it; that is why step 4 exists.

What the guide does not establish: it is clinical guidance for therapists delivering cognitive therapy for anxiety disorders and PTSD, drawn from the authors' treatment programs. It does not test self-run experiments by people treating their own ordinary beliefs, and the meetings example is this page's own.
