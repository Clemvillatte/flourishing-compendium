> **Kernel K400** — Word repetition

Start with a neutral word such as "milk." Say it once, slowly, and notice what shows up with it: images, sensations, maybe a taste. Then say the word out loud, over and over, as fast as you can, for thirty seconds.

Notice what happens by the end. Most people find the images have thinned out, and what remains is the sound of the word and the movements of speaking it — a word being produced, rather than a scene being delivered.

If it was tolerable with the neutral word, try it with a word that usually hooks you — "failure," "weak," "danger." The aim is not to prove the word false or to force its charge away, only to experience it for a moment as a word.

Adapted from Stoddard & Afari, *The Big Book of ACT Metaphors* (2014), printed pp. 71–72; see also Blackledge, *[[Cognitive defusion|Cognitive Defusion]] in Practice* (2017), ch. 7, which discusses the word-repetition study of Masuda, Hayes, Sackett & Twohig (2004).

### Evidence

In the original study, eight undergraduates used alternating-treatment designs to compare rapid word repetition with distraction and with thought control. Thirty seconds of repeating a one-word version of a negative self-referential thought reduced both its discomfort and its believability more than either comparison, and control studies suggested the effect was not just demand characteristics. A follow-up parametric study mapped the time course: discomfort bottoms out after three to ten seconds of rapid repetition, while believability takes twenty to thirty seconds. A small randomised trial on repetition rate found that saying the word about once per second best maintained the reductions at one-month follow-up. Honest caveats: the samples are small, mostly non-distressed students, and single-case designs, so treat this as a promising, well-mechanised technique rather than a validated treatment.
