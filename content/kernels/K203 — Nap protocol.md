> **Kernel K203** — Nap protocol

A deliberately short daytime sleep, taken early enough in the afternoon that it refreshes without stealing the night. The modern evidence base is the work of Leon Lack and colleagues at Flinders University, whose dose-finding study fixed the shape most sleep clinicians now recommend: short, and before mid-afternoon. Reach for it on an afternoon when sleepiness is dragging attention and performance, not as a substitute for a full night.

### Protocol

1. Time it before the post-lunch dip ends: for most people that means finishing the nap by about 3 p.m. The Sleep Foundation states the rule plainly: "Experts typically recommend that adults take naps eight or more hours before bedtime. For most people, that means napping before 3 p.m." ([Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/napping)).
2. Keep it short: "In general, the best nap length for adults is about 20 minutes and no longer than 30 minutes" ([Sleep Foundation](https://www.sleepfoundation.org/napping)). Set an alarm so the length is a decision, not an accident.
3. If you wake groggy, do not extend the nap: the grogginess is sleep inertia, and it fades on its own within roughly half an hour; wait it out before doing anything demanding.
4. If an afternoon nap repeatedly pushes your bedtime later, drop it rather than moving it later, because a late nap trades tonight's sleep for this afternoon's.

### Evidence

The duration rule comes from Brooks and Lack's nap-length study (24 healthy young adults, five-hour night, 3 p.m. naps of 5, 10, 20 or 30 minutes of measured sleep; *Sleep*, 2006, 29(6):831–840), in which the 10-minute nap improved every measure immediately with no inertia, while the 30-minute nap hurt performance before helping it. A later review summarises the comparison: "Brooks and Lack compared four different short, afternoon nap lengths and found that while a 10-min nap resulted in immediate performance improvements, a 30-min nap did not provide improvements until 35 mins or up to 95 mins after waking, depending on the task" ([Hilditch & McHill, *Nature and Science of Sleep*, 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6710480/)). The same review is candid about limits: "sleep inertia is less likely to occur after short naps (≤30 mins)," yet "a short nap, depending on the context, does not always avoid sleep inertia." The timing rule (before 3 p.m.) rests on expert sleep-medicine guidance about protecting nighttime sleep pressure, not on randomised trials. What these studies do not test: whether napping improves well-being or reduces suffering — the outcomes measured were lab alertness and performance, mostly after a deliberately restricted night.

### A guided version

None needed: a nap has no instructions beyond an alarm. A plain timer set to 20 minutes is the whole apparatus.
