> **Kernel K338** — Restorative conversation

When you have caused harm, initiate a structured conversation: acknowledge what you did, describe the impact as you understand it, express genuine regret, and ask what repair would look like. Research on restorative justice shows that this process, when voluntary and sincere, reduces recidivism and improves outcomes for both parties.

### Protocol

The conversation has four stations; the outcome study behind the form is quoted below. Intended focus: Self and Connection.

1. When you have caused harm, initiate the conversation rather than waiting to be asked, and open with the acknowledgement: what you did, stated plainly, without the passive voice and without the word but.
2. Describe the impact as you understand it, and let the harmed person correct the record; express genuine regret for the harm itself, not only for the trouble it caused you, and then ask the repair question: what would repair look like from where you stand.
3. Treat their answer as the working plan, not as an opening position; repair that the other party defines tends to be repair that holds. One note afterwards on what was asked and what you committed to.

### Evidence

From the matched study of victim-offender mediation:

> "When the meetings were held in the community post-release, however, participants were significantly more likely to spend a longer period of time under supervision in the community without returning to custody and were less likely to be revoked than their matched counterparts." (Abstract, [Stewart, Thompson, Beaudette, Buck, Laframboise & Petrellis, 2018, International Journal of Offender Therapy and Comparative Criminology 62(12), 3910-3927](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29392973/))

What this evidence does not test: the four stations. The study compared 122 Canadian federal offenders who took part in facilitated face-to-face mediation with a matched sample of non-participants; when the meetings happened in the community after release, participants stayed longer under supervision without returning to custody, and were less likely to be revoked; when they happened while incarcerated, the difference was not significant. The design is matched, not randomized, the authors themselves flag uncontrolled matching factors, and the abstract reports nothing about the structure of the conversation, sincerity, or both-party outcomes. What it supports is the kernel's wager in its honest form: participation in the restorative process went with better outcomes for those who went through it, with the timing mattering.
