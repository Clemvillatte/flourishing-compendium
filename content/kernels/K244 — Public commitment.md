> **Kernel K244** — Public commitment

Declare an intention publicly, or to someone whose opinion matters to you. The anticipated social cost of inconsistency is the mechanism, and it has a classic demonstration: the Iowa City energy-conservation studies of Pallak and colleagues, where public commitment outperformed private commitment even though the promised publicity never happened.

### Protocol

*Steps distilled from the Pallak research design; the distillation is the compendium's.*

1. Put the intention into words, to a person or an audience, not only to yourself.
2. Attach your name to it: the commitment should be attributable to you, not anonymous.
3. Expect to be asked about it, and let the asking do the work; the commitment needs no enforcement beyond the possibility of being seen to drop it.

### Evidence

In the Iowa City program, "residents were told that the results of the study would be publicized along with the names of participants." After one month, "participants who had made a public commitment had reduced their natural gas and electricity consumption by between 10 percent and 20 percent," while "there was no significant change in energy consumption for the group which made a private commitment." The striking follow-up: "participants who made a public commitment continued to consume less electricity and natural gas, even though they had been told that the study had concluded after one month, and that their names would not be publicized" ([Tools of Change case study of Pallak, Cook & Sullivan, 1980](https://toolsofchange.com/en/case-studies/detail/14/); original: Pallak, M. S., Cook, D. A., & Sullivan, J. J. (1980), "Commitment and energy conservation," in L. Bickman (ed.), *Applied Social Psychology Annual*, 235–253). What the study does not show: effects on any intention other than household energy use, or any well-being outcome; the persistence figure beyond the follow-up period is not established.
