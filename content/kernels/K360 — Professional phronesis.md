> **Kernel K360** — Professional phronesis

For a recurring professional decision, find the rule or protocol that governs it, then ask: when would following the rule produce a worse outcome than using judgement? The exercise is a proposed reflection on professional discretion, the working application of phronesis in institutional life. Reach for it when a rule you follow daily starts to feel like a bad fit.

### Protocol

1. Pick a recurring decision at work that a rule, script or protocol governs: an assessment form, a triage path, a teaching script, a client-onboarding checklist.
2. Write down what the rule assumes about the situations it covers, and whom it protects.
3. Review one concrete case: what evidence suggests the rule fitted or missed the situation? Note where discretion is permitted and where review or escalation is required.
4. Draft the one-line guidance you would give a newcomer for this decision: when to follow the rule exactly, when to use authorised discretion, and when to ask for review. Keep the guidance, keep the rule.

### Evidence

The kernel sits on two research traditions. On the phronesis side, Schwartz's argument for practical wisdom in professional life:

> "The will to do the right thing, and the skill to determine what the right thing is, together comprise what [[T002 — Aristotle|Aristotle]] called phronesis, or practical wisdom. Practical wisdom, thought Aristotle, is the master virtue, helping us to use our other virtues (honesty, kindness, courage, humility, perseverance, and the like) at the right time, and in the right way."
> — [Barry Schwartz, "Why a Good Teacher Must Be a Wise Teacher", Jubilee Centre Insight Series, PDF](https://www.jubileecentre.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/SchwartzB.pdf)

The same essay names what the exercise works against:

> "Rules are the enemy of developing the skilled judgment that professionals need, and incentives are the enemy of the motivation to do the right thing because it’s the right thing. In other words, rules and incentives are the enemies of practical wisdom. And the more we rely on them, the more we will drive wisdom out of professional practices."
> — [Schwartz, same PDF](https://www.jubileecentre.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/SchwartzB.pdf)

On the expertise side, Stuart Dreyfus's five-stage model of adult skill acquisition describes the shift from rule-following to situational judgement ([Dreyfus 2004, PDF](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/facdev-medicine/files/2012/03/Dreyfus-skill-level.pdf)):

> "Normally, the instruction process begins with the instructor decomposing the task environment into context-free features that the beginner can recognize without the desired skill."
> — [Stuart E. Dreyfus, "The Five-Stage Model of Adult Skill Acquisition", Bulletin of Science, Technology & Society 24(3), PDF](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/facdev-medicine/files/2012/03/Dreyfus-skill-level.pdf)

> "The expert not only sees what needs to be achieved; thanks to his or her vast repertoire of situational discriminations, he or she also sees immediately how to achieve this goal."
> — [Dreyfus 2004, same PDF](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/facdev-medicine/files/2012/03/Dreyfus-skill-level.pdf)

The model's stages run novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient, expert; the novice follows context-free rules, the expert responds to whole situations. The model describes skill acquisition, and the essay argues for ethical judgement; this exercise is a reflection informed by both accounts, not a tested training method.

What the sources do not test: Schwartz's case is an essay, not an experiment; the Dreyfus model is a descriptive framework, not a tested intervention; and neither source evaluated the exercise on this page.

### A guided version

The practice is a written reflection; no recording is needed.
