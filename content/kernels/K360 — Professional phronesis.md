> **Kernel K360** — Professional phronesis

For a recurring professional decision, find the rule or protocol that governs it, then ask: when would following the rule produce a worse outcome than using judgement? The exercise trains professional discretion, which is the working application of phronesis in institutional life. Reach for it when a rule you follow daily starts to feel like a bad fit.

### Protocol

1. Pick a recurring decision at work that a rule, script or protocol governs: an assessment form, a triage path, a teaching script, a client-onboarding checklist.
2. Write down what the rule assumes about the situations it covers, and whom it protects.
3. Recall two or three real cases where following the rule produced a worse outcome than judgement would have. What did the person who bent the rule see that the rule could not?
4. Draft the one-line maxim you would give a newcomer for this decision: when to follow the rule exactly, and when to depart from it. Keep both the maxim and the rule.

### Evidence

The kernel sits on two research traditions. On the phronesis side, Schwartz's argument for practical wisdom in professional life:

> "The will to do the right thing, and the skill to determine what the right thing is, together comprise what Aristotle called phronesis, or practical wisdom. Practical wisdom, thought Aristotle, is the master virtue, helping us to use our other virtues (honesty, kindness, courage, humility, perseverance, and the like) at the right time, and in the right way."
> — [Barry Schwartz, "Why a Good Teacher Must Be a Wise Teacher", Jubilee Centre Insight Series, PDF](https://www.jubileecentre.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/SchwartzB.pdf)

The same essay names what the exercise works against:

> "Rules are the enemy of developing the skilled judgment that professionals need, and incentives are the enemy of the motivation to do the right thing because it's the right thing. In other words, rules and incentives are the enemies of practical wisdom. And the more we rely on them, the more we will drive wisdom out of professional practices."
> — [Schwartz, same PDF](https://www.jubileecentre.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/SchwartzB.pdf)

On the expertise side, the Dreyfus five-stage model of skill acquisition describes the gap between rule-following and judgement as a matter of stage, not effort:

> "Normally, the instruction process begins with the instructor decomposing the task environment into context-free features that the beginner can recognize without the desired skill."
> — [Dreyfus & Dreyfus, five-stage model text, Bulletin of Science, Technology & Society reprint, PDF](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/facdev-medicine/files/2012/03/Dreyfus-skill-level.pdf)

> "The expert not only sees what needs to be achieved; thanks to his or her vast repertoire of situational discriminations, he or she also sees immediately how to achieve this goal."
> — [same reprint, PDF](https://www.bumc.bu.edu/facdev-medicine/files/2012/03/Dreyfus-skill-level.pdf)

The stages run novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient, expert; the novice follows context-free rules, the expert responds to whole situations. Professional discretion lives somewhere on that ladder, and the exercise is a way of noticing where.

What the sources do not test: Schwartz's case is an essay, not an experiment; the Dreyfus model is a descriptive framework, not a tested intervention; and neither source evaluated the exercise on this page.

### A guided version

The practice is a written reflection; no recording is needed.
