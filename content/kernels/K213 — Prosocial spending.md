> **Kernel K213** — Prosocial spending

Spend a small amount of money on someone else: a coffee carried to a colleague, a book posted to a friend, a stranger's bus fare. The claim the practice rests on is one of the cleanest in the well-being literature: money spent on others tends to buy more happiness than the same money spent on yourself. Reach for it when a little money is burning a hole in your pocket, or when giving has drifted into abstraction and needs a body.

### Protocol

The steps are this practice's own exercise, assembled from the experiments cited below; the lab gave participants a windfall and a deadline, and this page turns that into a household practice. The practice moves the Overt behaviour and Affect dimensions.

1. Choose a person and a small amount. In the original experiment participants received five or twenty dollars in the morning with an instruction to spend it on themselves or on someone else by 5 p.m.; the size of the sum did not matter, the direction did. Editorial addition, not from the carriers: start with an amount whose loss you would not notice.
2. Spend it on a specific other person by the end of the day, and keep what you did plain: a purchase, a gift, a paid fare. The evidence concerns money spent to benefit others, not donations of time.
3. Editorial addition, not from the carriers: notice the effect once, without turning the noticing into the goal. One round is one round; the practice is repeatable by nature.

### Evidence

The original finding, in the authors' own abstract:

> "Although much research has examined the effect of income on happiness, we suggest that how people spend their money may be at least as important as how much money they earn. Specifically, we hypothesized that spending money on other people may have a more positive impact on happiness than spending money on oneself. Providing converging evidence for this hypothesis, we found that spending more of one's income on others predicted greater happiness both cross-sectionally (in a nationally representative survey study) and longitudinally (in a field study of windfall spending). Finally, participants who were randomly assigned to spend money on others experienced greater happiness than those assigned to spend money on themselves."
> — [Dunn, Aknin & Norton (2008), *Science* 319(5870), PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18356530/)

The cross-cultural replication extends it beyond wealthy countries:

> "In Study 1, survey data from 136 countries were examined and showed that prosocial spending is associated with greater happiness around the world, in poor and rich countries alike."
> — [Aknin et al. (2013), *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 104(4), PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23421360/)

The same 2013 paper reports that participants "randomly assigned to buy items for charity reported higher levels of positive affect than participants assigned to buy the same items for themselves, even when this prosocial spending did not provide an opportunity to build or strengthen social ties". What the studies do not test: they measure self-reported happiness and positive affect around single purchases and recalled purchases, not a household practice of routine small giving, and the page's schedule is its own assembly; the windfall experiment also assigned spending for a single day.

### A guided version

None: the practice is a purchase, not a meditation, and no official guided track for it exists.
