> **Kernel K308** — Object-use phenomenology

Take an everyday object (a cup, a pen, a door handle). Use it with full attention to the experience: the weight, the texture, the temperature, the way your hand shapes itself to receive it. The exercise aims at showing that perception is not passive reception but active, embodied engagement; the research below is what makes that more than a slogan, since tool use measurably updates the body's action map.

### Protocol

Steps are a practice form; the research behind it is quoted below. Intended focus: Attention and Embodiment.

1. Take an everyday object (a cup, a pen, a door handle) and use it for real: pour, write, open.
2. Attend to the experience while using, not before or after: the weight, the texture, the temperature, the way the hand shapes itself to the object's shape.
3. Notice when attention shifts from the object-as-thing to the object-as-used; the felt difference between staring at a cup and drinking from it is the point of the exercise. Repeat with one object a day rather than many at once.

### Evidence

From the research on tools and the body schema:

> "We suggest that overall these findings speak in favour of genuine tool-use-dependent plasticity of the body representation for the control of action." (Abstract, [Cardinali, Jacobs, Brozzoli, Frassinetti, Roy & Farnè, 2012, Experimental Brain Research 218(2), 259-271](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22349501/))

What this evidence does not test: the attention exercise. The reviewed experiments measure how tool use changes movement kinematics and somatosensory localization, in laboratory tool paradigms like mechanical grabbers; they do not test whether attending to a cup changes perception. What the research supports is the exercise's premise: using an object changes the body representation that controls action, which is the "active, embodied engagement" the exercise points at. The claim that perception becomes richer through such attention is the practice's own wager, checked in step 3.
