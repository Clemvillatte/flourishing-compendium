> **Kernel K350** — Seasonal mourning

At the change of each season, notice what has been lost: a species that no longer appears, a waterway that has changed, a temperature that has shifted. Let the mourning run without rushing to fix anything. The seasonal timing is this practice's own; the grief it names has two research names, ecological grief and solastalgia, and both were coined by people watching home environments change.

## Protocol

1. Pick the season marker. First frost, the week the creek runs dry, the month the birds do not return.
2. List the losses plainly. What no longer appears? What has changed since last year? Write them down; solastalgia research notes that "The most poignant moments of solastalgia occur when individuals directly experience the transformation of a loved environment. Watching land clearing (tree removal) or building demolition, for example, can be the cause of a profound distress that can be manifest as intense visceral pain and mental anguish" ([Albrecht, 2005](https://obsant.eu/entrees/2005Solastalgia.pdf), p. 45).
3. Let it hurt. The researchers behind ecological grief argue that "grief is a natural and legitimate response to ecological loss, and one that may become more common as climate impacts worsen" ([Cunsolo and Ellis, 2018](https://www.uwosh.edu/sirt/wp-content/uploads/sites/86/2020/04/Cunsolo-and-Ellis-2018.pdf), p. 275). Treating the grief as illness is not part of the practice.
4. Separate the mourning from the fixing. This sitting is for mourning only; action, if any, gets its own hour.
5. Name one thing to watch through the next season. A species, a patch, a creek.

## A guided version

The mourning runs on your own losses, where you live; no guided recording is cited for it here.

## Evidence

The term for the grief: "the grief felt in relation to experienced or anticipated ecological losses, including the loss of species, ecosystems and meaningful landscapes due to acute or chronic environmental change" ([Cunsolo and Ellis, 2018](https://www.uwosh.edu/sirt/wp-content/uploads/sites/86/2020/04/Cunsolo-and-Ellis-2018.pdf), p. 275). The same paper reports three contexts for this grief: "grief associated with physical ecological losses (land, ecosystems and species), grief associated with disruptions to environmental knowledge and loss of identity, and grief associated with anticipated future ecological losses" (p. 276). The term for the home-sickness: "solastalgia is the pain or sickness caused by the loss or lack of solace and the sense of isolation connected to the present state of one's home and territory" ([Albrecht, 2005](https://obsant.eu/entrees/2005Solastalgia.pdf), p. 44).

What the research does not cover: both papers are conceptual and qualitative. Cunsolo and Ellis synthesize fieldwork in Northern Canada and the Australian Wheatbelt; Albrecht's interviews were with Upper Hunter residents near open-cut mining. Neither evaluated this seasonal routine.
