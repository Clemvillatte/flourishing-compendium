> **Kernel K335** — The witness practice

Once a week, tell someone a story from your week and have them tell it back. The retelling gives you a comparison to study: what the listener kept, dropped, and reweighted. Reading that comparison as a mirror of your own emphasis and omission is the practice's editorial premise, not a demonstrated effect; the retelling also reflects the listener's attention. It suits anyone curious about the narrative choices quietly constructing a sense of who they are.

### Protocol

The steps are my practice form; the finding the form leans on is quoted in the Evidence section.

1. Choose one story from the week, five minutes or less, and tell it to a willing listener straight through.
2. Ask the listener to retell it back to you in their own words. No corrections during the retelling; save corrections for the comparison afterward.
3. Compare the two versions: what survived, what vanished, what changed valence, what the retelling centered instead. Note one framing choice to watch for next week.

### Evidence

From the study of listener effects on speakers' long-term memory:

> "Because variations in the social context of recollection affect how we tell others about events, such variations can also come to influence long-term memory." (Abstract, [Pasupathi, Stallworth & Murdoch, 1998, Discourse Processes 26(1), 1-25](https://doi.org/10.1080/01638539809545035))

> "Our results show that attentive listeners facilitate long-term memory, whereas situations with distracted listeners are difficult to distinguish from the situations with no listener and with no recounting at all." (same abstract)

What this evidence does not test: the practice. The study measured recall of movie excerpts after recounting to attentive or distracted listeners; it did not test a weekly retelling exercise, and the practice's claim to reveal the teller's framing choices is my rationale, not a tested outcome.
