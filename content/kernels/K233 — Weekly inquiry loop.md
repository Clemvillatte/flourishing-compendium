> **Kernel K233** — Weekly inquiry loop

One question a week, one small test, one honest look at the data. On Monday, name something you do not know about your own life and could test: does coffee past noon cost you sleep, does a morning walk change the afternoon? Design the test, run it, and on Friday read what came back. The design borrows from N-of-1 trials, the single-person experiments medicine uses to learn what works for one person.

## Protocol

1. On Monday, name the question. One thing you do not know and could test this week.
2. Design the test with three parts: the variation (what you will change, and when it is on or off), the measure (what you will count or rate, and how), and the window (how long each period runs, and how many repeats). The research design behind this: "The N-of-1 trial is a replicate (multi-crossover) design whereby each individual receives each treatment (including potentially a control treatment) multiple times, usually in a randomized order. Outcomes are recorded one or more times during each treatment period (e.g., daily for a treatment that is given for 1 week)" (Konigorski, Ried-Larsen and Schmid, quoted verbatim).
3. Vary one thing at a time, and let a coin decide the order. The health psychology review puts it plainly: "We recommend that N-of-1 trials feature randomization or at least counterbalancing" (Shaffer et al., quoted verbatim).
4. On Friday, read the data. Compare on-days and off-days before concluding. One week is a pilot, not a verdict: "Repetition of treatment exposure is also integral to the success of N-of-1 trials, and number of repetitions is analogous to sample size in conventional RCTs" (Shaffer et al., quoted verbatim).
5. Write one sentence of what you now know, and what to test next week.

## A guided version

The loop runs on your own week and your own question; no guided recording is cited for it here.

## Evidence

Why repeats matter: "Because each individual receives each treatment multiple times, it is possible to obtain statistically valid estimates of individual treatment effects" (Konigorski et al., quoted verbatim). The design answers for one person, not for a population: "Thus, N-of-1 trials should be considered a supplement to, and not a replacement for, the standard parallel-arm RCT" (Konigorski et al., quoted verbatim). A systematic review of 54 N-of-1 trial publications with psychological or health behavior outcomes concluded that "N-of-1 randomized trials hold promise for precision therapeutics in health psychology, however systematic review of existing N-of-1 trials shows that they lack methodologic and statistical rigor" (Shaffer et al., 2018, quoted verbatim).

What the research does not cover: no study tested this weekly loop. The trials in these reviews examined treatments, mostly clinical ones, in multi-period designs that usually run longer than a week. A Monday-to-Friday loop is pilot-scale, with the limits the reviewers flag: too few repetitions, and no randomization when you skip the coin.
