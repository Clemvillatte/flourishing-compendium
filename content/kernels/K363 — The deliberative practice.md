> **Kernel K363** — The deliberative practice

In a group decision you are part of, make sure every voice is heard before the verdict. Structure the talk so it happens: silent thinking time first, then a round where each person speaks once before anyone speaks twice. Ask the quietest person what they think. Invite dissent directly, and mean it. The evidence backs the structure, not just the sentiment: groups where a few people dominated the conversation were measurably worse at the tasks they faced.

### Protocol

1. Before the discussion opens, state the question in one sentence and say how the decision will be made.
2. Give the group two or three minutes of silence to think. This stops people from drafting their answer while pretending to listen.
3. Run a round: each person speaks once, briefly, before anyone speaks twice. Passing is allowed, but you return to passers with a light "anything to add now?"
4. Invite the specific people who have not spoken, by name, for their view.
5. Ask for dissent explicitly: "What would make us wrong here?" Real disagreement counts; an assigned devil's advocate does not do the same work.
6. Only after the round and the dissent do you open free discussion, then take the decision, and state it aloud with the reasons.

### Evidence

Two findings carry the practice. First, Woolley and colleagues, in two studies with 699 people working in groups of two to five, found a general collective intelligence factor that explained group performance across tasks; it was "not strongly correlated with the average or maximum individual intelligence of group members" but was "correlated with the average social sensitivity of group members, the equality in distribution of conversational turn-taking, and the proportion of females in the group". In their words, "groups where a few people dominated the conversation were less collectively intelligent than those with a more equal distribution of conversational turn-taking" (Woolley et al. 2010). Second, Nemeth's program of research on dissent found that authentic dissent beat role-played devil's advocacy at "stimulating a greater proportion of original thoughts", "considering the opposite position", and direct attitude change (Nemeth et al. 2001), and her summary of the field is that dissent "stimulates thought that is broader, that takes in more information and that, on balance, leads to better decisions and more creative solutions" (Nemeth, Berkeley).

### A guided version

The [Round Robin sheet from the Brushy Fork Institute](https://legacy.berea.edu/brushy-fork-institute/wp-content/uploads/sites/38/2020/08/Facilitation-Techniques-for-Group-Discussions_June2020.pdf) is a one-page facilitation guide built around hearing every voice: when to use it (quiet members, a dominating speaker, checking for consensus), what the facilitator does, and how to run the rounds.

## Related concepts

- [[Deliberative democracy]] — the theory that legitimacy comes from reasons citizens can share; this kernel is the facilitation that makes deliberation actually happen.
- [[Collective intelligence]] — the measured ability of a group to perform across tasks; equal turn-taking is one of its few reliable predictors.
- [[Groupthink theory]] — the failure mode this practice guards against: premature consensus that silences the information the group needs.
- [[Devil's advocate]] — the role-played version of dissent; the evidence says it is weaker than the authentic kind.
- [[Psychological safety]] — people only speak once when speaking is safe; the passing rule protects that.
