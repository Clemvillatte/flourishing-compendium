> **Kernel K411** — Zuowang (sitting and forgetting)

Zuowang is the Daoist meditation of sitting and forgetting, first described by Zhuangzi: sloughing off the limbs, dismissing hearing and sight, separating from the body, and expelling knowledge, until what remains is open, quiet awareness. It is a release practice rather than a concentration practice. Nothing is pushed away; everything is allowed to drop of its own weight, including, in the classical texts, the sense of being the one who is meditating.

### Protocol

1. Sit on a cushion or the front edge of a chair, spine upright but not stiff, tailbone reaching gently down and the crown gently up. Rest the tongue against the upper palate and let the eyes close or rest half open.
2. Let the breath be natural, quiet, and easy, with the belly rising and falling. Fold the hands in the lap or rest them on the legs.
3. Now forget, in stages and without force. Let the body be forgotten; it keeps sitting safely on its own. Let the breath be forgotten; it continues by itself. Let thoughts, plans, and roles drift past like clouds, neither followed nor pushed.
4. If effort creeps in, forget the effort too. The instruction is not suppression; a thought is simply allowed to dissolve before it becomes a project. Even the idea of meditating can be forgotten.
5. Rest in what remains: open, clear awareness that holds everything and clings to nothing. Twenty to forty minutes morning and evening is the traditional recommendation; start with ten.

### Evidence

No randomised trial has tested zuowang by name, so the honest framing is by nearest studied relative. Zuowang's mechanism, nonreactive release of whatever arises, closely parallels what the meditation literature calls open monitoring, which a framework paper describes as the nonreactive monitoring of the content of experience from moment to moment. A randomised trial found that open monitoring improved executive attention where a relaxation control did not, and a review reports that it induces a broader attentional focus and enhances divergent thinking, though a two-week trial found no creativity benefit. The release-of-thoughts component also sits beside detached mindfulness, a construct from Wells' metacognitive therapy with its own outcome literature. Treat zuowang as a tradition-tested form of practices whose family has evidence, rather than as a directly validated intervention.
