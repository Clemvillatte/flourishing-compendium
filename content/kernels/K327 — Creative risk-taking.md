> **Kernel K327** — Creative risk-taking

Once a week, take a creative risk: share work that is unfinished, try a form you have not mastered, voice an idea that might be wrong. The list of risks is this practice's own. What the research supports is narrower and stranger than the usual confidence story, and it is worth knowing before you start.

## Protocol

1. Pick one risk for the week. Unfinished work shared, an unmastered form attempted, an idea voiced that might be wrong.
2. Size it to survive. One share, one attempt, one voicing. The point is the exposure, not the scale.
3. Choose the audience with care. In the longitudinal study, growth in creative confidence tracked other people's expectations: "increments in employee creative role identity and in perceptions of supervisor creative expectations both related to increases in employee creative self-efficacy over time" ([Tierney and Farmer, 2011](https://ia800108.us.archive.org/view_archive.php?archive=/24/items/wikipedia-scholarly-sources-corpus/10.1037%252Fa0017364.zip&file=10.1037%252Fa0020952.pdf)). A person who already expects you to make things is the right first audience.
4. Make the identity move. Name what you are: someone who makes things and shows them. In the study's growth findings, role identity carried the larger coefficient.
5. Log the outcome, not the applause. What was learned about the work, the form, the idea. Next week's risk comes from that log.

## A guided version

The risk is your own work in front of your own audience; no guided recording is cited for it here.

## Evidence

Creative self-efficacy is "defined as the self-view 'that one has the ability to produce creative outcomes'" (Tierney and Farmer, 2002, p. 1138, as quoted in the 2011 study). The longitudinal finding on growth: "increases in employee creative role identity and perceived creative expectation from supervisors over a 6-month time period were associated with enhanced sense of employee capacity for creative work," and "increases in creative self-efficacy corresponded with increases in creative performance as well" ([Tierney and Farmer, 2011](https://ia800108.us.archive.org/view_archive.php?archive=/24/items/wikipedia-scholarly-sources-corpus/10.1037%252Fa0017364.zip&file=10.1037%252Fa0020952.pdf), abstract). The within-person result: within-person changes in creative self-efficacy tracked creative performance improvement, coefficient .41, p < .01 (same article, reported as a hypothesis test).

What the research does not cover: the study did not test weekly creative risks, and it did not test sharing unfinished work, so it leaves the sharing question open either way. Its finding on demands is about workplace requirements, a different thing: "Contrary to what was expected, employees who experienced increased requirements for creativity in their jobs actually reported a decreased sense of efficaciousness for creative work" (abstract). What tracked confidence in the study was identity and other people's expectations. The sharing mechanism is this practice's bet, untested by this study.
