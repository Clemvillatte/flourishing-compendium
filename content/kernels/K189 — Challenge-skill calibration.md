> **Kernel K189** — Challenge-skill calibration

Adjust the difficulty of a task to match your current skill, and keep adjusting as skill grows. The rule is Csikszentmihalyi's, set out in the tennis example in *Flow*: too easy tips into boredom, too hard tips into anxiety, and the way back to absorption is always an adjustment on one of the two axes.

### Protocol

*Steps follow the tennis-player passage in the owner's copy of* Flow *(printed pp. 74–75); the framing is my.*

1. Notice which way the mismatch runs: boredom means the challenge has fallen below your skill; anxiety means your skill has fallen below the challenge.
2. If bored, raise the challenge: set "a new and more difficult goal that matches his skills," and you "would be back in flow."
3. If anxious, build the skill: "If Alex is anxious (A3), the way back to flow requires that he increase his skills. Theoretically he could also reduce the challenges he is facing... but in practice it is difficult to ignore challenges once one is aware that they exist."
4. Expect to keep calibrating: "One cannot enjoy doing the same thing at the same level for long. We grow either bored or frustrated; and then the desire to enjoy ourselves again pushes us to stretch our skills, or to discover new opportunities for using them."

### Evidence

The carrier is the primary text itself, verified in the owner's copy ([Csikszentmihalyi, *Flow*, 1990, printed pp. 74–75](https://drive.google.com/file/d/11wG6Y7PwYzlNIrcXJ8ErSZ7Pd8fQcEsb/view), PDF pp. 83–84). The challenge-skill model is the book's structural account of why flow activities produce growth, not an experiment with a control group; the diagram it describes (Alex at A1–A4) is a theoretical illustration. What the book does not test: this kernel's use of the model as a deliberate daily adjustment practice, or effects on well-being beyond the experience of the activity itself.

### A guided version

Not applicable: calibration is an adjustment made inside a live activity, not a recording.
