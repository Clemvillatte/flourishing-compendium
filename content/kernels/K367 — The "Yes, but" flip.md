> **Kernel K367** — The "Yes, but" flip

Notice when your own change-talk gets cancelled by a "but": "I really want to work out regularly, but I am so busy." Drapkin calls these "Yes, but's" — you give yourself some change talk and then snatch it back.

The flip: put the sustain talk first, and change "but" to "and." "I am so busy and I really want to work out regularly." The same facts, but the reason-for-change survives the sentence.

This is a language move, not a denial of the obstacle: the "busy" is still true. And note: sustain talk (speech about keeping the behavior as it is) is not the same thing as discord, which is friction in the helping relationship itself. If you hear friction about the *relationship* ("you don't understand," "you always say that"), that's a different problem, and pushing harder about the change won't fix it.

Adapted from Drapkin, *The Motivational Interviewing Path to Personal Change*, ch. 4, PDF p. 1402 (this edition has no printed page numbers).

### Protocol

1. Catch a sentence of your own that cancels itself: change talk in front, "but", obstacle behind.
2. Write the sentence down and mark which half is the reason-for-change and which half is the status quo.
3. Rewrite it with the two halves joined by "and", order swapped or kept; choose the order that leaves the reason-for-change standing at the end.
4. Say the rewritten version aloud once. The obstacle stays in the sentence; only the cancellation goes.

### Evidence

The flip rests on the motivational-interviewing account of ambivalent speech. In the authors' own words:

> "I need to do something about my weight [change talk] but I've tried about everything and it never lasts [sustain talk]. I mean, I know I need to lose weight for my health [change talk] but I just love to eat [sustain talk]." "Yes, but . . ." is the cadence of ambivalence.
> — [Miller and Rollnick, third edition, PDF](https://ia800603.us.archive.org/29/items/applications-of-motivational-interviewing-william-r.-miller-stephen-rollnick-mot/%28Applications%20of%20Motivational%20Interviewing%29%20William%20R.%20Miller,%20Stephen%20Rollnick%20-%20Motivational%20Interviewing,%20Third%20Edition_%20Helping%20People%20Change-The%20Guilford%20Press%20%282012%29.pdf)

The two halves have names, and the book distinguishes them from discord:

> "The opposite type is sustain talk—the person's own arguments for not changing, for sustaining the status quo."
> — [Miller and Rollnick, third edition, PDF](https://ia800603.us.archive.org/29/items/applications-of-motivational-interviewing-william-r.-miller-stephen-rollnick-mot/%28Applications%20of%20Motivational%20Interviewing%29%20William%20R.%20Miller,%20Stephen%20Rollnick%20-%20Motivational%20Interviewing,%20Third%20Edition_%20Helping%20People%20Change-The%20Guilford%20Press%20%282012%29.pdf)

> "Sustain talk is about the target behavior or change. Discord is about you or more precisely about your relationship with the client—signals of discord in your working alliance."
> — [Miller and Rollnick, third edition, PDF](https://ia800603.us.archive.org/29/items/applications-of-motivational-interviewing-william-r.-miller-stephen-rollnick-mot/%28Applications%20of%20Motivational%20Interviewing%29%20William%20R.%20Miller,%20Stephen%20Rollnick%20-%20Motivational%20Interviewing,%20Third%20Edition_%20Helping%20People%20Change-The%20Guilford%20Press%20%282012%29.pdf)

The flip's own MI-tradition formulation, in a published training toolkit:

> "Recall that the structure for double-sided reflections is to start with the sustain talk and ending with the change talk, because people tend to continue talking about the last topic they heard. Additionally, using "and" rather than "but" honors the presence of ambivalence without negating it."
> — [All Rise, Motivational Interviewing Toolkit, PDF](https://allrise.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/04/MI-VTC-Toolkit-Final.pdf)

A SAMHSA training handout shows the same joining in practice. The clinician reflects: "You can't imagine how you could not smoke with your friends, and at the same time you're worried about how it's affecting you." The handout defines a double-sided reflection as one that "attempts to reflect back both sides of the ambivalence the patient experiences" ([SAMHSA motivational interviewing handout, Vermont Department of Health, PDF](https://www.healthvermont.gov/sites/default/files/documents/pdf/ADAP_SAMHSA-Motivational_Interviewing.pdf)).

The rewrite keeps both sides of the ambivalence in the sentence. The MI findings quoted here do not establish its effect on behavior.

What the sources do not test: the passages quoted are the MI background; the specific "but to and" move is attributed to Drapkin's workbook, cited at second hand, and the sources opened here do not evaluate it. The move's MI-tradition form is carried in published MI training materials (see the All Rise MI Toolkit quote above), and no trial cited on this page isolates the flip as an intervention.
