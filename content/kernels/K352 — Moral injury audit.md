> **Kernel K352** — Moral injury audit

Review a decision or action that violated your moral code. Name what was violated. Acknowledge the impact on yourself and others. The audit is not about guilt. It is about the recognition that moral injury, like physical injury, requires attention to heal. Research on moral injury (Litz et al.) shows that unaddressed moral pain predicts PTSD, depression, and suicide risk.

### Protocol

The audit is attention, not self-punishment; the paper that named the injury is quoted below. Intended focus: Meaning and Self.

1. Choose one decision or action that violated your moral code, and write it down plainly: what was done, what was violated, and who was affected, yourself included.
2. Distinguish the injury from the guilt: the audit's purpose is to recognize the harm so it can be tended, the way a wound is tended, not to reopen it nightly. If the writing becomes rumination, stop and shorten.
3. Name one act of repair, however small, toward the people or values harmed; an injury that receives attention and a step of repair is what the healing literature points to. Note both the injury and the step in one entry.

### Evidence

From the paper that defined moral injury:

> "Potentially morally injurious events, such as perpetrating, failing to prevent, or bearing witness to acts that transgress deeply held moral beliefs and expectations may be deleterious in the long-term, emotionally, psychologically, behaviorally, spiritually, and socially (what we label as moral injury)." (Abstract, [Litz, Stein, Delaney, Lebowitz, Nash, Silva & Maguen, 2009, Clinical Psychology Review 29(8), 695-706](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19683376/))

What this evidence does not test: the audit. The paper is a conceptual review: it defines moral injury, argues that the lasting impact of morally injurious experience in war remained chiefly unaddressed, and proposes a set of intervention strategies designed to repair it. It contains no trial of an audit, no comparison of addressed against unaddressed moral pain, and no data of its own on depression or suicide risk; the later outcome literature the kernel gestures at is real but is not this paper, and is not cited here as if it were. What this source supports is the kernel's framing: the wound analogy, the long-term deleterious effects, and the claim that repair is something one attends to on purpose.
