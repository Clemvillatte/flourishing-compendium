> **Kernel K291** — Equity scan

In a decision you are making (hiring, resource allocation, task assignment), scan for bias: Am I favouring people like me? Am I overlooking someone whose contribution is less visible? The scan is not a guilt exercise. It is a practical check against a documented tendency: summarizing studies of cooperation, "we find a small to medium effect size indicating that people are more cooperative with ingroup, compared to outgroup, members (d = 0.32)" ([Balliet, Wu and De Dreu, Psychological Bulletin 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25222635/)). The same meta-analysis locates the bias in favoritism toward ingroup members rather than hostility to outsiders, which is why the scan asks whom you are favouring, not whom you hate. Dimensions: cognition (the criteria examined before the decision) and overt behaviour (the decision itself, made on stated grounds).

### Protocol

1. Before the decision, write the criteria: what a good hire, allocation or assignment looks like, in the order of their weight. Writing them before reviewing the candidates lets you later check whether you changed them.
2. Run the two questions over the field: Am I favouring people like me (in background, style, familiarity)? Whose contribution is less visible because of how this decision measures work?
3. Generate the counterfactual: for each strong candidate or option, write one sentence on why someone unlike you might rate them differently. This is the vary step; it widens the field before selection.
4. Select using the written criteria only. Score each candidate against them, and record the scores before discussion.
5. Retain the record: criteria, scores, and the outcome. Before the next similar decision, read the last one; the pattern across decisions is where the scan earns its keep.

### Evidence

The meta-analysis of cooperative decision making summarized the bias in one line: intergroup discrimination "is the result of ingroup favoritism rather than outgroup derogation" ([Balliet, Wu and De Dreu 2014](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25222635/)). A separate hiring study examined a procedure that concealed candidates' identities: when symphony orchestras adopted "blind" auditions with a screen to conceal the candidate's identity from the jury, Goldin and Rouse, "using data from actual auditions, in an individual fixed-effects framework", "find that the screen increases the probability a woman will be advanced and hired", with "the weight of the evidence" suggesting "the blind audition procedure fostered impartiality in hiring" ([Goldin and Rouse, American Economic Review 2000](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.90.4.715)). The authors' own abstract carries the scope limit: "some of our estimates have large standard errors and there is one persistent effect in the opposite direction". Written criteria do not conceal identity, and these studies do not establish the effectiveness of this scan; they show only that the structure of a decision can matter.
