> **Kernel K247** — Motivational interviewing (self-directed)

When ambivalent about a change, borrow the interviewer's chair and sit in it yourself. The method is William Miller and Stephen Rollnick's motivational interviewing, and the move that translates best to a solo version is the ruler: "On a scale from zero to ten, with ten being the highest, how important is it to you to change [insert target behavior]?" ([Resnicow and McMaster, 2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1479-5868-9-19)). The self-directed version is an adaptation; MI was built as a conversation between two people.

## Protocol

1. Name the change in one plain sentence. Not "get healthier"; the specific behavior.
2. Ask the two ruler questions, and write the numbers: how important, and how confident you could do it.
3. Ask the probes, in the clinical order. The article's examples: "Why did you not choose a lower number, like a three or a four?" and "What might it take to get you to a higher number, like a six or a seven?" (same article). Answered honestly, in writing, these are where your own case for the change comes from.
4. Weigh both sides as the method does. MI treats ambivalence as the normal material: "a person-centered counseling style for addressing the common problem of ambivalence about change" (Miller and Rollnick, professional definition, via [BC Renal](https://www.bcrenal.ca/resource-gallery/Documents/What_is_MI.pdf)). Costs of staying, benefits of changing, and the reverse.
5. Let the answer be a maybe. The questions explore ambivalence; they do not force a resolution, and the method claims nothing about what the number does next.

## A guided version

The interview is conducted by you, on yourself, on paper; no guided recording is cited for it here.

## Evidence

Why the probes matter: "These probes elicit positive change talk and ideas for potential solutions from the client," and "Expression of change talk, particularly a strong crescendo of commitment, appears to be a good predictor of future change, and a key mediator of the MI process" ([Resnicow and McMaster, 2012](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/1479-5868-9-19)). The spirit of the method, in its own words: "Partnership: Working together with another person and recognizing them as equal"; "Evocation: Bringing out another person's ideas, strengths, and knowledge about the situation and themselves" (Miller and Rollnick spirit definitions, via [BC Renal](https://www.bcrenal.ca/resource-gallery/Documents/What_is_MI.pdf)). And its caution about pushing: "If you tell someone what to do, there is a risk that they will push back, withdraw or appease you by saying they will do it and then not. MI is more like dancing with someone than wrestling" (same document).

What the research does not cover: MI has a large clinical trial literature, but those trials test the two-person counseling across health behavior change, not a self-administered questionnaire. The self-directed version keeps the questions and loses the relationship, the reflective listening, and the practitioner who notices your change talk; what it retains is the writing that makes your own reasons visible.
