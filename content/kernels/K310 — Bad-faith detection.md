> **Kernel K310** — Bad-faith detection

When you are about to say "I have no choice" or "I had to," pause. Ask: what am I avoiding by framing this as necessity? The exercise takes its cue from Sartre's diagnosis of bad faith, the philosophical account of describing one's choices as necessities; it is an invitation to examine the description, not evidence that every claim of necessity is an evasion. The point is not that constraints are unreal but that within them, a choice remains. Sartre's formula is blunt: "there is no determinism – man is free, man is freedom", and "we are left alone, without excuse" ([Sartre, Existentialism is a Humanism](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/sartre/works/exist/sartre.htm)). Dimensions: cognition (the frame examined at the moment it is used) and motivation (the avoidance the frame serves).

### Protocol

1. Generate the pause: catch the phrases as they form, "I have no choice", "I had to", "they left me no option", "anyone would have done the same". The catch is the whole first skill; the rest of the exercise takes a minute.
2. Select the avoidance: write what the necessity-frame is protecting. Usually it is one of three: fear of a consequence, unwillingness to disappoint someone, or refusal to own a want. Name which.
3. Restate the sentence with the choice visible: "I am choosing X because I prefer it to Y." If the restatement is false, the constraint was real and the exercise ends with an honest acceptance; if it is uncomfortable but true, the bad faith is found.
4. Retain a count, not a diary: how many caught this week. The number, not the details, is what shows whether the frame is a habit or a tool.

### Evidence

Sartre's ground for the diagnosis: if "existence precedes essence, one will never be able to explain one's action by reference to a given and specific human nature", and so "we have neither behind us, nor before us in a luminous realm of values, any means of justification or excuse" ([Sartre 1946](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/sartre/works/exist/sartre.htm)); man is "condemned, because he did not create himself, yet is nevertheless at liberty, and from the moment that he is thrown into this world he is responsible for everything he does" (same lecture). The empirical note cuts both ways and is worth knowing: passivity in the face of prolonged aversive events is "the default, unlearned response", which "can be overcome by learning control" ([Maier and Seligman, Psychological Review 2016](https://ppc.sas.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/learnedhelplessnessat50.pdf)). So "I have no choice" is sometimes the voice of a real constraint, sometimes a default passivity, and the kernel's step 3 is the prompt that asks which it is; the cited research does not validate the prompt as a way to tell them apart. Scope limits: the helplessness research is about aversive stimulation in animals and its neural circuitry, not about ordinary social excuses; Sartre's claim is ontological and does not itself supply the catch-and-restate procedure.
