> **Kernel K322** — Future-self dialogue

Write a letter from your future self, ten years from now, to your current self. What would that person tell you about the decision you are facing? What would they say you are worrying about unnecessarily? What would they say you are missing? The practice is distinct from the deathbed test in that it uses dialogue rather than a single question. Reach for it when a decision feels both weighty and foggy.

### Protocol

Suggested adaptation of the future-self-continuity research below; the studies used questionnaires and computer renderings, and this page turns the construct into a letter. The practice moves the Self and Cognition dimensions: who you take yourself to be, and what you expect of the years.

1. Pick a horizon: ten years is the default, but any distance far enough that the writer has seen the outcome of the decision works.
2. Write the letter in the future self's voice, answering the three questions above. Editorial addition, not from the carriers: write in the first person, sign with the age the writer will be, and let the writer disagree with you.
3. Read the letter back and mark one thing you will do differently because of it. One letter is a complete round; the letter can be rewritten in later years.

### Evidence

The construct behind the exercise, in the authors' own abstract:

> "In three experiments, we tested a 'future self-continuity' hypothesis that individual differences in the perception of one's present self as continuous with a future self would be associated with measures of saving in the laboratory and everyday life. Higher future self-continuity (assessed by a novel index) predicted reduced discounting of future rewards in a laboratory task, more matches in adjectival descriptions of present and future selves, and greater lifetime accumulation of financial assets (even after controlling for age and education)."
> — [Ersner-Hershfield, Garton, Ballard, Samanez-Larkin & Knutson (2009), *Judgment and Decision Making* 4(4), author PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/group/spanlab/Publications/he09jdm.pdf)

The authors' own caution: the findings "cannot specify the causal direction of the association between future self-continuity and saving". The experimental route, from the same research programme: "allowing people to interact with age-progressed renderings of themselves will cause them to allocate more resources to the future... In all cases, those who interacted with their virtual future selves exhibited an increased tendency to accept later monetary rewards over immediate ones" ([Hershfield et al. 2011, *Journal of Marketing Research* 48(SPL), author PDF](https://vhil.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj29011/files/media/file/hershfield-jmr-saving-behavior.pdf)). What the studies do not test: the renderings were pictures in virtual reality, not letters; the outcomes were monetary allocation and discounting tasks, not life decisions; and neither study tested the letter-writing exercise on this page.

### A guided version

None located; the practice is writing, not listening, and no official guided track for it was found.
