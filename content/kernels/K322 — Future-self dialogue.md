> **Kernel K322** — Future-self dialogue

Write a letter from your future self, ten years from now, to your current self. What would that person tell you about the decision you are facing? What would they say you are worrying about unnecessarily? What would they say you are missing? The practice is distinct from the deathbed test in that it uses dialogue rather than a single question. Reach for it when a decision feels both weighty and foggy.

### Protocol

Suggested adaptation of the future-self-continuity research below; the studies used questionnaires and computer renderings, and this page turns the construct into a letter. The practice moves the Self and Cognition dimensions: who you take yourself to be, and what you expect of the years.

1. Pick a horizon: ten years is the default, but any distance far enough that the writer has seen the outcome of the decision works.
2. Write the letter in the future self's voice, answering the three questions above. Use the first person, sign with your future age, and allow yourself to disagree with the imagined writer.
3. Read the letter back and mark one thing you will do differently because of it. One letter is a complete round; the letter can be rewritten in later years.
4. Optional variation: if you find it difficult to begin, try a different time horizon.

### Evidence

The construct behind the exercise, in the authors' own abstract:

> "In three experiments, we tested a "future self-continuity" hypothesis that individual differences in the perception of one's present self as continuous with a future self would be associated with measures of saving in the laboratory and everyday life. Higher future self-continuity (assessed by a novel index) predicted reduced discounting of future rewards in a laboratory task, more matches in adjectival descriptions of present and future selves, and greater lifetime accumulation of financial assets (even after controlling for age and education)."
> — [Ersner-Hershfield, Garton, Ballard, Samanez-Larkin & Knutson (2009), Judgment and Decision Making 4(4), author PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/group/spanlab/Publications/he09jdm.pdf)

Across the three studies, higher future self-continuity was associated with lower discounting of future rewards, greater overlap between present- and future-self adjective descriptions, and greater accumulated financial assets ([Ersner-Hershfield et al. 2009, author PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/group/spanlab/Publications/he09jdm.pdf)). The authors read the pattern as "consistent with the notion that increased future self-continuity might promote saving for the future" ([2009, author PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/group/spanlab/Publications/he09jdm.pdf)), and add their own caution: the findings "cannot specify the causal direction of the association between future self-continuity and saving". The same paper notes that self-continuity "represents one of the most enduring puzzles of personal identity, pondered by ancient Greek and Buddhist thinkers alike" ([2009, author PDF](https://web.stanford.edu/group/spanlab/Publications/he09jdm.pdf)).

The experimental route, from the same research programme:

> "In four studies, participants interacted with realistic computer renderings of their future selves using immersive virtual reality hardware and interactive decision aids. In all cases, those who interacted with their virtual future selves exhibited an increased tendency to accept later monetary rewards over immediate ones."
> — [Hershfield et al. 2011, Journal of Marketing Research 48(SPL), author PDF](https://vhil.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj29011/files/media/file/hershfield-jmr-saving-behavior.pdf)

What the studies do not test: neither paper evaluated the letter-writing exercise presented here; the 2011 renderings were computer images, and its outcomes were monetary choices.

### A guided version

The practice is writing rather than listening; no recording is needed.
