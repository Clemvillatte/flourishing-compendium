> **Kernel K271** — The "is this up to me?" check

Throughout the day, when a concern arises, ask: is this up to me? If yes, act. If no, release. The check is portable, invisible, and immediate, and it is not a modern improvisation. It is the closing instruction of the first chapter of Epictetus's Enchiridion, addressed to every harsh appearance: "examine it by those rules which you have, and first, and chiefly, by this: whether it concerns the things which are in our own control, or those which are not; and, if it concerns anything not in our control, be prepared to say that it is nothing to you" ([Epictetus, Enchiridion 1](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html)). It trains the habit of distinguishing what is within your control from what is not, the foundation Stoicism builds everything else on. The check works on cognition: the judgment is made at the moment a concern appears, not later.

### Protocol

1. Catch the concern. When you notice worry, complaint or frustration rising, phrase it in one sentence ("the meeting might go badly", "he should not have said that").
2. Run the check: is this up to me? Judge by Epictetus's test. Things in our control are "opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions"; things not in our control are "body, property, reputation, command", whatever is not our own doing ([Enchiridion 1](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html)).
3. If it is up to you, act now or schedule the action. The concern converts into a task with a time.
4. If it is not up to you, use the ready reply: it is nothing to you. Release means dropping the demand on the outcome, not dropping attention: whether you fall ill is not up to you; whether you see a doctor is.
5. Retain. Keep a small tally of borderline cases, concerns that were partly up to you. For each, separate the action you can attempt from the outcome you cannot guarantee, and keep the ones that keep coming back for weekly review.

### Evidence

The Enchiridion opens with the distinction itself: "Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions." ([Epictetus, Enchiridion 1](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html)). The psychological counterpart, locus of control, has been tied to symptoms at scale: a meta-analysis of 152 independent samples totalling 33,224 adults found moderately strong correlations between external locus of control and depression symptoms (r = .30, k = 123) and anxiety symptoms (r = .30) ([Cheng et al., Psychological Bulletin 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22642229/)). Scope limits: those studies measured beliefs about control, correlational, not the practice of this check, and the meta-analysis found the external-LOC link to anxiety weaker in more collectivist societies, so the connotations of "control" are partly cultural.
