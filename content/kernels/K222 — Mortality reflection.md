> **Kernel K222** — Mortality reflection

Once a month, write for 10 minutes in response to: "I am mortal. The timing is unknown. What does this make urgent?" The exercise is not morbid; it is clarifying. Mortality salience, when not defended against, sharpens values.

### Protocol

The reflection is monthly, short, and written; the survey of a tradition of the same practice is quoted below. Intended focus: Meaning and Self.

1. Once a month, set ten minutes and the kernel's prompt: I am mortal, the timing is unknown, what does this make urgent. Write by hand if you can, and do not stop to edit.
2. Treat whatever arrives as a draft of a priority list, not as morbid evidence; the exercise is clarifying, and the writing is where the clarification happens.
3. Close by naming one thing the month will actually change, and put it in the calendar before the ten minutes end; a reflection that changes nothing this month has been defended against, not done.

### Evidence

From the study of death contemplation in older meditators:

> "Death contemplation practice showed a significant negative association with depression (*p*=0.011) but not with well-being (*p*=0.116) or gratitude (*p*=0.226) after controlling for covariates. Positive death attitudes were independently associated with lower levels of depression (*p*<0.001), as well as higher levels of well-being (*p*<0.001) and gratitude (*p*<0.001)" (Abstract, [Glushich, DeMaranville, Wongpakaran, Wedding & Wongpakaran, 2025, Narra: Journal of Narrative Practices 5(3), e2753](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41743868/))

What this evidence does not test: the exercise. The study surveyed elderly Thai Buddhist meditation practitioners cross-sectionally, and the practice itself went with less depression while the outcomes the kernel might hope for ran through positive death attitudes instead of through practice frequency. It cannot show that beginning a monthly writing practice causes anything, it reports no writing prompt, no ten-minute format, and nothing about values sharpening. What it supports is the kernel's wager in its weak form: contemplating death is not associated with worse mental health in the people who do it, and the attitudes it can form are associated with better.
