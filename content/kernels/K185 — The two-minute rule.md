> **Kernel K185** — The two-minute rule

Start any task with a commitment of just two minutes. If you want to stop after two minutes, stop. The rule has two sources: David Allen's version from Getting Things Done (if a task takes less than two minutes, do it now) and James Clear's version for habits (scale the habit down to two minutes). The stop-if-you-want variant is Clear's, quoted below.

## Protocol

1. Name the task you are avoiding, the one sliding down the list.
2. Cut it to two minutes. Clear's examples, verbatim: "'Read before bed each night' becomes 'Read one page.' 'Do thirty minutes of yoga' becomes 'Take out my yoga mat.' 'Study for class' becomes 'Open my notes.' 'Fold the laundry' becomes 'Fold one pair of socks.' 'Run three miles' becomes 'Tie my running shoes.'" ([jamesclear.com](https://jamesclear.com/how-to-stop-procrastinating))
3. Do the two-minute version, and mean the two minutes. Clear's instruction for the forced feeling: "If the Two-Minute Rule feels forced, try this: do it for two minutes and then stop. Go for a run, but you must stop after two minutes. Start meditating, but you must stop after two minutes. Study Arabic, but you must stop after two minutes... Your habit can only last one hundred and twenty seconds" (same article); he frames the two minutes as the whole thing rather than a strategy for starting.
4. If the two minutes ends and you want to continue, continue. If you want to stop, stop; the rule holds either way, and the honest stop keeps the rule cheap to obey tomorrow.

## A guided version

The two minutes are your own task at your own desk; no guided recording is cited for it here.

## Evidence

The rule's own text: "The Two-Minute Rule states 'When you start a new habit, it should take less than two minutes to do'" ([jamesclear.com](https://jamesclear.com/how-to-stop-procrastinating)). The research nearest to the rule concerns what unfinished tasks do to the mind. In Masicampo and Baumeister's experiments, "Unfinished goals caused intrusive thoughts during an unrelated reading task (Studies 1 and 5B), high mental accessibility of goal-related words (Studies 2 and 3), and poor performance on an unrelated anagram task (Study 4)," and "Allowing participants to formulate specific plans for their unfulfilled goals eliminated the various activation and interference effects" ([Masicampo and Baumeister, 2011](https://users.wfu.edu/masicaej/MasicampoBaumeister2011JPSP.pdf), abstract). In Study 5B, "participants in the plan group (M = 2.14, SD = 1.36) reported significantly fewer task-related thoughts than did participants in the unfulfilled task group, F(1, 74) = 7.64, p = .007" (p. 12).

What the research does not cover: no study tested the two-minute rule. The intrusions research tested plan making, not two-minute starts, and it says nothing about whether starting leads to continuing. Clear's continuation claims ("you'll often find yourself writing for an hour") and his reader's weight loss are author's framing and anecdote, not data. The rule is a cheap opening move; that is all it is established to be.
