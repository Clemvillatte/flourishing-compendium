> **Kernel K408** — The downward arrow

Take a thought that hurts, say, "if I get sick in public, it will be a disaster." Instead of arguing with it, follow it down. Ask: suppose that did happen, what would be bad about that? Answer. Then ask about the answer: and if that were true, what would that mean? Keep going, assuming each answer is true for the sake of discussion, until the answers stop producing anything underneath.

The descent has two uses. Asked toward consequences ("what would happen then?"), it tests the catastrophe: the feared event, examined step by step, usually turns out to be survivable and shorter-lived than it feels. Asked toward meaning ("what would that mean about me?"), it reaches the load-bearing belief the surface thought stands on: "I'm undesirable," "I'm helpless." That belief can then be examined like any other belief: evidence for, evidence against, alternatives.

Treat what you reach as a hypothesis about what the thought means to you, not as a hidden truth.

### Protocol

Adapted from Tolin, *Doing CBT* (1st ed., 2016): the decatastrophizing use at printed pp. 337–338, the core-belief use at printed pp. 373–374 (after Greenberger & Padesky, 2016, and Burns, 1980, as cited there).

1. Identify the negative thought that is upsetting you. Write it down and draw an arrow underneath.
2. The arrow represents asking yourself the question: "If this thought were true, why would it be upsetting to me? What would it mean to me?"
3. Write the second negative thought that answers that question. Take that answer and ask the same question again, following the arrow down.
4. Repeat several times, as many as you can. Assume for the sake of the exercise that each answer is true.
5. When you have several thoughts, review what you wrote and ask: what do these negative thoughts tell me about my value system? What are my basic assumptions about the basis of my worth, lovability, adequacy, or helplessness?
6. Once the silent assumption (the core belief) is identified, treat it as a hypothesis and use a tool to examine its helpfulness and reality: evidence for, evidence against, alternatives.

### Evidence

The technique's originator's own instructions:

> "To use this technique, draw a downward arrow under a Negative Thought on a Daily Mood Log and ask your patient, 'Why would it be upsetting to you if this thought were true? What would it mean to you?' This will trigger a new Negative Thought. Tell the patient to write it down under the arrow and repeat the process several times. The NTs you and your patient generate will lead to the underlying beliefs at the core of his or her suffering."
> — [David D. Burns, MD, "Uncovering Self-Defeating Beliefs", feelinggood.com](https://feelinggood.com/2017/05/05/uncovering-self-defeating-beliefs-sdbs-for-therapists-and-interested-patients-only/amp/)

The clinical handout form of the same steps, from a university anxiety programme:

> "The arrow represents asking yourself the question: 'If this thought were true, why would it be upsetting to me? What would it mean to me?' ... After several thoughts have been generated (as many as you can), review what you've written and ask yourself the following questions: What do these negative thoughts tell me about my value system? What are my basic assumptions about the basis of my worth, lovability, adequacy, or helplessness?"
> — [University of Michigan Depression Center, "Cognitive Skills for Depression" handout, PDF](https://psych.med.umich.edu/anxiety-program/pdf/Cognitive-Skills-for-Depression.pdf)

The handout's own close:

> "Once the silent assumption (core belief) is identified, use a tool to help understand the helpfulness and reality of this belief."
> — [same source](https://psych.med.umich.edu/anxiety-program/pdf/Cognitive-Skills-for-Depression.pdf)

The technique is standard cognitive therapy, introduced by Burns in *Feeling Good* (1980) as the vertical-arrow technique and building on Aaron Beck's work ([feelinggood.com](https://feelinggood.com/2017/05/05/uncovering-self-defeating-beliefs-sdbs-for-therapists-and-interested-patients-only/amp/); [Mentalyc worksheet FAQ](https://www.mentalyc.com/worksheets-and-cheatsheets/downward-arrow-technique-worksheet)).

What the sources do not test: the downward arrow is a technique for finding beliefs, not an intervention with its own outcome trials; the trials attach to the cognitive restructuring it feeds into.

### A guided version

The exercise is written by the practicer; free printable worksheets are carried at [BehavioralTherapyTechniques.com](https://behavioraltherapytechniques.com/worksheets/downward-arrow) and [Therapist Assist](https://therapistassist.app/resources/downward-arrow).
