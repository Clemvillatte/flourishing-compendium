> **Kernel K419** — Microcosmic orbit (xiao zhoutian)

The microcosmic orbit (xiao zhoutian, "small heavenly circle") is a Daoist energy-circulation practice: qi is warmed and settled in the lower elixir field (dantian), then guided in a loop down to the pelvic floor, up the spine to the crown, and down the front of the torso through the central channels. In Kohn's account it grew out of Han-dynasty breath cultivation and became a core move of inner alchemy (neidan), where adepts circulate purified qi "along the central channels of the torso", opening a series of passes; a larger loop reaching to the feet is the macrocosmic orbit ([Kohn, Chinese Healing Exercises](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1WPlkEDK5FTR9LkFGrutj1YCqIDZ9dMx_)).

### Protocol

The steps below follow Kohn's descriptions of the classical pattern (printed pp. 15, 126); this is a traditional practice, not a clinically standardized routine.

1. Sit or stand upright with the spine long. Breathe deeply and easily; let the breath fill the chest and the mouth, where you mix it with saliva and swallow, letting the qi sink toward the lower abdomen — the Ocean of Qi, the elixir field.
2. Let the qi rest and become stable there. Repeat the breath-and-swallow cycle over many sittings; the classical texts describe the qi accumulating, then "sprouting" once it is stable.
3. Once movement is felt, guide it: push the qi down to the pelvic floor, then move it gradually up along the spine, in close coordination with deep breathing. Kohn's classical pattern has the energy travel up the back to the top of the head.
4. Complete the circle down the front of the torso along the central channels, back to the lower abdomen. One round at a time; the feeling of the passageway opening, not speed, is the measure.

### Evidence

No trial of the microcosmic orbit appears in the opened sources; Kohn presents it as traditional practice, and the modern versions she names (Mantak Chia and others) were not opened. The long-life claims are the tradition's own framing, not clinical findings. The nearest studied relatives are slow breathing and general meditative attention, carried by other kernels below; treat this as a tradition-tested practice without direct outcome evidence.

> "To guide the qi, allow it to enter deeply [by inhaling] and collect it [in the mouth]. As it collects, it will expand. Once expanded, it will sink down. When it sinks down, it comes to rest. After it has come to rest, it becomes stable. When the qi is stable, it begins to sprout. From sprouting, it begins to grow. As it grows, it can be pulled back upwards. When it is pulled upwards, it reaches the crown of the head." — Kohn, Chinese Healing Exercises, printed p. 15, translating the jade inscription ([owner's Drive copy](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=1WPlkEDK5FTR9LkFGrutj1YCqIDZ9dMx_))
