> **Kernel K259** — Particulars before rules

Before deciding, describe the particular situation first: who is involved, what is at stake, what the context is. Only then ask which principle applies. The grounding is [[T002 — Aristotle|Aristotle]]'s account of practical wisdom, which he ties to knowledge of particulars rather than general principles alone ([Nicomachean Ethics VI.8, Chase translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt)). Reach for it whenever a familiar rule is already sitting in the mind, waiting to be applied.

### What it rests on

Aristotle's own illustration, from the chapter on practical wisdom:

> "Nor again does Practical Wisdom consist in a knowledge of general principles only, but it is necessary that one should know also the particular details, because it is apt to act, and action is concerned with details: for which reason sometimes men who have not much knowledge are more practical than others who have; among others, they who derive all they know from actual experience: suppose a man to know, for instance, that light meats are easy of digestion and wholesome, but not what kinds of meat are light, he will not produce a healthy state; that man will have a much better chance of doing so, who knows that the flesh of birds is light and wholesome." — [Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.8, Chase translation (Project Gutenberg 8438)](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt)

His conclusion: "Since then Practical Wisdom is apt to act, one ought to have both kinds of knowledge, or, if only one, the knowledge of details rather than of Principles" (VI.8).

### Protocol

The sequence and the timing are editorial, informed by Aristotle's account of practical wisdom.

1. Before deciding, spend two minutes writing the particular situation: who is involved, what is at stake, what the context is.
2. Only then ask which principle applies, and check it against what was written.
3. Where the rule and the written particulars clash, treat the clash as a question about the rule rather than an error in the description; this step is editorial.

### Evidence

The carrier here is the primary text, not trials. Aristotle's diagnosis of what goes wrong when the universal is used without the particular, from his account of failing to act on knowledge:

> "there is no reason why a man may not act against his Knowledge, having both propositions in his mind, using the universal but not the particular, for the particulars are the objects of moral action." — [Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VII.3, Chase translation (Project Gutenberg 8438)](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8438/pg8438.txt)

### A guided version

Not applicable: the practice is a written description of one situation, not a recording.
