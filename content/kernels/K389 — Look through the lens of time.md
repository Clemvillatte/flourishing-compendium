> **Kernel K389** — Look through the lens of time

Pick something currently bothering you. Write a one-sentence description of it. Then run it through temporal lenses:

1. Describe the event that upset you.
2. Rate how important the event feels now, on a scale of 0 to 10.
3. Rate how important the event will feel in an hour, a day, a week, a month, a year, five years, and ten years.
4. Re-rate how important the event feels after you completed the exercise.
5. Reflect: write your new view under "How You Think About the Event Now."

The ratings do not tell you the problem is not real — they tell you its scale.

### Protocol

Adapted from Tompkins's CBT workbook, which turns the temporal-distancing research into a written reflection (the studies below used simple reflection prompts, not rating scales; the 0-to-10 ratings and the written close are the workbook's editorial additions).

1. Name the event in one sentence, in plain factual language. If the sentence carries a judgement, rewrite it as an observation.
2. Rate the felt importance now. Write the number, not a paragraph.
3. Walk the ladder: an hour, a day, a week, a month, a year, five years, ten years. Rate each. Where the number drops sharply, pause and notice what that drop tells you about the event's actual footprint.
4. Re-rate the present. If the number has moved, write one sentence under "How you think about the event now" describing the change in your own words.
5. If the ten-year number stays high, the event may be a real loss rather than a passing stress; use the next lens rather than forcing the number down.

### Evidence

The construct is temporal distancing, and the research programme behind it runs through a seven-study paper in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology:

> "Together, these findings demonstrate that temporal distancing plays an important role in emotional coping with negative events, and that it does so by directing individuals' attention to the impermanent aspects of these events."
> — [Bruehlman-Senecal & Ayduk (2015), "This Too Shall Pass", JPSP 108(2), 356–375 — author PDF](https://rascl.studentorg.berkeley.edu/assets/files/this_too_shall_pass.pdf)

The paper's own summary of its first four studies reads:

> "The results of our first four studies provided converging evidence that adopting a distant-future perspective on a variety of stressful events lowers distress more effectively than adopting a near-future perspective (Studies 1a, 1b, and 2) or an undefined future-oriented perspective (Studies 1b and 3)."
> — [same source](https://rascl.studentorg.berkeley.edu/assets/files/this_too_shall_pass.pdf)

The mechanism the ratings walk toward is impermanence focus:

> "Across studies, impermanence focus—that is, the extent to which participants focused on the transitory aspects of their stressors—helped to account for the affective benefits of temporal distancing."
> — [same source](https://rascl.studentorg.berkeley.edu/assets/files/this_too_shall_pass.pdf)

The paper also reports a boundary worth naming: temporal distancing did not affect longer-term academic functioning, which the authors read as suggesting the strategy does not undercut motivation to improve, at least within the academic domain ([same source](https://rascl.studentorg.berkeley.edu/assets/files/this_too_shall_pass.pdf)).

What the studies do not test: the research cued reflection on real and hypothetical stressors with short prompts; the workbook's rating ladder, and this page's step-by-step protocol, are editorial adaptations of the finding, not the tested intervention itself.

### A guided version

The practice is a written reflection; no recording is needed.
