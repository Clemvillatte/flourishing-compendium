Practice: **Motivational interviewing (MI)**

> **Kernel K372** — The decisional-balance caution

"Weigh the pros and cons" sounds neutral and wise. But there's a case where it doesn't help: when you've *already* decided you want to change and you're ambivalent about following through. In that case, an equal list of reasons-for and reasons-against strengthens the ambivalence — you end up with a balanced scale and no movement.

The source's distinction:
- **Already want the change?** Explore the reasons-for, without suppressing real risks or uncertainty. The point is not to hide obstacles but to stop giving them equal airtime when you've already chosen a direction.
- **Genuinely undecided between two options?** A full balance sheet is exactly right — give all four squares equal attention so you can be at peace with whichever choice you make.

The caution is narrow: don't use a decisional balance to *create* motivation for a change you already want. And note: the more a helper is personally invested in the outcome, the more carefully they should stay neutral rather than pushing one side.

### Protocol

Adapted from Miller & Rollnick, *Motivational Interviewing* (4th ed.), ch. 6, p. 92 (Box 6.1); the helper-investment caution at p. 106. The practice moves the Self and Motivation dimensions: which side of yourself you give airtime to, and what you do with the ambivalence.

1. Name the situation: have you already chosen the direction, or are you genuinely undecided between two options?
2. If already decided: explore the reasons-for, without suppressing real risks or uncertainty. Do not build a full four-square balance sheet for a decision already made.
3. If genuinely undecided: build the full balance sheet, giving all four squares equal attention, so that whichever choice you make is one you can stand behind.
4. If you are helping someone: check your own investment. The more you want a particular outcome, the more carefully you stay neutral.

## Evidence

The distinction this caution turns on, in the book's own words:

> "MI is also sometimes confused with a decisional balance technique of equally exploring the pros and cons of change. In this edition we discuss decisional balance as a way of proceeding when you wish to counsel with neutrality rather than move toward a particular change goal (Chapter 17)."
> — [Miller and Rollnick, third edition, PDF](https://ia800603.us.archive.org/29/items/applications-of-motivational-interviewing-william-r.-miller-stephen-rollnick-mot/%28Applications%20of%20Motivational%20Interviewing%29%20William%20R.%20Miller,%20Stephen%20Rollnick%20-%20Motivational%20Interviewing,%20Third%20Edition_%20Helping%20People%20Change-The%20Guilford%20Press%20%282012%29.pdf)

And the helper-investment caution, in the book's parenting discussion:

> "We simply don’t have the detachment (thank goodness!) to sit with our own children and dispassionately explore the pros and cons of their injecting drugs or engaging in criminal behavior."
> — [Miller and Rollnick, third edition, PDF](https://ia800603.us.archive.org/29/items/applications-of-motivational-interviewing-william-r.-miller-stephen-rollnick-mot/%28Applications%20of%20Motivational%20Interviewing%29%20William%20R.%20Miller,%20Stephen%20Rollnick%20-%20Motivational%20Interviewing,%20Third%20Edition_%20Helping%20People%20Change-The%20Guilford%20Press%20%282012%29.pdf)

What the sources do not test: the quoted passages give the book's account of when decisional balance fits; they do not evaluate the everyday self-use caution on this page.
