> **Kernel K413** — Box breathing

Box breathing, also called square breathing, paces the breath in four equal phases: inhale through the nose for four seconds, hold gently for four, exhale for four, and hold empty for four, then repeat. The pattern is popular in high-stress occupations because it can be run anywhere, needs no equipment, and gives attention a simple rhythmic job while the body settles.

### Protocol

1. Sit comfortably with a straight back, or stand relaxed. Let the shoulders drop and close the eyes if that helps.
2. Inhale slowly through the nose for a count of four, filling the belly first.
3. Hold the breath for four, keeping the throat and jaw soft rather than clamping down.
4. Exhale through the nose or mouth for four, letting the lungs empty evenly.
5. Hold empty for four, then begin the next round. Run five minutes before a stressful event, or as a daily reset. Counts of three or five work fine if four feels strained; keep the four phases equal.

### A 4-7-8 variant

A cousin pattern, 4-7-8 breathing, stretches the same idea toward a longer exhale: inhale for four, hold for seven, exhale for eight, which lands near three breaths per minute. Its long exhale suits an evening wind-down.

### Evidence

A 2026 PRISMA review of box-breathing randomised trials (Dujawara, Ali & Kumari, *Applied Psychophysiology and Biofeedback*) concluded that box breathing shows short-term benefits for perceived stress, anxiety, and mood appear in a non-uniform manner across studies, autonomic responses are mixed, and long-term data are sparse. The review found no spirometry or lung-function evidence. A 2026 RCT by McAllister and colleagues exposed 66 healthy adults to a virtual Trier Social Stress Test after five minutes of box breathing (4:4:4:4), prolonged exhalation, or normal breathing. Both breathing patterns blunted the stress response: heart rate fell 6.4 bpm in the box group while rising 4.4 bpm under normal breathing, state anxiety stayed flat instead of climbing, and salivary alpha-amylase was 60.0 U/mL lower than the control group after the stressor. Heart rate variability, cortisol, and cognition did not differ between groups, so the honest summary is an acute calming effect without a hormonal signature. A one-month randomized trial compared three daily five-minute breathwork practices with mindfulness meditation; its abstract reports greater improvement in mood with breathwork, especially cyclic sighing (Balban et al. 2023; see [[K410 — Physiological sigh (cyclic sighing)]]). The 4-7-8 variant rests on thinner ground: a review of breathwork for chronic stress (Siebieszuk, Płoński & Baranowski, *Medical Sciences*, 2025) describes one randomised trial in patients after bariatric surgery where 4-7-8 reduced state anxiety compared with a deep-breathing control, and one quasi-experimental study in healthy young adults reporting parasympathetic shifts; both studies were small, so treat 4-7-8 as a promising variant.
