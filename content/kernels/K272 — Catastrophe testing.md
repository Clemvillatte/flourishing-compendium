> **Kernel K272** — Catastrophe testing

When a feared outcome looms, ask the Stoic questions: is this really terrible? Has anyone endured worse? Will this matter in a year? In ten years? Cognitive therapy gives the same move a working name. Decatastrophizing "involves the identification of the 'worst-case scenario' associated with an anxious concern, the evaluation of the likelihood of this scenario, and then the construction of a more likely moderate distressing outcome" (Clark and Beck 2010, quoted in [Robertson 2024](https://donaldrobertson.substack.com/p/how-to-stop-catastrophizing)). The exercise restores proportion by testing the inflated probability and severity of feared outcomes, and it moves two dimensions: cognition (a written test of the appraisal) and affect (the anxiety the imagery provokes).

### Protocol

1. Name the feared outcome in one sentence, in your own words.
2. Picture the worst case as if it were happening now, patiently, for five to ten minutes. Robertson, summarizing Beck's method, notes that anxiety may increase at first and typically reduces during the exercise, and that the imagery step is the one the worried mind tends to skip ([Robertson 2024](https://donaldrobertson.substack.com/p/how-to-stop-catastrophizing)).
3. Test severity. Ask: what is the worst that could realistically happen here? What is so bad about that? How might it affect my life a year from now? Look for exaggeration, signs of safety, rescue factors, and people who could help.
4. Test probability. Estimate how likely the worst case is, using evidence rather than dread: of the many things you have worried about, what fraction happened as predicted?
5. Build the coping plan. Even if the worst happened, how would you cope? What resources, people and actions are available? Write the plan.
6. Retain. Write the more likely moderate outcome, and re-rate the fear. Keep the script: when the same fear returns, start from what you wrote rather than from scratch.

### Evidence

Beck's method, as summarized by the psychotherapist and Stoicism writer Donald Robertson, "is based on the premise that the situations we fear are, in reality, seldom as bad was we imagine when feeling anxious", and its emphasis "is for the patient to see whether he can learn to accept and tolerate the experience he fears" ([Robertson 2024](https://donaldrobertson.substack.com/p/how-to-stop-catastrophizing)). Affective-forecasting research offers a reason to examine predictions about future distress; it does not validate this particular routine. In Wilson and Gilbert's review of that research, "the most prevalent error found in research on affective forecasting is the impact bias", "the tendency to overestimate the enduring impact that future events will have on our emotional reactions" ([Wilson and Gilbert, "Affective Forecasting"](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Wilson-Affective_Forecasting.pdf)). Robertson also notes the Stoic ancestor of the drill: premeditation of adversity targeted a range of misfortunes preventatively, rehearsing a philosophical attitude toward them, with the dichotomy of control asked during the exercise. Scope limits: the affective-forecasting studies measure predictions in general, not this drill, and the Stoic comparison is Robertson's interpretation.
