> **Kernel K265** — The advisor consultation

Before a significant decision, consult at least two people whose judgement is respected, and ask what they would consider if they were in the position, not what they would do. The question collects reasoning instead of verdicts. The nearest research is on collective intelligence: what makes groups perform well when they think together ([Woolley et al., 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20929725/)). Reach for it when a decision is significant enough to spend other people's attention on, and one's own view has already hardened.

### What it rests on

The collective-intelligence finding, from the study that tested groups of two to five on varied tasks:

> "This 'c factor' is not strongly correlated with the average or maximum individual intelligence of group members but is correlated with the average social sensitivity of group members, the equality in distribution of conversational turn-taking, and the proportion of females in the group." — [Woolley, Chabris, Pentland, Hashmi & Malone, 2010, Science 330(6004), Abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20929725/)

What a group thinks with matters less than how it takes turns and reads each other. The two-advisor rule and the what-would-you-consider question are the compendium's editorial design, not the study's protocol.

### Protocol

The form of the consultation is editorial, informed by the research on how groups think well.

1. Before deciding, name at least two people whose judgement is respected, ideally people who will see different sides of the matter.
2. Ask each one the same question: what would you consider if you were in this position?
3. Write down what they name, and keep the verdicts out of it; the harvest is the considerations, not the instructions.
4. Decide alone with the written considerations in view.

### Evidence

The two studies behind the quote involved 699 people working in groups of two to five on varied tasks:

> "In two studies with 699 people, working in groups of two to five, we find converging evidence of a general collective intelligence factor that explains a group's performance on a wide variety of tasks." — [Woolley et al., 2010, Abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20929725/)

What this evidence does not test: the cited research did not evaluate an advisor-consultation protocol, and the outcomes were group task scores, not the quality of anyone's personal decisions.

### A guided version

Not applicable: the practice is a conversation with people, not a recording.
