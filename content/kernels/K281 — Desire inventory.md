> **Kernel K281** — Desire inventory

List your current desires, all of them, from the grand to the petty. For each, ask three questions: is it aligned with what you value? does pursuing it serve your flourishing? what would happen if you let it go? The inventory is the Stoic discipline of desire made concrete, and the goal-contents research gives the third question teeth: what you pursue, not only whether you attain it, tracks well-being. Reach for it when wanting has become a weather system rather than a list.

### Protocol

The practice moves the Motivation dimension above all, with Cognition alongside: desire is inspected rather than obeyed.

1. Write the desires down without editing; an unlisted desire cannot be examined.
2. For each desire, ask Epictetus's question about its object: is the thing you want in your power or not? His rule, in Long's translation: "Remember that desire contains in it the profession (hope) of obtaining that which you desire... and he who fails in his desire is unfortunate; and he who falls into that which he would avoid is unhappy" ([Epictetus, *Encheiridion* 2, Long trans., full text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10661/10661-h/10661-h.htm)). Desires for the unattainable are not shameful; they are weather reports for the unfortunate.
3. Editorial addition, not from the carriers: sort each desire into keep, transfer (pursue differently), or release, and let one release happen this week. An inventory that changes nothing is only a diary.

### Evidence

The empirical grounding for the sorting questions, from three studies and a year-long prospective study:

> "Both what goals people pursue (i.e., whether they strive for extrinsic vs. intrinsic goal contents) and why people pursue them (i.e., whether they strive for autonomous vs. controlled motives) make significant independent contributions to psychological well-being."
> — [Sheldon, Ryan, Deci & Kasser 2004, *PSPB* 30(4), PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15070476/)

The Stoic ground for the first question is the dichotomy of power: a desire aimed at what is not up to you is a standing appointment with misfortune, whatever its content.

What the studies do not test: the research concerns self-reported goal contents and well-being, mostly correlational with one prospective sample; no study tests a written desire inventory, and the page claims no measured effect for it.

### A guided version

No official recording exists. A worked guide does: the Universal Human Values course's [Practice Session 3, List of Desires](https://uhv.org.in/UHV-II%20Course%20Material%202024/PS%203%20List%20of%20Desires%20v2.pdf) is a step-by-step worksheet that has you list your desires, thoughts and expectations, then categorise each as a need of the self or a need of the body, and by its source of motivation (preconditioning, sensation, or natural acceptance), with worked examples like a house kept for protection versus a house kept to show others.
