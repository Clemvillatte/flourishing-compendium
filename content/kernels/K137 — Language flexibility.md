> **Kernel K137** — Language flexibility

A language-flexibility pass rewords a self-description and watches the meaning shift. "I failed" becomes "this attempt did not work." "I am anxious" becomes "I notice tension and quickened breath." The exercise below combines two research methods, Ethan Kross's non-first-person self-talk and Matthew Lieberman's affect labeling, and neither study tested the combined routine. Reach for it when a verdict sentence is doing the judging.

## Protocol

1. Catch the sentence. Notice a first-person verdict in the stream: "I failed," "I am anxious" (examples, not study wording).
2. Re-run the sentence with the research instruction. In the non-first-person condition of Kross et al. (2014), participants were told (p. 305):

> Please try to understand why you felt the way you did in the experience you just recalled using the pronoun "you" and "[your own name]" as much as possible. In other words, if your name was Jane, you would ask yourself, "Why did Jane feel this way? What were the underlying causes and reasons for Jane's feelings?"

3. Name the state in plain words. The scanner task adapted here had participants pick the word that fit from pairs such as "scared," "angry," "happy," "surprised" (Lieberman et al., 2007, p. 423); applying that to your own feeling is an adaptation, not the tested procedure.
4. Read old and new back to back; note what changes in tone and body. Treat the rewording as a fact-check, not a wish.

## A guided version

The exercise runs on your own sentences in your own voice, so no third-party guided recording is listed for it here.

## Evidence

Kross et al. (2014), seven studies with a total of 585 participants, reported that "non-first-person language use (compared with first-person language use) leads people to appraise future stressors in more challenging and less threatening terms" (p. 314; also stated in the abstract, p. 304), and concluded that "small shifts in the language people use to refer to the self during introspection consequentially influence their ability to regulate their thoughts, feelings, and behavior under social stress, even for vulnerable individuals" (p. 304). Lieberman et al. (2007) found that "affect labeling, relative to other forms of encoding, diminished the response of the amygdala and other limbic regions to negative emotional images" (p. 421), with the amygdala region of interest showing that "Significantly less activity was present in the amygdala ROI in the affect-label condition than in the gender-label condition, t(29) = 2.14, prep = .93, d = 0.79" (p. 424).

What the research does not cover: neither paper tested this combined exercise. Kross et al. studied introspective self-talk around social stress, and the rephrasings in the first paragraph do not come from their materials. Lieberman et al. had participants choose labels for emotional pictures in a scanner; carrying that to rewording one's own self-judgments is an inference.
