> **Kernel K137** — Language flexibility

A language-flexibility pass rewords a self-description and watches the meaning shift. The seed examples are editorial: "I failed" becomes "this attempt did not work," and "I am anxious" becomes "I notice tension and quickened breath." The scientific relatives are Ethan Kross's non-first-person self-talk and Matthew Lieberman's affect labeling, both of which test small changes in wording. Reach for it when a verdict sentence is doing the judging.

## Protocol

1. Catch the sentence. Notice a first-person verdict in the stream: "I failed," "I am anxious" (editorial examples from the seed).
2. Re-run the sentence with the opened instruction. In the non-first-person condition of Kross et al. (2014), participants were told, verbatim: "Please try to understand why you felt the way you did in the experience you just recalled using the pronoun "you" and "[your own name]" as much as possible. In other words, if your name was Jane, you would ask yourself, "Why did Jane feel this way? What were the underlying causes and reasons for Jane's feelings?""
3. Name the state in plain words. If the sentence is a feeling verdict, label the feeling the way the scanner task did. In Lieberman et al. (2007), "subjects chose the correct affect label (e.g., ''scared,'' ''angry,'' ''happy,'' ''surprised'') from a pair of words shown at the bottom of the screen" (quoted verbatim).
4. Read old and new back to back; note what changes in tone and body. Treat the rewording as a fact-check, not a wish.

## A guided version

Exempt as inherently self-run: the rewording happens on the practitioner's own sentences in their own inner voice, and no third-party script can supply them. Recorded in the band ledger per the supervisor disposition of 2026-10-22.

## Evidence

Kross et al. (2014), seven studies with a total of 585 participants, reported that "non-first-person language use (compared with first-person language use) leads people to appraise future stressors in more challenging and less threatening terms," and concluded that "small shifts in the language people use to refer to the self during introspection consequentially influence their ability to regulate their thoughts, feelings, and behavior under social stress, even for vulnerable individuals" (both quotes verbatim from the opened full text). Lieberman et al. (2007) found that "affect labeling, relative to other forms of encoding, diminished the response of the amygdala and other limbic regions to negative emotional images," with increased activity in right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (quote verbatim from the opened full text).

What the studies do not test: neither trial ran this page's written sentence-swap. Kross et al. tested introspective self-talk around social stress, and the editorial rephrasings in the seed are not from the paper. Lieberman et al. had participants choose labels for emotional pictures in a scanner; the transfer to rewording one's own self-judgments is an inference, not a tested result.
