> **Kernel K131** — Perspective-taking

When locked in a view, step into another position: the other person's, a neutral observer's, a future self's, the "Dao's vantage." Each position reveals what the others conceal.

## Facets

- **effects**
- **perspective-getting**

### Protocol

The step is deliberate and brief; the trial of the instructed version is quoted below. Intended focus: Self and Connection.

1. When you notice you are locked in a view, name the position you are standing in: what you want, what you fear, what you assume.
2. Step into one other position and stay a moment: the other person's, a neutral observer's, a future self's, the widest vantage you can hold. Ask what the situation looks like from there, and say it in one sentence.
3. Return to your own position and decide with both views in hand; the exercise does not require you to adopt the other position, only to have genuinely visited it. Note which position revealed what.

### Evidence

From the randomized trials of a perspective-taking instruction:

> "Intervention students outscored controls in patient satisfaction in all studies: Study 1: P = .01, standardized effect size = 0.16; Study 2: P = .001, standardized effect size = 0.31; Study 3: P = .009, standardized effect size = 0.13." (Abstract, [Blatt, LeLacheur, Galinsky, Simmens & Greenberg, 2010, Academic Medicine 85(9), 1445-1452](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20736672/))

What this evidence does not test: the four positions. In three randomized studies, 608 student-clinicians received either a perspective-taking instruction or a neutral one before meeting standardized patients, and the instructed students were rated as more satisfying to be treated by, with small to moderate effects. The trial tested one instruction in a medical examination, not a practice of stepping between the Dao's vantage and a future self, and the abstract reports nothing about being locked in a view or about decision quality. It also reports a boundary: students with low baseline perspective-taking tendencies showed no benefit in Study 3. What it supports is the kernel's core wager: deliberately adopting another's position measurably changes how the encounter goes.
