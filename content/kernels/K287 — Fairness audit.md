> **Kernel K287** — Fairness audit

For a decision that affects others (a workplace allocation, a family rule, a community policy), ask: Is the process fair? Does everyone affected have a voice? Are the criteria transparent? Research on [[Procedural justice|procedural justice]] (Tyler) shows that people accept outcomes they disagree with if they perceive the process as fair. The audit is not about the outcome but about the process. Rawls gives the audit its standing: "justice is the first virtue of social institutions, as truth is of systems of thought... laws and institutions no matter how efficient and well-arranged must be reformed or abolished if they are unjust" ([Rawls, A Theory of Justice](https://wisc.pb.unizin.org/app/uploads/sites/26/2017/05/Rawls.pdf)). Tyler gives it its checklist: "there are four key procedural justice principles: voice, neutrality, respect, and trust" ([Tyler, Court Review](https://www.sog.unc.edu/sites/default/files/course_materials/Procedural%20Justice%20in%20the%20Courts%20-%20Tyler.pdf)).

### Protocol

1. Generate the audit questions before the decision is made, not after: who is affected, who speaks, by what criteria, applied by whom, visible to all?
2. Select the weakest answer and fix it first, since the audit's four principles, voice, neutrality, respect and trust, are checked in order of what the affected parties will notice first: the missing voice, the hidden criterion, the brusque treatment, the unexplained decision.
3. Retain the audit as a written record with the decision, so the process can be inspected later by those who had to live under it. Rawls's subject is "the way in which the major social institutions distribute fundamental rights and duties and determine the division of advantages from social cooperation" (quoted above), and the audit is that inspection at the scale of a workplace or a family.
4. Rework the process, not the outcome, when the audit fails. A fair process may still produce a contested outcome; the audit's success is measured in whether the affected parties could see how it was reached.

### Evidence

Both carriers are quoted above. The empirical ground is Tyler's synthesis of the procedural-justice literature for the courts, where "procedural justice approaches provide a mechanism for managing conflicts that produces authoritative decisions while sustaining, and even building, trust and confidence in the courts and the law" (Tyler 2007-08, [UNC PDF](https://www.sog.unc.edu/sites/default/files/course_materials/Procedural%20Justice%20in%20the%20Courts%20-%20Tyler.pdf)). The philosophical ground is Rawls's opening of A Theory of Justice, quoted above, plus his statement of the subject of justice, also above. Scope limits: Tyler's evidence concerns legal authorities and courts, and the transfer to workplace allocations and family rules is by analogy; Rawls's theory addresses the basic structure of society, and the audit borrows its standard, the priority of justice over efficiency, rather than claiming the family rule is a basic institution. This page audits the whole process; K288 checks the voice element in detail.
