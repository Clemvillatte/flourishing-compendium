> **Kernel K216** — Nonviolent communication

Use the four-step structure: observe what happened, name your feeling, identify the need, make a request. The structure aims to give conflict a shared language instead of a counterattack; the trial below is what moved measurable outcomes.

### Protocol

Steps are a practice form; the trial behind it is quoted below. Intended focus: Interpersonal behaviour.

1. Before a hard conversation, write the four steps out for yourself: what happened (observation without evaluation), what you feel, what you need, what you request.
2. In the conversation, speak the steps in order and slowly. The request is askable and refusable; a demand in the grammar of a request is still a demand.
3. When attacked, run the same steps on the attacker's words: what happened for them, what they might feel and need. You do not have to say it out loud for it to change your listening.

### Evidence

From a randomized trial of the training:

> "In the primary mixed-effects models, NVC training produced a significantly larger increase in JSPE than control (group-by-time interaction β = 16.95; 95% CI, 15.40-18.50; P < 0.001) and a significantly larger improvement in SCCG performance (β = 4.44; 95% CI, 3.41-5.47; P < 0.001)." (Abstract, [Jiang, Yuan, Wang, Qiu & Bian, 2026, BMC Medical Education, 10.1186/s12909-026-09621-w](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42231339/))

What this evidence does not test: your conflicts. The trial taught the four-step framework to fourth-year medical students (N=198, active control) and measured physician-empathy scores and observed role-play communication, finding larger gains than a matched standard curriculum. It did not test couples, workplaces or friendships, the effects were short-term, and the authors call for multicenter trials with longer follow-up. What it supports is that the structure is learnable and that training moves measured empathy and communication; whether it defuses real attacks is the practice's own wager.
