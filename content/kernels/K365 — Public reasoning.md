> **Kernel K365** — Public reasoning

When you take a public position on a question of rights or justice, build the strongest honest case for the opposing view before you state your own, and offer reasons the people you are trying to persuade could reasonably accept. The exercise trains the thinking Rawls's duty of civility calls for: he defines public justification as "argument addressed to others", reasoning that proceeds from premises others could reasonably accept (Rawls 1997).

### Protocol

1. Write your position in one sentence, then list the two or three reasons you actually hold it.
2. Reconstruct the strongest case against it. Use the best sources the other side offers, not the weakest comment you can find, and name the value each side is protecting.
3. Apply the endorsement test: ask a fair-minded holder of the opposing view, in person when you can, whether they accept your summary as theirs. If not, revise until they would.
4. State what the other side gets right before you state your case, then argue from premises they could reasonably share.
5. Afterwards, record one correction the reconstruction forced on you, and use it the next time the question comes up.

### Evidence

Rawls's account of public reason is the normative ground for the exercise, not a study of it. He writes that citizens are to think of themselves "as if they were legislators and ask themselves what statutes, supported by what reasons satisfying the criterion of reciprocity, they would think it most reasonable to enact" (Rawls 1997). The duty of civility, in the classic formulation, is a moral rather than legal duty "to be able to explain to one another" how the policies one advocates and votes for "can be supported by the political values of public reason", and it "also involves a willingness to listen to others and a fair-mindedness in deciding when accommodations to their view should reasonably be made" (Political Liberalism, p. 217, quoted in Brown 2003). The steelman exercise above is a preparation for that duty, not Rawls's own prescription.

### A guided version

The [Steel-Man Challenge Worksheet from Argumend for Educators](https://argumend.org/for-educators/worksheets/steel-man-challenge) is a written worksheet, not a recording: it walks through stating your position, restating the opposing view fairly, listing at least three reasons a reasonable person holds it, writing its strongest argument, and recording your confidence in your own position before and after.

## Related concepts

- [[Steelmanning the opposing view]] — the concept this kernel operationalises: reconstructing the strongest reasonable version of a view you reject.
- [[Deliberative democracy]] — the theory that legitimacy comes from reasons citizens can share; this kernel is one citizen-sized piece of that practice.
- [[The common good]] — public reasoning is addressed to people who will live under the outcome; the good at stake is shared rather than private.
- [[Justice reasoning]] — the domain where the duty bites hardest: Rawls scopes it to constitutional essentials and basic justice.
- [[Epistemic humility]] — the reconstruction step assumes the other side knows something you do not, which is the humility in practice.
