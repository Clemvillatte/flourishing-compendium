> **Kernel K365** — Public reasoning

When you take a public position, build the strongest honest case for the opposing view before you state your own, and offer reasons the people you are trying to persuade could reasonably accept. This is Rawls's duty of civility in practice: he defines public justification as "argument addressed to others", reasoning that starts from premises others could reasonably accept (Rawls 1997).

### Protocol

1. Write your position in one sentence, then list the two or three reasons you actually hold it.
2. Reconstruct the strongest case against it. Use the best sources the other side offers, not the weakest comment you can find, and name the value each side is protecting.
3. Apply the endorsement test: would a fair-minded holder of the opposing view accept your summary as theirs? If not, revise until they would.
4. State what the other side gets right before you state your case, then argue from premises they could reasonably share.
5. Afterwards, keep what you learned in the reconstruction (concessions, evidence you lacked) for the next round.

### Evidence

Rawls grounds the practice in the structure of public reason itself: citizens are to think of themselves "as if they were legislators and ask themselves what statutes, supported by what reasons satisfying the criterion of reciprocity, they would think it most reasonable to enact" (Rawls 1997). The duty of civility, in the classic formulation, is a moral rather than legal duty "to be able to explain to one another" how the policies one advocates and votes for "can be supported by the political values of public reason", and it "also involves a willingness to listen to others and a fair-mindedness in deciding when accommodations to their view should reasonably be made" (Political Liberalism, p. 217).

### A guided version

The [Steel-Man Challenge Worksheet from Argumend](https://argumend.org/for-educators/worksheets/steel-man-challenge) walks through the exercise on paper: state the opposing view fairly, list at least three reasons a reasonable person holds it, write its strongest argument, and record what the exercise did to your confidence.
