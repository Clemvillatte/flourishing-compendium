> **Kernel K126** — Cognitive defusion

When a sticky thought arises, reframe it: "I notice I'm having the thought that..." or "I'm having the thought that I'm a failure." The reframe creates distance between you and the thought, with the aim of reducing the thought's impact without disputing its content. The technique comes from acceptance and commitment therapy, where it is defined precisely: "cognitive defusion techniques are designed to reduce the functions of thoughts by altering the context in which they occur, rather than the attempting to alter the form, frequency, or situational sensitivity of the thoughts themselves" ([Masuda, Hayes, Sackett and Twohig, Behaviour Research and Therapy 2004](https://contextualscience.org/sites/default/files/305%20Defusion%20word%20repetition%20BRAT%202004.pdf)). Dimensions: cognition (the thought seen as a thought) and self (the observer position the reframe opens).

### Protocol

1. Generate the reframe at the moment the thought bites: "I notice I'm having the thought that I will fail." Said aloud once, the sentence moves the thought from the world into the mind, where it can be watched.
2. Select the sticky thought, not the trivial one: the reframe works best on a thought you believe completely, one that arrives with a push. Write the thought in a single word ("failure", "alone", "fraud") for the next step.
3. Repeat the word rapidly for thirty seconds, Titchener's old exercise: "rapidly repeating a single word" until it becomes sound. The word loses meaning first, then the believability of the thought it came from follows.
4. Retain the distance, not the debate. After the repetition, return attention to what you were doing. The thought may return; it arrives now as an event you can notice, which is the whole skill.

### Evidence

In the original comparison, run as eight single-case alternating treatment designs, "the cognitive defusion technique reduced both discomfort and believability more so than the comparison approaches" of distraction and thought control ([Masuda et al. 2004](https://contextualscience.org/sites/default/files/305%20Defusion%20word%20repetition%20BRAT%202004.pdf)). A later meta-analysis of the laboratory studies found "twelve studies (22 samples; n = 659)", in which "defusion produced large reductions in believability (Hedges' g = 1.04, SE = 0.12) and discomfort (g = 1.12, SE = 0.17)", with the word-repetition subgroup showing "larger effects on believability (g = 1.29) than other techniques (g = 0.59)" ([Journal of Contextual Behavioral Science 2026](https://contextualscience.org/article/defusion_techniques_selfreferential_thought_laboratorybased_experimental_research)). Scope limits: the samples are non-clinical adults in brief laboratory settings, the outcomes are ratings of a single target thought rather than life outcomes, and the original authors themselves cautioned that their data "do not show... that Titchener's defusion procedure, or defusion procedures more generally, are actually clinically useful" ([Masuda et al. 2004](https://contextualscience.org/sites/default/files/305%20Defusion%20word%20repetition%20BRAT%202004.pdf)).
