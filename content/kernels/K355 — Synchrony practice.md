> **Kernel K355** — Synchrony practice

In your next conversation, attend to the bodily dimension of the exchange: posture, gesture, the rhythm of turn-taking. Do not try to produce or stage any of it. Notice it. The practice is a noticing drill for the layer of social life that runs underneath words, the layer research measures as the coordination of two people's movement. Reach for it when a conversation matters and you want to be fully in it rather than only in its content.

### Protocol

The steps are this practice's own exercise, an adaptation of the research tradition cited below rather than a procedure taken from a study. The practice moves the Attention dimension: where attention rests during interaction.

1. Pick an ordinary conversation and give it three or four minutes of this attention: posture, small gestures, the pace at which the two of you hand the floor back and forth.
2. Notice what is already happening without directing it. The research behind this kernel found that coordination of movement arises on its own in genuine interactions and tracks the felt quality of the relationship; the practice asks you to watch that layer, not to imitate the other person. Deliberate mimicry is a different, contrived thing.
3. Editorial addition, not from the carriers: when you notice yourself planning your next sentence instead of attending, come back to the bodily layer. One conversation is a complete round; there is nothing to achieve.

### Evidence

What the movement-coordination research found, in the authors' own abstract:

> "Results: We found that nonverbal synchrony is higher in genuine interactions contrasted with pseudointeractions (a control condition generated by a specifically designed shuffling procedure). Furthermore, nonverbal synchrony is associated with session-level process as well as therapy outcome: It is increased in sessions rated by patients as manifesting high relationship quality, and in patients experiencing high self-efficacy. Higher nonverbal synchrony characterized psychotherapies with higher symptom reduction."
> — [Ramseyer & Tschacher (2011), *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* 79(3), author PDF](https://www.embodiment.ch/research/papers/Ramseyer_Tschacher_JCCP.pdf)

The study quantified synchrony as "the coordination of patient's and therapist's movement" with an automated video-analysis algorithm, in 104 videotaped sessions of 70 outpatients. What the study does not test: it concerns therapy dyads and measured movement, not posture, breathing, or gesture separately, and it did not test this page's noticing exercise; the association it reports is between naturally arising synchrony and relationship quality, not an effect of deliberately attending to it.

The acted-synchrony literature runs the other direction: making people move together. Its abstract result:

> "Across three experiments, people acting in synchrony with others cooperated more in subsequent group economic exercises, even in situations requiring personal sacrifice... In total, the results suggest that acting in synchrony with others can increase cooperation by strengthening social attachment among group members."
> — [Wiltermuth & Heath (2009), *Psychological Science* 20(1)](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2008.02253.x/)

That evidence is about walking in step and singing together, not about noticing a conversation, and the page makes no claim that the noticing exercise produces the cooperation effect.

### A guided version

No official recording of the in-conversation noticing exists; a guided dyadic form of the same movement coordination exists: the [Fun House Mirror partner game](https://www.meditatehappier.com/meditations/freeguidedmeditations) is a short guided audio in which two partners mirror each other's movements, exploring body awareness through matched motion. It trains the same eye the kernel then turns on live conversation, where the coordination is breath, gesture and pace rather than arms.
