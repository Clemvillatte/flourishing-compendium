> **Kernel K100** — Breath anchoring

Sit upright, eyes half-open or closed. Rest attention on the physical sensation of breathing at the nostrils or belly. When attention wanders, note where it went ("thinking," "planning"), and gently return to the breath. The return is the practice, not a failure. Start with 10 minutes.

### Protocol

1. Sit upright, alert but at ease. Close the eyes or lower the gaze. Take a few slow breaths to settle.
2. Choose one anchor point and stay with it: the feeling of air at the nostrils, or the rise and fall of the belly. Let the breath be natural rather than controlled.
3. When the mind wanders, and it will, briefly note where it went, then return attention to the anchor. If focus keeps slipping, count breaths in cycles of ten and begin the count again on the next wandering.
4. Ten minutes is a sound daily dose. If the session is part of a longer sit, breath anchoring works well as the opening stretch before open monitoring.

### Evidence

In the framework paper that distinguished the two styles of meditation, focused attention practice is described as the voluntary focusing of attention on a chosen object. In a randomised study, undergraduates took either a two-week mindfulness course or a nutrition course. The course taught mindfulness practices generally rather than breath anchoring alone, so the gains cannot be credited to this kernel by itself. Still, the mindfulness group improved GRE reading-comprehension scores and working memory capacity while reporting fewer distracting thoughts, and the gains were carried mostly by reduced mind wandering among participants who were prone to distraction at the start.

### A guided version

A free, high-quality guided session is the [Breathing Meditation (5 minutes) from UCLA Mindful](https://www.uclahealth.org/uclamindful/guided-meditations), recorded by Diana Winston, director of mindfulness education at UCLA's Mindful Awareness Research Center; the same page offers a whole library of guided practices.
