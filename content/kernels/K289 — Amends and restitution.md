> **Kernel K289** — Amends and restitution

When you have caused harm, however small, make amends. Name what you did. Acknowledge the impact. Offer repair. The practice is not about guilt. It is about restoring the relationship and the balance that your action disrupted. The study below measured what apology and restitution each did for victims' forgiveness and stress.

### Protocol

The steps are the practice form; the study behind them is quoted below. Intended focus: Interpersonal behaviour.

1. Name what you did, plainly, without the softening clauses ("I'm sorry you felt that way" is not a naming).
2. Acknowledge the impact on the other person as they have described it, not as you would have received it.
3. Offer repair, and make it concrete: what will be returned, fixed, changed or done differently. Then do it. The doing is the amends; the sentence is only its announcement.

### Evidence

From the study comparing apology and restitution:

> "Apology and restitution each independently increased empathy, forgiveness, gratitude, and positive emotions, while reducing unforgiveness, negative emotion, and muscle activity above the brow (corrugator supercilii, CS)." (Abstract, [Witvliet, Root Luna, Worthington & Tsang, 2020, Frontiers in Psychology 11, 284](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32231606/))

What this evidence does not test: your amends. Participants imagined themselves as victims of an armed robbery and burglary while measures of emotion, facial muscle activity and cardiac stress were taken, in a scenario design; the wrongdoer's responses were described, not performed by a real person making real repair. Within that design, both apology and restitution moved the victim-side measures, with restitution showing more potent effects on unforgiveness and anger than apology alone. The abstract reports no test of wrongdoers deliberately making amends for everyday harms, and the "not about guilt" framing is the practice's own, not a finding.
