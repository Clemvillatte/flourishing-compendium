> **Kernel K159** — Affect labeling

Name the emotion precisely: not "stressed" but "tight chest, dread, buzzing." Fine-grained labeling reduces amygdala reactivity and improves regulation.

## Facets

*This kernel is presented in facets; the protocol within each facet is the compendium's editorial addition.*

- Name the emotion with a specific word, not a report of circumstances; "I feel resentment" does work that "this meeting is terrible" does not.
- If one word will not hold it, use three; granularity, not poetry, is the aim.
- Label in the present tense, at the moment of the feeling; a label applied afterwards is a filing, not a regulation.

### Evidence

The practice is Lieberman's affect labeling: "the results indicated that affect labeling, relative to other forms of encoding, diminished the response of the amygdala and other limbic regions to negative emotional images. Additionally, affect labeling produced increased activity in a single brain region, right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (RVLPFC). Finally, RVLPFC and amygdala activity during affect labeling were inversely correlated" ([Lieberman et al., *Psychological Science*, 2007, abstract via APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/psychological-science/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x/)). The authors conclude that "affect labeling may diminish emotional reactivity along a pathway from RVLPFC to MPFC to the amygdala." What this study does not test: the fine-grained, multi-word labeling the kernel prescribes; the study used single-word labels chosen from lists, and the granularity instruction is editorial. The neural findings are also specific to dominant native languages.

### A guided version

Not applicable: the practice is a word found in the moment, not a recording.
