> **Kernel K143** — Self-complexity mapping

List as many aspects of yourself as you can: roles, interests, relationships, values, skills, histories. The more differentiated your self-concept, the less any single failure or setback can overwhelm your identity.

### Protocol

*Steps are my own protocol; the construct they exercise is Linville's self-complexity, defined below.*

1. List your self-aspects on one page: roles, relationships, skills, interests, values, histories; keep listing past the obvious.
2. Mark which aspects share little with which; the point is differentiation, not volume alone.
3. When a failure lands, name which aspect it strikes, and name two aspects it does not; that is the buffer being used.

### Evidence

The construct is Linville's: "the Stress X Self-Complexity interaction provided strong support for the buffering hypothesis. Subjects higher in self-complexity were less prone to depression, perceived stress, physical symptoms, and occurrence of the flu and other illnesses following high levels of stressful events" ([Linville, *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1987, abstract via PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/3572732/)). The earlier experiments found that those lower in self-complexity experienced greater swings in affect following success or failure. What this literature does not test: the mapping exercise as a way of increasing self-complexity (a later intervention study found none of four attempts raised it); and the replication record is mixed, with a meta-analysis finding little support for the stress-buffering claim, so the buffering effect should be read as contested rather than settled.

### A guided version

Not applicable: the practice is writing a list of one's own aspects, not a recording.
