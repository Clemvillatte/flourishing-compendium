You leave a conversation certain you heard a criticism. Later, the
message on your phone does not quite match the version in your head.
This lesson is about what to do with that gap: examine perception,
learning, memory and judgment without deciding that either your mind
is broken or every confident thought must be true.

## Entry, outcomes and source route

Level: L2, Guided application. Bring M1's distinction between observation
and interpretation and M2's description of an emotion, its three function
levels, a goal and a revisable response. You should also be able to compare
M2's two accounts without pretending to settle them. No mathematics or
clinical vocabulary is needed.

By the end, you can:

- **Learning:** distinguish a cue–event association from learning an
  action's consequences in a new fictional example.
- **Perception:** explain one way context helps interpret sensory input,
  and distinguish useful inference from a claim that all perception
  is false.
- **Memory:** distinguish working, episodic and semantic memory; map
  encoding, storage and retrieval in a case; design a meaningful encoding
  cue without promising perfect recall.
- **Judgment:** identify a possible framing or confirmation problem and
  propose a specific countercheck, without diagnosing a bias from
  disagreement alone.
- **Recurring thought and evidence:** distinguish process from content
  in repetitive negative thinking; appraise a commentary's experimental
  claim while naming the primary-study information it does not provide.

## Start with what is being learned

Watch the two lecture-4 windows and read Bouton's selections. Classical
conditioning concerns learning relationships between stimuli and
significant events; instrumental or operant conditioning concerns
relationships between an action and its consequences. The useful
question is what relationship is being learned, rather than whether
someone is "a conditioned person"
([Bloom, learning windows](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rCKK6r15fro&t=865);
[Bouton](https://nobaproject.com/modules/conditioning-and-learning)).

Consider a deliberately simplified fictional case. A notification sound
repeatedly precedes a welcome message; later, hearing the sound alone
brings anticipation. That illustrates a cue–event relation. Separately,
someone checks messages more often after checking sometimes brings a
welcome reply: that proposes an action–consequence relation. One episode
does not establish either learning history, and repetition alone does
not guarantee learning; predictive information matters
([Bouton, prediction-error paragraph](https://nobaproject.com/modules/conditioning-and-learning)).

Complete the distinction: a café tune comes to signal a pleasant break;
choosing a particular queue is repeated after it saves time. Which relation
is proposed in each case? Feedback: tune and event in the first; action
and consequence in the second. A plausible answer names both terms.
"The café conditioned them" is too vague. Neither answer requires you
to reproduce the situation, reward someone, or create an aversive cue.

Compare two explanations of the same learning question. Suppose a sound
already predicts an event reliably. A new light is then paired with that
sound and event. Is repeated pairing alone enough to explain learning
about the light? A simple pairing-sufficiency explanation predicts
learning from those pairings; a prediction-error account asks whether
the event supplies any new information. Bouton explains why an outcome
being surprising matters, rather than merely occurring again
([assigned paragraph](https://nobaproject.com/modules/conditioning-and-learning)).

Worked comparison: "Both explanations concern the added light. They
disagree over whether contiguity alone is sufficient; the second requires
an error in prediction. The lecture's introductory pairing description
is therefore not the complete learning rule." Replace sound/light with
two hypothetical reminder cues and complete the comparison. Feedback:
name what is already predicted, the newly added cue and the disagreement.
Classical and operant conditioning themselves concern different learned
relations, not these two accounts of the same question.

## Perception is an interpretation with constraints

Watch lecture 7's perception chapter. Bloom illustrates how assumptions
about illumination, grouping and depth help make sense of an ambiguous
sensory array. Those assumptions can be useful in ordinary settings and
produce errors in particular displays. The lesson is not that the world
is unreal or that every perception is equally unreliable
([assigned perception chapter](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DmWrOUK2zgA&t=1086)).

The clip's remarks about the limits of computer vision describe its
2007 teaching context. Do not carry them forward as a claim about present
AI. Likewise, treat its visual illusions as examples under their specific
conditions, not a reason to distrust a person reporting an experience
([historical lecture context](https://oyc.yale.edu/psychology/psyc-110/lecture-7)).

Work a visual explanation before making the analogy. In the Ponzo
display discussed in the clip, two physically equal horizontal lines
sit between converging lines resembling a receding track. Bloom's
depth-cue explanation is that linear perspective suggests the upper
line is farther away; interpreting equal projected extent at a greater
distance can make it look larger. It is an explanation of the displayed
conditions, not a claim that every size illusion has only one mechanism
([Ponzo and perspective discussion in the assigned chapter](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DmWrOUK2zgA&t=1086)).

For a text-accessible version, use that description as the display:
equal projected horizontal lengths, converging background, upper line
interpreted as farther away. Explain which cue suggests distance and
how that changes inferred size; seeing an illusion personally is not
required. Completion: "The lines are equal in ___; the converging
background supplies ___; the farther interpretation changes ___."
Feedback: physical/projected extent, a linear-perspective cue, inferred
size. "My eyes are broken" explains neither the cue nor the conditions.

Here is a fully worked transfer that does not depend on seeing an image.
A colleague writes, "That was an interesting choice." Mara interprets
it as criticism after a tense meeting, but cannot hear tone in the text.
The words are an observation; criticism is an interpretation influenced
by context. Asking what the colleague meant can add information. This
language example is our analogy for contextual interpretation, not a
claim that a visual-depth experiment directly tested workplace messages.

Now complete the same reasoning for "We should talk tomorrow."
Name the actual information, two plausible interpretations and a question
that could distinguish them. Feedback: the message gives a request to
talk, not its purpose; planning or a concern are both possible. "Is there
anything I should prepare?" requests information rather than merely
repeating a feared interpretation. This is also the text-accessible
additional transfer, not a replacement for the visual-cue explanation.

## Memory has kinds and stages

Read the memory selections, then watch the encoding clip. Working memory
holds and works with information briefly; episodic memory concerns
experienced events; semantic memory concerns general knowledge. Encoding
is initial learning, storage maintaining information over time, and
retrieval accessing it when needed. The stages interact, so a failure
to recall does not by itself reveal which stage failed
([McDermott and Roediger](https://nobaproject.com/modules/memory-encoding-storage-retrieval)).

In a worked example, Mara learns the word "retrieval." She connects it
to finding a document she previously saved, writes a definition, and
later explains it without the note. Keeping a question in mind while
answering uses working memory; remembering that study session is episodic;
knowing the definition is semantic. Forming the connection is encoding,
the intervening maintenance is storage, and producing the explanation
later is retrieval. These are descriptions of the example, not separate
compartments that can be diagnosed from one answer.

Bloom illustrates giving meaning and structure to otherwise arbitrary
material. The clip does not compare every mnemonic against spacing or
retrieval practice, nor establish that one technique always works best
([lecture 8 encoding window](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Yuwqr5o58Z0&t=802)).

Choose two terms from M2 and make a meaningful connection for each.
Close the notes and explain them later, then check for omissions.
For example, "situation modification" can connect to changing the
slide-editing procedure, not just repeating its label. If recall fails,
record "could not retrieve now"; do not conclude that the knowledge
has been erased. The exercise is judged by a sensible encoding plan
and an honest correction, not by a perfect memory score.

For completion practice, classify remembering a first meeting versus
knowing what "meeting" means, and identify the stage when you first
connect a new name to a face. Feedback: episodic, semantic, encoding.
If later you forget the name, the case does not establish whether initial
encoding, later maintenance or access is responsible.

## A bias label is the beginning of a check

Watch the two judgment windows. A frame can alter how equivalent options
are presented; confirmation-seeking can favour information consistent
with a current hypothesis. Naming either tendency does not prove that
your interlocutor is biased, or that the opposite of your first judgment
must be right
([Bloom, framing and confirmation](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AMsQfgV07vU&t=2383)).

Use this fully worked fictional decision. A volunteer programme says
"eight of ten applicants completed the training"; another description
of the same stipulated data says "two of ten did not complete." Mara
notices a different first impression. She writes both descriptions and
checks that the denominator, time period and outcome really are the
same before comparing options. The countercheck is not simply "ignore
your feelings"; it preserves the actual information.

Mara also believes a shorter meeting format always improves participation.
Rather than collecting only praise, she asks who participated less and
what would count against the claim. Negative feedback is not automatically
decisive either: she checks what it measures and how representative it
is. That is a proposed information-seeking practice, not a validated cure
for bias.

Complete a check for "My new study routine always helps." Supply one
observation that would count against it, and one reason the observation
might still be inconclusive. Feedback: a planned learning check may show
no improvement, but differences in task difficulty or study time would
make that comparison ambiguous. Merely finding one positive testimonial
would not test "always." The exercise assesses the question you design,
not a personal improvement claim.

## Recurring thought: inspect the process and the source

Read Ehring's assigned excerpt. His transdiagnostic account describes
repetitive negative thinking through repetition, intrusiveness, difficulty
disengaging, perceived unproductiveness and capture of mental capacity,
rather than one particular topic. He also summarises a distinction
between abstract, decontextualised processing and more concrete,
context-specific processing
([Ehring, RNT definition and processing-mode paragraphs](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8429319/)).

Practise matching evidence to the five features. For the fictional
sentence "Mara keeps returning to the same negative question," repetition
is supplied. Whether it arrives unwanted, is hard to disengage from,
feels unproductive, or captures working capacity is not supplied.
Make a five-row table of feature, evidence and unknown. Feedback:
one feature can be supported while the other four remain unknown;
negative topic alone is not the whole process definition. Do not
complete a checklist by inventing Mara's experience.

These research descriptions are not a self-diagnosis. A difficult question
that returns to mind is not, just by recurring, evidence of a disorder.
For an educational exercise, contrast "Why does everything always go
wrong?" with "Which part of tomorrow's task is unclear, and what information
is missing?" The second is more specific in this invented comparison;
we are not asserting that rewriting a sentence treats rumination.

Ehring's experimental-evidence paragraph summarises research that induced
rumination and examined adverse outcomes, including problem solving.
It does not present one experiment's participants, assignment, comparator,
measurement or numerical effect. That makes the appropriate appraisal
source-limited
([experimental-evidence paragraph](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8429319/)).

Here is a fully worked appraisal:

> This is a commentary, not one experiment's methods and results.
> Ehring reports experimental evidence linking induced rumination to
> poorer problem solving and other outcomes. I cannot audit one trial's
> assignment, comparator or effect estimate from this paragraph. I would
> need its cited primary report to assess those details and whether its
> setting applies to my question. Missing detail here is not evidence
> that no experimental research exists.

This is a lesson-authored model answer, not a quotation from Ehring.
Its attribution is limited to the claim the commentary actually makes
([claim being appraised](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8429319/)).

For completion practice, fill in: "To assess causal strength I need ___;
to judge practical size I need ___; this paragraph supplies ___."
Feedback: assignment/comparison procedures; effect estimate and
uncertainty; a synthesis claim and references, not those study-level
details. "Not reported" earns credit only when you name what is missing
and explain why it matters. Inventing a sample size or treatment result
fails even if the surrounding argument sounds persuasive.

## Independent task and exit

Use this unused fictional case. Sam is learning an unfamiliar computer
workflow. A reminder sound often precedes a helpful tutorial; reopening
the tutorial sometimes solves a task. Sam remembers a supervisor's
message as "You are too slow," but the saved text says "This stage took
longer than planned." A training advertisement reports that 70 percent
completed a course, while another description says 30 percent did not.
Sam repeatedly thinks "Why am I always hopeless?" No clinical history,
causal experiment or complete comparison dataset is supplied.

Submit a 900–1,100-word case analysis, with:

- **Relations:** a proposed cue–event and action–consequence relation,
  including the observation needed before claiming a learning history.
  Add a hypothetical already-predicted outcome and a new cue; compare
  pairing sufficiency with prediction error and state the disagreement.
- **Interpretation:** distinguish the saved words from Sam's recalled
  interpretation; give a non-accusatory information check. Separately
  explain the described Ponzo display's cue, equal lengths and size
  inference. Use the verbal display if needed.
- **Memory:** name the three kinds and stages with examples; propose
  one meaningful encoding cue and a later retrieval check.
- **Judgment:** restate the course claim in both frames, say what
  equivalence needs checking, and propose a possible disconfirming
  observation for "always hopeless" without treating that phrase as a
  testable diagnosis.
- **Source appraisal:** attribute Ehring's experimental claim, name
  three missing primary-study details and why they matter, and
  distinguish abstract from more concrete processing without a
  treatment promise. Map all five RNT features to Sam-case evidence
  or unknowns, keeping the topic distinct from the thinking process.

Add a private transfer note for each family: learning, perception,
memory, judgment and recurring thought. Choose an ordinary, low-stakes
lived instance; identify what the framework helps you understand or
choose, and what remains uncertain. Keep identifying details to yourself.
A shareable version may replace them with placeholders while retaining
the reasoning. If no recurring-negative-thought instance is suitable,
say so and apply its process/content distinction to an ordinary recurring
question without claiming it meets the RNT definition. You do not have
to disclose a painful episode or demonstrate improvement.

| Criterion | Pass | Revise |
|---|---|---|
| Learning | Distinguishes relations, limits inference and compares pairing sufficiency with prediction error. | Repetition alone as proof, or no same-question disagreement. |
| Perception/interpretation | Explains the display's perspective cue and size inference; distinguishes message observation from interpretation. | No depth cue, "all perception is wrong," or mind-reading. |
| Memory | Correct kinds/stages, meaningful cue, honest retrieval limit. | Treats recall failure as proof of erased storage or promises perfect memory. |
| Judgment | Checks equivalent information and designs a specific countercheck. | Bias label alone, assumed equivalence, or opposite opinion treated as automatically true. |
| RNT and evidence | Five features mapped to evidence/unknown; process/content distinction; source-limited appraisal. | Assumed features, invented study details, diagnosis or treatment promise. |

All five criteria must pass. Each private-transfer note must contain
an instance, a useful understanding/choice and a remaining uncertainty;
disclosure and emotional change are not graded. Revise failed portions after checking
the worked examples. A passing evidence answer can say "not reported"
with reasons; it need not manufacture a complete study appraisal.
An answer inventing a randomised sample and numerical effect must fail.

After a day or a different activity, recall the three memory stages and
give one observation/interpretation distinction without notes. Correct
the account, then retrieve one condition that would make a judgment
check inconclusive. The main case remains fictional; personal transfer
notes can stay private or use redacted details.

You are ready for M4 when you can explain a visual illusion by its depth
cue, distinguish memory kinds/stages, compare the learning explanations,
design a judgment check, map RNT features without invented evidence,
and state a commentary's source limits.
M4 narrows the focus to attention; it does not require clinical
screening, a better mood or successful self-treatment.
