# Choosing a feasible wish and making a plan

A wish can express something worth caring about while leaving the next
step unclear. I use this exercise to examine a modest wish, picture what
matters about it and decide what I could do when a familiar obstacle
appears. The sequence follows Gabriele Oettingen's WOOP instructions;
her guidance also allows changing, postponing or relinquishing a wish
that cannot be pursued
([author's practice guide](https://woopmylife.org/en/practice)).

## The short route

Open the [free English WOOP kit](https://woopmylife.org/s/WOOP-kit-EN-PDF.zip).
Use pages 1–4 of `2 Written WOOP Guide [EN].pdf`, followed by the
one-page `4 WOOP Review [EN].pdf`; the kit gives the sequence and
the checklist used below.

Keep the [practice-page FAQ](https://woopmylife.org/en/practice) available.
Read “Can I WOOP any wish I have?” and “What if my obstacle is in fact
insurmountable?” before choosing a personal example; these explain
when adjusting or letting go of a wish is appropriate.

By the end, aim to explain how the imagined outcome and obstacle differ,
write a cue-response plan for a feasible action, and identify something
the plan cannot control. Learning those distinctions is the task;
achieving the wished-for result is not a completion requirement.

## From a reason to a response

Oettingen calls WOOP mental contrasting with implementation intentions.
The first part brings an imagined desired future together with a
present inner obstacle; the planning part links that obstacle to an
action or thought in an if-then form
([Oettingen's explanation](https://psychwire.com/free-resources/q-and-a/1ux8o97/the-science-of-motivation)).

In the written guide, I first choose a wish that is personally important,
challenging and feasible within a suitable time frame. I then imagine
the best outcome, imagine the main inner obstacle, and specify one
response; the guide asks me to repeat and imagine the resulting plan
([Written WOOP Guide, pages 1–4](https://woopmylife.org/s/WOOP-kit-EN-PDF.zip)).

The following case is invented for practice. It illustrates the
sequence without reporting a study or predicting an outcome.

Leah wants to send a short message to a friend this week. The best
outcome she imagines is making contact with someone she misses.
Her inner obstacle is the urge to postpone until she can write a
perfect update. Her plan is: if she notices that urge while drafting,
she will write two honest sentences and decide whether to send them.
The friend's reply remains outside her control.

Compare that plan with “I will be a better friend.” What situation
would trigger the second intention, and what action would it specify?
The first plan names both; the second leaves them open.

## Complete a plan, then check its limits

Use another invented case before trying your own. Amir wants to ask a
colleague for information about an unfamiliar task. He imagines the
relief of understanding what is expected, but notices a habit of
putting off the question because he fears appearing inexperienced.

Write the if-part and one manageable then-part. A possible answer
is: if he notices himself postponing the question while preparing
the task, he will draft one specific question and choose a suitable
time to ask it. Other responses may work; check that the response
addresses the named obstacle and is available in that situation.

Now suppose the colleague is absent and no one else can provide
the information. What would need to change in the plan or the wish?
Explain why describing Amir as unmotivated would leave out an
important part of the case.

For a personal attempt, choose an ordinary, low-stakes wish you are
comfortable examining privately. Follow the author's four-step
guide in order and use its Review checklist; the checklist asks
whether the wish matters and is achievable, whether the outcome and
obstacle were imagined, and whether the plan links an obstacle to
a response
([Written Guide and Review](https://woopmylife.org/s/WOOP-kit-EN-PDF.zip)).

Add a separate note naming one external constraint. If the obstacle
cannot reasonably be changed or worked around, consider revising,
postponing or relinquishing the wish, as the author's FAQ permits
([feasibility guidance](https://woopmylife.org/en/practice)).

## What the evidence allows me to expect

A 2021 meta-analysis found a small-to-medium pooled effect on goal
attainment, with a smaller estimate after a publication-bias adjustment.
The result averages across studies and cannot tell me whether this
particular wish will be fulfilled
([Wang, Wang and Gai](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202/full)).

Later findings make the context important. A 2025 UK workplace trial
found no substantive improvement in its primary goal-progress
outcome and reported increased anxiety as a secondary outcome;
the authors discussed external resources and timing among possible
explanations
([Kudrna and colleagues](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11941803/)).
The abstract of a 2026 student trial reports improvement in
task aversiveness and willingness to begin over one week, while
the abstract of another 2026 paper reports no significant
benefit in two physical-activity trials
([student-trial abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41601124/);
[physical-activity abstract](https://cipp.ug.edu.pl/Does-mental-contrasting-with-implementation-intentions-MCII-help-to-increase-physical,225999,0,2.html)).

I therefore treat the exercise as something to examine and revise.
If I choose to try my plan at an ordinary opportunity, I can record
whether the cue appeared, whether the response was available, and
whether the exercise was useful or burdensome. A single attempt
cannot establish the method's causal effect.

## Check the work

Compare your attempt with the criteria below. Revise the plan if
something essential is missing; you can complete the exercise with
an honest decision to change direction.

| Criterion | A clear attempt | Revise this |
|---|---|---|
| Wish and outcome | Names a feasible action or concern and the personally meaningful result imagined | “Make everything better” without a time frame or workable scope |
| Obstacle and response | Identifies an inner obstacle and a specific response linked to a recognisable cue | “Try harder” without a cue or action |
| External limits | Names something the plan cannot control and considers revision | Treats illness, missing resources or someone else's decision as a character defect |
| Evidence | Explains why learning the procedure does not guarantee its benefit | Claims the research proves this plan must succeed |

For a final check, explain the difference between mental contrasting
and the if-then planning component without looking at the guide.
Then name one circumstance in which revising the wish would serve
your life better than pursuing it unchanged.
