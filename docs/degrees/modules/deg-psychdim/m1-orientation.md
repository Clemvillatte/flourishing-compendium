First module of Psychological Dimensions. The degree follows one question
through eight modules: what do psychologists currently know about the
processes that carry a life, and how can you read that research well enough
to use it? This module installs the map and the reading habits. We will
use six dimensions to describe one ordinary difficulty, and the map is
offered as a proposal, not a promise: it may help you notice an influence
or an option you had missed, and asking which processes and circumstances
may be involved is where alleviating suffering,
choosing more wisely and flourishing
under uncertainty can start. It is not a diagnosis, not an exhaustive
account of the mind, and not a guarantee that your circumstances will be
easy to change; you will test its usefulness on your own material as the
degree proceeds.

## Level and placement

L1 Orientation. Entry module of the degree; no prerequisites. The exit
criteria below are the entry competencies of the next module (m2, the
affective dimension).

## Outcomes

1. Define the six psychological dimensions of the extended evolutionary
   meta-model (affect, cognition, self, attention, motivation, overt
   behaviour) in your own words, giving one example of each from your own
   life, so the dimensions work as levers you can locate rather than
   categories to memorise.
2. Explain the variation-selection-retention-context frame in your own
   words and apply it to one behaviour of your own, naming which part is
   the variation, which the selection, which the retention, and what in
   your context does the selecting.
3. Name the levels of analysis the model uses (psychological,
   biophysiological, sociocultural) and give one example process at each
   level.
4. Report one study's design, evidence type, estimate and uncertainty
   using a worked model, state what the study can and cannot support, and
   say what further information you would need to judge whether the effect
   matters in practice.
5. State what this degree covers and excludes, and for the excluded
   territories that are owned elsewhere, name the owner.

Every outcome is taught, practised and assessed below; the alignment map at
the end shows where.

([university biography](https://www.unr.edu/psychology/steven-hayes)).
Only this public clip is assigned; no conference package, purchase or
clinical training is needed.

The interview is addressed to clinicians. Listen for an organising
proposal, not a diagnostic method or proof that the map will improve
everyone's life. Hayes proposes six psychological dimensions and
acknowledges that evidence for subdividing the additional levels is
still limited. The explanation below brings the terms into an
ordinary-life case, not a treatment plan
([assigned interview segment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sDGpXQLHLfY&t=3797)).

Three terms will help. A process is a changeable psychological event,
something that can be strengthened, weakened or redirected. An adaptation
is a strategy that meets a goal in a context. A dimension is one family
of processes, like affect or attention
([Hofmann and Hayes's framework overview](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11303929/)).

Pause at the end and name one dimension, one column and one additional
level without looking. A valid example is attention, context and the

### Step 2: what the six dimensions are (Outcome 1)

The clip names the dimensions; here is what the names mean as
families of processes, so you can find each one in your own life. The
degree's later modules define each dimension in full, with its research;
these working senses are enough for orientation.

- Affect: feeling states and emotions, and what they do to you (dread,
  delight, anger, calm).
- Cognition: learning, memory, judgment, perception; the thinking processes
  that interpret what comes in.
- Attention: the processes that select which information gets processed,
  and when.
- Self: who you take yourself to be: self-concept, identity, self-esteem.
- Motivation: what energises and directs behaviour: needs, goals, wants.
- Overt behaviour: the actions other people could see you take.

Now see all six at once in one everyday case. The case is fictional
(Jordan is not a real person; the details are constructed for teaching,
and you should test them against your own experience rather than take
them as findings). Jordan wants to start running again after two years
off. Affect: running feels bad at first (heavy legs, embarrassment when
seen), while the evening couch feels good; those feelings steer more
than Jordan admits. Cognition: Jordan remembers being "a runner" from
university, and believes people who exercise are disciplined, a belief
that makes every skipped day feel like evidence
about character. Attention: the phone's lights and buzzes pull attention
away from the running shoes by the door. Self: "I'm not a gym person" has
become part of how Jordan answers "who am I". Motivation: the doctor's
warning (extrinsic) and the memory of how good the runner's high felt
(intrinsic) compete to energise the behaviour. Overt behaviour: what
actually happens, the runs taken or skipped, the shoes worn or left.

Before the case moves on, one habit of reflection gets taught here, because
the graded work asks it of every example you bring. For each dimension you
name, you will also state three things: what matters in the case, what the
framework helps you understand or choose there, and one thing the case
leaves uncertain. Watch the move once, demonstrated on Jordan's attention.
What matters in the case: the run is the goal, the evenings are the hours
that could hold it, and the couch's ease keeps winning those hours. What
the framework helps with: attention selects which information gets
processed, so the lever is what the selecting process has to work with,
the phone left in another room for the run window, rather than willing
the run to feel urgent while the phone's lights keep interrupting. What
it leaves uncertain: this case alone cannot tell whether the trouble is
in selecting, sustaining or shifting attention, and knowing which would
change the fix. Now complete the same three-part reflection yourself, in
writing, on Jordan's motivation, where the doctor's warning and the memory
of the runner's high compete to energise the same behaviour. What matters
in the case is supplied: the warning is about Jordan's health, the memory
is about how the pursuit itself once felt, and they point the same
behaviour in different motivational directions. You supply the other two
parts: what the framework helps Jordan understand or choose there, and
one thing the case leaves uncertain. You will use the same three-part
move on your own examples in the graded deliverable.

Retrieval activity 1, first attempt (closed book, now): from memory,
write one sentence defining each of the six dimensions, with one example
of each from your own life. Keep what you write; it is practice and is
not submitted.

Run the same case through the variation-selection-retention-context frame
from the Maitland reading. The variation: Jordan tries mornings, lunchtimes
and evenings, alone and with a friend, over some weeks. The selection:
what survives is the Tuesday evening run with the friend, because it
carries companionship, a fixed appointment and a route both enjoy; the
context, Jordan's life with its time pressures and social ties, is what
does the selecting. The retention: with repetition the Tuesday run stops
needing a decision; the friend expects it, the shoes are by the door, and
the routine holds itself in place as a learned pattern. Whether that
retained pattern is adaptive is a question about Jordan's context, which
is exactly the EEMM's point: nothing in the pattern itself is healthy or
unhealthy apart from the life it sits in.

Finally, place the case at the three levels of analysis the model names.
Psychological: the dread, the self-belief, the attentional pull, the
motives; everything in the paragraph above. Biophysiological: what
Jordan's body does in and around the runs, the breath and heart rate
that rise with effort, the fatigue that follows, the restfulness of the
couch after a stressful day; observations a person could notice without
claiming to know their mechanism. Sociocultural: the friend who expects
Tuesday company, the workplace culture where "busy" is worn as a badge,
the medical system whose warning carried weight. One behaviour, three
levels; an account that wants to explain or change the pattern will
usually need more than one of them, and which ones depends on the
question being asked.

### Step 3: research literacy (Outcome 4)

The degree promises current knowledge, which means you will meet studies
all the way through. This step installs the reading habits once, here,
before any dimension material needs them.

Begin with two clips from Kristin Sainani's
[Statistics Fundamentals](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKiX54NglCo):
**25:46–44:11** (18:25) and **54:02–57:21** (3:19), total **21:44**.
Sainani teaches epidemiology and statistics at Stanford
([institutional profile](https://profiles.stanford.edu/kristin-sainani)).
You are learning to interpret results, not to calculate a t-test or
memorise critical values. Stop at the printed boundaries; the rest of
the lecture is not assigned.

One prerequisite will help. A standard error describes the sampling
variability of an estimate, rather than the spread of individual people's
scores. An estimate is the value calculated from a sample; a confidence
interval adds an uncertainty range. In the repeated-sampling account
used here, a 95 percent interval procedure covers the true parameter
in 95 percent of repetitions when its assumptions hold. That is not
a 95 percent probability assigned to the fixed parameter after seeing
one interval. If these ideas are new, the optional **03:29–09:21**
segment (5:52) introduces samples and standard error before the assigned
clips ([Sainani, inference and intervals](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKiX54NglCo&t=209)).

Pause after the first clip. In the hypothetical cherry-juice example,
say what the estimate describes and why an interval containing both
benefit and harm calls for caution. Feedback: the estimate describes
the sample's mean difference; an interval spanning benefit and harm
leaves both directions compatible with the data and model, rather than
establishing no effect. After the second, rewrite this
claim: "p = .05 means a five percent chance the finding is false."
Feedback: a p-value concerns the observed statistical summary and
more extreme summaries under a specified model, not the probability
that the hypothesis is false. The lecture's interval/test correspondence
uses the same two-sided t-model; it does not license comparing two
coefficients simply by checking whether their separate intervals overlap
([Sainani's worked model](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKiX54NglCo&t=2021)).

Keep the [American Statistical Association's
statement](https://www.stat.berkeley.edu/~aldous/Real_World/ASA_statement.pdf)
beside you: read only "What is a p-Value?" and principles 2 and 5 with
their explanations (about 300 words). These distinguish the conditional
probability from the probability a hypothesis is true, and statistical
significance from the size or practical importance of an effect.
The precise definition matters when you meet a striking claim about
something that might change your life.

Then read only the following parts of Erin I. Smith's Noba module,
[Thinking like a Psychological Scientist](https://nobaproject.com/modules/thinking-like-a-psychological-scientist)
(free; about 3,200 words in the selected spans):

- "Scientific Versus Everyday Reasoning" through the end of "The
  Interpretation of Research Results"; stop before "Why Should I Trust
  Science If It Can't Prove Anything?"
- "Scientific Theories," "Is Science Objective?" and "Science in Context";
  stop before the self-test answers.

The self-test boxes embedded in the first span are optional; use the
module's own worked appraisal below for assessed practice. The selected
reading supplies the non-statistical background: observations, induction,
testability, theory, research quality and levels of analysis. The omitted
NHST section and statistics glossary are not assigned; use Sainani and
the ASA statement for those definitions. When the caffeine example calls
a null difference "evidence against" a hypothesis, remember the narrower
inference just taught: not detecting a difference does not, on its own,
establish no effect ([Sainani, hypothesis testing](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LKiX54NglCo&t=2537)).

Retrieval activity 1, second attempt (closed book, after the reading):
from memory, without looking at your first attempt, write the six
sentences again. This is the draft of graded deliverable a. Compare
the attempts to find omissions and corrections; they differ in further
study as well as delay, so they do not isolate a spacing effect.

Then study the worked appraisal in Step 4 before attempting the faded
practice in the graded activity.

### Step 4: worked example with fading (Outcome 4)

The same skill (appraising a study) is demonstrated three times with the
guidance faded. All three appraisals use one real study, so you learn the
moves without needing new content.

The study: [Woolley, Giurge and Fishbach 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13428958/),
Study 1 (free on PMC; the module page quotes the numbers you need, so
reading the paper itself is optional). Question: do people stick with New
Year's resolutions better when their motivation is intrinsic (the pursuit
itself is enjoyable) or extrinsic (the pursuit is a means to an end)?
Design: a longitudinal survey. The researchers surveyed 2,000 United
States participants recruited through Amazon Mechanical Turk (mean age
38.12, standard deviation 12.02; 47.9 percent female, 52.1 percent male)
four times throughout a year. In the first survey, participants listed
the resolution they had set for the upcoming year and rated how
intrinsic and how extrinsic their motivation for it was; the three
follow-up surveys, at roughly four-month intervals, repeated those
ratings and asked how successful participants had been at sticking
with their resolution. Not everyone stayed: 1,046 completed the second
survey, 709 the third, and 577 the fourth. The analysis used
full-information maximum likelihood for the missing responses, and the
authors reported a similar completer-only analysis; these checks do not
establish that attrition could not affect the result. The analysis uses
cross-lagged panel models, which the authors describe as stationary
autoregressive cross-lagged panel models: motivation at one survey
predicts adherence at the next, and each variable's earlier measure
predicts its later measure where one exists. The first prediction,
from the first survey's motivation to the second survey's adherence,
cannot adjust for earlier adherence, because adherence was not
measured at the first survey: participants were asked about it only in
the follow-up surveys, after some months of pursuit had happened.
Checking what a model actually adjusts for is part of appraising it, and
neither the word longitudinal nor the phrase cross-lagged panel is by
itself a guarantee of causal control. ([Study 1 method: the first
survey and the follow-up surveys](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13428958/).)

1. Fully worked (demonstrated). Appraisal of the first result. What was
   found: adherence at the second survey was predicted by intrinsic
   motivation measured at the first, beta = 0.216, 95 percent confidence
   interval [0.13, 0.30]. (The paper also reports a standard error and a z
   statistic for each estimate; you do not need them for this module's
   moves, so they are left out of the required path. If you meet them
   elsewhere, the same caution applies: they are not extra evidence of
   importance.) The appraisal: the design is longitudinal and correlational, so this is an
   observational prediction. The coefficient is an association measured on
   that sample, and its confidence interval gives the range of plausible
   values for that association; under this statistical model the estimate
   reads as a positive association, and that is all the interval shows.
   An interval excluding zero is not protection from bias and not a
   measure of how much the association matters in practice. One further
   move belongs in every appraisal, and this is where it is demonstrated:
   say what you would need to judge whether this association matters in
   practice. To judge practical importance, we would need to establish
   how this coefficient is scaled and what difference on the adherence
   measure would matter; neither is established by what you have read
   here. The honest answer at this module's level is to report the
   estimate and its interval, and to state that practical magnitude
   cannot be judged from this report alone, naming what would be needed.
   Nothing was
   manipulated, so the study cannot show that intrinsic motivation causes
   adherence; it shows that people who rated their resolutions as more
   intrinsic at one survey were adhering better at the next. What the
   study can support: that prediction, in this population, over this
   year. The paper itself reports the association was primarily between
people: those who were generally more intrinsically motivated adhered
better ([Study 1 hypothesis testing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13428958/)).
That is a statement about differences among surveyed people,
not a promise that raising your own enjoyment will improve your own
adherence. What it cannot support: a causal claim. (The authors know
this; Study 4 in the paper randomly assigns an intrinsic-versus-
extrinsic framing for a health app and measures product scans a day
later.)
2. Steps named (partially worked). Appraisal of the second result. What
   was found: adherence at the third survey was predicted by intrinsic
   motivation at the second, beta = 0.099, 95 percent confidence
   interval [0.04, 0.20]. Before you
   begin the faded practice, one caution about the scale of these
   numbers: the betas are regression coefficients, so they
   are neither percentage improvements nor individual success
   probabilities nor treatment benefits. Your task in
   the faded practice: name the design, the evidence type, the estimate
   and its interval, and what this result can and cannot support, then add
   the magnitude move: what further information you would need to judge
   whether it matters in practice. One difference beyond the
   numbers: the first prediction had no earlier adherence to adjust
   for, because adherence was not measured at the first survey; this
   second prediction sits in a model that also carries the previous
   survey's adherence measure. One new wrinkle to notice: this coefficient is smaller than the
   first. Before concluding the association weakened across waves, ask
   what would establish that: a comparison between the coefficients, with
   its own uncertainty. The paper's authors read the overlapping
   intervals as suggesting the effect did not diminish significantly
   over time. Overlap alone cannot establish
that conclusion, because the separate intervals are not a test of the
difference between the coefficients; a direct comparison would be
needed. For this exercise, report the estimates and their uncertainty,
and leave the change-over-time question open: whether the association
   genuinely weakened is not settled by the results you have inspected
   ([Greenland and colleagues, misinterpretation 21](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4877414/)).

   Feedback to check your faded practice against, once you have written
   yours: the design is longitudinal and correlational; the estimate is
   0.099 with an interval from 0.04 to 0.20 on this sample; the result
   cannot support a causal claim because nothing was manipulated; and
   the practical-importance question stays open, because how the
   coefficient is scaled and what difference on the adherence measure
   would matter are not established by what you have read. An appraisal
   that names those four things is at this module's bar.
3. Independent (the graded deliverable). Appraisal of the third result
   and a robustness note. What was found: adherence at the fourth survey
   was predicted by intrinsic motivation at the third, beta = 0.080, 95
   percent confidence interval [0.01, 0.15]. The paper also reports that
   restricting the
   analysis to the 577 participants who completed all four surveys gave
   similar results, a sensitivity check on whether people dropping out of
   the survey might account for the pattern. Appraise this result and
   the robustness note on your own, in writing, using the same moves,
   including the magnitude move.

### The bounded domain, in plain terms

This degree covers what is currently known about the six dimensions and
how to read the research behind that knowledge. Three neighbouring
territories sit outside it, each owned elsewhere. Clinical treatment
protocols are excluded as training in delivering treatment (that
territory stays with the legacy therapeutic material); clinical studies
that test psychological mechanisms still count as dimension evidence
here. Wellbeing as an outcome science is owned by the Positive Psychology
walk; outcome evidence still counts here wherever an opened dimension
source uses it. Contemplative doctrine is owned by the contemplative
practica; contemplative science appears here as attentional evidence,
and where an opened contemplative-science source speaks to affect, self
or motivation it may serve those dimensions too. Beyond those, material
is admitted only where it serves the six dimensions for this degree's
learner: the interpersonal and attraction material and the group
perception material are excluded (no learner need on record), and the
same test applies to anything else that merely fits the topic. If you
want an excluded territory, this degree points you to it and stays in
its lane.

## Rubric (observable criteria)

Each criterion with a strong example and a rejection example, so the
learner can self-check.

1. The six definitions match the framework's usage, each with a life
   example, and each example names what matters in the case, what the
   framework helps the learner understand or choose there, and one
   thing the framework leaves uncertain. Strong: "Attention is the
   family of processes that select which information gets processed; my
   ability to read in a noisy cafe is attention doing that selecting;
   what mattered in the case was that the essay was due the next day and
   my phone kept lighting up; the framework helps me see that I can
   change what the selecting process has to work with, not just try
   harder; what it leaves uncertain is whether my trouble is selecting,
   sustaining or shifting, which this example alone cannot tell."
   Rejection: "Attention is paying attention to things" (everyday
   usage, no dimension meaning, no example), or a correct definition
   with an example that names none of the three things (what matters,
   what the framework helps with, what remains uncertain).
2. The variation-selection-retention-context application is real and each
   part is named correctly. Strong: "I tried running: the variation was
   the different times of day I tried; what selected the 6 a.m. slot was
   that no one needed anything from me then; the retention is that the
   6 a.m. run stopped needing a daily decision and now holds itself as my
   routine; the context that does the selecting is my household
   morning." Rejection: "Everything adapts, like evolution, so my habits
   evolved" (the four parts never named, no personal behaviour), or
   naming the running shoes by the door as the retention when they are a
   cue the pattern uses (retention is the learned pattern persisting,
   which the cues help hold in place).
3. The three given examples are labelled at the right level and the
   three own examples sit at their stated levels. Strong: "The village
   greeting example is sociocultural because the community does the
   selecting; my example is my family's dinner-table rule that selects
   homework before screens." Rejection: labelling the village example
   biophysiological (the selecting agent misidentified), or offering
   three examples all at the psychological level.
4. The appraisal names design, evidence type, estimate and interval,
   limits, what the result can and cannot support, and what further
   information would be needed to judge practical magnitude. Strong:
   "This is a longitudinal correlational prediction; the coefficient is
   0.080 with an interval from 0.01 to 0.15, on this sample; it cannot
   show causation because nothing was manipulated, and the completer
   analysis is a sensitivity check on drop-out, which is a different
   question from causation; and I cannot judge whether an association of
   this size matters in practice from this report alone, because how the
   coefficient is scaled and what difference on the adherence measure
   would matter are not established by what I have read." Rejection: "The study proves intrinsic
   motivation works because p < .05" (no design named, p value read as
   proof, causal claim from a correlational design), copying the
   coefficient, standard error and p-value without the design, the
   limits or the magnitude statement (three numbers are not an
   appraisal), "the effect is small" with no stated basis for the
   magnitude claim, or "the authors said the intervals overlap so there
   is no difference" (deferring to a shortcut that is not a test of the
   difference).
5. The scope statement states the degree's positive coverage in the
   learner's own words (the six dimensions as levers on the learner's own
   life, and the reading of the evidence behind claims that reach it),
   then names the three owned exclusions with their owners, then states
   the admission test for the rest. Strong: "This degree teaches the six
   dimensions (affect, cognition, attention, self, motivation, overt
   behaviour) as levers for reading and changing my own episodes, and
   how to appraise the research behind claims that reach my life; it
   excludes wellbeing as a separate outcome-science subject, which the
   Positive Psychology walk owns, treatment protocols, which stay with
   the legacy therapeutic material, and contemplative doctrine, which
   stays with the contemplative practica; anything that merely fits the
   topic, like the attraction material, is out unless a learner need is
   stated." Rejection: "This degree covers everything about psychology"
   (no exclusions named), naming only one or two of the three owned
   exclusions, or an answer that names every exclusion and its owner
   correctly but never states what the degree teaches (the positive
   half of the outcome unassessed).
