You put a book where you will see it, then discover that the first
thing you reach for is still your phone. A label such as "weak"
leaves the sequence of events unexplained. Here you will examine a modest
behaviour in context, distinguish a learned tendency from an intention,
and ask what the evidence actually supports.

## Entry and outcomes

Level: L2 to L3. Begin with guided evidence comparisons, then select
and analyse your own low-stakes pattern with less scaffolding.
Bring M3's classical/operant distinction, M4's measurement caution
and M6's mixed-motive and adherence boundaries. This is education,
not treatment, a productivity contest or training to manipulate others.

By completion, you can:

- **Habit:** explain a cue–response association, distinguish habit
  from mere repetition and intention, and critique a universal
  formation-time promise.
- **Learning:** distinguish stimulus learning from action/consequence
  learning, reinforcement from punishment and negative from positive
  without using those terms as moral labels.
- **Self-regulation:** apply identification, selection, implementation
  and monitoring to a competing-goal episode; distinguish three
  measurement families and their population limits.
- **Contested evidence:** compare a meta-analysis with a later
  preregistered multisite test, retaining numerical estimates,
  analysis status, uncertainty and limits on everyday inference.
- **Functional inquiry:** keep a brief private habit diary and build
  a revisable account of an own pattern, including the narrow
  EEMM/FAP application without conducting therapy.

## Investigating the cue–response pattern

Carden and Wood describe habits as learned context–response associations
arising through repetition in context. Repeated action alone does
not show the association controls the action; a person can deliberately
repeat something for a goal. A habit may support an endorsed goal
or conflict with a newer intention
([mechanism paragraphs](https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/Carden.Wood_.2018.pdf)).

Worked example: fictional Lee finishes a work session, notices the
phone beside the keyboard and opens a familiar feed. A diary might
show this sequence repeatedly. It would support investigating that
cue and response, but not prove unconscious control, identify a brain
mechanism or establish that the phone caused every delay.

For completion practice, compare "I intentionally call my sibling
each Sunday" with "After hearing a particular alert, I open the app
before considering why." Feedback: both may repeat; the second more
directly suggests cue-linked automatic responding, but both need
further observation. Habit and intention are not mutually exclusive.

Wood illustrates friction as time, distance and effort. The review
also discusses disruption of familiar cues and changes to environments;
it does not support the claim that sheer repetition is the only
possible influence on habit expression
([selected talk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Ywec1MbeQDk&t=76);
[review, environmental change](https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/Carden.Wood_.2018.pdf)).

Singh and colleagues included 20 studies with 2,601 participants;
11 were rated high risk of bias, and only four reported formation
time. Reported times varied substantially. Their pooled change in
self-reported habit scores was a pre/post standardised difference,
not a universal number of days or necessarily a randomised between-group
effect
([Abstract](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11641623/)).

Correct this claim: "Repeat any action for 21 days and it becomes
automatic." Feedback: neither a universal duration nor inevitable
automaticity follows from that evidence. A three-day diary in this
course can illuminate context, not certify a newly established habit.

## Keep learning terminology functional

Classical conditioning concerns learning relations between stimuli;
operant conditioning concerns learning relations between action
and consequences. Reinforcement increases subsequent behaviour;
punishment decreases it. Positive/negative refer to adding/removing
an event, not good/bad character
([Bouton's explanation](https://nobaproject.com/modules/conditioning-and-learning)).

Worked example: if silencing an irritating notification by opening an
app makes that opening more frequent, removing the irritation is
a possible negative-reinforcement account. It is not established
merely because Lee dislikes the sound. The subsequent increase
and the contingency would need evidence.

For completion practice, a pleasant acknowledgement follows a small
task and the task becomes more frequent; in another fictional case,
losing a privilege after an action makes that action less frequent.
Feedback: possible positive reinforcement and negative punishment.
If frequency does not change as specified, the function cannot
be inferred from the pleasantness of the event or the organiser's intent.
Do not create punishment experiments for this course.

## A process, several measures

Werner and Berkman organise self-control into identification of a
conflict/need to regulate, selection of a strategy, implementation
as a concrete tactic, and monitoring whether to maintain, switch
or stop. This is a conceptual process account, not proof that one
strategy works for everyone
([four-stage discussion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11423838/)).

Lee wants a short rest and also wants to read a chosen chapter.
Identification names the actual competing goals; selection might
consider a different reading location; implementation specifies where
the book and phone will be; monitoring asks whether the arrangement
is feasible and what else changed. A legitimate result might be
choosing rest rather than forcing the reading.

Supply your own strategy and concrete tactic for a different ordinary
conflict, with a stopping rule. Feedback: "avoid distraction" is too
general to be a tactic; "put the phone in an accessible nearby drawer
for one planned ten-minute reading opportunity" is specific. Safety,
accessibility and necessary communication take priority.

Reed's study measured executive function, physiological indices
including heart-rate variability, and subjective self-regulation in
149 adults aged 60–93, followed semi-annually for five years.
These indices were only modestly related. The selected sample and
observational design do not make them three interchangeable scores
for everyone or show that changing HRV causes better self-regulation
([Abstract and Participants](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7250393/)).

Alarcón-Espinoza's review concerns under-18s in natural contexts.
Its concern with recording duration and sequence can inform a question
about diary quality, but it is not an adult effectiveness review
([Abstract and eligibility](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9171140/)).

Practise by mapping a self-report of regulation, a physiological
recording and an executive-task result to the three measure families.
Feedback: subjective, physiological, task-based executive function.
For a private phone-use diary, record context, sequence and approximate
timing; do not pretend that diary is an HRV measure or an adult
validation study. A useful observation and a validated measure are
different things.

## Reading ego-depletion evidence

Ego depletion concerns the hypothesis that initial self-control
exertion reduces later self-control performance. A proposed limited
resource is an explanation of that pattern, not a resource measured
merely by finding a between-group difference
([Dang, Introduction and strength-model discussion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6013521/)).

Inzlicht's 2019 interview explains why publication selection and
replication changed his confidence, while resisting a simple universal
no-effect conclusion. Keep its date visible: it cannot discuss a
later 2021 project
([historical interview window](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fuA1l7bL954&t=2992)).

Dang's all-task meta-analysis reports Hedges' \(g=0.38\),
95% CI [0.31, 0.45], versus trim-and-fill-adjusted \(g=0.24\),
[0.16, 0.32], with substantial heterogeneity. The adjustment and
task-selection assumptions matter; the paper itself does not treat
its tentative selected-task result as conclusive
([Table 2 and final caution](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6013521/)).

Vohs and colleagues' 36-laboratory project tested 3,531 people before
exclusions; 1,068 were excluded from confirmatory analyses under
preregistered criteria. The confirmatory effect was \(d=0.06\),
not statistically significant. The exploratory analysis retaining
the full sample found \(d=0.08\), statistically significant.
Do not substitute the latter for the planned result or treat \(g\)
and \(d\) as identical analyses
([2021 Abstract and Participants](https://bura.brunel.ac.uk/bitstream/2438/28246/1/FullText.pdf)).

Worked appraisal: "A positive meta-analysis proves my willpower tank
is empty" conflates an effect estimate, one mechanism and a personal
diagnosis. "The later small null means tiredness is imaginary" also
goes beyond the studied sequential-task effect. A better comparison
asks which tasks, samples, corrections and analyses support which
claim, while leaving genuine everyday exhaustion and constraints
outside an unsupported inference.

Complete a two-row evidence card: design/sample, effect estimate,
analysis status, uncertainty and one applicability limit for each
paper. Feedback: distinguish the meta-analytic \(g\) estimates and
adjustment from the preregistered multisite \(d\), and distinguish
non-significance from certainty of no possible effect.

## Independent diary and functional inquiry

Choose one safe, ordinary pattern that already occurs in your life.
Over **three ordinary days**, spend no more than **ten minutes
per day** noting a few opportunities: context/cue, observable action,
what immediately followed, and a plausible competing goal or alternative
explanation. Mark "not observed" and missing opportunities honestly.
Do not record other people, alter medication, restrict sleep/food,
provoke distress or choose a dangerous/addictive behaviour as an
unsupervised change target.

Use this course's functional organiser: what occurred before, what
the action was, what followed, and which relation you hypothesise.
Separate observation from inference; name one observation that would
weaken the favoured account. This is a tentative educational analysis,
not proof of behavioural function or a clinical assessment.

Maitland's Table 1 links overt behaviour to clinically relevant
behaviour in FAP, a therapy-specific target. That row does not define
all overt action as a clinical problem or authorise learners to evoke
and shape another person's behaviour in a relationship
([Table 1 and note](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12392214/)).

For final assessment, write 700–900 words plus the private/redacted diary:

- **Pattern:** justify the chosen cue–response hypothesis and its
  alternative; distinguish repetition, intention and evidence of habit.
- **Learning:** use a new daily example to distinguish classical/
  operant learning and the direction/addition-removal logic.
- **Process and measures:** map one own goal conflict through all
  four stages; distinguish diary observations from the three study
  measures, and state the older-adult/under-18 population limits.
  For that same episode, say what subjective, executive-task and
  physiological information would each contribute and which you
  did not collect. Do not invent task results or HRV recordings.
- **Evidence comparison:** include the two-row depletion card,
  preserving numbers, adjustments, planned/exploratory status and
  what neither establishes about your own exhaustion.
- **Framework and transfer:** distinguish the EEMM lens from the
  FAP target; for each outcome family state a private lived instance,
  useful understanding or choice and remaining uncertainty. Propose
  a safe next observation, not a guaranteed fix.

| Criterion | Pass | Revise |
|---|---|---|
| Habit/diary | Genuine bounded observations, cue hypothesis, alternative and no duration promise. | Invented entries, repetition equated with proven habit or three-day automaticity claimed. |
| Learning | Correct stimulus/action and increase/decrease plus add/remove distinctions. | Negative means bad or intended reward automatically called reinforcement. |
| Process/measures | Four stages applied; measure families and population/design limits explicit. | One diary treated as physiological measurement or universal validation. |
| Contested evidence | Accurate estimates, adjustment and planned/exploratory distinction with uncertainty. | Preferred p-value selected, mechanism inferred or null made universal. |
| Functional/framework transfer | Revisable own-pattern account; FAP bounded; each family useful but uncertain. | Clinical authority, unsupported causal claim or improvement/disclosure graded. |

All criteria must pass. After another activity, retrieve the four
stage names and the difference between a planned and exploratory
result, then correct from the sources. You are ready for M8 when
you can investigate one pattern honestly and safely, not when you
have acquired a "good habit" or demonstrated superior self-control.
