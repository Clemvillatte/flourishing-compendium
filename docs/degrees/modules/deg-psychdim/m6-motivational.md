A goal can matter deeply and still be difficult to pursue. You might
value a friendship yet find arranging a call exhausting, or enjoy
learning while feeling pressured to prove your worth. This module
helps separate those reasons without deciding that struggle shows a
defective character.

## Entry and outcomes

Level: L2, Guided application. You have practised independent inquiry
in M5; this new theoretical framework returns to worked examples
before application to an unused case and one of your own goals.
Bring the distinction between observation and interpretation, and
between a prediction, an association and a causal test.

By completion, you can:

- **Motivation:** distinguish all six positions on the SDT autonomy
  continuum, allow mixed motives and distinguish the three proposed
  basic psychological needs without equating autonomy with independence.
- **Regulation goals:** distinguish pleasantness from usefulness,
  explain the 2024 review's proposed eudaimonic category as a proposal,
  and apply control/value appraisals to an achievement-related case.
- **Evidence:** compare importance-only and experienced-interest
  predictions of adherence, distinguishing the 2025 study designs,
  result magnitude and limits on transfer.
- **Walk-of-life analysis:** examine one own goal, link motive and
  affect, and design a modest, revisable observation without treating
  adherence, pleasure or self-discipline as a measure of human worth.

## Reasons differ in quality, not just strength

In SDT, autonomy means volition or endorsement, not doing everything
alone or having no rules. Relatedness concerns connection and mattering;
competence concerns felt effectiveness and development. These are
proposed psychological needs within the theory, not three required
personality traits or a promise that every constraint can be removed
([Ryan, needs and distinctions](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ww4QGMV94A8&t=518)).

Use this plain-language continuum map. It combines the selected talk
with the distinctions in the short preprint passage
([Ryan and Deci, Figure 1 and definitions](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/2020_RyanDeci_CEP_PrePrint.pdf)).

| Form | Question about the reason | Fictional example |
|---|---|---|
| Amotivation | Is intentional motivation absent, perhaps through low value or perceived inability? | "I see no point and don't know how to begin." |
| External regulation | Is action regulated by a separable reward or penalty? | "I'll do it for the payment." |
| Introjected regulation | Is there internal pressure such as guilt or contingent self-worth? | "If I skip, I'll feel like a worthless person." |
| Identified regulation | Is the activity's value personally endorsed? | "I value keeping the shared space usable." |
| Integrated regulation | Does that endorsed value fit other core values and commitments? | "Caring for this space fits how I want to live with others." |
| Intrinsic motivation | Is the activity itself interesting or enjoyable? | "I enjoy tending the plants." |

The four middle forms are extrinsic because action serves a separable
outcome or purpose; the latter two can be autonomous. An important
activity need not become enjoyable to have an endorsed reason, and
several motives can coexist. The continuum is not a timetable through
which everyone must progress
([Ryan and Deci, definitions and multiple motives](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/2020_RyanDeci_CEP_PrePrint.pdf)).

Worked case: Nia attends a free community language group because
conversation is enjoyable, contact with relatives matters, and missing
would trigger guilt. The first reason is intrinsic, the second identified,
the third potentially introjected; this mixed description does not
establish that all reasons are equally strong. If Nia freely asks a
more experienced learner for guidance, dependence on help need not
contradict autonomy.

For completion practice, classify "I enjoy the puzzles," "I need the
certificate for a reward" and "Learning this fits the other commitments
I have deliberately chosen." Feedback: intrinsic, external if the
reward actually regulates action, and potentially integrated, respectively.
For the last, congruence across commitments must be supplied, not
inferred merely because the goal sounds admirable.

Now compare three statements about attending the same language group:

- **A:** “I see no point in joining, do not feel able to begin, and have
  no intention of attending.”
- **B:** “I intend to attend because otherwise I will feel worthless.”
- **C:** “I intend to attend because contact with my relatives matters,
  even when I do not enjoy the session.”

Which statement suggests absent intentional motivation, which suggests
internal pressure, and which suggests endorsed value? What information
would make you revise each interpretation?

Feedback: A is consistent with amotivation as described in the assigned
definitions; B suggests introjected regulation; C suggests identified
regulation, not amotivation merely because enjoyment is absent.
These are interpretations of the stated reasons, not diagnoses.
Nonattendance alone cannot establish amotivation: someone may value
the group and intend to attend but lack transport or time. Check
intention, the reason offered and practical constraints before choosing
the label.
([Ryan and Deci, definitions and multiple motives](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/04/2020_RyanDeci_CEP_PrePrint.pdf))

Link each proposed need to one observation in Nia's case, and mark what
is missing. Feedback: chosen guidance may indicate volition; feeling
welcomed might inform relatedness; a manageable task with useful feedback
might inform competence. Attendance alone establishes none of these.
Do not diagnose a need deficit from a missed meeting.

## What is the emotion for?

Tamir distinguishes seeking a pleasant experience from seeking an emotion
for what it may help accomplish. Pleasantness and usefulness can coincide
or diverge. This is a question about goals, not an instruction to make
yourself anxious or angry
([selected interview](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6R2ALQfEZwA&t=561)).

The 2024 review classifies goals as hedonic, instrumental/contra-hedonic
and eudaimonic, with its eudaimonic example directed toward another's
good. It finds none of its reviewed models explicitly prioritises
that third category and calls for empirical consolidation. Treat this
as the reviewers' theoretically derived extension, not a settled
universal taxonomy or a clinical test of virtue
([classification, Results and conclusion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11479914/)).

Worked case: before a language presentation, Nia wants relief from
anxiety, enough alertness to prepare, and a way to welcome a nervous
newcomer. Relief is a hedonic aim; alertness for preparation is instrumental;
welcoming the newcomer can illustrate the review's other-directed
proposal. These aims may overlap. They do not make discomfort inherently
good or justify neglecting Nia's own safety and needs.

Pekrun's control-value account asks about appraised control over an
achievement activity/outcome and its subjective value. High importance
with uncertain control can help explain exam anxiety; felt competence
and interest can help explain enjoyment. Appraisals are the person's
perceptions, not proof of actual control or an emotion diagnosis
([Pekrun's selected model](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=igSLOQoWuPQ&t=951)).

For completion practice, a fictional learner values an exam but doubts
being able to prepare; another feels able and enjoys the topic.
Explain the different appraisals and one uncertainty in each. Feedback:
importance/uncertain control versus competence/interest are relevant
to the proposed account, but the sketch does not establish an individual's
emotion, all its causes, or what intervention would help.

## What adherence studies show

An importance-only prediction says that a highly valued goal should
be easier to sustain because of its future benefit. An interest-based
prediction asks whether the experience of doing it also predicts
continuation. These are comparison hypotheses for this exercise,
not a claim that SDT ignores value or that every valued goal can be fun.

Woolley and colleagues report year-long, self-reported goal tracking
in a U.S. sample, a one-month Chinese follow-up, retrospective records
of 14 days of steps with screenshot checks, and a randomised app-framing
experiment with a 24-hour follow-up.
The longer-term predictions and short-term experimental effect are
different evidence, not one year-long randomised test
([Abstract and Study 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13428958/)).

In Study 4's primary analysis of 763 participants after exclusions,
the intrinsic framing group scanned a mean 11.55 products, versus
9.18 with extrinsic framing; the reported standardised effect was
\(d=0.21\), with 95% confidence interval [0.07, 0.36].
The authors excluded zero scanners from that primary analysis, then
reported robustness analyses; the condition did not significantly
change the probability of any versus zero scanning in their hurdle
model. These distinctions matter when someone claims the intervention
made previously inactive people begin
([Study 4 Method and Results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13428958/)).

This supports a bounded causal claim about framing and app use in
that short study, not better health, universal motivation or guaranteed
year-long adherence. The authors explicitly leave generalisation beyond
online participant pools open
([Study 4 and General Discussion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13428958/)).

Practise appraising "Enjoyable framing makes anyone maintain any
healthy goal for a year." Feedback: the statement combines the wrong
population, outcome and time horizon with excessive certainty. Replace
it with the actual short-term causal finding and separate the longer-term
observational prediction. Keep the small effect and uncertainty rather
than reduce the evidence to whether a p-value crosses a threshold.

## Guided own-goal analysis and unused case

Choose an ordinary goal you already have, such as keeping in touch,
reading for interest or completing a small shared task. Keep identifying
details private. Map each plausible motive with the words or observation
supporting it; mark absent information instead of force-fitting all six.
Then note a possible need support, a regulation goal and a control/value
appraisal. This is an analysis, not a demand to improve motivation.

Select one low-cost, safe observation for the next ordinary opportunity:
for example, note whether one version of a reading activity is interesting
and feasible. Set a stopping point and identify constraints such as time,
access, care responsibilities or fatigue. Do not remove essential rewards,
test another person or change treatment/health behaviour for the course.
Feedback: a plausible prediction and honest uncertainty suffice;
an observation cannot establish a causal effect on your life.

For the final task, use this unused case: Rowan repairs shared bicycles
to earn a small payment, because reliable transport for neighbours
matters, and because solving mechanical puzzles is enjoyable. Rowan
also says, "If I cannot finish, I am a failure." Before demonstrating
a repair, Rowan wants to feel less nervous and appear capable, but
is unsure the tools will be available.

For contrast, Cory values helping and intends to join the repair
session, but has not attended because the workshop is closed. Use
this second invented statement to distinguish absence of behaviour
from absence of intentional motivation.

Write 600–800 words, plus your private/redacted own-goal note:

- **Motives and needs:** classify supported reasons, explain why mixed
  motives are possible, distinguish identified from integrated regulation
  and state which evidence for integration or need support is missing.
  Contrast Cory's barrier with amotivation: explain why not attending,
  by itself, does not establish the latter and name the intention/reason
  information needed before interpreting a different case.
- **Affect and goals:** distinguish hedonic/instrumental aims, supply
  one hypothetical other-directed aim with the proposal label, and
  explain control/value appraisals without predicting an inevitable emotion.
- **Comparison and evidence:** compare the importance-only and
  interest-based predictions for Rowan; use the 2025 design, result
  magnitude and zero-scanning caveat to limit advice.
- **Own transfer:** for motivation, regulation-goal and adherence
  families, show a private lived instance, useful understanding/choice
  and uncertainty. Present your observation plan, not invented results.

| Criterion | Pass | Revise |
|---|---|---|
| Motivation | Correct supported forms and mixed reasons; distinguishes amotivation from intentional action and a practical barrier using stated intention/reasons; explains the integration boundary. | Nonattendance or low enjoyment treated as amotivation, extrinsic equated with controlled, or integrated inferred from any good intention. |
| Needs | Distinguishes volition, effectiveness and connection with missing evidence. | Autonomy means solitude, no rules, or a need deficit diagnosed from attendance. |
| Goals/appraisals | Distinguishes pleasant/useful; labels the third-category proposal; applies control/value with limits. | Unpleasant emotion prescribed or theoretical category treated as universal virtue score. |
| Evidence comparison | Same-question predictions; observational/experimental distinction; actual small effect, horizon and zero/nonzero caveat. | Year-long causal guarantee, better-health inference or p-value alone. |
| Walk-of-life plan | Own goal, bounded observation, constraints, safe stopping point and honest uncertainty for each family. | Fabricated outcomes, self-blame or disclosure/improvement graded. |

### Calibrating the criteria

The following specimens are invented teaching responses, not new study
findings, genuine learner records or source quotations.

- **Motivation:** “Rowan's payment suggests external regulation, valued
  transport identified regulation, enjoyment intrinsic motivation and
  the self-worth threat introjection. I lack evidence that the value is
  integrated with other commitments. Cory intends to help but faces a
  closed workshop; that is not the absence of intentional motivation
  described by amotivation.” Rejection example: “Both have missed a
  session, so neither is motivated; any socially useful goal is integrated.”
- **Needs:** “I would ask whether Rowan endorses the task, feels able
  to do it and experiences connection; taking paid work establishes none
  of these by itself. Asking a skilled neighbour for chosen guidance
  need not remove volition.” Rejection example: “Autonomy requires
  working alone, and needing help proves a competence deficit.”
- **Goals/appraisals:** “Wanting less nervousness is a hedonic aim;
  wanting alertness to demonstrate safely is instrumental. Helping a
  nervous newcomer could illustrate the review's proposed other-directed
  category, not a proven virtue score. Valued work with uncertain access
  to tools gives a control/value question, not an inevitable emotion.”
  Rejection example: “Rowan must feel anxious to care, and discomfort
  proves the eudaimonic category is working.”
- **Evidence comparison:** “An importance-only prediction stresses
  valued transport; an interest-based prediction also considers the
  pleasure of repair. The app-framing experiment concerns 24-hour app
  use, not a year-long randomised health benefit. Its reported
  \(d=0.21\), 95% CI [0.07, 0.36], is small; the zero/nonzero result
  does not establish that framing made inactive people start.”
  Rejection example: “The study proves that enjoyment makes anyone
  maintain any healthy goal for a year.” Check the distinction against
  the [assigned Study 4 results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13428958/).
- **Walk-of-life plan:** a hypothetical private-response form might say,
  “For my ordinary goal A, interest and a valued connection are distinct
  reasons; less pressure and being useful to someone are distinct
  regulation aims. I will note interest, feasibility and the reason I
  actually give at one ordinary opportunity, stopping if it becomes
  burdensome. Time and fatigue may explain noncompletion; this will not
  test a causal theory or require a positive result.” A learner must
  use their own actual goal and retain an uncertainty for each required
  family, not relabel this invented specimen as lived evidence.
  Rejection example: “I will invent a successful observation, remove
  necessary rewards or blame myself if motivation does not improve.”

All criteria must pass. Retrieve the six motive forms without the
table after another activity, then correct your distinctions; classify
"I value it, though I do not enjoy it" once more. You are ready for
M7 when you can explain motives and adherence without confusing a
good reason with an automatic habit or an established causal mechanism.
