# m4 — Yangsheng: nourishing life

**Level**: L3 Independent analysis (the scaffolding from m2 and m3 is

## What this module is for

The first three modules read the core texts as manuals for living.
This module turns to the tradition's own name for the life side of
that reading: yangsheng, nourishing life. The term is quoted in m1
from the talk's transcript, where Komjathy defines it as "the
traditional Chinese and Daoist name for health and longevity
techniques" and then widens it: "a fuller sense of what we do to
nourish life, to tend to our foundational vitality and our core
aliveness", "caring for or tending to life"
([transcript](https://youtubetotranscript.com/transcript?v=WDwPln1CczI&current_language_code=en),
passage locator "Now I want to discuss Daoist yangsheng"; the
definition was verified when the m1 ledger was written, and its
verification record is in the degree ledger).

The assigned chapters make claims about life in three registers.
Chapter 12 names what floods and dulls the senses: color, tone,
flavor, the chase, rare objects, and says the sage is "for the belly
and not for the eye". Chapter 44 asks which is dearer, your
reputation or your body, and lands on knowing what is enough and when
to stop. Chapter 50 describes one "good at protecting his life" and
says the reason is that "he has no death place in him". The talk gives
the living tradition's frame around the same territory: its printed
key moments name sections on the elements of nourishing life, on
stillness and emptiness meditation, on sleeping posture and walking,
on quiet standing, and close on the question "How Do We Nourish
Life".

What the module asks you to do with this material is harder than what
m2 and m3 asked, because the demonstration is gone. You read the
chapters with marking prompts, and the analysis, the source check and
the application to your own life are yours to perform. The Walk-of-Life
question here is direct: the tradition treats tending life as a
practice with a rationale you can state, and the module asks you to
state it, test it against one paraphrase, and design one bounded
piece of it for your own days.

## Outcomes

On completing this module the learner can:

1. **Explain yangsheng in their own words.** The learner can state
   what the term names, anchored in the verified definition and at
   least one assigned chapter, and can say what the term adds beyond
   "looking after your health", naming at least one thing the
   tradition includes that ordinary health talk does not, and one
   thing the sources do not promise (longevity itself is claimed by
   the talk's title, and the learner can say honestly what the
   chapters do and do not promise).

2. **Analyse an assigned chapter without a template.** The learner
   can read chapter 12, 44 or 50, locate the sentence or sentences in
   which the chapter makes a claim about what nourishes or exhausts
   life, quote them, cite the chapter, and separate what the passage
   says from any application the learner proposes, without a worked
   demonstration in front of them. This is the m2 Outcome 2
   distinction performed independently.

3. **Evaluate a paraphrase against the sources.** Given the
   paraphrase "Daoism is a system for living long", the learner can
   check it against the assigned chapters and the talk's printed
   sections, quote the passages that support or resist it, and state
   precisely what the sources support (a set of claims about the
   senses, enoughness and the protection of life) and what they do not
   (a method for extending lifespan, which the chapters do not
   teach).

4. **Design one bounded nourishing-life practice and teach it
   back.** The learner can select one concrete adjustment from their
   own life in the territory the chapters name (an excess of the
   senses, a place where they do not know what is enough, or a way
   they treat life as a thing to manage), design a bounded practice
   with a stated duration and limit, give the reasons in terms of the
   assigned passages, state what the passages cannot establish, and
   explain the practice to a peer in a short teach-back that a reader
   could act on.

## Steps

### Step 1: Read the three chapters (about 9 minutes estimated on the
primary text with the three-times study multiplier; about 3 minutes
raw on two readings of chapters 12 and 50 plus one re-reading of
chapter 44)

Read chapter 12 twice, chapter 50 twice, and chapter 44 once (you read
it in m2). On your readings, mark:

- In chapter 12, the list of what dulls the senses, and the closing
  line about the belly and the eye.
- In chapter 44, the three questions, and the lines about knowing
  what is enough and when to stop.
- In chapter 50, the sentence that gives the reason the three in ten
  die ("Because they treat life as life"), and the closing sentence
  about the death place.

There is no worked demonstration in this module. In m2 you watched a
contrast from chapter 11 worked in front of you; in m3 you watched
the Cook Ding passage worked. Here you mark the passages yourself and
bring your reading to the retrieval practice and the graded
deliverable. The distinction you are carrying over from those modules
is the same one: what the passage says, and what you propose to do
with it, are different things, and both halves have to appear in your
work.

### Step 2: The term and the talk (reading time included in the
module-prose allowance)

The word yangsheng is already in your vocabulary from m1. This
module's job is to connect it to the chapters you just read. The
definition quoted above names techniques of health and longevity and
then a fuller sense of tending foundational vitality. The chapters
name the territory in their own way: chapter 12's senses and their
excess, chapter 44's enoughness, chapter 50's protection of life. The
talk's own printed key moments show how the living tradition
organizes that territory: its sections are printed on the watch page
as "Ten Elements of Yang of Nourishing Life", "Stillness and
Emptiness Meditation Practice", "Sleeping Posture", "Quiet Standing"
and the closing question "How Do We Nourish Life".

One caution belongs in the open. The talk's title promises
nourishing life, and the tradition's literature historically includes
longevity claims. The assigned chapters do not teach a method for
living long. Chapter 44 promises that knowing enough escapes shame
and danger; chapter 50 describes a quality of life, not a quantity of
years. In your graded work you will state this boundary yourself,
because the honest version of this walk does not sell the tradition
as a longevity system, and the sources you were assigned do not
support that sale.

### Step 3: Watch the assigned segments (about 11 minutes 42 seconds
measured on the assigned segments)

Watch the four assigned segments, identified by the talk's own
printed key moments:

- 8:50 to 9:42, "Ten Elements of Yang of Nourishing Life" (about 52
  seconds).
- 19:14 to 23:28, from "Stillness and Emptiness Meditation Practice"
  through "Sleeping Posture" (about 4 minutes 14 seconds).
- 37:57 to 42:50, from "Basic Yangchang Practice" through "The Three
  Fields" (about 4 minutes 53 seconds).
- 52:00 to the end, "How Do We Nourish Life" (about 1 minute 43
  seconds).

While watching, keep one question: which of the three chapter
registers does each section belong to, the senses and their excess,
enoughness, or the protection of life? Some sections will not fit any
of the three cleanly. Note those too. You will use your notes in
retrieval activity 3 and in the graded paraphrase check.

### Step 4: Retrieval activities (about 20 minutes, estimate; about 5
minutes each)

1. Without looking back, write the chapter 12 closing line about the
   belly and the eye. Check it against the text. Then write one
   sentence on what the line claims, in your own words, without
   adding anything about your life or modern habits.

2. Chapter 50 says the three in ten who die treat life as life. In
   two sentences, state what you take that sentence to claim, and one
   thing the sentence does not claim. Check your second sentence
   against the chapter before moving on.

3. From your viewing notes, name one talk section you placed in one
   of the three chapter registers and one you could not place, and
   give your reason for each in one sentence.

4. Ungraded practice for the graded practice design: pick one thing
   in your week that floods your attention the way chapter 12's list
   describes. In two sentences, name a bounded adjustment with a
   duration and a limit. You will do this again, graded and with
   reasons tied to the passages, in deliverable c.

### Step 5: Graded deliverables (about 55 minutes)

**Deliverable a (about 20 minutes).** Chapter analysis. Choose one of
the three chapters. Then:

- Quote the sentence or sentences in which the chapter makes its
  claim about what nourishes or exhausts life. Cite the chapter.
- State in your own words what the passage claims, before any
  application.
- Propose one application of the passage to a concrete situation in
  your life, and label it as your proposal.
- State one limit of the application: something the passage does not
  establish about your situation.

**Deliverable b (about 15 minutes).** Paraphrase check. Take the
paraphrase "Daoism is a system for living long". Then:

- Quote the assigned passage that comes closest to supporting it,
  and the assigned passage that resists it most clearly. Cite both.
- State what the sources support: name the set of claims the
  chapters make (the senses, enoughness, the protection of life) and
  what the talk's printed sections organize.
- State what the sources do not support, and say where the boundary
  comes from: the chapters do not teach a method for extending
  lifespan.
- Say whether you would keep, revise or discard the paraphrase, and
  give your reason in one sentence.

**Deliverable c (about 20 minutes).** Bounded practice and
teach-back. Design one nourishing-life practice for your own days.
Then:

- Name the territory it addresses (an excess of the senses, a
  question of enough, or a way of treating life as a thing to
  manage) and the concrete situation.
- State the practice, its duration, its frequency and its limit. A
  practice with no limit is not bounded and will not pass.
- Give your reasons in terms of the assigned passages, quoting at
  least one.
- State what the passages cannot establish about your practice.
- Write a short teach-back, about 150 words, explaining the practice
  to a peer so that the peer could act on it. The teach-back names
  the passage, the practice and the limit.

### Step 6: Self-assessment (about 5 minutes)

Re-read the rubric below. Check each criterion against your three
deliverables. Revise anything you find weak. Then submit.

## Rubric

Graded by rubric. Each criterion is scored Pass or Revise.

| Criterion | What it looks like to pass | What does not pass |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The learner quotes and cites an assigned chapter and separates passage from application | The learner names the chapter, quotes the sentence that carries the life claim, states the passage's claim in their own words first, and labels the application as their own proposal with a stated limit. | The learner reflects on life or health without quoting or citing a chapter, or quotes one and applies it without ever stating what the passage itself claims. |
| 2. The learner explains yangsheng beyond "looking after your health" | The learner anchors the explanation in the verified definition and at least one chapter, names something the tradition includes that ordinary health talk does not, and names something the sources do not promise, with the boundary stated in their own words. | The learner repeats the definition and stops, or sells the tradition as a longevity system the assigned sources do not support. |
| 3. The learner checks the paraphrase against both supporting and resisting passages | The learner quotes a supporting passage and a resisting passage, cites both, states what the set of sources supports and what it does not, and gives a reasoned verdict on the paraphrase. | The learner agrees or disagrees with the paraphrase from general impressions, quotes nothing, or quotes only passages that agree with them. |
| 4. The learner designs a bounded practice with reasons and limits | The practice names its territory and situation, has a duration, a frequency and a limit, gives reasons that quote at least one assigned passage, states what the passages cannot establish, and the teach-back lets a peer act on it. | The practice is "be more mindful" with no duration, frequency or limit, or its reasons never touch a passage, or the teach-back could not be acted on by a reader. |
| 5. The learner performs the analysis without a template | The learner's chapter analysis identifies the right sentence, states its claim, and draws the passage-versus-application line on their own, with at most the marking prompts from Step 1. | The learner copies the phrasing of m2's or m3's worked examples onto a new chapter, or waits for a demonstration the module does not give. |

Strong example for criterion 4: "The territory is enoughness, chapter
44: 'Know what is enough and escape shame; know when to stop and
escape danger.' My situation is that I take on freelance work past the
point where the money matters. The practice: for four weeks, I close
my work app at 7 pm on weekdays, and I write down each evening
whether anything I declined to do that afternoon actually cost me
anything. The limit: if a client has a real deadline, I finish it;
the practice governs the default, not the exception. The passages
support the practice's direction, that not knowing what is enough has
costs. They cannot establish that my evening work is the problem, or
that stopping will protect me from anything. Teach-back: chapter 44
asks which is dearer, reputation or body, and tells you to know what
is enough and when to stop; my practice is a four-week test of
whether my extra hours are actually buying anything, with an
exception for real deadlines."

Rejection example for criterion 4: "I will practice moderation and
balance in all things, as the Dao teaches." This names no chapter,
quotes no passage, has no duration, frequency or limit, and no
teach-back; the phrase "as the Dao teaches" attributes to the sources
a generality they do not state.
