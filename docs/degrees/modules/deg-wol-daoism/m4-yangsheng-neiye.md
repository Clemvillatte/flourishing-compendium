# m4 — Daoist yangsheng and the Neiye: practising the arts of living

**Level**: L3 Independent analysis. The lecture and the primary text are
new to you, and this module asks you to work with them with less
scaffolding than m2 and m3 gave: one worked demonstration, then your
own passage reading, your own practice design and your own teach-back.
**Position**: Fourth module of the deg-wol-daoism band.
**Prerequisites**: completion of m1, m2 and m3. The observable
competencies required: you can define yangsheng in the orientation
sense given in m1 (Outcome 3 there), you can read a short primary-text
passage, quote it exactly and distinguish what it says from an
application you propose (m2 Outcome 2, m3 Outcome 1), and you can
distinguish a training history from the state the training produces

## What this module is for

The first three modules of this walk were mostly about reading: the
Daodejing as a practice manual, the Zhuangzi's stories of skill. This
module is about the other half of the Daoist arts of living, the half
m1 promised and deferred: yangsheng, nourishing life. The scope of
this walk commits to the basics of the tradition's practice side and
to being able to explain their rationale, and this module is where
that happens.

Komjathy introduces yangsheng as "the traditional Chinese and Daoist
name for health and longevity techniques" and immediately widens it:
it is "a fuller sense of what we do to nourish life" than technique
lists suggest, "caring for or tending to life". His lecture works
through the informing Daoist view, general principles (seasons,
relationships, fresh local seasonal food, sleep, walking), the four
postures ("Walk like the wind, stand like a tree, sit like a bell,
sleep like a bow"), breathing ("expelling the old and ingesting the
new"), and seasonal attunement that includes the Suwen's rule to
"nourish yang in spring and summer". Throughout he ties the practices
to what he calls the fundamental sacred connection with the Dao: the
practices are not a life-hacks list, they are how the tradition
understands a life to be tended.

The Neiye is the oldest layer of that practice thinking that you can
read in a free open English translation. It is a short, dense chapter
of the Guanzi, and it describes an inward work: vitality that is
"naturally full, naturally overflowing" in the heart/mind and lost
through "worries and happiness, love and anger, desire for profit";
a mind that "benefits from calmness and the tranquility that comes
from it"; a Qi that "can not be brought to rest by using force, But
can be calmed by using De". The chapter does not give a schedule. It
gives a picture of what a settled, nourished inner life is, and leaves
the working out to the reader.

The Walk-of-Life question this module serves is direct: you have a
life that is feeling strained, and you want to tend it rather than
optimize it. The Daoist answer you will examine here has three parts:
a definition of the tending (yangsheng), a picture of what a
well-tended inner life looks like (the Neiye), and a set of ordinary
practices (sleep, food, seasons, walking, posture, breath) that the
lecture connects to both. What this module does not promise: health
outcomes. Neither the lecture nor the Neiye reports clinical trials,
and neither is a medical source. What you are learning is the
tradition's own account of its practice, which you may then test in
your own life with a bounded, reasoned design.

## Outcomes

On completing this module the learner can:

1. **Explain yangsheng in the tradition's own terms.** The learner
   can state Komjathy's definition in the lecture's words, name the
   general principles and elements the lecture lists, and explain in
   their own words why the tradition treats this as nourishing life
   rather than as a wellness routine, using the lecture's own
   connection of the practices to the Dao.

2. **Read a Neiye passage and locate its claim.** The learner can
   read an assigned Neiye section, identify the sentences in which
   the text states what the inward work is and what disturbs it,
   quote those sentences exactly as printed, and distinguish what the
   passage says from any application the learner proposes to their
   own life.

3. **Design one bounded yangsheng adaptation with reasons and
   limits.** The learner can select one element from the lecture's
   account (for example sleep, seasonal food, walking, one of the four
   postures, or breath), design a one-to-two-week practice adapted to
   their own constraints, state the reason the tradition gives for
   the practice, name one limit they will keep, and state what would
   count as evidence the practice helps them and what would count as
   evidence it does not.

4. **Teach the module's core back, with its limits.** The learner can
   explain to a peer, in their own words, what yangsheng is, what the
   Neiye says a settled inner life is, and one honest limit of both
   sources: neither establishes health outcomes, and the Neiye's
   cosmology is the tradition's own account, not a modern scientific
   description.

## Steps

### Step 1 — Watch the lecture in full (53 minutes 43 seconds measured)

Watch Komjathy's Lundao 5 talk once through without pausing, then a
second time with a notebook. On the second viewing, note:

- The sentences where he defines yangsheng and says it is more
  complicated than a techniques list.
- The general principles he lists, and which of them are ordinary
  daily things (food, sleep, walking, relationships) rather than
  special techniques.
- The four postures, and the seasonal and cosmological attunement
  material.
- What he says about the connection with the Dao, and any cautions he
  gives about losing that connection.

Keep the lecture's own framing in view: this is a talk by a teacher
inside the tradition, describing the tradition's self-understanding.
It is not a medical lecture and does not cite studies.

### Step 2 — Read the assigned Neiye sections (about 25 minutes
estimated on the primary text with the three-times study multiplier;
about 8 minutes raw on two readings of the 550 assigned words)

Read sections 1, 2, 3 and 12 of the [Linnell
translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/38585/pg38585-images.html)
twice each (the four sections are the chapter's opening movement and
one middle section; 550 words measured on the assigned sections). On
the second reading, mark:

- Section 1: the sentences saying the Qi "can not be brought to rest
  by using force, But can be calmed by using De" and "can not be
  summoned by using your voice, But can be made welcome by using your
  intent".
- Section 2: the sentence naming what loses the fullness of the
  heart/mind ("worries and happiness, love and anger, desire for
  profit") and the sentence about calmness and tranquility.
- Section 3: the closing sentences naming what you cannot see or hear
  but whose accomplishments have an order.
- Section 12: the fountainhead sentences ("As long as the fountainhead
  does not dry up, The four limbs are then strong") and the
  whole-and-complete sentences.

### Step 3 — Worked demonstration: passage versus application on
section 12 (reading time included in the module-prose allowance, not
additional)

This is the module's one worked example. It is shorter than the m3
demonstration, because at L3 you take over the method.

The passage says, in its own words: that where essence exists there
is naturally life; that calmness flourishes outside you and collects
inside you as a primal spring; that as long as that fountainhead does
not dry up, the four limbs are strong; that when the center is
without doubt or desire, the outside is without demonic disasters;
and that those whose heart/mind is whole and complete in the center
have a form whole and complete on the outside.

The passage does not say: that sitting still will fix your sleep. It
does not say that emotions are bad and should be suppressed. It does
not say what a practice schedule looks like, or that the fountainhead
is a physical organ. It offers a picture of inner sustenance and
outer composure going together, and it uses the tradition's own
cosmology (essence, Qi, demonic disasters) to say it.

One application, marked as an application: if you take the passage as
a description of depletion, it suggests you look at what drains you
and what collects you, which is why the lecture's practices are
ordinary (sleep, food, seasons, walking) rather than exotic. That
inference is mine, this module's, not the passage's. In deliverable b
you will do this same work yourself on section 2, and the pass
condition is that you mark your own applications as yours.

### Step 4 — Retrieval activities (about 15 minutes; about 5 minutes
each)

1. Without looking back, write Komjathy's one-line definition of
   yangsheng as near to his words as you can recall. Check it against
   the lecture. If your recall flattened it into "Chinese wellness",
   note what the original wording says that your version dropped.
2. Write the Neiye's list of what loses the heart/mind's fullness,
   then name the one item on that list you would personally find
   hardest to leave behind, and one sentence on why.
3. Name the four postures from memory, then check them against the
   lecture. State one way your own daily sitting, standing or walking
   already matches or mismatches one of them.

### Step 5 — Graded deliverables (about 50 minutes)

**Deliverable a (about 20 minutes).** Passage reading on the Neiye.
Take section 2. Then:

- Quote exactly the sentence naming what makes the heart/mind lose its
  fullness, and the sentence about what its nature benefits from.
- State in your own words what the section claims about the
  heart/mind, with no application mixed in.
- Propose one application to your own life, marked as an application,
  and state in one sentence what the passage cannot establish about
  your life.

**Deliverable b (about 15 minutes).** Practice design. Choose one
element from the lecture's account of yangsheng. Design a bounded
practice for one to two weeks. Then:

- Name the element and the exact practice, small enough to keep.
- State the reason the tradition gives for the practice, citing where
  the lecture gives it.
- Name one limit you will keep (a reason to stop or not extend).
- State what would count as evidence the practice helps you, and what
  would count as evidence it does not, without claiming the sources
  promised either.

**Deliverable c (about 15 minutes).** Teach-back. Explain to a peer, in
under 300 spoken words: what yangsheng is, what the Neiye says a
settled inner life is, and one honest limit of both sources. Then
write down the one question your peer asked that you could not answer
from the module, and take it to the m5 synthesis work as an open
question.

### Step 6 — Self-assessment and feedback (about 5 minutes)

Before submitting, re-read the rubric below. Check each criterion
against your own drafts. Revise anything weak. Then submit.

## Rubric

Graded by rubric. Each criterion is scored Pass or Revise.

| Criterion | What it looks like to pass | What does not pass |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The learner quotes an assigned Neiye section exactly and separates passage from application | The learner quotes the section 2 sentences exactly as printed (including the translator's heart/mind notation), states the section's own claim in their own words, and marks their application as an application. | The learner paraphrases the passage as though it were their own reflection, quotes loosely, or blends the passage's claim and their application into one claim. |
| 2. The learner explains yangsheng in the lecture's own terms | The learner states Komjathy's definition close to his words, names the ordinary principles the lecture lists, and explains the lecture's connection of practice to the Dao. A definition flattened to "Chinese wellness techniques" with no tending-life sense does not pass. | The learner lists wellness practices with no definition, or attributes to the lecture claims it does not make (for example clinical evidence). |
| 3. The learner designs a bounded practice with reason, limit and evidence criteria | The learner names one element, one small keepable practice, the tradition's reason for it, a limit with a reason, and what would count as evidence for or against in their own life. | The learner proposes a self-improvement programme with no limit, gives no reason from the sources, or states no evidence criteria. |
| 4. The learner teaches the core back with its limits | The learner's teach-back states what yangsheng is, what the Neiye claims, and one honest limit of the sources (no clinical outcomes; the cosmology is the tradition's own account), and records the peer question they could not answer. | The learner's teach-back sells the practices as proven health methods, or omits the limits entirely. |

Strong example for criterion 3: "Element: sleep. Practice: for two
weeks, set a sleep window that follows the lecture's seasonal
attunement idea, fixed lights-out at the same time nightly. Reason the
tradition gives: the lecture lists sleep among the general principles
of nourishing life and treats the daily cycle as part of the
connection with the Dao, not as downtime. Limit: if two nights of bad
sleep stack up, I drop the fixed time and protect total sleep instead,
because the lecture's principle is nourishing life, not keeping a
rule. Evidence: my own note on restedness in the morning, taken
before the two weeks start so the comparison is honest. What the
sources do not establish: that this sleep pattern improves health
outcomes; the lecture gives no studies."

Rejection example for criterion 3: "I will start a full Daoist
morning routine with all the postures and breathing every day for the
rest of the year, because the Neiye says Qi must be calmed." This
does not pass: it takes one image from the passage as a prescription,
has no limit, no reason drawn from the lecture's actual account, and
no evidence criteria.
