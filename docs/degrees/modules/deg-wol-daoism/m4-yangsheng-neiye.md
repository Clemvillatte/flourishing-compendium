# m4: Daoist yangsheng and the Neiye, practising the arts of living

**Level**: L3 Independent analysis. The lecture and the primary text are
new to you, and this module asks you to work with them with less
scaffolding than m2 and m3 gave: one worked demonstration, then your
own passage reading, your own practice design and your own teach-back.
**Position**: Fourth module of the Daoism walk.
**Prerequisites**: completion of m1, m2 and m3. The observable
competencies required: you can define yangsheng in the orientation
sense given in m1 (m1 Outcome 2, the four working terms), you can read
a short primary-text passage, quote it exactly and distinguish what it
says from an application you propose (m2 Outcome 2, m3 Outcome 1), and
you can distinguish a training history from the state the training
produces (m3 Outcome 2).
**Total time budget**: about 2 hours 50 minutes (about 168 minutes of
labelled components; arithmetic below).
**Primary text**: assigned sections of the Neiye (the "Inward
Training" chapter of the Guanzi) in the [Study of Inner Cultivation
translation by Bruce R. Linnell](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/38585/pg38585-images.html)
(Project Gutenberg eBook #38585, free, no paywall, no login; license
CC BY-NC-ND 3.0, quoted as printed). The translator states he works
deliberately symbol by symbol, so the English can read as stilted;
this module pairs his wording with plain glosses rather than replacing
it. The translation prints no section numbers; sections are separated
by horizontal rules, and this module numbers them in reading order:
section 1 begins "Always : the essence of creatures", section 2 begins
"Always : the form of the heart/mind is", and the fourth assigned
section begins "Where essence exists, there is naturally life".
**Assigned video**: [Komjathy's "Lundao 5: Daoist Yangsheng (Nourishing
Life)"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WDwPln1CczI), 53 minutes 43
seconds total. The required watch is the first 26 minutes 22 seconds
(0:00-26:22, ending at the talk's own Chapter 2 marker, as printed on
the video's watch page); the remainder (26:22-53:43) is optional
further viewing for learners who want the tradition's
internal-alchemical account, which this module does not test and
makes no health claims about. Watch the required segment once with a
notebook; the assigned use is the topics named in Step 1.

## What this module is for

The first three modules of this walk were mostly about reading: the
Daodejing as a practice manual, the Zhuangzi's stories of skill. This
module is about the other half of the Daoist arts of living, the half
m1 promised and deferred: yangsheng, nourishing life. The scope of
this walk commits to the basics of the tradition's practice side and
to being able to explain their rationale, and this module is where
that happens.

Komjathy introduces yangsheng as "the traditional Chinese and Daoist
name for health and longevity techniques" and immediately widens it:
the talk presents it as broader than modern qigong, "a fuller sense of
what we do to nourish life", "caring for or tending to life". His
lecture works through the informing Daoist view, general principles
(fresh local seasonal food, sleep, walking, relationships), the four
postures ("Walk like the wind, stand like a tree, sit like a bell,
sleep like a bow"), "expelling the old and ingesting the new", and
seasonal attunement that includes the Suwen's "nourish yang in spring
and summer". Throughout he ties the practices to what he calls the
fundamental sacred connection with the Dao: the practices express how
the tradition understands a life to be tended.

The Neiye is an early Daoist text on inward cultivation, and the
sections assigned here describe its core picture: a heart/mind
"naturally full, naturally overflowing" that is lost through
"worries and happiness, love and anger, desire for profit"; a mind
that "benefits from calmness and the tranquility that comes from it";
a Qi that "can not be brought to rest by using force, But can be
calmed by using De". The sections you will read give a picture of what
a settled, nourished inner life is, and leave the working out to the
reader.

The Walk-of-Life question this module serves is direct: you have a
life that is feeling strained, and you want to tend it rather than
optimize it. The Daoist answer you will examine here has three parts:
a definition of the tending (yangsheng), a picture of what a
well-tended inner life looks like (the Neiye), and a set of ordinary
practices (sleep, food, seasons, walking, posture) that the lecture
connects to both. This module makes no health-outcome promises. The
sources are a classical text and a teacher's talk inside the
tradition; the module assigns no clinical evidence and makes no
medical claim. What you are learning is the tradition's own account of
its practice, which you may then test in your own life with a
bounded, reasoned design.

## Outcomes

On completing this module the learner can:

1. **Explain yangsheng in the tradition's own terms.** The learner
   can state Komjathy's definition in the lecture's words, name the
   general principles the lecture lists, and explain in their own
   words why the tradition treats this as nourishing life rather than
   as a wellness routine, using the lecture's own connection of the
   practices to the Dao.

2. **Read a Neiye passage and locate its claim** (elective deep-dive
   track). The learner can read an assigned Neiye section, identify
   the sentences in which the text states what the inward work is and
   what disturbs it, quote those sentences exactly as printed, and
   distinguish what the passage says from any application the learner
   proposes to their own life.

3. **Design one bounded yangsheng adaptation with reasons and
   limits.** The learner can select one element from the lecture's
   account (for example sleep, seasonal food, walking, or one of the
   four postures), design a one-to-two-week practice adapted to their
   own constraints, state the reason the tradition gives for the
   practice, name one limit they will keep, and state what would
   count as evidence the practice helps them and what would count as
   evidence it does not.

4. **Teach the module's core back, with its limits.** The learner can
   explain to a peer, in their own words, what yangsheng is, what the
   Neiye says a settled inner life is, and one honest limit of the
   material: the module assigns no clinical evidence for the
   practices, and the Neiye's cosmology (essence, Qi) is the
   tradition's own account rather than a modern scientific
   description.

## Steps

### Step 1: Watch the lecture (required segment 0:00-26:22 measured)

Watch the assigned segment of Komjathy's Lundao 5 talk (0:00 to
26:22, ending at the talk's Chapter 2 marker) once through with a
notebook, pausing as you need to. The watch page's own markers
locate the assigned topics within this segment (stillness and
emptiness meditation practice 19:14; expelling the old and ingesting
the new 19:51; the four postures 21:05-23:28). The remainder of the
talk (26:22-53:43) is optional further viewing. Keep your notes on
these topics:

- The sentences where he defines yangsheng and says it is more
  complicated than a techniques list.
- The general principles he lists, and which of them are ordinary
  daily things (food, sleep, walking, relationships) rather than
  special techniques.
- The four postures, and the seasonal attunement material.
- What he says about the connection with the Dao, and any cautions he
  gives about losing that connection.

Keep the lecture's own framing in view: this is a talk by a teacher
inside the tradition, describing the tradition's self-understanding.

### Step 2: Read the assigned Neiye sections (about 25 minutes
estimated on the primary text with the three-times study multiplier;
about 8 minutes raw on two readings of the 550 assigned words)

This is the elective deep-dive track the walk's scope promises. If
the inward-training side is why you are here, take it; if you are
taking the walk for the reading side only, Step 2 and the Neiye
variant of deliverable a are optional for you, and the lecture-based
variant of deliverable a below is your required route.

Read sections 1, 2, 3 and 12 (as numbered above) of the [Linnell
translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/38585/pg38585-images.html)
twice each (550 words measured on the assigned sections). On the
second reading, mark:

- Section 1: the sentences saying the Qi "can not be brought to rest
  by using force, But can be calmed by using De" and "can not be
  summoned by using your voice, But can be made welcome by using your
  intent".
- Section 2: the sentence naming what loses the fullness of the
  heart/mind ("worries and happiness, love and anger, desire for
  profit") and the sentence about calmness and tranquility.
- Section 3: the closing sentences naming what you cannot see or hear
  but whose accomplishments have an order.
- Section 12 ("Where essence exists, there is naturally life"): the
  fountainhead sentences ("As long as the fountainhead does not dry
  up, The four limbs are then strong") and the whole-and-complete
  sentences.

### Step 3: Worked demonstrations (reading time included in the
module-prose allowance, not additional)

Two worked examples, one for the reading skill and one for the design
skill. Both are shorter than m3's demonstration, because at L3 you
take over the method.

Passage versus application, on section 12. The passage says, in its
own words: that where essence exists there is naturally life; that
calmness flourishes outside you and collects inside you as a primal
spring; that as long as that fountainhead does not dry up, the four
limbs are strong; that when the center is without doubt or desire,
the outside is without demonic disasters; and that those whose
heart/mind is whole and complete in the center have a form whole and
complete on the outside. The passage does not say that sitting still
will fix your sleep, that emotions are bad and should be suppressed,
or what a practice schedule looks like. It offers a picture of inner
sustenance and outer composure going together, and it uses the
tradition's own cosmology (essence, Qi, demonic disasters) to say it.
One application, marked as an application: if you take the passage as
a description of depletion, it suggests you look at what drains you
and what collects you, which is why the lecture's practices are
ordinary (sleep, food, seasons, walking) rather than exotic. That
inference is this module's, and the passage's silence about schedules
is a silence of the sections you read, which describe states and
leave the working out to the reader.

A worked practice design, offered as a hypothetical, with the reason
kept to what the lecture supports. Element: walking. Hypothetical
constraint: an office worker with fifteen free minutes at lunch.
Hypothetical design: a ten-minute walk after lunch, five days a week,
for two weeks. Reason the tradition gives: the lecture lists walking
among the general principles of nourishing life. Limit: in heavy rain
the walk is skipped without compensating, because the point is
nourishing life and the learner's own judgment is that forcing the
practice would work against that. Evidence criteria: a one-line note
each afternoon on energy, written before the two weeks start so the
comparison is honest. What the sources do not establish: that the
walk improves health outcomes; the module assigns no clinical
evidence. In the graded deliverable you will do this same work on an
element of your own choosing.

### Step 4: Retrieval activities (about 17 minutes; activities 1,
2 and 3 at about 5 minutes each, activity 4 about 2 minutes)

1. Without looking back, write Komjathy's one-line definition of
   yangsheng as near to his words as you can recall. Check it against
   the lecture. If your recall flattened it into "Chinese wellness",
   note what the original wording says that your version dropped.
2. Write the Neiye's list of what loses the heart/mind's fullness,
   then name the one item on that list you would personally find
   hardest to leave behind, and one sentence on why. (Elective
   track.)
3. Name the four postures from memory, then check them against the
   lecture. State one way your own daily sitting, standing or walking
   already matches or mismatches one of them.
4. Draft your own two-minute practice design: one element, one small
   keepable practice, one limit. You will do this again, graded and
   with full reasons, in deliverable b.

### Step 5: Graded deliverables (about 50 minutes)

**Deliverable a (about 20 minutes).** Passage reading, two variants;
take the Neiye variant if you are on the elective track, otherwise
the lecture variant.

Neiye variant: take section 2 ("Always : the form of the heart/mind
is"). Quote exactly the sentence naming what makes the heart/mind lose
its fullness, and the sentence about what its nature benefits from.
State in your own words what the section claims about the heart/mind,
with no application mixed in. Propose one application to your own
life, marked as an application, and state in one sentence what the
passage cannot establish about your life.

Lecture variant: quote exactly the sentences in which Komjathy defines
yangsheng (the "traditional Chinese and Daoist name" sentence and the
widening that follows it). State in your own words what the definition
claims, with no application mixed in. Propose one application to your
own life, marked as an application, and state in one sentence what the
definition cannot establish about your life.

**Deliverable b (about 15 minutes).** Practice design. Choose one
element from the lecture's account of yangsheng and design a bounded
practice for one to two weeks. Name the element, the exact practice
(small enough to keep), your own constraint that shapes the design,
the reason the tradition gives for the practice with the place the
lecture gives it, one limit you will keep with its reason, and what
would count as evidence the practice helps you and what would count as
evidence it does not. Do not claim the sources promised either
outcome.

**Deliverable c (about 15 minutes).** Teach-back with explanation.
Explain to a peer, in under 300 spoken words: what yangsheng is
(stating Komjathy's definition and at least two general principles
the lecture lists, and the connection to the Dao as the lecture gives
it), what the Neiye says a settled inner life is (if you took the
elective track; otherwise what the lecture's practices are), and one
honest limit of the material (the module assigns no clinical
evidence; the Neiye's cosmology is the tradition's own account).
Then write down the one question your peer asked that you could not
answer from the module, and take it to the m5 synthesis work as an
open question.

### Step 6: Self-assessment and feedback (about 5 minutes)

Before submitting, re-read the rubric below. Check each criterion
against your own drafts. Revise anything weak. Then submit.

## Rubric

Graded by rubric. Each criterion is scored Pass or Revise.

| Criterion | What it looks like to pass | What does not pass |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The learner quotes an assigned passage exactly and separates passage from application | The learner quotes the section 2 sentences or the definition sentences exactly as printed (including the translator's heart/mind notation where present), states the passage's own claim in their own words, and marks their application as an application. | The learner paraphrases the passage as though it were their own reflection, quotes loosely, or blends the passage's claim and their application into one claim. |
| 2. The learner explains yangsheng in the lecture's own terms | The learner states Komjathy's definition close to his words, names at least two ordinary principles the lecture lists, and explains the lecture's connection of practice to the Dao. | The learner lists wellness practices with no definition, or attributes to the lecture claims it does not make (for example clinical evidence). |
| 3. The learner designs a bounded practice with constraint, reason, limit and evidence criteria | The learner names one element, one small keepable practice, their own constraint that shapes it, the tradition's reason for it, a limit with a reason, and what would count as evidence for or against in their own life. | The learner proposes a self-improvement programme with no limit, gives no reason from the sources, or states no evidence criteria. |
| 4. The learner teaches the core back with its limits | The learner's teach-back states what yangsheng is with the definition and principles, what the Neiye claims (or the lecture's practices), and one honest limit of the material, and records the peer question they could not answer. | The learner's teach-back sells the practices as proven health methods, or omits the limits entirely. |

Strong example for criterion 1 (Neiye variant): "The passage prints,
'The reason that you lose it Is certainly due to worries and
happiness, love and anger, desire for profit.' The section itself
claims the heart/mind is naturally full and is drained by those five
states; it does not mention my job or my habits. My application is
that a two-week period of deliberately leaving work concerns at the
office is worth trying against that list; the passage cannot
establish that doing so would restore anything, since it describes a
state, not a technique."

Rejection example for criterion 1: "The Neiye says stress is bad and
we should calm down, which is exactly what I do with meditation
already." This does not pass: it quotes nothing, translates the
passage into a modern platitude, and turns the passage's claim and the
learner's habit into one claim.

Strong example for criterion 2: "Komjathy calls yangsheng 'the
traditional Chinese and Daoist name for health and longevity
techniques' and then widens it to caring for or tending to life; the
lecture's principles include sleep, fresh local seasonal food and
walking, and it ties them to the fundamental sacred connection with
the Dao, which is why the tradition speaks of nourishing life rather
than following a routine."

Rejection example for criterion 2: "Yangsheng is basically the
original wellness lifestyle, with diet and exercise for longevity."
This does not pass: it replaces the lecture's own words and its Dao
connection with a modern gloss the lecture does not give.

Strong example for criterion 4: "What it is: a Daoist way of tending
life, defined by Komjathy as health and longevity techniques but
really meaning caring for life, with principles like sleep and
seasonal food tied to the Dao. What the Neiye adds: a picture of a
mind that is naturally full and drained by worry, anger and profit.
The limit I have to name: none of this is clinical evidence, and the
essence-and-Qi picture is the tradition's cosmology, not science.
Question I could not answer: whether the seasonal rules matter in a
southern-hemisphere climate."

Rejection example for criterion 4: "These practices have kept Daoists
healthy for thousands of years, so they clearly work." This does not
pass: it claims evidence the sources do not give and drops the limit
the criterion requires.

Strong example for criterion 3: "Element: sleep. Practice: for two
weeks, a fixed lights-out at the same time nightly. My constraint:
shift work twice a week, so the fixed time applies to the five
non-shift nights only. Reason the tradition gives: the lecture lists
sleep among the general principles of nourishing life. Limit: if two
nights of bad sleep stack up, I protect total sleep and drop the
fixed time, because the principle is nourishing life, not keeping a
rule. Evidence: my own note on restedness in the morning, taken
before the two weeks start so the comparison is honest. What the
sources do not establish: that this pattern improves health outcomes;
the module assigns no clinical evidence." (Hypothetical example,
offered as a demonstration of the format, not as a recommendation.)

Rejection example for criterion 3: "I will start a full Daoist
morning routine with all the postures and breathing every day for the
rest of the year, because the Neiye says Qi must be calmed." This
does not pass: it takes one image from the passage as a prescription,
has no limit, no reason drawn from the lecture's actual account, and
no evidence criteria.
