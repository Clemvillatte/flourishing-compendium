# m5: Capstone, a personal synthesis of the walk, against one serious objection

**Level**: L4 Synthesis. You design, execute and self-assess an
integrative piece of work: a written synthesis of this walk that
defends a position you actually hold against one serious objection.
**Position**: Fifth and final module of the Daoism walk.
**Prerequisites**: completion of m1 through m4. The observable
competencies required: you can quote an assigned passage exactly and
separate what it says from your application of it (m2 Outcome 2,
m3 Outcomes 1 and 4), you can distinguish a training
history from the state it produces (m3 Outcome 2), and you can
design a bounded practice with a reason, a limit and evidence
criteria (m4 Outcome 3).
**Total time budget**: about 3 hours 28 minutes (about 208 minutes of
labelled components; arithmetic below).
**Primary text**: the four capstone readings, Daodejing chapters 20,
44, 50 and 67 in the [Eno translation](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/iuswrrest/api/core/bitstreams/7031cb99-72c3-4e59-8ced-9d9783638945/content)
(chapter 44 you met in m2 and re-read here; 466 words measured on the four
chapters: whitespace-separated tokens with the chapter-number lines and
the subsection markers (a>, b>, c>, d>) excluded and dash tokens
counted; free, educator-licensed, no paywall), plus re-reads of the
passages you already hold from m2 (Daodejing 11, 36, 78), m3 (Cook
Ding, the Hunchback, the Butterfly Dream) and m4 (Neiye sections 1,
2, 3, 12; elective track). No new video is assigned; the primary text carries the objection material.

## What this module is for

The exit capability this walk promised from the start is a written
one: a personal synthesis of the walk against one serious objection.
This module is that promise, delivered.

The four capstone readings carry sharp material for objections to this
walk. Chapter 20 has
the sage saying "I alone am still, without visible sign... like one
with no home... I alone wish to be different from others". Chapter 44
asks the reader to weigh the body against reputation and goods, and
on this module's reading the weighing lands on the body; it tells
the reader to "know what is enough". Chapter 50 describes one "good at protecting
his life" who "has no death place in him". Chapter 67 names the
three treasures: compassion, frugality, and not daring to take the
lead. Read together with what you already hold, these chapters supply the
passages from which the three candidate objections below are built.
The objections are the module's constructions; the passages are the
text's own words. That division is what makes the synthesis honest:
you will state a strong objection, check it against the quoted
passages, and answer it from those same passages.

The three candidate objections below are this module's reading
prompts: each is grounded in the assigned chapters, and each is the
kind of question a reader presses after finishing a walk like this
one. You may also propose your own objection, if you can ground it
in an assigned passage. The strong versions here are the module's
constructions of the chapters, and you should check them against the
chapters as you write:

1. **The withdrawal objection.** The sage of chapter 20 stands apart
   from the banqueting, brilliant, perceptive crowd; a life guided
   by this text can look like a retreat from responsibility, a
   refusal to take part. If wu wei and non-forcing are the center,
   what happens to the work the world needs done?
2. **The self-preservation objection.** Chapter 44 asks you to
   weigh your body against your reputation and your goods; chapter 50 centers
   protecting your life; m4 taught yangsheng, nourishing life. A
   life guided by these texts can look like refined self-interest:
   tending your own vitality while the world's needs go unanswered.
3. **The standards question.** The Butterfly Dream ends with
   Zhuang Zhou not knowing whether he had dreamt he was a butterfly
   or a butterfly was dreaming he was Zhou, and names "the
   transformation of things" without saying which state is real. The
   walk has repeatedly let questions stand unresolved. A reader can
   take that comfort with open questions as a refusal to commit to
   any standard; whether the passages teach that refusal or simply
   refuse to close questions they cannot close is your call to
   argue.

Choose the one that actually presses on you. The synthesis is yours,
and the module grades the quality of the engagement, not the
conclusion you reach.

## Outcomes

On completing this module the learner can:

1. **State one serious objection to the walk at full strength.** The
   learner can write the objection in 300 words or fewer, as the
   strongest version of itself, grounded in quoted passages from
   the assigned chapters, without caricature and without answering
   it yet.

2. **Write a personal synthesis of the walk that answers the
   objection.** The learner can write 1,200 to 1,800 words that:
   state what they take from the walk (naming at least one thing
   they keep, one thing they decline, with reasons); address one
   concrete situation from their own life involving suffering,
   uncertainty or responsibility, name one practical implication the
   walk's material has for it, and name one limit they keep; quote
   at least three assigned passages from at least two of the walk's
   modules exactly, citing each; answer the chosen objection using
   those passages; and mark every application of a passage as an
   application.

3. **Self-assess the synthesis against the rubric.** The learner can
   score their own draft against each rubric criterion, name the
   weakest criterion with a reason, and make one revision in
   response, recording what changed.

## Steps

### Step 1: Read the four capstone readings (about 21 minutes estimated
on the primary text with the three-times study multiplier; about 7
minutes raw on two readings of the 466 assigned words)

Read [Daodejing chapters 20, 44, 50 and
67](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/iuswrrest/api/core/bitstreams/7031cb99-72c3-4e59-8ced-9d9783638945/content)
twice each. On the second reading, mark:

- Chapter 20: the sentences in which the sage describes standing
  apart from the crowd, and the sentence "I alone wish to be
  different from others, and value feeding from the mother".
- Chapter 44: the three questions (reputation or body, body or goods,
  gaining or losing) and the closing "know when to stop" sentence.
- Chapter 50: the "good at protecting his life" passage and the
  "no death place in him" close.
- Chapter 67: the three treasures and the sentence about what
  happens if they are discarded.

Then re-read the passages you already hold that bear on your chosen
objection: for the withdrawal objection, Cook Ding (m3) and chapter
67's compassion; for the self-preservation objection, chapter 44's
questions and chapter 67's compassion and frugality (and the Neiye
sections from m4, if you took the elective track); for the standards
question, the Butterfly Dream (m3). One re-read of each passage you
will use is enough; you are not re-reading whole modules.

### Step 2: Write the objection at full strength (about 30 minutes)

Write 300 words or fewer stating the objection as strongly as you
can. Requirements: quote at least one assigned primary passage exactly
(one of chapters 20, 44, 50 or 67, or, for the standards question,
the Butterfly Dream); write it as a person who holds the objection would
write it, not as a straw man; do not answer it yet. If you cannot
write it at full strength, you have not understood it; re-read the
grounding chapters before continuing.

### Step 3: Draft the synthesis (about 90 minutes)

Write 1,200 to 1,800 words with these movements, in whatever order
serves your argument:

- What you take from the walk. Name at least one thing you keep
  and one thing you decline, each with a reason. The reason may be
  personal, but it must touch the passages.
- The answer to the objection. Use at least three quoted passages
  from at least two modules of the walk (m2 chapters, m3 stories,
  m5 chapters, and the m4 Neiye sections if you took the elective
  track), each cited, each applied as an application. Some material
  the passages offer: for the withdrawal objection, Cook Ding's
  absorbed skill, which the story shows at work carving an ox for a
  lord, and chapter 67's compassion, which the chapter links to
  valor and says cannot be traded away for it; for the
  self-preservation objection, chapter 44's questions, which ask
  the reader to weigh the body against reputation and goods rather
  than against other people, and chapter 67's compassion and frugality; for the
  standards question, the Butterfly Dream's open ending. These
  pointers are material you may use or decline, and how you read
  them is part of what the synthesis argues; the synthesis is
  yours.
- One concrete situation. Address one situation from your own life
  involving suffering, uncertainty or responsibility; name one
  practical implication the walk's material has for it; and name
  one limit you keep. The situation is yours, so the passages
  cannot settle it, and the synthesis should say so where true.
- What you cannot settle. Name at least one thing the passages do
  not resolve for you, stated honestly. A synthesis that resolves
  everything has not met the material.

While drafting, keep the m2 discipline: every quotation exact, every
application marked as yours.

### Step 4: Self-assessment and revision (about 20 minutes)

Score your draft against each rubric criterion. Name the weakest
criterion with a reason. Make one revision in response and record
what changed in one sentence.

### Step 5: Teach-back close (about 10 minutes)

Read your synthesis aloud once. Then write the one sentence of it
you would say to a friend who has not taken this walk, and the one
question you still cannot answer. Keep both with the open question
you carried from m4's deliverable c; they are the walk's honest
residue.

## Rubric

Graded by rubric. Each criterion is scored Pass or Revise.

| Criterion | What it looks like to pass | What does not pass |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The objection is stated at full strength, grounded and unanswered | The learner states one objection in 300 words or fewer, quotes at least one assigned primary passage exactly (one of chapters 20, 44, 50 or 67, or the Butterfly Dream for the standards question), writes it as a holder of the objection would, and does not answer it in this deliverable. | The learner states a weakened or caricatured objection, quotes nothing, or smuggles the answer in early. |
| 2. The synthesis quotes and cites at least three passages from at least two modules, and marks applications as applications | The learner quotes at least three passages exactly with citations spanning at least two modules of the walk, and separates each passage's claim from their application of it. | The learner paraphrases passages as their own reasoning, quotes loosely, or draws every passage from a single module. |
| 3. The synthesis answers the objection using the passages | The learner's answer engages the objection as stated in deliverable a, uses the quoted passages as its substance, and does not merely assert that the objection is mistaken. | The learner ignores the objection as stated, answers a different one, or answers by assertion without passages. |
| 4. The synthesis takes a personal position with keeps and declines, on a concrete situation | The learner names at least one thing kept and one declined, each with a reason touching the passages; addresses one concrete situation involving suffering, uncertainty or responsibility with a practical implication and a limit they keep; and names at least one thing the passages do not resolve for them. | The learner offers a summary of the walk with no personal position, or a position with no passages behind it, or keeps the whole thing literary with no concrete situation, or resolves everything. |
| 5. The self-assessment scores the draft and drives one revision | The learner scores each criterion, names the weakest with a reason, makes one revision, and records what changed. | The learner claims all criteria pass with no reason, or makes no revision. |

Strong example for criterion 2 (illustrative excerpt; a passing
response continues to two more passages from a second module, each
cited and each applied): "Chapter 67 prints, 'Compassionate,
thus I can have valor; ...' The passage claims a link between
compassion and courage; it does not mention my job or my
commitments. My application is that the withdrawal objection misses
this chapter's valor, but that is my reading of the link, and the
passage leaves the link unexplained."

Rejection example for criterion 2: "The Daodejing is full of wisdom
about not forcing things, which I have definitely found true in my
life." This does not pass: it quotes nothing, cites nothing, and
turns the passage's claim and the learner's biography into one
claim.

Strong example for criterion 1: "The withdrawal objection, at full
strength: chapter 20's sage says 'I alone am still, without visible
sign... like one with no home,' stands apart from people who are
'as if banqueting,' and wishes to be different from others. If the
walk's ideal is this apartness, then the harder my circumstances get,
the more this teaching pays me to disengage, and the people who need
me are the price. (Held at strength; not answered here.)"

Rejection example for criterion 1: "The Daoist sage is basically an
escapist, which is obviously wrong." This does not pass: it quotes
nothing from the assigned chapters, states the objection at a slogan
level rather than full strength, and answers itself with "obviously
wrong" in the same breath.

Strong example for criterion 3: "The objection says the sage
withdraws. Cook Ding's answer is that his absorbed skill is work:
'Cook Ding was carving an ox carcass for Lord Wenhui,' and his
knife moves 'in rhythm.' My reading of the passage is that its
highest skill is engaged and responsive work; the passage itself
narrates the work and does not say this. My application is that the
reading answers the withdrawal charge for work one is competent at,
and
chapter 67's 'Compassionate, thus I can have valor' extends it, on
my reading: compassion as the condition of courage rather than
apartness from others."

Rejection example for criterion 3: "Obviously Daoists care about
others, so the objection fails." This does not pass: it dismisses
the objection by assertion and uses no passage to do it.

Strong example for criterion 4: "What I keep: the passage-versus-
application discipline; on my current uncertainty about my father's
care, chapter 44's questions put the body first in a way that gives
me one practical implication: his rest matters more than my
reputation for coping alone, so I will ask for help this week. The
limit I keep: the chapter cannot tell me what to do when rest and
reputation pull the same way. What I decline: treating the seasonal
rules as rules; chapter 44 asks the reader to weigh and to 'know
what is enough', and that weighing falls to me each time.
Unresolved: whether wu wei scales to decisions I fear."

Rejection example for criterion 4: "I keep everything the walk
taught me and feel much wiser now." This does not pass: no keeps
and declines with reasons, no concrete situation, no limit, nothing
unresolved.

Strong example for criterion 5 (illustrative excerpt; a passing
response scores every criterion before naming the weakest): "Weakest
criterion: 1, because my
objection was 380 words and still answered itself in the last
sentence. Revision: cut to 290 words, deleted the answer. What
changed: the objection now stands at full strength unanswered."

Rejection example for criterion 5: "All criteria pass strongly."
This does not pass: no scoring, no reason, no revision recorded.
