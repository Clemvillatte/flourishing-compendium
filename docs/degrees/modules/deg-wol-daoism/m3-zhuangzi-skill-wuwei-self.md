## What this module is for

The Zhuangzi is a foundational Daoist text with a distinctive
concern for skill. Two of its most famous stories are about people
who have practised something until the practice changes shape from
the inside: a butcher whose knife moves through a carcass without
hitting a bone, a hunchback who catches cicadas with a sticky pole
while describing his attention as narrowed to cicada wings. The
stories do not say "practise more" as advice. They say something
more specific: that at the highest point of a skill the performer's
own description changes. Cook Ding says he meets the ox with his
spirit and does not look with his eyes. The hunchback says he
perceives nothing but cicada wings.

The interpretation this module proposes, and which you will test
against the passages rather than take on trust, is that these
descriptions are compatible with a reduced kind of self-monitoring:
the performer is not watching themselves perform. Whether that
compatibility amounts to the passages teaching a "loss of self" is
something the module asks you to check passage by passage. The
Butterfly Dream, which you re-read in this module, frames the
question differently, as a puzzle about which state is the real
one, and the passage does not itself say whether the loss of a
divided perspective is a gain, a problem or neither.

[Ziporyn's lecture](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EJ1bB2w2gBk),
on the assigned segment, gives the Daodejing's version of the
same point before quoting the Zhuangzi: the highest form of a
capacity is the one you do not notice yourself doing, like the
beating of your own heart ([transcript](https://youtubetotranscript.com/transcript?v=EJ1bB2w2gBk&current_language_code=en)).
Then he introduces the Zhuangzi line that is this module's spine:
when the shoe fits you forget your feet. Ziporyn explicitly names
Zhuangzi as the source of the shoe line, so the videos are not
equal on this point: one gloss is attributed to Zhuangzi by the
speaker, the other is offered as a practitioner's description.
Keep the difference in mind when you do the checking in
deliverable b.

The documentary chapter on martial arts and wu wei gives a
practitioner's first-person description of a similar state. The
module does not use that description in the graded work, because
the transcript service does not print timestamps beside the
speaker's words and the module will not claim a verified
placement it cannot show. The graded comparison in deliverable b
uses Ziporyn's gloss, which is printed in his lecture segment
with its own chapter label.

## Outcomes

On completing this module the learner can:

1. **Read a Zhuangzi skill story and locate its claim.** The
   learner can read one assigned skill story (Cook Ding or the
   Hunchback), identify the exact sentence or sentences in which
   the story states what the skill feels like from the inside,
   quote that sentence, and distinguish the story's own words from
   any application the learner proposes to their own life. This
   is the same passage-versus-application distinction required in
   m2, now applied to a longer and more narrative text.

2. **Distinguish skill as story from skill as advice.** The
   learner can state, in their own words, that the assigned stories
   describe states of absorbed skill rather than prescribe a
   training programme, and can identify at least one sentence in
   an assigned passage where the story gives the training detail
   (for example the Hunchback's months of balancing balls, or
   Cook Ding's account of his progression from seeing the whole
   carcass at the beginning to no longer seeing it whole after
   three years) as distinct from the
   state the training produces. The learner can explain why this
   distinction matters for how they use the text.

3. **Test the "losing the self" interpretation against one
   concrete situation.** The learner can select one situation
   from their own life, describe what the activity is, describe
   any change in their self-monitoring during it (including no
   change, if that is what they find), state whether the
   "loss of self" reading is warranted for that situation or
   not, and make a reasoned call on whether self-monitoring
   there is avoidable strain or useful vigilance, naming any
   bounded change they would make or a reasoned decision not to
   change, and any limit they would keep.

4. **Check an interpretation against the text.** Given a modern
   paraphrase of a Zhuangzi theme (the module supplies Ziporyn's
   mastery-without-awareness gloss), the learner can check
   whether the paraphrase is supported by an assigned passage,
   quote the passage that supports it or state that it does not,
   and name the difference between what the passage says and what
   the paraphrase adds.

## Steps

### Step 1 — Read Cook Ding (about 19 minutes estimated on the
primary text with the three-times study multiplier; about 6 minutes
20 seconds raw on two readings of the 413-word story)

Read the Cook Ding story ([Inner Chapters, section
3.2](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/iuswrrest/api/core/bitstreams/5dc89a23-c6bf-4190-a1e4-6762dc5359cc/content)).
It is 413 words measured on the story proper, excluding the
translator's closing paragraph. Read it once straight through,
then a second time with a pencil. On the second reading, mark:

- The sentence in which Cook Ding says he no longer sees the
  carcass whole and meets it with his spirit rather than his eyes.
- The sentence about the knife having no thickness and the gaps
  between the joints.
- The sentence about the tangled knot, where he slows down and
  focuses his sight before flicking the blade.
- The closing exchange: Lord Wenhui says he has learned how to
  nurture life.

Notice that the story is not a summary of a teaching. It is a
narrated demonstration with one direct claim inside it, spoken by
Cook Ding himself, and one interpretive claim at the end, spoken
by Lord Wenhui. The translator's closing paragraph, which you
also read, names the set of stories this one belongs to and calls
them the "knack passages."

### Step 2 — Worked demonstration: what the story says versus what
it does not (reading time included in the module-prose allowance
above, not additional)

This is a worked example the module performs in front of you so
you can see the passage-versus-application distinction applied to
a longer text before you do it yourself in the graded deliverable.

The story says, in its own words, that after three years Cook
Ding could no longer see the carcass whole, and that now he meets
it with his spirit and does not look with his eyes. It says
perception and understanding cease and spirit moves as it will.
It says the blade has no thickness and the gaps are wide enough
to wander freely. It says that when a tangled knot lies ahead he
spots the challenge, focuses his sight, and slows down his hand.

The story does not say: that you should stop trying at your job.
It does not say that the supervising self is bad or that you
should aim to dissolve it. It does not say that any activity
performed automatically is Daoist. It does not say that skill
arrives without the years of practice the story itself
narrates.

Notice that the two passages describe two different kinds of
attention. In the knot sentence Cook Ding's noticing, slowing and
focusing are all task-directed responses to a difficult knot:
vigilance aimed at the job, not at himself. The story does not
say this attention is a failure or that something has gone wrong
with him; it says he spots the challenge and slows down. In the
spirit sentence the opposite is described: perception and
understanding cease and the spirit moves. The module's proposed
interpretation, which is yours to check and not a settled
reading, is that the first kind of attention is task-directed
vigilance and the second is what skilled performance looks like
when it is going well. The story does not state this distinction
itself; it narrates both states and leaves the comparison to the
reader.

For the Walk-of-Life question, which is the reason this module
exists: self-monitoring is not free. Watching yourself perform
costs attention, and in some situations that cost is avoidable
strain. In other situations the watching is doing necessary work:
a surgeon mid-operation, a driver in unfamiliar traffic, a
teacher checking whether a class has understood. The question
for your own situation is which of the two you are looking at.
Not every moment of self-monitoring should be reduced. In the
graded deliverable you will make this call on one situation of
your own, with a limit you would keep.

### Step 3 — Read the Hunchback and the Butterfly Dream (about 12
minutes estimated on the primary text with the three-times study
multiplier; about 4 minutes raw on two readings of the Hunchback
plus one reading of the Butterfly Dream)

Read the Hunchback and Cicadas story (Inner Chapters translation,
section A.1, printed as from chapter 19) twice. It is 217 words
on the story proper. On the second reading, mark:

- The sentence in which the hunchback describes holding his body
  like a twisted tree and his arm like a withered limb.
- The sentence in which he says that no matter how huge heaven
  and earth, he perceives nothing but cicada wings.
- Confucius's closing comment, "His will undivided, his spirit
  coalesced."

Then re-read the Butterfly Dream (chapter 2, section 20; 67 words
measured on the story proper; you read it in m1). Notice that the
two readings describe different things. The Hunchback describes an
absorbed skill from the inside, from the point of view of the
person performing it. The Butterfly Dream frames the same
general territory as a puzzle about which state is the real one.
Do not assume the passage takes a side: it ends with the
transformation of things and does not say whether the loss of a
divided perspective is a gain, a problem or neither. That
question is yours to answer in deliverable c, with the passage in
front of you.

### Step 4 — Watch the assigned video segments (about 5
minutes 22 seconds measured on the assigned segments)

Watch Ziporyn's segment "Highest virtue is without virtue:
mastery and unconscious action" (0:38:17 to 0:39:38, about 1
minute 21 seconds) and his segment "When the shoe fits you forget
your feet: Zhuangzi on wu wei" (0:39:38 to 0:43:39, about 4
minutes 1 second). These are printed chapter labels on the
video's own description; the durations are computed from those
labels. Total assigned video on this module: about 5 minutes 22
seconds.

While watching, keep one question: does Ziporyn's description
of mastery and effortlessness match what the assigned Zhuangzi
passages actually say? You will answer this in the graded
deliverable.

### Step 5 — Retrieval activities (about 20 minutes, estimate;
about 5 minutes each)

1. Without looking back at the module page, write the Zhuangzi
   line about the shoe. Then check it. If you cannot recall it,
   look it up and copy it exactly. Then, in one sentence, state
   what the line says about fit and forgetting, without adding
   anything about jobs, motivation or self-improvement.

2. Ungraded practice for deliverable a: pick any activity you
   are competent at. In two sentences, call whether the
   self-monitoring in it is avoidable strain or useful vigilance,
   and name one limit you would keep. You will do this again,
   graded, on a situation of your choosing in deliverable a.

3. Name one training detail from an assigned story (the
   Hunchback's months of balancing balls, or Cook Ding's
   beginning-to-three-years progression) and, in one sentence,
   state how the story distinguishes that training history from
   the description of the state the training produces. Then check
   your answer against the passage.

4. Ungraded practice for deliverable b: take Ziporyn's
   mastery-without-awareness line. Find the closest sentence in an
   assigned passage. Write one sentence saying whether the gloss
   adds something the passage does not say. You will do this
   again, graded, in deliverable b.

### Step 6 — Graded deliverables (about 50 minutes)

**Deliverable a (about 25 minutes).** Choose one situation from
your own life in which you are competent. Describe the activity.
Then:

- Describe any change in your self-monitoring during the
  activity, including no change if that is what you find.
- Quote the sentence from one assigned Zhuangzi passage (Cook
  Ding or the Hunchback) that describes the closest thing to that
  moment in the story's own words. Cite the passage by its
  section label.
- State whether self-monitoring in this situation creates
  avoidable strain, or whether it is doing necessary work. Give
  your reason.
- Either propose one bounded change you would make to the
  self-monitoring, or state a reasoned decision not to change
  it. Name any limit you would keep either way.
- State in one sentence what the passage cannot establish about
  your situation.
- State in one sentence what the shoe-fits line says about your
  situation, and distinguish the line's own claim from your
  application of it.

**Deliverable b (about 15 minutes).** Compare a modern gloss
with its source. Take Ziporyn's statement that the highest
virtuosity is the things we do without realising we are doing
them. Then:

- Find the sentence in an assigned Zhuangzi passage that comes
  closest to supporting the gloss. Quote it and cite the passage.
- Identify one training detail in that passage (the Hunchback's
  months of balancing balls, or Cook Ding's account of his
  progression from seeing the whole carcass at the beginning to
  no longer seeing it whole after three years) and state how the
  story distinguishes that training history from the description
  of the resulting state. Say why the distinction matters:
  neither the story nor the gloss supplies a universal training
  prescription. Note that Cook Ding's nineteen years with the
  same knife is evidence of the state, not a stated preparation
  period.
- State whether the gloss is faithful to that sentence, adds
  something the sentence does not say, or both. Name the specific
  addition.
- State whether the gloss is closer to Cook Ding, to the
  Hunchback, to the Butterfly Dream, or to none of them, and say
  why.

**Deliverable c (about 10 minutes).** Chapter check. The module
has described the Hunchback story as showing the absorbed skill
from the performer's point of view and the Butterfly Dream as
framing the same territory as a puzzle about which state is real.
Support that comparison with evidence from both passages, or
challenge it with evidence from either passage if you think it is
the wrong description. Then state which framing you find more
useful for thinking about your own situation in deliverable a,
and why. Agreement with the module's description is not the pass
condition; evidence is.

### Step 7 — Self-assessment and feedback (about 5 minutes)

Before submitting, re-read the rubric below. Check each
criterion against your own draft. Revise anything you find weak.
Then submit.

## Rubric

Graded by rubric. Each criterion is scored Pass or Revise.

| Criterion | What it looks like to pass | What does not pass |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The learner quotes an assigned Zhuangzi passage and cites it, and distinguishes what the passage says from what they propose about their own situation | The learner names the passage (Cook Ding section 3.2, or the Hunchback section A.1, or the Butterfly Dream 2.20), quotes the sentence that describes the absorbed moment, and states in their own words what the passage itself claims before applying it. A generic reflection about flow or being in the zone that never quotes or cites an assigned passage does not pass. | The learner describes their own experience vividly but never quotes or cites an assigned passage, or quotes it without distinguishing the passage's claim from their application. |
| 2. The learner identifies the training detail in the story as distinct from the state it produces | The learner points to the Hunchback's months of balancing balls or Cook Ding's account of his progression from seeing the whole carcass at the beginning to no longer seeing it whole after three years, and states that the story gives both a training history and a description of the resulting state, and that these are different things. | The learner treats the story as though it prescribed effortlessness directly, or as though the state it describes could be had without the training, or cites Cook Ding's nineteen years with the knife as a stated preparation period. |
| 3. The learner checks Ziporyn's gloss against an assigned passage | The learner takes Ziporyn's mastery-without-awareness line, finds the closest sentence in an assigned passage (Cook Ding or the Hunchback), quotes it, and states whether the gloss adds something the sentence does not say. The learner also identifies a training detail in that same passage, distinguishes the training history from the resulting state, and explains why neither supplies a universal training prescription. The learner names the addition rather than saying vaguely that the gloss "captures the spirit." | The learner accepts the gloss without checking it against a passage, or checks it but does not name what the gloss adds, or treats the story as prescribing effortlessness directly. |
| 4. The learner engages the inside-view versus puzzle-frame comparison with passage evidence | The learner supports or challenges the module's comparison of the Hunchback (performer's inside view) and the Butterfly Dream (puzzle about which state is real) with quoted evidence from both passages, and says which framing they find more useful and why. Agreement with the module's description is not required; evidence is. | The learner treats the two passages as interchangeable, or describes only one of them, or asserts the comparison without quoting either passage. |
| 5. The learner makes a reasoned application to their own situation | The learner describes a concrete situation from their own life, describes any change in self-monitoring (including no change), gives a reason for calling that self-monitoring avoidable strain or useful vigilance in that situation, and either proposes a bounded change or gives a reasoned decision not to change, naming a limit they would keep. The learner also states what the passage cannot establish about their situation. | The learner quotes a passage and separates it from their application but never makes the strain-versus-vigilance call, or makes the call without a reason, or proposes a change with no limit. |

Strong example for criterion 1: "The Hunchback says, 'No matter
how huge heaven and earth or how numerous the myriad things, I
perceive nothing but cicada wings.' The passage itself claims that
the hunchback's attention has narrowed to the point where nothing
else is present; it says nothing about jobs or about me. My
application is that a similar narrowing happens when I am playing a
familiar piece on the piano, and the passage supports that
application in the sentence about perceiving nothing but cicada
wings, but does not tell me to seek that state out. On the strain
question: the self-monitoring while I play is avoidable strain,
because the piece is one I have practised for years and the
monitoring is not catching anything; I would keep a light check on
tempo, which is doing necessary work, but drop the running
commentary on my hands."

Rejection example for criterion 1: "When I am really in flow at
work everything just happens and I forget myself, which is exactly
what the Zhuangzi teaches." This does not pass: it names no
passage, quotes nothing, and attributes to the Zhuangzi a general
teaching the assigned passages do not state in those words.
