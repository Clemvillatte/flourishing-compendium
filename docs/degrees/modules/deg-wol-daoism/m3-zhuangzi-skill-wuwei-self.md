## What this module is for

The Zhuangzi is the second foundational Daoist text and the one
most concerned with skill. Its most famous stories are about
people who have practised something until the practice disappears:
a butcher whose knife moves through a carcass without hitting a
bone, a hunchback who catches cicadas with a sticky pole because
he stops perceiving anything but cicada wings. The stories do not
say "practise more" as advice. They say something more specific:
that the highest form of a skill stops being experienced as a
performance by a self who is trying.

The point for the Walk-of-Life frame is that this is the most
concrete and least mystical part of the Daoist teaching. Almost
everyone has experienced the state the stories describe: driving
on a familiar road and realising you cannot remember the last ten
minutes, playing music and losing track of your hands, typing a
sentence without thinking about the keys. The Zhuangzi's claim,
read through its own stories, is that this kind of unselfconscious
absorption is not a lapse but a model. The self that supervises
the action is a kind of friction.

Ziporyn's lecture, read on its own assigned segment, makes the
same point in the Daodejing's vocabulary before turning to the
Zhuangzi: the highest form of a capacity is the one you do not
notice yourself doing, like the beating of your own heart. Then
he quotes the Zhuangzi line that is the module's spine: when the
shoe fits you forget your feet. The advice is not "become
effortless." It is that noticing your own performance is a sign
that something has gone wrong, not a sign of mastery.

Van Norden's documentary, on its assigned segment, gives the same
point in a first-person register from a martial-arts context: the
learner is told to take the idea of the move out of his head, and
the documentary describes the result as moving away from a forced
kind of thinking into a non-forced one. The transcript's phrase,
quoted verbatim into this module with its locator, is that it
feels like it is not you doing the thing but that it goes through
you. The two assigned videos do not attribute this claim to the
Zhuangzi. The module attributes it to them as a modern
description of the same phenomenon the stories describe, and asks
the learner to check that attribution against the assigned
passages rather than take it on trust.

## Outcomes

On completing this module the learner can:

1. **Read a Zhuangzi skill story and locate its claim.** The
   learner can read one assigned skill story (Cook Ding or the
   Hunchback), identify the exact sentence or sentences in which
   the story states what the skill feels like from the inside,
   quote that sentence, and distinguish the story's own words from
   any application the learner proposes to their own life. This
   is the same passage-versus-application distinction required in
   m2, now applied to a longer and more narrative text.

2. **Distinguish skill as story from skill as advice.** The
   learner can state, in their own words, that the assigned stories
   describe states of absorbed skill rather than prescribe a
   training programme, and can identify at least one sentence in
   an assigned passage where the story gives the training detail
   (for example the Hunchback's months of balancing balls, or Cook
   Ding's nineteen years with the same knife) as distinct from the
   state the training produces. The learner can explain why this
   distinction matters for how they use the text.

3. **Apply the "losing the self" description to one concrete
   situation.** The learner can select one situation from their
   own life, describe what the activity is, identify the moment
   at which the supervising self disappears (or state that it
   does not disappear and why), and state in one sentence what
   the Zhuangzi's shoe-fits line would say about that situation,
   distinguishing the line's own claim (the fit is forgotten when
   it works) from the learner's application of it.

4. **Check an interpretation against the text.** Given a modern
   paraphrase of a Zhuangzi theme (the module supplies Ziporyn's
   mastery-without-awareness gloss and Van Norden's
   it-goes-through-you description), the learner can check
   whether the paraphrase is supported by an assigned passage,
   quote the passage that supports it or state that it does not,
   and name the difference between what the passage says and what
   the paraphrase adds.

## Steps

### Step 1 — Read Cook Ding (about 8 minutes estimated on the
primary text with the three-times study multiplier; about 3
minutes raw on a single reading)

Read the Cook Ding story (Inner Chapters, section 3.2). It is 523
words including the translator's closing paragraph; the story
proper is shorter. Read it once straight through, then a second
time with a pencil. On the second reading, mark:

- The sentence in which Cook Ding says he no longer sees the
  carcass whole and meets it with his spirit rather than his eyes.
- The sentence about the knife having no thickness and the gaps
  between the joints.
- The sentence about the tangled knot, where he slows down and
  focuses his sight before flicking the blade.
- The closing exchange: Lord Wenhui says he has learned how to
  nurture life.

Notice that the story is not a summary of a teaching. It is a
narrated demonstration with one direct claim inside it, spoken by
Cook Ding himself, and one interpretive claim at the end, spoken
by Lord Wenhui. The translator's closing paragraph, which you
also read, names the set of stories this one belongs to and calls
them the "knack passages."

### Step 2 — Worked demonstration: what the story says versus what
it does not (about 15 minutes)

This is a worked example the module performs in front of you so
you can see the passage-versus-application distinction applied to
a longer text before you do it yourself in the graded deliverable.

The story says, in its own words, that after three years Cook
Ding could no longer see the carcass whole, and that now he meets
it with his spirit and does not look with his eyes. It says
perception and understanding cease and spirit moves as it will.
It says the blade has no thickness and the gaps are wide enough
to wander freely. It says that when a tangled knot lies ahead he
spots the challenge, focuses his sight, and slows down his hand.

The story does not say: that you should stop trying at your job.
It does not say that the supervising self is bad or that you
should aim to dissolve it. It does not say that any activity
performed automatically is Daoist. It does not say that skill
arrives without the nineteen years.

The application the module now proposes, and which is yours to
check against the story: when you are already competent at
something, the moment you notice yourself performing is usually
the moment something has gone slightly wrong. The story supports
this in the knot sentence, where the noticing, the slowing down
and the focusing of sight are all responses to difficulty. The
story does not support the stronger claim that you should never
notice yourself. Note the difference. In the graded deliverable
you will make the same check on your own situation.

### Step 3 — Read the Hunchback and the Butterfly Dream (about 10
minutes estimated on the primary text with the three-times study
multiplier; about 3 minutes 20 seconds raw on a single reading of
both passages)

Read the Hunchback and Cicadas story (Inner Chapters translation,
section A.1, printed as from chapter 19). It is 223 words. Mark:

- The sentence in which the hunchback describes holding his body
  like a twisted tree and his arm like a withered limb.
- The sentence in which he says that no matter how huge heaven
  and earth, he perceives nothing but cicada wings.
- Confucius's closing comment, "His will undivided, his spirit
  coalesced."

Then re-read the Butterfly Dream (chapter 2, section 20; 71 words;
you read it in m1). Notice that the two readings describe
different things. The Hunchback describes an absorbed skill from
the inside, from the point of view of the person performing it.
The Butterfly Dream describes an absorbed experience from the
outside, in the form of a puzzle about which state is the real
one. Both are Zhuangzi's ways of getting at the same underlying
point: the self that stands apart and comments is not always
there, and its absence is not a problem.

### Step 4 — Watch the two assigned video segments (about 9
minutes 58 seconds measured on the assigned segments)

Watch Ziporyn's segment "Highest virtue is without virtue:
mastery and unconscious action" (0:38:17 to 0:39:38, about 1
minute 21 seconds) and his segment "When the shoe fits you forget
your feet: Zhuangzi on wu wei" (0:39:38 to 0:43:39, about 4
minutes 1 second). These are printed chapter labels on the
video's own description; the durations are computed from those
labels. Then watch the Van Norden documentary segment printed at
0:29:44, labelled "Laozi, the Martial Arts, and Wúwéi
('nonaction')" in the description chapters, running to 0:34:20,
about 4 minutes 36 seconds on that chapter. Total assigned video
on this module: about 9 minutes 58 seconds.

While watching, keep one question: does the speaker's description
of mastery, effortlessness or non-forced action match what the
assigned Zhuangzi passages actually say? You will answer this in
the graded deliverable.

### Step 5 — Retrieval activities (about 10 minutes, estimate;
about 5 minutes each)

1. Without looking back at the module page, write the Zhuangzi
   line about the shoe. Then check it. If you cannot recall it,
   look it up and copy it exactly. Then, in one sentence, state
   what the line says about fit and forgetting, without adding
   anything about jobs, motivation or self-improvement.

2. Name the two assigned Zhuangzi stories you read in this module,
   and for each, state in one sentence the moment at which the
   story says the performer stops seeing the activity from the
   outside. Then check your answer against the passages.

### Step 6 — Graded deliverables (about 55 minutes)

**Deliverable a (about 25 minutes).** Choose one situation from
your own life in which you are competent. Describe the activity.
Then:

- Identify the moment, or state that there is no such moment, at
  which the supervising self disappears. Describe what the
  activity is like from the inside at that moment.
- Quote the sentence from one assigned Zhuangzi passage (Cook
  Ding or the Hunchback) that describes the closest thing to that
  moment in the story's own words. Cite the passage by its
  section label.
- State in one sentence what the shoe-fits line says about your
  situation, and distinguish the line's own claim from your
  application of it.
- State whether the situation supports or complicates the
  interpretation that noticing yourself perform is a sign of
  difficulty rather than mastery, and give your reason.

**Deliverable b (about 20 minutes).** Compare one modern gloss
with its source. Take either Ziporyn's statement that the highest
virtuosity is the things we do without realising we are doing
them, or Van Norden's description that it feels like it is not
you doing the thing but that it goes through you. Then:

- Find the sentence in an assigned Zhuangzi passage that comes
  closest to supporting the gloss. Quote it and cite the passage.
- State whether the gloss is faithful to that sentence, adds
  something the sentence does not say, or both. Name the specific
  addition.
- State whether the gloss is closer to Cook Ding, to the
  Hunchback, to the Butterfly Dream, or to none of them, and say
  why.

**Deliverable c (about 10 minutes).** Chapter check. In two or
three sentences, explain why the module asked you to notice the
difference between the Hunchback story, which describes the
absorbed skill from the inside, and the Butterfly Dream, which
describes it as a puzzle about which state is real. Then state
which framing you find more useful for thinking about your own
situation in deliverable a, and why.

### Step 7 — Self-assessment and feedback (about 5 minutes)

Before submitting, re-read the rubric below. Check each
criterion against your own draft. Revise anything you find weak.
Then submit.

## Rubric

Graded by rubric. Each criterion is scored Pass or Revise.

| Criterion | What it looks like to pass | What does not pass |
|---|---|---|
| 1. The learner quotes an assigned Zhuangzi passage and cites it, and distinguishes what the passage says from what they propose about their own situation | The learner names the passage (Cook Ding section 3.2, or the Hunchback section A.1, or the Butterfly Dream 2.20), quotes the sentence that describes the absorbed moment, and states in their own words what the passage itself claims before applying it. A generic reflection about flow or being in the zone that never quotes or cites an assigned passage does not pass. | The learner describes their own experience vividly but never quotes or cites an assigned passage, or quotes it without distinguishing the passage's claim from their application. |
| 2. The learner identifies the training detail in the story as distinct from the state it produces | The learner points to the Hunchback's months of balancing balls or Cook Ding's nineteen years with the same knife, and states that the story gives both a training history and a description of the resulting state, and that these are different things. | The learner treats the story as though it prescribed effortlessness directly, or as though the state it describes could be had without the training. |
| 3. The learner checks a modern gloss against an assigned passage | The learner takes one specific gloss (Ziporyn's mastery-without-awareness line, or Van Norden's it-goes-through-you line), finds the closest sentence in an assigned passage, quotes it, and states whether the gloss adds something the sentence does not say. The learner names the addition rather than saying vaguely that the gloss "captures the spirit." | The learner accepts the gloss without checking it against a passage, or checks it but does not name what the gloss adds. |
| 4. The learner distinguishes the Hunchback's inside view from the Butterfly Dream's outside view | The learner states that the Hunchback describes the absorbed state from the performer's point of view while the Butterfly Dream frames it as a puzzle about which state is the real one, and says which framing they find more useful and why. | The learner treats the two passages as interchangeable, or describes only one of them. |

Strong example for criterion 1: "The Hunchback says, 'No matter
how huge heaven and earth or how numerous the myriad things, I
perceive nothing but cicada wings.' The passage itself claims that
the hunchback's attention has narrowed to the point where nothing
else is present; it says nothing about jobs or about me. My
application is that a similar narrowing happens when I am playing a
familiar piece on the piano, and the passage supports that
application in the sentence about perceiving nothing but cicada
wings, but does not tell me to seek that state out."

Rejection example for criterion 1: "When I am really in flow at
work everything just happens and I forget myself, which is exactly
what the Zhuangzi teaches." This does not pass: it names no
passage, quotes nothing, and attributes to the Zhuangzi a general
teaching the assigned passages do not state in those words.
