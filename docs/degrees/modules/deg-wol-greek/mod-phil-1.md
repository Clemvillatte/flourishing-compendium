# mod-phil-1 Foundations of Virtue (deg-wol-greek)

Rebuilt to the curriculum-design skill from the legacy deg-philosophy
mod-phil-1 (see the pilot audit in pilots/mod-phil-1.md). Source verification
evidence is in ledgers/deg-wol-greek.md. This file is the module design
deliverable for the integrator band; the pilot review status is recorded in
pilots/mod-phil-1.md.

## Level and placement

L1 Orientation moving into early L2 Guided application. Entry module of the
Greek Philosophy walk; no prerequisites. The next module (Stoicism, ancient and
modern) takes the exit criteria below as its entry competencies.

## Outcomes

1. Define virtue, vice, eudaimonia and phronesis in your own words, giving one
   example of each from your own life.
2. Explain, in a paragraph a peer could follow, why Aristotle grounds the good
   life in virtuous activity: eudaimonia as the final end, the function
   argument, and habituation as the way virtue is acquired.
3. Take one real dilemma from your own life, analyse it once as a deontologist
   and once as a consequentialist, then state at least one thing a virtue
   ethics analysis adds that the other two analyses miss.

Every outcome is taught, practised and assessed below; the alignment map at
the end shows where.

## Instruction (the sequence a learner follows)

### Step 1: vocabulary from the wiki reading (Outcome 1)

Read the Book I overview page, then the three layer indexes with their
principles:

- Layer I (First Principles): A001 Make eudaimonia the organising end of your
  life; A002 Live according to your function (ergon); A003 Make virtue your
  primary investment.
- Layer II (Virtues of Character): A004 Become virtuous by doing virtuous
  acts; A005 Choose the mean, not the extreme; A042 Recognise that virtue is a
  hexis, not a feeling.
- Layer IV (Practical Wisdom): A043 Develop phronesis, practical wisdom.

Before your second pass through these pages, do Retrieval activity 1 (below):
free recall first, then re-read to check yourself. The four terms are used the
way the wiki uses them: virtue is a settled disposition (hexis) expressed in
action, vice its corrupt counterpart, eudaimonia the flourishing life that
activity over a whole life adds up to, phronesis the practical wisdom that
reads the particular situation and finds the action that fits it.

### Step 2: lectures (Outcomes 1 and 2)

- AR-04 Aristotle, Edith Hall, Gresham College, whole talk, 44:16. The
  definitional lecture. Hall introduces eudaimonia as happiness, flourishing,
  living well, and virtue as character expressed through the mean between
  excess and deficiency. Phronesis is not covered here; that term is carried
  by principle A043 and checked in the rubric.
- AR-01 Justice: What's The Right Thing To Do? Episode 10 "The Good Citizen",
  Michael Sandel, Harvard University. Assigned segment 0:00 to 40:15 (40
  minutes 15 seconds): Aristotle on the purpose of politics as cultivating
  virtue, then the Casey Martin golf case, where fairness arguments collide
  with the question of what the practice of golf is for. This segment is the
  model comparison for Outcome 3: the fairness argument and the freedom
  argument do work that the teleological argument does differently, and the
  learner should watch for the collision. Part Two (40:15 to 55:11, freedom
  based objections to Aristotle, including his defence of slavery) is
  optional.

Optional lectures, verified and linked for learners who want more:

- AR-06 Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics book 1, Gregory B. Sadler, 1:01:15, a
  close reading of the primary text (means, ends, and the question what
  happiness is).
- ST-03 Stoicon 2020, John Sellars, 28:04, a first look at Stoicism, the
  subject of the next module.

### Step 3: worked example with fading (Outcome 3)

The module page itself carries the teaching for the three-way analysis. The
same skill (analysing one dilemma through three frameworks) is demonstrated
three times with the guidance faded:

1. Fully worked (demonstrated). The found wallet: you find a wallet on a park
   bench with 200 dollars and an ID card. The deontologist asks what duties
   are in play: the wallet is not yours, keeping it treats a person as a
   means, returning it is what honesty requires whatever the consequences.
   The consequentialist tots up outcomes: the owner's loss, your gain, the
   trust costs if finder-keeping spread, and returns the wallet because the
   world where people return wallets goes better. The virtue ethicist asks
   what an honest and just person does here, and what the act trains in you:
   keeping it would rehearse rationalisation, returning it rehearses the
   honesty you want as your default. All three reach the same action; they
   differ in what they take the question to be.
2. Steps named (partially worked). The promised reference: a former student
   asks you to write a reference for a job you think they are wrong for. The
   learner is given the three analyses with the moves named but not filled
   in: deontology (what did you promise, and to whom does a reference owe
   truth), consequentialism (who is helped and harmed by each version of the
   letter), virtue ethics (what would honest support without flattery look
   like, and what does writing it, or refusing, train in you). The learner
   writes the three paragraphs.
3. Independent (the learner's own case). The graded activity below.

### Step 4: practice and assessment (all outcomes)

- Retrieval activity 1 (Outcome 1, spaced): from memory, write one sentence
  defining each of virtue, vice, eudaimonia and phronesis, with one example
  of each from your own life. Do this before re-reading the wiki pages, then
  again at the module midpoint (after Step 2), and compare the two attempts.
- Retrieval activity 2 (Outcome 2, after the lectures): state the function
  argument in three sentences of your own, then check against principle A002.
  Answer guidance: the argument runs from what a thing does uniquely well
  (its ergon) to what its excellence consists in; for humans that is rational
  activity; so the human good is rational activity done well, over a whole
  life.
- Interleaved review (forward link): the Stoics, next module, claim that
  virtue alone is sufficient for happiness. After reading A006 (flourishing
  requires external goods, listed as optional reading), write two sentences
  on where that claim sharpens Aristotle's position and where it breaks from
  it.
- Graded activity (Outcome 3): write a 300-word analysis of one real dilemma
  from your own life: analyse it as a deontologist, analyse it as a
  consequentialist, then state at least one thing the virtue ethics analysis
  adds that the other two miss. Self-assess with the rubric before
  submitting; the self-assessment step is part of the deliverable.

## Rubric (observable criteria)

Each criterion with a strong example and a rejection example, so the learner
can self-check.

1. Definitions match the wiki reading's usage, each with a life example.
   Strong: "Virtue is a settled disposition (hexis) to feel and act at the
   right time and in the right way; my standing habit of returning
   overcharged change is the small-scale version." Rejection: "Virtue means
   being a good person and doing nice things" (everyday usage, no hexis, no
   example from the learner's life).
2. The dilemma analysis names the framework it is using at each step, and
   runs each analysis on the learner's own case rather than a summary of the
   lecture cases. Strong: each paragraph opens by naming its framework and
   applies it to the learner's own dilemma. Rejection: the two analyses blur
   into one general "what should I do" discussion with no frameworks named.
3. The "what virtue ethics adds" paragraph states at least one thing both
   other analyses missed, in the learner's own case. Strong: "Both frameworks
   told me what to decide; neither asked what deciding it well would train in
   me." Rejection: "Virtue ethics is about being a good person, so it is
   also important" (adds nothing the other two analyses missed).

## Workload arithmetic

- Wiki reading (measured word counts): 7 principle dossiers plus 4 index
  pages, 5935 words. At 180 words per minute (text with some new concepts;
  the 238 wpm default adjusted down by purpose) that is 33 minutes of raw
  reading; with study overhead at three times raw reading, about 1 hour 40
  minutes (estimate, labelled).
- Required video (measured): AR-04 44:16 plus AR-01 assigned segment 40:15,
  total 1:24:31.
- Retrieval activities: about 30 minutes (estimate).
- Worked example study and the faded practice: about 30 minutes (estimate).
- Graded activity including self-assessment: about 45 minutes (estimate).

Total: about 4 hours 50 minutes. The legacy module claimed 4 hours with no
arithmetic behind it; the figure above is the computed total. Optional
material (AR-06 1:01:15, ST-03 28:04, AR-01 Part Two 14:52) is not counted.
