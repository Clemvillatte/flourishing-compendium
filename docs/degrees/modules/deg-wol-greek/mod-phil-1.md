## Level and placement

L1 Orientation moving into early L2 Guided application. Entry module of the
Greek Philosophy walk; no prerequisites. The next module (Stoicism, ancient
and modern) takes the exit criteria below as its entry competencies.

## Outcomes

1. Define virtue, vice, eudaimonia and phronesis in your own words, giving one
   example of each from your own life.
2. Explain, in a paragraph a peer could follow, why Aristotle grounds the good
   life in virtuous activity: eudaimonia as the final end, the function
   argument, and habituation as the way virtue is acquired.
3. Take one real dilemma from your own life, analyse it once as a deontologist
   and once as a consequentialist, then compare what each framework's reasons
   pick out and what a virtue ethics analysis adds, naming where the accounts
   overlap and where they disagree.

Every outcome is taught, practised and assessed below; the alignment map at
the end shows where.

expressed in action, vice its corrupt counterpart (the excess or deficiency

to human goods (NE VI.5, quoted in A043).

### Step 2: the three frameworks in brief (Outcome 3)

Before the worked example, a short introduction so the comparison is not
heard as three slogans. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (free)
defines the two rivals. Read the assigned sections:

- Deontological Ethics, section 1, Deontology's Foil: Consequentialism
  (1122 words), which sets the two families against each other:
  https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-deontological/
- Consequentialism, section 2, What is Consequentialism? (620 words):
  https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/consequentialism/

The deontological family judges choices by conformity to moral norms, some
forbidden whatever their effects; the Kantian version judges the principle
(maxim) the agent acts on and forbids using others merely as means. The
consequentialist family holds that whether an act is right depends only on
its consequences, with classical utilitarianism as the paradigm (Bentham,
Mill, Sidgwick). The worked example below illustrates one Kantian-style
duty analysis and one classical utilitarian tally; other versions of each
family differ, and the learner should treat them as families, not single
doctrines.

### Step 4: worked example with fading (Outcome 3)

The module page itself carries the teaching for the three-way analysis. The
same skill (analysing one dilemma through three frameworks) is demonstrated
three times with the guidance faded:

1. Fully worked (demonstrated). The found wallet: you find a wallet on a
   park bench with 200 dollars and an ID card. A Kantian-style duty
   analysis: the wallet is not yours, keeping it treats the owner merely
   as a means to your ends, and honesty is a norm that holds whatever the
   consequences. A classical act-utilitarian analysis: compare the
   likely consequences of returning versus keeping this wallet for
   everyone affected; the owner loses 200 dollars and her cards and her
   week if you keep it, you gain 200 dollars once, and returning it
   costs you an hour of delivery; on any reasonable weighting of those
   effects, returning produces more good overall. (Whether a rule of
   wallet-returning makes the world go better is a further, rule-based
   comparison; it is noted here so the learner can tell the two apart,
   and it reaches the same answer here.) A virtue ethics analysis: what
   would an honest and just person do here, and what does each act train
   in you; keeping it would rehearse rationalisation, returning it
   rehearses the honesty you want as your default. All three reach the
   same action; they differ in what they take the question to be, and a
   careful consequentialist can also count the long-term effects
   of your habits, so the difference is one of emphasis, not a clean
   exclusion.
2. Steps named (partially worked). The promised reference: a former
   student asks you to write a reference for a job you think they are
   wrong for. The learner is given the three analyses with the moves
   named but not filled in: the duty analysis (what did you promise, and
   to whom does a reference owe truth), the utilitarian tally (who is
   helped and harmed by each version of the letter, including effects on
   your own habits of truthfulness), virtue ethics (what would honest
   support without flattery look like, and what does writing it, or
   refusing, train in you). The learner writes the three paragraphs.
3. Independent (the learner's own case). The graded activity below.

### Step 5: practice and assessment (all outcomes)

- Retrieval activity 1 (Outcome 1, spaced): from memory, write one
  sentence defining each of virtue, vice, eudaimonia and phronesis, with
  one example of each from your own life. Do this before re-reading the
  wiki pages, then again after AR-04, and compare the two attempts. The
  second pass is a submitted deliverable, self-assessed against rubric
  criterion 1.
- Retrieval activity 2 (Outcome 2, after AR-01 segment 1): state the
  function argument in three sentences of your own, then explain in two
  more why habituation (A004) is needed if virtue is a hexis (A042).
  Check against A002 and A004. Answer guidance: the argument runs from
  what a thing does uniquely well (its ergon) to what its excellence
  consists in; for humans that is rational activity; so the human good is
  rational activity done well, over a whole life; and because virtue is a
  settled disposition rather than a mood, it is acquired by doing the
  acts, as builders become builders by building.
- Retrieval activity 3 (Outcome 3, after AR-01 segment 2): in the Casey
  Martin debate, name one argument that reasons from fairness and one
  that reasons from the purpose of the practice, and say which framework
  each is closer to and why.
- Graded activity (Outcomes 2 and 3): two deliverables, self-assessed
  with the rubric before submitting; the self-assessment step is part of
  each deliverable.
  a. The paragraph (Outcome 2): one paragraph, written for a peer, on
     why Aristotle grounds the good life in virtuous activity.
  b. The dilemma analysis (Outcome 3): 300 words on one real dilemma from
     your own life, analysed as a deontologist and as a consequentialist,
     then a comparison of what each framework's reasons pick out, where
     they overlap and disagree, and what the virtue ethics analysis adds.

## Rubric (observable criteria)

Each criterion with a strong example and a rejection example, so the learner
can self-check.

1. Definitions match the wiki reading's usage, each with a life example.
   Strong: "Virtue is a settled disposition (hexis) to feel and act at the
   right time and in the right way; my standing habit of returning
   overcharged change is the small-scale version." Rejection: "Virtue means
   being a good person and doing nice things" (everyday usage, no hexis, no
   example from the learner's life).
2. The Outcome 2 paragraph names the function argument, habituation and
   the final end, and connects them: flourishing as activity over a whole
   life, virtue acquired by doing the acts. Strong (sample): "Aristotle
   starts from what a thing does uniquely well: a knife cuts, and a good
   knife cuts well. Human beings reason and choose, so the human good is
   reasoning and choosing well, sustained across a whole life; that is
   eudaimonia, and it is activity rather than a mood. Because it is
   activity, you get it by doing it: you become honest by doing honest
   acts, as builders become builders by building. So virtue is not a rule
   you follow once but a settled capacity you build, and flourishing is
   what a life of that building adds up to." Rejection: "Aristotle
   thought being good makes you happy" (no function argument, no
   habituation, no connection between them).
3. The dilemma analysis names the framework it is using at each step and
   applies it accurately on the learner's own case: the duty analysis
   identifies the relevant norm and justifies it, the consequentialist
   analysis compares consequences for everyone affected, and the virtue
   analysis asks what a person with the relevant virtues would do and
   what the act trains. Strong (sample, on the reference-letter case):
   "Duty: I promised to support her, but a reference also owes the
   employer truth, and a promise cannot license a lie; so the letter must
   be truthful, which may mean writing a warm letter that states her real
   gaps. Consequences: the employer staffs a role badly if the letter
   misleads, she loses the job later and worse if she cannot do it, and I
   lose future referees' trust if I am known to inflate; the honest
   letter risks her disappointment now but harms least overall.
   Virtue: an honest advocate neither puffs nor damns; writing the
   truthful-warm letter trains me to be useful without being deceptive."
   Rejection (sample): "Utilitarian: the letter helps me because she
   might return the favour, and it helps her get the job, so write the
   strong version" (counts only the writer's and the applicant's
   benefits, omits the employer and the downstream harms, and states no
   comparison of alternatives).
4. The comparison paragraph states where the two frameworks' reasons
   overlap and disagree, and what the virtue ethics analysis adds, in the
   learner's own case. Strong: "Both frameworks told me what to decide;
   the virtue ethics analysis judged the act by the character it expressed
   and cultivated, where the utilitarian tally judged it by outcomes and
   the duty analysis by conformity to a norm, and a far-sighted
   utilitarian could still count character effects among the outcomes."
   Rejection: "Virtue ethics is about being a good person, so it is also
   important" (adds nothing; no overlap or disagreement named).
