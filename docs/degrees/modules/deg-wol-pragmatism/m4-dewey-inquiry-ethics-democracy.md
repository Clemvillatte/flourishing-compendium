# m4-dewey-inquiry-ethics-democracy: Dewey on Inquiry, Deliberation and Democracy (deg-wol-pragmatism)

Fourth module of A Walk of Life: Pragmatism. The first three modules gave
you the origin of the method in Peirce, its face in James, and the truth
question between them. This one hands you Dewey's tools for the places the
method was built for: a stuck situation, a hard choice, and a disagreement
you keep having with someone you live with. You will read Dewey's account
of where thinking starts, his account of what deliberation is, and his
account of what discussion does for shared problems, then run all three on
one recurring disagreement of your own. Source verification evidence is
in ledgers/deg-wol-pragmatism.md. STATUS: DRAFT, NOT CLEARED; awaits
supervisor module-QC and advisor review.

## Level and placement

L3 Independent analysis. Prerequisites (the m3 exits): you can explain
James's method and his account of truth with the qualifier that keeps it
from pleasant-belief, run the three genuine-option tests on a decision,
and run the objection-and-constraint pattern on a claim. This module
adapts L3 to a theory course: its independent analysis is applying the
inquiry-deliberation pattern to a novel case without a template, and its
teach-back is explaining the pattern to the person you disagree with.
The exit criteria below are the entry competencies of m5, the capstone.

## Outcomes

1. Explain Dewey's account of reflective inquiry in your own words:
   where thinking starts, what steadies it, and what its end is.
2. Explain deliberation as Dewey describes it, and apply it to a proposed
   action in a case, showing what imagination tries out and what would
   count as the rehearsal failing.
3. Apply the whole pattern to one recurring disagreement of your own
   without a template, and appraise the case: separate what you
   observed, what you recall, what you assume and what you only
   imagined; name what evidence supports each proposed explanation;
   adapt your proposed action to your constraints with reasons; and
   state what evidence or feedback would change your judgment.
4. Compare Dewey with Peirce on the same question, where inquiry starts
   and what ends it, naming what each selection emphasizes and where
   each selection stops.
5. Explain Dewey's account of what discussion does for shared problems,
   and teach the pattern back to the person in your disagreement.

Every outcome is taught, practised and assessed below; the alignment map
at the end shows where.

## Instruction (the sequence a learner follows)

### Step 1: where thinking starts (Outcome 1)

Read Reading A from [How We Think](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37423/37423-h/37423-h.htm)
(Dewey 1910, Project Gutenberg #37423, public domain): the contiguous
passage that begins "Another instance, commonplace also, yet not quite so
trivial, may enforce this lesson" and ends "is wholly futile" (778 words
measured on the downloaded plain text of the ebook, line breaks joined;
the word count was computed on the extracted passage). Take notes on the
three moves the passage makes: the traveller at the forked road, the
general rule about where thinking begins, and the recapitulation about
perplexity and suggestion.

The passage's three moves, in his words. First, the situation: a
traveller "comes to a branching of the roads" with "no sure knowledge to
fall back upon" and is "brought to a standstill of hesitation and
suspense"; his reflection "is aimed at the discovery of facts that will
serve this purpose" of a signboard or map. Second, the general rule:
"Thinking begins in what may fairly enough be called a forked-road
situation, a situation which is ambiguous, which presents a dilemma,
which proposes alternatives", and while activity glides smoothly there is
"no call for reflection"; the steadying factor is that "Demand for the
solution of a perplexity is the steadying and guiding factor in the
entire process of reflection", so that "The problem fixes the end of
thought and the end controls the process of thinking." Third, the
recapitulation: "the origin of thinking is some perplexity, confusion,
or doubt. Thinking is not a case of spontaneous combustion"; given the
difficulty, "the next step is suggestion of some way out", and the
suggestions draw on "past experience and prior knowledge", so general
appeals to think "irrespective of the existence in his own experience
of some difficulty that troubles him and disturbs his equilibrium, are
as futile as advice to lift himself by his boot-straps."

Practice (Outcome 1): write two or three sentences stating, in your own
words, where Dewey says thinking starts, what steadies it, and what its
end is. Check against the quoted sentences.

### Step 2: what deliberation is (Outcome 2)

Read Reading B from [Human Nature and Conduct](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41386/41386-h/41386-h.htm)
(Dewey 1922, Project Gutenberg #41386, public domain), Part Three, the
section "The Nature of Deliberation": the contiguous passage that begins
"Our first problem is then to investigate the nature of ordinary
judgments" and ends "the object is that which objects" (476 words
measured on the downloaded plain text by pinned opening and closing
strings, whitespace-normalized).

Deliberation, in his words: "deliberation is a dramatic rehearsal (in
imagination) of various competing possible lines of action." It starts
when overt action is blocked by a conflict of habit and impulse; then
"each habit, each impulse, involved in the temporary suspense of overt
action takes its turn in being tried out", and "Deliberation is an
experiment in finding out what the various lines of possible action are
really like." The trial is in imagination, so it is cheap in a way real
action is not: "Thought runs ahead and foresees outcomes, and thereby
avoids having to await the instruction of actual failure and disaster";
"An act overtly tried out is irrevocable... An act tried out in
imagination is not final or fatal. It is retrievable." The rehearsal is
not idle daydream: "Each conflicting habit and impulse takes its turn in
projecting itself upon the screen of imagination", where it "unrolls a
picture of its future history", and the imagined run meets objects that
check and deflect it, because "the object is that which objects."

Practice (Outcome 2): take a proposed action in your own case, an email
you mean to send or a rule you mean to propose. Write the rehearsal:
what each competing course unrolls, and what object would rebuff it in
imagination. State what would count as the rehearsal failing, that is,
what the imagined run foresees going badly.

### Step 3: what discussion does (Outcome 5)

Read Reading C from [The Public and Its Problems](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/71000)
(Dewey 1927, Project Gutenberg #71000, public domain), Chapter VI: the
contiguous passage that begins "The strongest point to be made in behalf
of even such rudimentary political forms" and ends "it may be successful
in becoming a majority" (374 words measured on the downloaded plain
text by pinned opening and closing strings; the passage ends at that
sentence inside the source's final printed line).

The passage, in his words: the asset of even "rudimentary political
forms as democracy has already attained" is "that to some extent they
involve a consultation and discussion which uncover social needs and
troubles"; "The man who wears the shoe knows best that it pinches and
where it pinches, even if the expert shoemaker is the best judge of how
the trouble is to be remedied." Majority rule "never is merely majority
rule", because what Samuel J. Tilden said a long time ago holds: "The
means by which a majority comes to be a majority is the more important
thing", and the important thing is "antecedent debates, modification of
views to meet the opinions of minorities, the relative satisfaction given
the latter by the fact that it has had a chance and that next time it
may be successful in becoming a majority." Earlier in the book,
Chapter I defines the concept that makes a shared problem public: "The
public consists of
all those who are affected by the indirect consequences of transactions
to such an extent that it is deemed necessary to have those consequences
systematically cared for" (Chapter I; the sentence is quoted from the
same downloaded text, verified verbatim). Note the boundary: A and B
are direct parties to their own transactions, not bystanders hit by
indirect consequences, so the flat is not a Deweyan public. The flat
meeting borrows the same idea at small scale: those who live inside
the friction know where it pinches, and the settlement comes from
consultation and modification rather than from one side's verdict.

Hold the two ideas together for the graded work: the person inside the
friction knows where it pinches, and the settlement is what the
discussion and modification produce, not the vote that ends them.

Retrieval activity (Outcome 5): from memory, write one sentence on what
the shoe passage says discussion does, and one on what the "never is
merely majority rule" sentence says the important thing is.

### Step 4: worked example with fading (Outcomes 2, 3 and 5)

The pattern applied to one recurring disagreement, demonstrated with the
guidance faded. The stipulated case is constructed for teaching; test it
against your own disagreement rather than take it as a finding.

The case, stipulated: two people share a flat. One wants a strict weekly
rota for cleaning and shared costs; the other wants flexibility. The
disagreement recurs every week or two, and each episode leaves both
irritated. Call them A and B.

1. Fully worked (demonstrated). The forked road: the recurring fight is
   the perplexity, and the forked-road question is what the fight is
   actually about. Two candidate descriptions: the fight is about whether
   a rota gets the chores done in this flat (a question evidence could
   settle), or the fight is about what each counts as a fair share (a
   question of value). The Deweyan move is to notice that the
   disagreement has not stated which question it is, so it cannot start.
   Suggestions from past experience: A's rota worked in a previous flat;
   B's flexibility worked when work schedules were erratic. The steadying
   demand: the problem is the recurring friction, and any proposal is
   tested against whether it removes the friction, not against whether
   it wins the argument. The deliberation, in imagination: A rehearses
   proposing the rota at a flat meeting and sees B hear it as A calling
   B lazy, because in a past episode it came out that way; the rehearsal
   fails on that run, so the proposal is re-formed (a rota with a swap
   clause, proposed as an experiment rather than a verdict). The
   appraisal: whether the rota gets chores done is testable (a two-week
   trial with a record); what each counts as fair is not settled by
   the chore log alone, but it is not beyond inquiry either: reasons,
   circumstances and consequences still bear on it, and the democratic
   move applies there, consultation that uncovers what the friction
   actually is for each person. The rehearsal of B's reaction is an
   assumption until checked: A imagines B hearing the proposal as a
   verdict, and checking that assumption, by asking or by watching the
   next episode, belongs to the inquiry, not to the imagination
   alone. The worked appraisal, run on the constructed case:
   observed, the shared-costs reconciliation was missed in three of
   the last six weeks, and both people were in the flat at each
   missed one; recalled, A's previous flat kept a rota for two
   years without a missed week; assumed, that B hears the rota as a
   verdict on B's effort (an assumption until checked, and the next
   conversation can check it); imagined only, that a strict rota
   would end the friction. Evidence judgment: the observed record
   shows only that the misses happened; it does not by itself show
   why, so any explanation of the misses stays provisional.
   Adaptation with reasons: B's irregular shifts are a stipulated
   constraint of the case, not something the record shows, and A
   adapts the proposal to that constraint with a swap clause and a
   two-week trial, because the constraint makes a fixed rota
   unrealistic whatever the misses' cause. Revision trigger: the
   trial must record, each week, whether the reconciliation
   happened and what changed near it (shifts, travel, workload);
   if the misses continue under the trial with the same pattern,
   the explanation and the proposal are both revised, and the
   forked-road question is reopened.
2. Steps named (partially worked). Take the stipulated next episode: B
   skipped the shared-costs reconciliation again. Write the forked-road
   question for this episode, one suggestion from each person's past
   experience, the rehearsal of B opening the conversation, and the
   appraisal: mark each item in your write-up as observed, recalled,
   assumed or imagined only, judge what the record supports, and
   name your revision trigger. Feedback to check against once you
   have written yours: a strong piece names the forked road as a
   question, gives each person's suggestion from their own experience,
   runs the rehearsal until it meets an object that rebuffs it,
   marks the appraisal's material as observed, recalled, assumed or
   imagined, and separates what a record could settle from what
   reasons, circumstances and consequences bear on.
3. Independent (the graded deliverable). Take one recurring
   disagreement of your own, with a partner, a parent, a colleague or
   a flatmate. Write the full pattern: forked-road question, suggestions,
   rehearsal of your next move in it, and the appraisal. Deliverable c
   in Step 6 is this piece, written up cleanly.

### Step 5: the comparison (Outcome 4)

Re-read your m1 and m2 notes on Peirce: the maxim, from [How to Make
Our Ideas Clear](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/How_to_Make_Our_Ideas_Clear)
as assigned in m1, and the doubt-belief-inquiry passages from
[The Fixation of
Belief](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Fixation_of_Belief) as
assigned in m2-belief-and-doubt.md. Write the comparison on the same
question: where inquiry starts, and what ends it. Ground both sides
in the selections you were actually assigned, and compare those
selections directly: for Peirce, the m2 passages (doubt "is an uneasy
and dissatisfied state", belief a habit that guides action, inquiry
"a struggle to attain a state of belief"); for Dewey, the three
readings of this module: the [forked
road](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37423/37423-h/37423-h.htm), the
[dramatic
rehearsal](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41386/41386-h/41386-h.htm),
the [consultation that uncovers
needs](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/71000). Where the selections agree:
inquiry starts from a real disruption, not from a general duty to
reflect. What each selection emphasizes: Peirce's gives the logical
shape of how belief settles and, with the maxim from m1, a test for
what a conception means; Dewey's starts from the disruption as a
trouble in living, a blocked course of activity, and follows the
settlement into deliberation among competing goods and into shared
problems. Where each selection stops: the m2 passages stop at the
logic of belief and do not follow inquiry into deliberation or
shared problems; Dewey's passages in this module supply no logical
test like the maxim. Take your own position on which selection you
would use first on your case, and why. Deliverable d is this piece.

### The bounded domain, in plain terms

This module covers Dewey's account of where thinking starts, what
deliberation is, and what discussion does for shared problems, read in
his own texts, applied to one recurring disagreement of your own. It
does not cover Dewey's educational writings beyond the inquiry
passage, his aesthetics, his political theory beyond the two assigned
passages, or the scholarly debate over how Dewey's ethics relates to
Aristotle's. The Dewey ethics elective anchors here and goes further;
material that merely fits the topic does not qualify, and the module
admits only what serves learning to run the pattern on your own case.

## Rubric (observable criteria)

1. The inquiry explanation states where thinking starts, what steadies
   it, and what its end is, in the learner's own words. Strong: "Dewey
   says thinking starts at a forked road, a real perplexity that blocks
   you; the demand for its solution is what steadies the process, and
   the problem fixes the end of thought, so suggestions from past
   experience are tested against removing the trouble, not against
   winning." Rejection: "Dewey believed in critical thinking" (no
   content), or an account that starts thinking from a general duty to
   reflect.
2. The rehearsal shows what imagination tries out and what would count
   as its failing. Strong: "I rehearsed proposing the rota and watched B
   hear it as a verdict on her effort, because that is how it landed
   last time; the rehearsal fails if the imagined run foresees the same
   fight, so I re-formed the proposal as an experiment with a swap
   clause." Rejection: a description of the action with no imagined
   run, or a rehearsal that meets no object and foresees nothing.
3. The independent application runs the whole pattern on the learner's
   own case and appraises it. Strong: the forked-road question is
   stated as a question about the case, each side's suggestion comes
   from their own experience, the rehearsal meets a specific rebuff,
   and the appraisal separates what the learner observed from what
   they assumed, names the evidence for each proposed explanation,
   adapts the proposed action to their constraints with reasons, and
   states what record or feedback would change their judgment.
   Rejection: an application that stays abstract, or one whose
   appraisal offers no observation-assumption separation and no
   evidence for its explanation of the case.
4. The comparison names what each selection emphasizes and where
   each selection stops, on the same question. Strong: "Both
   selections start inquiry from a real disruption, not from a duty
   to reflect; Peirce's gives the logical shape of how belief
   settles and the m1 maxim as a test of what a conception means,
   while Dewey's follows the disruption into deliberation and shared
   problems, which is where the m2 passages stop; on my case I
   would start with Dewey's forked road, because my trouble is in
   the living, and use the maxim on what 'fair share' means."
   Rejection: two biographies side by side, or a claim that they
   simply disagree with nothing grounded in either assignment.
5. The teach-back piece states Dewey's account of what discussion does
   accurately and reports what the explanation to the other person
   surfaced. Strong: "Dewey's point is that consultation uncovers
   needs the other side cannot see from outside, and the settlement
   is the modification, not the vote; when I explained this to my
   flatmate she said the fight was never about the rota, which moved
   my appraisal of what a trial could settle." Rejection: a report
   that the talk went well with nothing surfaced, or a piece that
   misstates the account (for example, that Dewey says the majority
   should just outvote the minority).
