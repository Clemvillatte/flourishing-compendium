# m2-belief-and-doubt: Peirce on How Beliefs Settle (deg-wol-pragmatism)

Second module of A Walk of Life: Pragmatism. The first module gave you
the maxim and the map of the tradition. This one takes you into Peirce's 1877
essay [The Fixation of
Belief](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Fixation_of_Belief), where
he sets out the question that matters for a walk of life: when a
belief of yours is settled, what settled it, and would it survive contact with
something outside your wishing? That question is where alleviating
suffering and becoming wiser meet in this tradition: a belief fixed by
tenacity, authority or taste can look solid while quietly running your
life, and learning to notice how it was fixed is the first move in
testing it. Source verification evidence is in
ledgers/deg-wol-pragmatism.md. STATUS: DRAFT, NOT CLEARED; awaits
supervisor module-QC and advisor review.

## Level and placement

## Outcomes

1. Explain Peirce's account of doubt, belief and inquiry in your own
   words, naming the three contrasts he draws between doubt and belief
   and his definition of inquiry.
2. Name the four methods of fixing belief and state, for each, how
   it settles beliefs and what Peirce's criticism of it is.
3. Diagnose one of your own beliefs under all four methods, naming
   which plausibly fixed it, then design the method-of-science test
   for it, naming what plays the role of the external permanency.
4. State the choice Peirce puts to the reader and take your own
   position on it, with a reason.

Every outcome is taught, practised and assessed below; the alignment map
at the end shows where.

## Instruction (the sequence a learner follows)

### Step 1: doubt, belief, inquiry (Outcome 1)

Read Reading A from
[The Fixation of Belief](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Fixation_of_Belief)
(public domain, Wikisource): the two sections that begin "We generally
know when we wish to ask a question" and end "it would be without a
purpose" (834 words measured on normalized raw wikitext of the page). Take notes on
one question only: what does each sentence say doubt and belief DO to a
person? You will use the notes in the retrieval activity and the graded
work.

As you read, hold onto three contrasts Peirce draws, and the pairing
that runs through them. First, a practical one: "Our beliefs guide
our desires and shape our actions", and the feeling of believing
indicates "there being established in our nature some habit which will
determine our actions", while "Doubt never has such an effect"
([Section III](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Fixation_of_Belief)).
Doubt has its own effect, a different one: it stimulates the struggle
of inquiry, as the fourth section's definition below shows. Second, a felt one: doubt "is an uneasy and dissatisfied
state from which we struggle to free ourselves", while belief "is a calm
and satisfactory state which we do not wish to avoid". Third, a timing
one: "Belief does not make us act at once, but puts us into such a
condition that we shall behave in some certain way, when the occasion
arises." So the pair runs: belief settles us into habits that guide
action when the occasion arises, and the irritation of doubt starts
the struggle that inquiry is. From the fourth section, the definition
that names this
module: "The irritation of doubt causes a struggle to attain a state of
belief. I shall term this struggle inquiry", and "the sole object of
inquiry is the settlement of opinion."

One caution before the next step: Peirce's claim that inquiry's sole
object is the settlement of OPINION is deliberately provocative. The felt
goal of inquiry, on his account, is the end of doubt, which is why
methods that end doubt without touching facts can satisfy us. Hold
that provocation for Step 3.

Retrieval activity (Outcome 1, closed book): from memory, write the
three contrasts between doubt and belief and the definition of
inquiry, each in one sentence, including both sides of the pairing
between belief's action-guiding habits and doubt's stimulation of
inquiry, with one example of your own for any one of them.

### Step 2: the four methods, part one (Outcome 2)

Read Reading B1 from the same essay: the passage that begins "If the
settlement of opinion is the sole object of inquiry" and ends "slaves
they ought to remain" (1,368 words measured on normalized raw wikitext of the page).
It covers the first two methods. Build a two-row note table as you
read, one row per method, with three columns: how the method settles
beliefs, Peirce's criticism or limit of the method, and one line of
his own wording for each. A fourth question belongs to Step 4, kept
separate: what would challenge a particular belief held this way.

The two methods in brief, with his words. The method of tenacity:
taking "as answer to a question any we may fancy, and constantly
reiterating it", learning "to turn with contempt and hatred from
anything that might disturb it". It yields "great peace of mind", and
Peirce declines to call it irrational from outside ("that only amounts
to saying that his method of settling belief is not ours"), but it
"will be unable to hold its ground in practice. The social impulse is
against it": other people's opinions look "quite as good as his own",
and that shakes it. The method of authority: "Let the will of the
state act, then, instead of that of the individual", an institution
that reiterates correct doctrines, teaches the young, keeps people
ignorant of reasons to think otherwise, and punishes forbidden beliefs.
Peirce allows its "immeasurable mental and moral superiority" to
tenacity and its majestic results, and writes that for the mass of
mankind "there is perhaps no better method than this" (with his
reasons, in the essay); what breaks it is the wider social feeling that
notices the accident of one's upbringing and doubts
([Section V](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Fixation_of_Belief)).

Practice (Outcome 2): complete your two-row table for these two
methods before Reading B2; check it against the passages above.

### Step 3: the four methods, part two (Outcome 2)

Read Reading B2: the passage that begins "But no institution can
undertake to regulate opinions" and ends "he is bound to adhere"
(2,371 words measured on normalized raw wikitext of the page). It covers the a
priori method, the method of science, and the choice Peirce puts to
the reader. Extend your table to four rows with the same three
columns. For the science row, note the difference the module asked you
to keep separate: on Peirce's account a belief can be challenged and
revised by further inquiry under the method of science without the
method being discredited, which is why his criticism of the first three
methods (they have no reason to make opinions coincide with the fact)
does not read as a criticism of the fourth.

The a priori method: beliefs develop "in harmony with natural causes"
among people conversing, with fundamental propositions adopted because
they seem "agreeable to reason", which Peirce glosses as "that which we
find ourselves inclined to believe". It is "far more intellectual and
respectable" than the first two, but it makes inquiry resemble "the
development of taste", and taste "is always more or less a matter of
fashion", so metaphysicians have never come to fixed agreement. The
method of science: a method "by which our beliefs may be determined by nothing human, but by some external permanency — by something upon which our thinking has no effect", resting on the hypothesis that "There are Real things, whose
characters are entirely independent of our opinions about them", such
that anyone with sufficient experience and reason "will be led to the
one True conclusion." It is the only method that distinguishes a right
and a wrong way of proceeding, which is why bad reasoning as well as
good reasoning is possible under it. Then the choice: a person
"should consider that, after all, he wishes his opinions to coincide
with the fact, and that there is no reason why the results of those
three first methods should do so", and this choice is "one of the
ruling decisions of his life"
([Section V](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Fixation_of_Belief)).

### Step 4: worked example with fading (Outcome 3)

The four-method diagnosis applied to one belief, demonstrated with the
guidance faded. The first two items use stipulated beliefs so you can
watch the moves without new content; the third moves to your own. The
stipulated cases are constructed for teaching; test them against your
own material rather than take them as findings.

The belief: "my brother will never change."

1. Fully worked (demonstrated). Tenacity: the belief is fixed by
   clinging to it, dwelling on every confirmation (every cancelled
   plan, every old argument) and learning to turn from anything that
   might disturb it (the new job he took, the apology he offered).
   What would break it: the social impulse, his own or someone else's
   counter-opinion looking equally good, or a fact that refuses to be
   turned from. Authority: the belief is fixed by an institution or
   voice with the standing to declare it, the family's settled
   verdict, repeated at every gathering, taught to newcomers, with
   deviations gently punished by tone. What would break it: contact
   with people whose verdicts run otherwise, or noticing the accident
   of which family holds which verdict. A priori: the belief is fixed
   by what is agreeable to reason, the story that fits the family's
   self-understanding, "people don't change" as the sort of thing
   everyone sensible simply inclines to believe. What would break it:
   the fashion changing, a rival framing that inclines differently.
   Science: the test is built on something outside the wishing, and it
   needs the belief bounded into something observable. Read as the
   claim that he will not keep a weekly commitment over the next
   eight weeks, the test runs: the observations are each week's
   promised commitment, kept or missed, entered in a note made the
   same day; the result that would revise the claim is any kept
   commitment in the record, and a majority of kept commitments over
   the period would end it. The external permanency here is his
   conduct as it occurs, independent of anyone's opinion about it.
   The operational choices (the eight-week period, the same-day
   entries) are constructed teaching choices for this example, not a
   procedure Peirce prescribes; they are what make the claim testable.
2. Steps named (partially worked). Take the belief: "the way I work
   under pressure is just who I am." Your task: write the four-method
   diagnosis, at least one sentence per method, naming what fixes the
   belief under that method and what would break it, and end with the
   science test design: what observable pattern would count, and what
   plays the external permanency. One step is supplied for the science
   test: an observation base made at the time (notes, calendar
   entries, delivered work) is the design choice made here, on the
   ground that a record made at the time is harder for the belief to
   reshape than a memory of the period. Feedback to check against once you
   have written yours: a strong diagnosis names, for each method, a
   concrete fixing mechanism (self-reiteration, a standing verdict, an
   agreeable story, an outside pattern) and a concrete breaker, and
   the science test names data that exists whether or not the belief
   is true.
3. Independent (the graded deliverable). Take one live belief of your
   own, something you hold about a person, yourself, or what is
   possible, where you feel genuine uncertainty. Write the diagnosis
   under all four methods, distinguishing which method plausibly
   fixed this belief in your actual history from how the other three
   would have held it, and write the science test design with its
   observations and the result that would revise the belief.
   Deliverable b in Step 5 is this piece, written up cleanly.

### The bounded domain, in plain terms

This module covers Peirce's account of how beliefs settle: doubt,
belief, inquiry, and the four methods, read in his own text. It stays
inside the walk's scope: the classical pragmatist method as a way of
living with your beliefs. It does not teach the history of logic
beyond what the essay itself uses, it does not cover Peirce's later
semiotics or his classification of the sciences, and it does not
cover the psychology of habit formation (the psychdim walk owns the
research on habit change). Material that merely fits the topic does
not qualify; the module admits only what serves learning to notice how
your beliefs were fixed and how to test them.

## Rubric (observable criteria)

1. The doubt-belief-inquiry explanation names all three contrasts and
   the definition of inquiry, each in the learner's own words.
   Strong: "Doubt feels uneasy and drives us to struggle out of it;
   belief feels calm and settles us into habits that shape action
   when the occasion arises; beliefs guide desires and actions while
   doubt stimulates inquiry instead; and inquiry is the struggle the
   irritation of doubt causes, aimed at settling opinion."
   Rejection: "Doubt is when
   you are unsure" (everyday gloss, no contrast, no inquiry
   definition), or a correct definition of inquiry with none of the
   contrasts.
2. Each of the four methods is named with how it settles beliefs and
   Peirce's criticism or limit of it, and the quoted wording is
   Peirce's. Strong: "A priori: settles beliefs by what is agreeable
   to reason, what we find ourselves inclined to believe; Peirce's
   limit: inquiry becomes the development of taste, always more or
   less a matter of fashion, so metaphysicians never reach fixed
   agreement." Rejection: "Authority means believing what the
   government says" (one institution substituted for the method), a
   table with settling mechanisms but no criticisms, or wording
   attributed to Peirce that the essay does not contain.
3. The diagnosis is real (the learner's own belief), applies all
   four methods to it, distinguishes which method plausibly fixed it
   in the learner's actual history from how the others would have
   held it, and the science test names bounded observations, a result
   that would revise the belief, and data that exists whether or not
   the belief is true. Strong: for "I can't write", the diagnosis
   names tenacity, authority, a priori and science in turn, says
   which plausibly fixed this belief and why, and the science test
   names pieces of writing delivered on dated occasions over a
   stated period, entered at the time, with the count of delivered
   pieces that would revise the belief. Rejection: a test whose data
   does not match the claim ("I'll notice if I feel like a writer"
   reports a feeling, and the claim is about writing performance), a
   diagnosis that applies fewer than four methods, or a science test
   that only works if the belief is already true.
4. The position statement states Peirce's choice and takes a position
   with a reason. Strong: "He asks whether I want my opinions to
   coincide with the fact, given that the first three methods have no
   reason to deliver that; I choose the outside check because my
   settled story about my colleague has run our work for two years
   and I want the record, whatever it says." Rejection: "I agree with
   Peirce" (no choice stated, no reason), or a statement of the
   choice with no position taken.
