# m5-capstone: The Method on One Live Belief (deg-wol-pragmatism)

Fifth and last module of A Walk of Life: Pragmatism. The first four
modules gave you Peirce's account of how beliefs settle, James's
account of truth and genuine options, and Dewey's account of inquiry,
deliberation and shared problems. This one hands you the whole toolkit
and one job: take one live belief or decision of your own, run the
tools that fit it, and write a synthesis that defends where you land,
against the strongest objection to it. The walk's promise is on the
table here: you finish able to alleviate a real uncertainty in your
own life with a method you can name, show and repeat. Source
verification evidence is in ledgers/deg-wol-pragmatism.md. STATUS:
DRAFT, NOT CLEARED; awaits supervisor module-QC and advisor review.

## Level and placement

L4 Synthesis. Prerequisites (the m4 exits): you can state Dewey's
account of where thinking starts, run a deliberation rehearsal, appraise
a case separating what you observed from what you assumed, compare the
Peirce and Dewey selections on where inquiry starts, and teach the
pattern back to the person you disagree with. This module's exit is
the walk's exit: you can design, execute and self-assess an integrative
capstone on your own material.

## Outcomes

1. Frame one live belief or decision of your own as a bounded
   question, name the evidence you actually have, and name which tools
   from the walk fit it and which fail their own tests on it.
2. Apply the selected tools to your case, showing the work: the
   forked-road question, the maxim where a conception's meaning is in
   dispute, the genuine-option tests where a decision is live, forced
   and momentous, the rehearsal where action is blocked, the
   consultation where others are affected.
3. Write the synthesis: your position (which may be qualified
   commitment, suspension or revision), the strongest objection to
   it, your answer to that objection, and what would change your
   judgment.
4. Self-assess your synthesis against the rubric, naming the passage
   that meets each criterion, and revise it once in response.

Every outcome is taught, practised and assessed below; the alignment
map at the end shows where.

## Instruction (the sequence a learner follows)

### Step 1: frame your case (Outcome 1)

Choose one live belief or decision, something you actually face this
year: a career move, a relationship question, a commitment to a practice,
a money decision, a place to live. Write the proposal, 150 words or
less: the belief or decision stated as a question, the evidence you
have now, and the tools you expect to use. Check the tool fit before
you commit to it:

- The maxim fits where the dispute turns on what a conception means
  ("support myself", "good enough", "ready"). It asks: "Consider what
  effects, which might conceivably have practical bearings, we
  conceive the object of our conception to have. Then, our conception
  of these effects is the whole of our conception of the object"
  (Peirce, [How to Make Our Ideas
  Clear](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/How_to_Make_Our_Ideas_Clear),
  the m1 assignment). If your question is about facts, the maxim is
  the wrong tool and evidence work is the right one.
- The forked road fits where a real perplexity blocks you: thinking
  "begins in what may fairly enough be called a forked-road
  situation" (Dewey, [How We
  Think](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/37423/37423-h/37423-h.htm),
  the m4 Reading A). If nothing actually blocks you, you have a
  preference, not an inquiry.
- The genuine-option tests fit where a decision must be made on
  incomplete evidence and both branches are live (m3). If the
  evidence can settle it, or the option is avoidable, the tests do
  not apply and you should gather evidence instead.
- The rehearsal and consultation fit where action is blocked by a
  conflict of habits, or where others share the consequences (m4).

Practice (Outcome 1): the proposal, with a one-line reason for each
tool you kept and each you dropped.

### Step 2: the demonstration, then your run (Outcome 2)

The tools run once on a stipulated case, so you can see the shape
before you produce yours. The stipulated belief: "I can support
myself with freelance work within a year." Watch what each tool does,
and note that not all of them are needed:

1. The forked-road question: the perplexity is whether to leave
   salaried work. The question is not "is freelancing good" but
   "what would have to be true of my next twelve months for this
   belief to be working". That is a question a record can answer.
2. The maxim on "support myself": the effects are specific (rent
   paid, food bought, no new debt, at what monthly figure, for how
   many consecutive months). Until the figure is named, "support
   myself" means nothing checkable, and the belief cannot succeed
   or fail.
3. The genuine-option tests: leaving now is live but not forced
   (staying is a standing place) and not momentous (the market for
   this work recurs), so the passional permission does not apply;
   the decision belongs to the evidence. This is a worked failed
   application, and failing the tests is a result: it tells you the
   question is not the kind James's permission is for.
4. The record: three months of invoices is the verifying. James's
   constraint runs the case: "True ideas are those that we can
   assimilate, validate, corroborate and verify. False ideas are
   those that we can not" (Lecture VI of [Pragmatism: A New Name for
   Some Old Ways of
   Thinking](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Pragmatism:_A_New_Name_for_Some_Old_Ways_of_Thinking/Lecture_VI),
   the m3 Reading B3). If the months boil over, "Experience, as we
   know, has ways of boiling over, and making us correct our present
   formulas" (same lecture), and the belief is false on its own
   terms, at the named figure.

Now your run: apply the tools you kept to your own case, and write
the working notes (not the synthesis yet). The notes are not graded
on polish; they are graded on the tool work being visible. Deliverable
b in Step 3 is these notes, cleaned up.

### Step 3: the synthesis and the objection (Outcome 3)

Write the synthesis, 800 to 1,200 words, in four movements:

1. The case: the question, the evidence, the tools and why these.
2. The work: the tool applications from Step 2, condensed; show the
   maxim's effects where you used it, the tests' verdicts where you
   ran them, the rehearsal's rebuffs, the consultation's findings.
3. The position: where you land, in one of three honest forms. A
   qualified commitment (I act on this, and here is the revision
   trigger), a suspension (the evidence does not settle it and the
   option is not genuine, so I wait and gather), or a revision (the
   work changed my belief; here is what changed it).
4. The objection: the strongest objection to your position, in its
   strongest form, and your answer. Do not pick a weak objection. If
   your position is a commitment, the natural objection is that the
   method only confirms what you wanted to believe; your answer must
   name the check that would catch that (the record, the boil-over,
   the other party's evidence). If your position is a suspension, the
   natural objection is that waiting is itself a decision with
   costs; your answer must name what waiting buys and what it costs.

Deliverables (self-assessed with the rubric before you call any of
them done; the self-assessment step is part of each):
a. The proposal (Outcome 1): Step 1's piece, 150 words or less.
b. The tool work (Outcome 2): Step 2's notes on your own case,
   300 to 500 words.
c. The synthesis (Outcome 3): 800 to 1,200 words, the four
   movements above, ending with what would change your judgment.

### Step 4: self-assess and revise once (Outcome 4)

Read your synthesis against each rubric criterion below. For each
criterion, write one line naming the passage that meets it (quote
yourself, a few words) or stating that no passage does. Then revise
the synthesis once, in response to what the self-assessment found.
Deliverable d is the self-assessment lines and the revision, with a
short note on what changed and why. The revision is part of the
capstone; a synthesis submitted without its self-assessment is
incomplete.

### The bounded domain, in plain terms

This module covers the integrative application of the walk's tools
to one live belief or decision of your own, the written defence of
your position against its strongest objection, and the self-assessed
revision. It does not cover new pragmatist material, the scholarly
debates around Peirce, James or Dewey, or any practice regimen. The
Misak lecture from m3 stays optional here; it is not required
teaching for the capstone. Material that merely fits the topic does
not qualify; the module admits only what serves your own synthesis.

## Rubric (observable criteria)

1. The proposal frames a live belief or decision as a bounded
   question with named evidence and named tools. Strong: "Question:
   do I take the regional job or stay? Evidence: the offer letter,
   two years of city expenses, one conversation with someone who
   made the move. Tools: the tests (the deadline makes it forced),
   the maxim on 'better life'; the maxim may fail if 'better' names
   nothing checkable. Dropped: the consultation, no one else is
   affected." Rejection: a topic with no question, or a tool list
   with no fit reasons.
2. The tool work shows each tool's verdict on the case, including
   failed applications. Strong: the maxim run on "support myself"
   down to a monthly figure and a month count; the tests run with
   the momentous verdict honestly reported as not met; the
   rehearsal's rebuff named. Rejection: tool names with no work, or
   every tool applied with no verdicts.
3. The synthesis takes a position in one of the three honest forms,
   answers its strongest objection, and states what would change
   the judgment. Strong: a qualified commitment with a dated
   revision trigger; the objection stated in the skeptic's own
   voice; the answer naming the check that would catch
   self-deception. Rejection: a position with no objection, an
   objection answered by assertion, or a "whatever works for me"
   close with no check named.
4. The self-assessment names a passage for each criterion and the
   revision responds to it. Strong: four lines quoting the
   synthesis, one revision that changes the objection answer, and a
   note on why. Rejection: "all criteria met", or a revision that
   changes nothing.
