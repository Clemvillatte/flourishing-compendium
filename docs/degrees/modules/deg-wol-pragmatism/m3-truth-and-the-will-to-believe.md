# m3-truth-and-the-will-to-believe: James on Truth and Genuine Options (deg-wol-pragmatism)

Third module of A Walk of Life: Pragmatism. The second module gave you
Peirce's account of how beliefs settle and the four methods. This one
follows the method into William James: what his instrumental view of
theories and truth amounts to, and when our passional nature may
lawfully decide a belief the intellect cannot settle. The question for
a walk of life is direct: which of your own choices are genuine
options in James's sense, and what does his view of truth let you do
with them? Source verification evidence is in
ledgers/deg-wol-pragmatism.md. STATUS: QC PASS (supervisor module-QC 1026e, 2026-10-26, review reviews/deg-wol-pragmatism-m3-supervisor-1026e.md).

## Level and placement

L2 Guided application. Prerequisites (the m2 exits): you can explain
doubt, belief and inquiry, name the four methods with Peirce's
criticisms, diagnose one of your own beliefs under all four methods,
and state the choice Peirce puts to the reader. The exit criteria
below are the entry competencies of m4 (Dewey on inquiry, ethics and
democracy).

## Outcomes

1. Explain James's method and his account of truth in your own words,
   including the qualifier that keeps the view from collapsing into
   pleasant-belief.
2. Define a genuine option (live, forced, momentous) and apply the
   test to one decision of your own, stating whether the passional
   permission applies.
3. State the strongest objection to James's view of truth (it makes
   truth whatever is pleasant to believe) and the constraint his text
   builds against it, and take your own position.
4. From the Misak lecture, state what Frank Ramsey takes from Peirce
   and what he adds, including his objection to reading James as
   making a belief true simply because it is useful to the believer.

Every outcome is taught, practised and assessed below; the alignment map
at the end shows where.

## Instruction (the sequence a learner follows)

### Step 1: genuine options (Outcome 2)

Read Reading A from [The Will to
Believe](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Will_to_Believe) (public
domain, Wikisource; James 1896): the contiguous passage that begins
"They may be (a) living or dead; (b) forced or avoidable; (c)
momentous or trivial" and ends with the thesis passage "decide an
option between propositions, whenever it is a genuine option that
cannot by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds" (2,532 words
measured on normalized raw wikitext of the page: templates, tags, wiki
quotes, entities and markup residue removed, whitespace collapsed; the
span was checked to contain no templates or entities after
normalization). Take notes on the
three tests and the thesis sentence.

The three tests, in his words: a "living option is one in which both
hypotheses are live ones"; a forced option leaves "no standing place
outside of the alternative" ("Either accept this truth or go without
it"); a momentous option is "your only similar
opportunity", a unique opportunity where "He who refuses to embrace a
unique opportunity loses the prize as surely as if he tried and
failed"; the reverse side is his too: the option is trivial "when the
opportunity is not unique, when the stake is insignificant, or when
the decision is reversible if it later prove unwise". An option is
genuine
"when it is of the forced, living and momentous kind". The thesis:
"Our passional nature must, and lawfully may, decide an option between
propositions, whenever it is a genuine option that cannot by its
nature be decided on intellectual grounds" ([The Will to
Believe](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Will_to_Believe)). Note the
permission's boundaries before the worked example: it
applies only where the option is genuine AND the intellect cannot
settle it. Where evidence decides, or the option is avoidable or
trivial, the permission does not apply.

Retrieval activity (Outcome 2, closed book): from memory, write the
three tests and the thesis, each in one sentence, and mark which part
of the thesis each test protects.

### Step 2: the method and the truth (Outcome 1)

Read Reading B1 from [Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of
Thinking, Lecture II: "What Pragmatism
Means"](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Pragmatism:_A_New_Name_for_Some_Old_Ways_of_Thinking/Lecture_II)
(James 1907, public domain): the
contiguous passage that begins "To take in the importance of Peirce's
principle" and ends "make nature over again by their aid" (952 words
measured on the page's rendered text; the page's zero-width-space
marks were inspected one by one: in this span they sit between
complete words and are read as separators, not joins). Then read Reading B2 from the
same lecture: the passage that begins "I am well aware how odd it must
seem" and ends "our duty would be to shun truth, rather" (264 words
measured on the same rendered text, page-split words joined and
verified joined). Finally read Reading B3 from
[Lecture VI, "Pragmatism's Conception of
Truth"](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Pragmatism:_A_New_Name_for_Some_Old_Ways_of_Thinking/Lecture_VI)
of the same book: the four selections quoted in full in the Step 2
teaching below (157 words measured on the printed selections; the
selections are noncontiguous passages of the lecture), which carry the
fuller account Lecture II explicitly defers ("To answer this
difficulty fully is impossible at this stage of my account... I can
not discuss with detail until my sixth lecture").

The method, in his words: pragmatism is "to interpret each notion by
tracing its respective practical consequences", and "Theories thus
become instruments, not answers to enigmas, in which we can rest. We
don't lie back upon them, we move forward, and, on occasion, make
nature over again by their aid." The truth passage carries the
qualifier you will need for the objection work: "truth is one species
of good", and "The true is the name of whatever proves itself to be
good in the way of belief, and good, too, for definite, assignable
reasons" (Lecture II, [Pragmatism: A New Name for Some Old Ways of
Thinking](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Pragmatism:_A_New_Name_for_Some_Old_Ways_of_Thinking)).
[Lecture VI](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Pragmatism:_A_New_Name_for_Some_Old_Ways_of_Thinking/Lecture_VI)
supplies what that qualifier comes to. First, the process:
"The truth of an idea is not a stagnant property inherent in it.
Truth happens to an idea. It becomes true, is made true by events",
and "True ideas are those that we can assimilate, validate, corroborate
and verify. False ideas are those that we can not." Second, the
long-run constraint: "'The true,' to put it very briefly, is only the
expedient in the way of our thinking... expedient in the long run and
on the whole of course; for what meets expediently all the experience
in sight won't necessarily meet all farther experiences equally
satisfactorily. Experience, as we know, has ways of boiling over, and
making us correct our present formulas." Third, his own name for the
objection you will run: "A favorite formula for describing Mr.
Schiller's doctrines and mine is that we are persons who think that by
saying whatever you find it pleasant to say and calling it truth you
fulfil every pragmatistic requirement. I leave it to you to judge
whether this be not an impudent slander." Hold the two lectures
together: an idea is true only if believing it works, and the working
is a verifiable process that experience can boil over.

Practice (Outcome 1): write two or three sentences stating James's
method and account of truth in your own words, including the qualifier.
Check against the quoted passages.

### Step 3: worked example with fading (Outcomes 2 and 3)

The genuine-option test applied to one decision, demonstrated with the
guidance faded. The stipulated cases are constructed for teaching; test
them against your own decisions rather than take them as findings.

The option, framed as competing propositions (the thesis governs
options between propositions, so the diagnosis states them): "I can
complete this two-year course and move into that line of work" against
its denial (stipulated). Acting (enrolling) follows the believing; the
tests run on the propositions.

1. Fully worked (demonstrated). Live: both hypotheses appeal as real
   possibilities; you can genuinely imagine completing and genuinely
   imagine the denial. Forced: not established for the propositions;
   suspension of belief remains a standing place between them, and
   drifting is itself a way of suspending. Momentous: the test
   asks what is irreversibly lost by waiting, and it must be answered
   honestly. The course repeating every two years establishes delay,
   not loss, so on the stipulated facts alone this test is not yet
   met; it is met only if something in the situation is non-repeatable
   (a funding window that does not reopen, an age-bound requirement),
   and a strong application names that thing or reports that the test
   fails. Genuine: on the stated facts, NOT ESTABLISHED. Live: yes.
   Forced: not established (an enrolment deadline can force an
   action without forcing the belief). Momentous: not established
   (the cycle establishes delay, not loss). This is a worked failed
   application, and that is
   the point: on these facts the diagnosis cannot show a genuine
   option, so the passional permission is not shown to apply. A real
   application succeeds or fails on its own facts: it either
   supplies the missing conditions (a forced frame with no standing
   place, a non-repeatable stake, and a question the evidence cannot
   settle by its nature) or reports, as this one does, that the test
   fails and the decision belongs to the evidence.
2. Steps named (partially worked). Take the stipulated decision:
   whether to tell a friend a hard truth about their drinking (both
   friendship and honesty feel live). Write the three tests, each with
   a one-line verdict, and the final statement of whether the passional
   permission applies and why. Feedback to check against once you have
   written yours: a strong application names, for each test, the
   concrete feature of the decision that satisfies or fails it, and
   the permission statement names what the intellect can and cannot
   settle here. Then run the objection-and-constraint pattern once,
   guided: state the "pleasant to say" formula as it would apply to
   this decision, and name the verification that would boil over
   against it.
3. Independent (the graded deliverable). Take one live decision of
   your own, something you actually face. Write the full genuine-option
   application and the permission statement. Deliverable b in Step 4 is
   this piece, written up cleanly.

The objection work (Outcome 3), demonstrated on the stipulated study
decision: the objection is James's own "favorite formula", that
pragmatists think "by saying whatever you find it pleasant to say and
calling it truth you fulfil every pragmatistic requirement"; the
constraint his Lecture VI supplies is the verification process and the
long run: "True ideas are those that we can assimilate, validate,
corroborate and verify. False ideas are those that we can not", and
"experience... has ways of boiling over, and making us correct our
present formulas", so a belief that works only while it goes untested
(for example, that the course guarantees a job) is, on the view's own
terms, false the moment the verifying runs against it. Your task in
the graded work is to run that objection-and-constraint pattern on the
objection you find strongest, and take your own position.

### The bounded domain, in plain terms

This module covers James's instrumental method, his account of truth
with its qualifier, and the genuine-option permission, read in his own
texts, with Misak's lecture carrying the Peirce-James-Ramsey line. It
does not cover James's psychology (the psychdim walk owns the research
on emotion and habit), his religious writings beyond the passages
assigned, or the later analytic debate about Ramsey's redundancy
theory. Material that merely fits the topic does not qualify; the
module admits only what serves learning to apply the genuine-option
test and to state the objection-and-constraint pattern on truth.

## Rubric (observable criteria)

1. The explanation states the method, the instrumental view of
   theories, and the truth account with the qualifier. Strong: "James
   reads a notion by its practical consequences; theories are
   instruments we move forward with; an idea is true when believing it
   proves good in the way of belief for definite, assignable reasons,
   so the working has to be checkable, which is what keeps the view
   from pleasant-belief." Rejection: "James said truth is whatever
   works for you" (no method, no qualifier), or the method without the
   truth account.
2. The genuine-option application runs all three tests with concrete
   verdicts and states the permission question. Strong: for the
   friend-and-drinking decision, each test gets the feature that
   satisfies or fails it, and the permission statement names what the
   intellect can settle here and what it cannot. Rejection: "it felt
   like a big decision so it's momentous" (feeling substituted for the
   test), or three tests with no permission statement. Model response
   for the strong side (about 70 words): "Live: both hypotheses are
   live ones; I can genuinely imagine finishing and genuinely imagine
   the denial. Forced: not established; suspension of belief remains
   a standing place, and the deadline forces an action, not the
   belief. Momentous: the funding window closes this year and does
   not reopen;
   refusing loses it as surely as failing. Permission: not
   established; the deadline forces an action, and the prospectus and
   my record settle most of what the belief is about, so the
   application says the decision belongs to the evidence."
3. The objection-and-constraint piece states a real objection and runs
   the text's constraint against it, ending in a position. Strong: the
   objection is stated in its strongest form, the constraint (definite,
   assignable reasons) is applied to it with an example, and the
   position says where the learner stands and why. Rejection: an
   objection stated and dismissed by assertion, or a position with no
   objection run. Model response for the strong side (about 70 words):
   "Objection: on James's view, whatever I find pleasant to say I may
   call truth. Constraint from Lecture VI: true ideas are those we can
   assimilate, validate, corroborate and verify, and experience has
   ways of boiling over and making us correct our present formulas; a
   pleasant belief that boils over fails the view's own test.
   Position: I accept the constraint, and I hold it answers the
   slander wherever evidence can reach, which is further than the
   objection admits."
4. The Ramsey sentences name what Ramsey takes from Peirce and what he
   adds, as the lecture presents it. Strong: "Ramsey takes Peirce's
   belief-as-habit and adds the test that the belief that P will be
   useful only if P, which is his answer to reading James as making a
   belief's truth rest on its usefulness to the believer; he links
   truth with action without reducing truth to it." Rejection:
   "Ramsey was a genius who died young" (biography with no content), or
   sentences that could describe any philosopher.
