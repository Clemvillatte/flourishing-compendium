## Walk context

The walk's school map turns to Zen, the Japanese inheritance of the
Chan tradition. The two lectures assigned here were given at The
Buddhist Society in London in its 100th-anniversary lecture series on
Japanese Buddhism, by a scholar-monk who lives the tradition he
teaches. For this walk, Zen is a further answer to the suffering
question the learner has been carrying since b2, with a distinctive
meditation culture the lectures examine rather than prescribe.

## Objectives

1. Zen's answer to the question of suffering (Lecture 1).
2. Zen meditation: the Rinzai and Soto approaches as the lecture
   frames them through koan practice and shikantaza (Lecture 3).
3. The place of meditation in Buddhism: the lecture's claim, the
evidence the lecturer gives for it, and a separate personal
reflection on how it lands against your own walk so far.

## Outcomes

1. State the lecture's account of how the Western image of Zen was
   shaped (the Zen-master figure, Rinzai and Hakuin named in the
   series description), and what the lecture says about suffering as
   the motivation for practice.
2. Distinguish the Rinzai and Soto approaches to meditation as the
   lecture frames them — koan practice (which the lecturer ties to
   Rinzai monks) and shikantaza (which the lecturer calls the Soto
   practice) — in two lines each, and state which you would try first
   and why.
3. State the lecture's claim about the place of meditation in Buddhism
   (described as less extensive than one might expect), the evidence
   the lecturer gives for it, and, separately, one paragraph of
   personal reflection on how that claim lands against your own walk
   so far (b4's training plan, the b1 metta sittings). The
   reflection is a reflection, and the claim is assessed through the
   lecturer's evidence; your sitting log supports the reflection, it
   does not prove the historical point.
4. Sit one logged sitting in your existing b4 form, and log one
   observation on what the lecture's account of the Soto register
   suggests about that sitting (what "just sitting" would mean for
   the form you already practise). The module does not teach a new
   meditation technique; no guided Soto sitting instruction is
   attached to a verified source, so none is assigned.

## Instruction sequence

### Step 1 — The suffering question, Zen's way (~1 h 28 min, video, whole lecture, two viewing stops)

**Thomas Yuhō Kirchner, "Buddhism, Zen, and the Question of Suffering",
Lecture 1 of Part II (Zen Buddhism) of The Buddhist Society's
100th-anniversary memorial lecture series on Japanese Buddhism,
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UVXpdAavGgQ, 1:28:04** (held in person
at The Buddhist Society and online, 27 June 2024; series supported by
the Numata Foundation; The Buddhist Society channel). The series
description notes that the Western image of Zen has been shaped by the
figure of the Zen master, with Rinzai and Hakuin named.

The speaker: born Maryland, USA, 1949; studied at Waseda University;
lay monk at Shōfuku-ji under Yamada Mumon Roshi; ordained 1974; seven
years of monastic training at Kenchō-ji (Kamakura) and Kennin-ji
(Kyoto); MA in Buddhist Studies, Otani University; positions at the
Nanzan Institute for Religion and Culture and the International
Research Institute for Zen Buddhism; translator of The Record of
Linji, Entangling Vines and Dialogues in a Dream; resides at Tenryū-ji,
Kyoto (credentials opened on the Society's series page).

Watch in two stops (viewing stops, not content claims, since the
page prints no chapter list): 00:00:00-00:44:00 and 00:44:00-01:28:04. Activity:
one line per stop on the thread of the argument.

### Step 2 — Zen meditation, examined (~1 h 40 min, video, whole lecture, two viewing stops)

**Thomas Yuhō Kirchner, "Thoughts on Meditation", Lecture 3 of the same
series, https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=jPxuvitdsRI, 1:40:17** (3 July
2024, same series and channel). The lecture discusses Zen meditation
and the place of meditation in Buddhism, which it describes as less
extensive than one might expect (in Kirchner's account most
Buddhists are not interested in meditating; the Pure Land traditions
offer devotional practice, and the Shingon and Tendai schools focus
far more on esoteric ritual — said in the talk's opening). Its
treatment of the Rinzai and Soto approaches is framed through
individual teachers' views of koan practice (which the lecturer ties
to Rinzai monks) and zazen and shikantaza (which the lecturer calls
the Soto practice) — the talk's own examples are Yamada Mumon Roshi,
Harada Shodo Roshi, and a Rinzai master who spent decades as a Soto
monk — rather than a systematic school-by-school comparison, so the
two-lines-each activity draws the distinction from those examples.

Watch in two stops (viewing stops, not content claims, since the page
prints no chapter list): 00:00:00-00:50:00 and 00:50:00-01:40:17. Activity: after
the Rinzai-Soto discussion, write the two approaches in two lines each,
in your own words.

### Step 3 — One sitting, viewed through the lecture

Sit ten minutes in your existing b4 form, then log one observation on
what the lecture's account of the Soto register suggests about that
sitting (what "just sitting" would mean for the form you already
practise). The lecture is the lens for the observation; it is not a
substitute for guided instruction, and the module claims none.

## Practice

The Step 3 sitting plus your continuing b4 habit (one more logged
sitting this week, your choice of form). The comparison in outcome 3
is the module's analysis: the lecturer's evidence carries the claim,
your log carries the reflection.

## Assessment

1. The two-lines-each Rinzai/Soto statement (outcome 2).
2. The claim-and-reflection piece (outcome 3, ~200 words): the
   lecture's claim stated with the lecturer's evidence, then a
   separate reflection paragraph citing two of your own log entries
   as reflection, not as proof of the historical point.
3. The Zen-informed sitting log line (outcome 4).
4. A 200-word answer to outcome 1's suffering question as you now
   hold it (after the lecture, in your own terms).

Rubric (observable):

| criterion | exemplar of strong work | rejection example |
|---|---|---|
| Rinzai/Soto distinction | two lines each, distinguishable by what the meditator does | "Rinzai is intense, Soto is calm" with no practice content |
| claim and reflection | the lecture's claim quoted or paraphrased with the lecturer's evidence stated, then a separate reflection paragraph naming two of your own log entries | the claim restated with no evidence, or a log entry presented as proof of the historical point |
| Zen-informed observation | one b4-format sitting line with one observation on what the Soto-register account suggests about it | the sitting skipped, or logged with no observation |
| suffering answer | the learner's own current answer, changed or confirmed by the lecture, stated in their terms | the lecture's topic list |

Self-assessment: one line on which lecture changed your picture of
meditation more, and why. That line is part of the module.

## Boundaries and passing mentions

Zen's doctrinal furniture (Buddha-nature, enlightenment claims) appears
as the tradition's own frame, taught at the level the lectures teach
it; the walk's exclusion rule governs any cosmological extension. The
koan tradition is named via Rinzai's approach but not taught as
practice in this module (no verified guided koan source is attached).
No rebirth-dependent content is assigned.
