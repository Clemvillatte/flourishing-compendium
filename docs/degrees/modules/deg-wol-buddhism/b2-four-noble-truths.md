## Walk context

The Buddha's first teaching frames the whole walk: suffering, where it
comes from, the fact that it can end, and the path of practice that ends
it. The walk treats the four truths as four tasks, the way the discourse
itself assigns them: something to understand, something to give up,
something to realize, something to develop. The learner does not memorize
a doctrine here; they run the four tasks on one real difficulty in their
own life.

## Objectives

1. The four noble truths and their four tasks, from the discourse itself.
2. Dukkha (suffering, unsatisfactoriness) examined in a guided scholarly
   lecture, with its limits marked.
3. Craving (tanha) distinguished from desire-to-act (chanda), so the
   second truth never becomes a command to stop caring.
4. The rebirth frame of the discourse placed honestly: passing mention,
   not cosmology.

## Outcomes

1. State the four truths and the task the discourse assigns to each, in
   your own words.
2. Run the four tasks on one concrete difficulty in your life: name the
   suffering as you experience it, trace the craving that feeds it,
   describe what its ending would actually be, and list three path
   factors (from the discourse's path list) you could develop toward
   that ending.
3. Distinguish craving from desire-to-act using Harvey's glossary entry,
   and give one example of a wholesome desire (chanda) in your own case
   work from outcome 2.
4. State where the discourse's frame assumes rebirth, and explain in two
   sentences why the walk marks that as a passing mention rather than
   teaching it as cosmology.

## Instruction sequence

### Step 1 — The discourse itself (~20 min, reading, with re-reading)

**SN 56.11, Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, "Setting the Wheel of Dhamma in
Motion," Thanissaro Bhikkhu translation on Access to Insight** (verified
receipt B-T01; the page states the CC BY-NC 4.0 license and lists the
Sujato and Bodhi alternate translations). Read the whole discourse. Mark
four places: each truth stated, and the task attached to it ("to be
comprehended... to be abandoned... to be realized... to be developed").

The discourse's religious frame stays visible on purpose: its account of
craving reaches for future lives, and its closing narrative includes
divine beings. The walk does not teach these as cosmology; you are asked
only to notice them and place them in the discourse's traditional frame.
This is the passing mention the scope promised, and outcome 4 asks you to
write it plainly.

### Step 2 — Dukkha examined (~1 h 21 min, video, whole teaching)

**Georges Dreyfus, session 1 of a three-session Four Noble Truths series
(Shantideva Center, 2024-04-23), 1:20:35.** Credentials: Jackson
Professor of Religion at Williams College (verified in the Williams
religion catalogue), former monk and scholar of Tibetan Buddhism.

Two boundaries are marked for you before you press play. First, this
session covers the first truth, dukkha, only; the origin of suffering
is the next session and the remaining truths the third, as Dreyfus says
himself at 00:17-00:40 and 1:20:13 of the opened transcript. The other
three truths are not taught in this video; the discourse in Step 1 is
where all four are supplied together. Second, Dreyfus uses a
"homeodynamic system" analogy around 09:55, which he clarifies at 1:01:04
is his own explanatory vocabulary, not a Buddhist term; and his
physiological illustration at 11:15-11:21 makes survival-time claims that
this module does not endorse as science. Enjoy the analogy as a scholar's
explanation, and keep the translated discourse, the scholar's analogy and
empirical science separate in your notes.

### Step 3 — Craving is not every desire (~10 min, reading)

**Peter Harvey's "Craving" glossary entry** (his SN 56.11 translation and
commentary on Access to Insight). Harvey distinguishes taṇhā, demanding
craving, from chanda, which can include the wholesome desire to act. The
second truth targets the first, not the second. The learner who takes
"end craving" as "stop wanting anything" has misread the task; the
worksheet asks you to show you have not.

### Step 4 — Continue the sitting practice

One 10-minute metta sitting, on the same pattern as b1. Spaced practice
is how the walk builds the sitting habit; the cmp practicum will extend
it.

## Practice

**The four-tasks worksheet** (about 45 min): take one difficulty you
actually face this month. For each truth, one paragraph:
- The dukkha: how this difficulty is suffering for you, in plain words.
- The tanha: what demanding craving feeds it, and one chanda (wholesome
  desire to act) you hold in the same situation, named as such.
- The nirodha: what the ending of that craving would concretely be (not
  the problem vanishing; the craving's grip releasing).
- The magga: three path factors from the discourse's list (right view,
  intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness,
  concentration) you could develop here, with one action each.

## Assessment

The worksheet itself is the assessed deliverable, plus the two-sentence
rebirth-frame statement (outcome 4) and one sentence on whether the
Dreyfus analogy helped or hindered your understanding of dukkha.

Rubric (observable):

| criterion | exemplar of strong work | rejection example |
|---|---|---|
| four tasks stated correctly | each truth paired with its task in the learner's own words | truths recited with no tasks, or tasks swapped |
| case work is real and specific | names an actual difficulty with a concrete craving and a named chanda | a hypothetical with no craving traced |
| ending described as cessation of craving | describes the grip releasing, distinguishes it from the problem disappearing | "I will stop suffering" with no mechanism |
| path factors chosen with reasons | three factors with one action each, matched to the case | the full eight listed with no actions |
| rebirth frame placed | two sentences naming where the discourse assumes rebirth and why the walk marks it as passing mention | silence, or a defense or refutation of rebirth |

Self-assessment: one line on which of the four tasks you found hardest
to run on your case, and why. That line is part of the module.

## Boundaries and passing mentions

The rebirth-dependent frame of SN 56.11 is the walk's promised passing
mention: marked, placed, not taught as cosmology, and not argued for or
against. Dreyfus's analogy and physiology are marked as his vocabulary
and unverified claims respectively. Abhidharma scholastics and tantric
ritual remain excluded.

## What this module does not do

It does not compare the schools' readings of the path (b3-b5), and it
does not teach meditation technique beyond continuing the metta sitting
(the cmp practicum owns that). The three-truths remainder of Dreyfus's
series is deliberately not assigned: the discourse itself supplies all
four truths, and the series' later sessions are not verified.
