# m1-four-truths: The Buddha's First Teaching and the Four Tasks (deg-wol-buddhism)

Welcome to the first module of A Walk of Life: Buddhism. This degree walks
the Buddhist path the way living communities walk it: ethics first,
practice in the body, and doctrine as a map you check against your own
experience. This module gives you the frame the whole degree rests on, the
teaching the tradition counts as the Buddha's first.

A note before you start. Buddhist teaching uses the word "suffering" for
something wider than agony: every ache of wanting things to be other than
they are, every low-grade restlessness, every ordinary disappointment.
Keep that wide meaning in mind, because the whole path starts from
looking at it honestly.

## Level and placement

- Level: L1 (introduction, no prior knowledge assumed).
- Placement: first module of the degree. The ethics material in the
  assigned video segment is the ethics-first entry point; m2 develops
  the ethical core (precepts and the four immeasurables) in depth.
- Prerequisites: none.
- Time: about 3 hours 37 minutes of labelled work, including 45 minutes
  of practice spread across three days (see Workload arithmetic).

## Outcomes

By the end of this module you can:

1. State the four noble truths in the wording of the first discourse
   (SN 56.11) and name the task attached to each: suffering is to be
   understood, its cause is to be given up, its cessation is to be
   realized, and the path to that cessation is to be developed.
2. Name the eight factors of the noble path as the first discourse gives
   them, and explain the middle way: what the two extremes are and why
   the discourse calls both of them ignoble and pointless. (The meanings
   of the individual factors are taught in m2 and m3.)
3. Take one difficulty, from your own life or a hypothetical case, and
   map its parts: the situation, your response, any craving you can
   identify in that response, and an action that fits. Use Ajahn Brahm's
   itch-and-scratch teaching as one lens, while allowing that some
   difficulties are harms from outside you that call for protection and
   change rather than inner work.
4. Tell apart the canonical teaching (SN 56.11) from a living teacher's
   interpretation of it (Ajahn Brahm's happiness and unhappiness
   reframing), naming at least one thing each source does that the other
   does not.
5. State Koṇḍañña's insight from the close of the first discourse
   ("Everything that is liable to arise is liable to cease") and connect
   it to the frame of impermanence that runs through this degree.

## Instruction (the sequence a learner follows)

### Step 1: watch the ethics-first entry (Outcomes 3 and 4)

Watch [Ajahn Brahm, "What the Buddha Taught"](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=W6pGAvtBmCw) (a dhamma talk recorded on 21 November 2003, remastered and published by the Everyday Dhamma Network), from 25:46 to the end at 49:44. That is 24 minutes of viewing. The [Everyday Dhamma episode page](https://everydaydhamma.net/podcast/ajahn-brahm-podcast/episode/what-the-buddha-taught-ajahn-brahm) carries the talk's description and an AI-generated transcript you can follow along with (treat its wording as rough; it has errors).

- 25:46-31:32, ethics and tolerance. Listen for how Ajahn Brahm presents
  the ethics he attributes to the Buddha's advice to his son Rāhula:
  actions that hurt yourself or others point one way, actions that give
  happiness to yourself and others point the other. Listen also for his
  claim that guilt helps nobody, and his acknowledge, forgive, learn
  sequence.
- 31:32-37:33, making ethical decisions. Listen for the idea that, in
  Ajahn Brahm's presentation, Buddhism does not make your decisions for
  you; it helps you make them: will this help or hurt, myself or others?
- 37:33-49:44, the four truths. Listen for the passage where Ajahn Brahm
  reframes the four truths as a teaching about happiness and
  unhappiness, and for the image he uses near the end of the talk: when
  you blame other people for your suffering, you are scratching
  somewhere other than where it itches, and you end up with two itches.
  Where it hurts is where you scratch.

Scope note: the first half of this talk (0:00-25:46) works through rebirth
and kamma cosmology. This degree treats rebirth cosmology as passing
context only, so the module assigns the later segment. If those questions
matter to you, hold them; later modules and your own reading can take
them up.

While you watch, keep a pen handy and note the exact moment something
Ajahn Brahm says lands on a current difficulty of yours. That note
becomes the seed of your practice journal and your assessment.

### Step 2: read the first discourse with the four-tasks worksheet (Outcomes 1, 2 and 5)

Read the first discourse, ["Rolling Forth the Wheel of Dhamma" (SN 56.11)](https://suttacentral.net/sn56.11/en/sujato), in Bhikkhu Sujato's translation on SuttaCentral.

Read the whole page, translator's notes included. The notes are plain
explanations, and several of them will save you from the two most common
misreadings: that all desire causes suffering (the note at the second
truth explains that some desires lead out of suffering), and that
craving is a selective picker (the note explains it is a restless
searcher, "each and every place").

As you read, fill in a four-row worksheet. The discourse states each
truth three times: once as knowledge ("this is the noble truth of..."),
once as a task ("this noble truth should be..."), and once as completion
("has been..."). Your four rows:

| Truth | The task the discourse attaches | In my own words |
|---|---|---|
| Suffering | should be completely understood | |
| Origin (craving) | should be given up | |
| Cessation | should be realized | |
| The path | should be developed | |

Copy the task wording from the discourse into the middle column, then
close the page and write the right column from memory in your own words.

One wording point worth your attention: Sujato renders the eight path
factors as right view, right purpose, right speech, right action, right
livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right immersion. These
are his English renderings; when you quote the discourse, quote the
translation you read, and when you meet other renderings of the same
factors, recognize them as renderings.

### Step 3: the teaching in plain terms (Outcomes 1, 2 and 5)

The frame in three sentences: things hurt (suffering); the hurting is fed
by a wanting that keeps reaching for the next thing (craving); the
reaching can wind down (cessation); and there is a trainable way of
living that winds it down (the path). The four truths are less a creed
than a diagnosis and a treatment plan, and the discourse itself encodes
that: each truth comes with a task.

The middle way opens the discourse. The Buddha tells his first five
listeners that a person gone forth should cultivate neither indulgence
in sensual pleasures nor self-mortification, and he calls both extremes
ignoble and pointless. The middle way is then named, and it is simply
the eightfold path: a life of view, purpose, speech, action, livelihood,
effort, mindfulness and immersion, trained together. Notice the shape:
the path is a whole way of life, and it includes explicitly ethical
factors, with speech, action and livelihood among its eight. That is
why this degree walks ethics first.

The first truth lists what suffering includes: birth, aging, illness,
death, being coupled with the disliked, separation from the liked, not
getting what you wish for, and in brief the five grasping aggregates. The
list reads as a description of human life, and that is its point. The
second truth names craving with its three channels (sensual pleasure,
continued existence, nonexistence). The third truth names the cessation
of that very same craving, in the language of letting go: giving it
away, releasing it, not clinging. The fourth truth returns to the path.

The discourse closes with Koṇḍañña's realization: "Everything that is
liable to arise is liable to cease." Hold onto that line. It is the
whole Buddhist frame in miniature: whatever arises, including your
suffering and the craving that feeds it, is the kind of thing that
passes. Later modules turn that from a comfort into a practice.

Now compare your two sources. Ajahn Brahm's segment reframes the four
truths as a teaching about happiness and unhappiness: the cause of
happiness, the cause of unhappiness, and so on. He says outright that he
has switched them around and claims the right to do so because the
meaning is the same. SN 56.11 gives the canonical structure: the middle
way, the eight factors by name, the three rounds and twelve aspects, the
closing insight. The comparison is Outcome 4: the canonical text carries
the frame; the living teacher carries the warmth and the application.
Both are Buddhism as living communities hold it. Learning to hold them
together, without flattening either, is a skill you will use in every
module of this degree.

### Step 4: a worked example, then your practice (Outcome 3)

Here is a worked example of the mapping you will do yourself, using an
ordinary case. A driver cuts you off in traffic. The situation: a car
swerved in front of you and you had to brake hard. Your response: a
burst of anger, a flash of "people like that should not be on the
road", and twenty minutes of replaying it. The craving you can identify
in that response: the wanting things to go your way on the road, the
wanting other drivers to follow the script you keep in your head. The
action that fits: you can drive more defensively around this person, and
you can notice that the twenty minutes of replaying is where the
suffering actually lived, longer than the two seconds of danger. That is
the itch on your head. Nothing in this mapping says the other driver
was right, or that you should blame yourself: the situation, the
response, the craving and the action are all separate parts, and you
keep them separate.

Note the limit of this exercise honestly. In the canonical frame, the
third truth names the cessation of craving. What a journal entry can do
is show you an everyday easing: the anger that loosens when you stop
replaying the event. That easing is real and it is what practice works
with; it is a demonstration of the direction the discourse points, and
you are the one who observes it. It is not the realization the
discourse describes, and this module does not ask you to claim it.

For three days, 15 minutes per day, run the practice on your own material.

1. When something bothers you today (a rude message, a traffic jam, a
   worry loop, a conflict that needs addressing), pause.
2. Write down the four parts: the situation (what happened, in one
   sentence); your response (what you felt, said or did); any craving
   you can identify in the response (the wanting, resisting, blaming or
   rehearsing); and an action that fits (something protective, kind or
   useful, including actions that change the situation).
3. Some difficulties are mostly harm from outside you. If that is what
   you find, write that down too, and let the action be protection or
   change. The practice trains the movement of attention; it does not
   tell you everything is your fault.
4. End each entry with one sentence: where did the suffering actually
   live, and where were you scratching?

Keep the entries small and honest. If you would rather work with a
hypothetical case than your own material, do that; the skill is the
same.

### Step 5: assessment (all outcomes)

Deliverable A, the four-truths mapping (about 60 minutes, 400 to 600
words). Take one difficulty from your three journal entries, or your
hypothetical case, and map it onto the four truths. Name the suffering
concretely. Name the craving at its root where you can identify one
(which of the wanting, resisting or blaming was it?); if you cannot
identify a craving in this case, say so plainly and map the rest,
because an honest "craving not established here" is a legitimate
finding, not a failure. Describe what an everyday easing of that craving
would mean here, in the language of letting go rather than of victory;
if no craving was established, describe what you would look for next
time instead. Name the action that fits, including protective action where
the difficulty is a harm from outside. The path factors are named in
this module and taught in m2 and m3, so your mapping names them where
relevant without needing to apply their meanings yet. Then add a short
paragraph comparing SN 56.11 and Ajahn Brahm's presentation of the
truths: name at least one thing the discourse does that the talk does
not, and one thing the talk does that the discourse does not.

Deliverable B, retrieval checks (about 10 minutes, closed book):

1. List the four tasks, one per truth, in the discourse's own wording.
2. List the eight path factors as Sujato renders them.
3. Explain in two sentences why the discourse calls each of the two
   extremes ignoble and pointless, in your own reading of its wording.
4. State Koṇḍañña's insight and connect it, in one or two sentences, to
   the passing nature of the difficulty you mapped in Deliverable A.
5. Give one difference between the canonical text and Ajahn Brahm's
   reframing.

## The bounded domain, in plain terms

This module teaches the frame only: the four truths, their tasks, the
middle way, the eight factors by name, and one comparison between a
canonical text and a living teacher's reading of it. It does not teach
the precepts or the four immeasurables (m2), meditation method (m3),
the schools (m4-m6), Buddhist psychology (m7), or rebirth and kamma
cosmology, which this degree excludes except as passing context. The
practice here is a noticing practice, an orientation of attention; it is
a preparation for meditation training, and it is not meditation training
itself.

  58 seconds of viewing, about 24 minutes. One rewatch of the four-truths

  checks: about 10 minutes (estimate).

video, 46 reading study, 20 teaching, 45 practice, 70 assessment). Optional further exploration (the first
