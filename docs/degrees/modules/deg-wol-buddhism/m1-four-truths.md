# m1-four-truths: The Buddha's First Teaching and the Four Tasks (deg-wol-buddhism)

Welcome to the first module of A Walk of Life: Buddhism. This degree walks
the Buddhist path the way living communities walk it: ethics first,
practice in the body, and doctrine as a map you check against your own
experience. This module gives you the frame the whole degree rests on, the
teaching the Buddha himself began with.

A note before you start. Buddhist teaching uses the word "suffering" for
something wider than agony: every ache of wanting things to be other than
they are, every low-grade restlessness, every ordinary disappointment.
Keep that wide meaning in mind, because the whole path starts from
looking at it honestly.

## Level and placement

- Level: L1 (introduction, no prior knowledge assumed).
- Placement: first module of the degree. The ethics material in the
  assigned video segment is the ethics-first entry point; m2 develops
  the ethical core (precepts and the four immeasurables) in depth.
- Prerequisites: none.
- Time: about 2 hours 45 minutes of labelled work (see Workload
  arithmetic), plus a three-day practice journal woven through your days.

## Outcomes

By the end of this module you can:

1. State the four noble truths in the wording of the first discourse
   (SN 56.11) and name the task attached to each: suffering is to be
   understood, its cause is to be given up, its cessation is to be
   realized, and the path to that cessation is to be developed.
2. Explain the middle way between indulgence and self-mortification, and
   list the eight factors of the noble path as the first discourse gives
   them.
3. Take one difficulty from your own life and locate the suffering in
   your own craving and response rather than in other people or
   circumstances, using Ajahn Brahm's itch-and-scratch teaching.
4. Tell apart the canonical teaching (SN 56.11) from a living teacher's
   interpretation of it (Ajahn Brahm's happiness and unhappiness
   reframing), naming at least one thing each source does that the other
   does not.
5. State Koṇḍañña's insight from the close of the first discourse
   ("everything that is liable to arise is liable to cease") and connect
   it to the frame of impermanence that runs through this degree.

## Instruction (the sequence a learner follows)

### Step 1: watch the ethics-first entry (Outcomes 3 and 4)

Watch Ajahn Brahm, "What the Buddha Taught" (a dhamma talk recorded at
Dhammaloka, Perth, on 21 November 2003, remastered and published by the
Everyday Dhamma Network), from 25:46 to the end at 49:44. That is 24
minutes of viewing.

- 25:46-31:32, ethics and tolerance. Watch for the teaching the Buddha
  gave his son Rāhula: if it hurts yourself or others, it is bad kamma;
  if it gives happiness to yourself and others, it is good kamma. Watch
  for the claim that guilt helps nobody, and the acknowledge, forgive,
  learn sequence.
- 31:32-37:33, making ethical decisions. Watch for the idea that
  Buddhism does not make your decisions for you; it helps you make them:
  will this help or hurt, myself or others?
- 37:33-49:44, the four truths. Watch for the passage where Ajahn Brahm
  reframes the four truths as happiness and unhappiness, and the image
  that closes the talk: when you blame other people for your suffering,
  it is like having an itch on your head and scratching your bottom. You
  get two itches instead of one. Where it hurts is where you scratch.

Scope note: the first half of this talk (0:00-25:46) works through rebirth
and kamma cosmology. This degree treats rebirth cosmology as passing
context only, so the module assigns the later segment. If those questions
matter to you, hold them; later modules and your own reading can take
them up.

While you watch, keep a pen handy and note the exact moment something
Ajahn Brahm says lands on a current difficulty of yours. That note
becomes the seed of your practice journal and your assessment.

### Step 2: read the first discourse with the four-tasks worksheet (Outcomes 1, 2 and 5)

Read the first discourse, "Rolling Forth the Wheel of Dhamma" (SN 56.11),
in Bhikkhu Sujato's translation on SuttaCentral:
https://suttacentral.net/sn56.11/en/sujato

Read the whole page, translator's notes included. The notes are plain
explanations, and several of them will save you from the two most common
misreadings: that all desire causes suffering (the note at the second
truth explains that some desires lead out of suffering), and that
craving is a selective picker (the note explains it is a restless
searcher, "each and every place").

As you read, fill in a four-row worksheet. The discourse states each
truth three times: once as knowledge ("this is the noble truth of..."),
once as a task ("this noble truth should be..."), and once as completion
("has been..."). Your four rows:

| Truth | The task the discourse attaches | In my own words |
|---|---|---|
| Suffering | should be completely understood | |
| Origin (craving) | should be given up | |
| Cessation | should be realized | |
| The path | should be developed | |

Copy the task wording from the discourse into the middle column, then
close the page and write the right column from memory in your own words.

One wording point worth your attention: Sujato renders the eight path
factors as right view, right purpose, right speech, right action, right
livelihood, right effort, right mindfulness, and right immersion. Other
translations say "right intention" and "right concentration". Same
factors, different English. When you quote the discourse, quote the
translation you read; when you hear other wordings, recognize them as
renderings.

### Step 3: the teaching in plain terms (Outcomes 1, 2 and 5)

The frame in three sentences: things hurt (suffering); the hurting is fed
by a wanting that keeps reaching for the next thing (craving); the
reaching can wind down (cessation); and there is a trainable way of
living that winds it down (the path). The four truths are less a creed
than a diagnosis and a treatment plan, and the discourse itself encodes
that: each truth comes with a task.

The middle way opens the discourse. The Buddha tells his first five
listeners that a person gone forth should cultivate neither indulgence
in sensual pleasures nor self-mortification, both of which he calls
ignoble and pointless. The middle way is then named, and it is simply the
eightfold path: a life of view, purpose, speech, action, livelihood,
effort, mindfulness and immersion, trained together. Notice the shape:
the path is a whole way of life, and most of its factors are ethical or
relational (speech, action, livelihood). That is why this degree walks
ethics first.

The first truth lists what suffering includes: birth, aging, illness,
death, being coupled with the disliked, separation from the liked, not
getting what you wish for, and in brief the five grasping aggregates. The
list reads as a description of human life, and that is its point. The
second truth names craving with its three channels (sensual pleasure,
continued existence, nonexistence). The third truth names the cessation
of that very same craving, in the language of letting go: giving it away,
releasing it, not clinging. The fourth truth returns to the path.

The discourse closes with Koṇḍañña's realization: "Everything that is
liable to arise is liable to cease." Hold onto that line. It is the
whole Buddhist frame in miniature: whatever arises, including your
suffering and the craving that feeds it, is the kind of thing that
passes. Later modules turn that from a comfort into a practice.

Now compare your two sources. Ajahn Brahm's segment reframes the four
truths as a teaching about happiness and unhappiness: the cause of
happiness, the cause of unhappiness, and so on. He says outright that he
has switched them around and claims the right to do so because the
meaning is the same. SN 56.11 gives the canonical structure: the middle
way, the eight factors by name, the three rounds and twelve aspects, the
closing insight. The comparison is Outcome 4: the canonical text carries
the frame; the living teacher carries the warmth and the application.
Both are Buddhism. Learning to hold them together, without flattening
either, is a skill you will use in every module of this degree.

### Step 4: practice, the itch-and-scratch journal (Outcome 3)

For three days, 15 minutes per day, run this small practice.

1. When something bothers you today (a rude message, a traffic jam, a
   craving for coffee, a worry loop), pause.
2. Ask: where did I first put the cause? Write it down: a person, a
   circumstance, a deadline.
3. Then ask: where did the suffering actually show itself? Write down
   what you find: the wanting things to be otherwise, the resisting, the
   blaming, the rehearsing. This is Ajahn Brahm's point: the itch is on
   your head. The husband, the job, the kids are where you have been
   scratching.
4. End each entry with one sentence: what would it mean, here, to
   scratch where it actually itches?

Keep the entries small and honest. You are training the movement of
attention the first truth asks for: suffering is to be understood, and
understanding starts with seeing where it lives.

### Step 5: assessment (all outcomes)

Deliverable A, the four-truths mapping (about 60 minutes, 400 to 600
words). Take one difficulty from your three journal entries and map it
onto the four truths. Name the suffering concretely. Name the craving at
its root (which of the wanting, resisting or blaming was it?). Describe
what cessation would mean here, in the language of letting go rather
than of victory. Name two path factors you would actually develop in
this situation, and say what each would look like in practice. Then add
a short paragraph comparing SN 56.11 and Ajahn Brahm's presentation of
the truths: name at least one thing the discourse does that the talk
does not, and one thing the talk does that the discourse does not.

Deliverable B, retrieval checks (about 10 minutes, closed book):

1. List the four tasks, one per truth, in the discourse's own wording.
2. List the eight path factors as Sujato renders them.
3. State Koṇḍañña's insight in your own words.
4. Name the two extremes the middle way avoids.
5. Give one difference between the canonical text and Ajahn Brahm's
   reframing.

## The bounded domain, in plain terms

This module teaches the frame only: the four truths, their tasks, the
middle way, the eight factors, and one comparison between a canonical
text and a living teacher's reading of it. It does not teach the
precepts or the four immeasurables (m2), meditation method (m3), the
schools (m4-m6), Buddhist psychology (m7), or rebirth and kamma
cosmology, which this degree excludes except as passing context. The
practice here is a noticing practice, an orientation of attention; it is
a preparation for meditation training, not meditation training itself.

  58 seconds of viewing, about 24 minutes. One rewatch of the four-truths
  chapter (37:33 to end, about 12 minutes) is expected when the mapping
  is drafted. Total about 36 minutes (measured segment, estimated
  rewatch).
- Reading: the SN 56.11 page on SuttaCentral measured at 2,009 words on
  the opened page (sutta translation with the translator's notes). At
  130 words per minute (canonical text with many new concepts; the 238
  adult non-fiction default adjusted down by purpose) that is about 15.5
  minutes raw. With study overhead at three times raw reading for a
  primary text with the worksheet, about 45 minutes (estimate,
  labelled).
- Module teaching prose (Step 3 and the notes around the steps): about
  1,300 words of module prose, about 15 minutes of careful reading
  (estimate).
- Practice: 15 minutes per day for 3 days, 45 minutes total (estimate).
- Deliverable A: about 60 minutes (estimate, labelled). Retrieval
  checks: about 10 minutes (estimate).

Total: about 2 hours 51 minutes (36 video, 45 reading study, 15 teaching,
45 practice, 70 assessment). Optional further exploration (the first
half of the talk on rebirth and kamma, or SuttaCentral's adjacent
discourses) is not counted.
