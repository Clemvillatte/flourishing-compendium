## Walk context

Meditators meet the same five visitors again and again: sensual desire,
ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and worry, doubt. The tradition
names them the hindrances and treats them as the mind's weather to be
recognized and worked with, not as failures. The same tradition analyzes
experience into five aggregates (khandhas) — form, feeling, perception,
fabrications, consciousness — and the study guide this module reads frames
them as activities you employ, not objects you own. This elective turns the
learner's own sitting log into the laboratory: both threads are studied
where they were met.

## Objectives

1. The five hindrances, named and recognized in the learner's own
   practice.
2. Their traditional countermeasures, from the canon and a living
   teacher.
3. The meditation-log read as data about one's own mind.
4. The five aggregates named as activities in one's own sitting, per the
   study guide's framing.

## Outcomes

1. Name the five hindrances in your own words and match each to at
   least one entry in your own b4/b8 sitting log (a line where desire,
   ill will, dullness, agitation or doubt was what you observed), or,
   for a hindrance your log never shows, record that plainly with a
   note on what those sittings were like instead. The honest absence
   is itself a finding; an invented entry is not.
2. For the two hindrances your log shows most often, state the
   countermeasure the sources teach and one experiment you will run in
   your next three sittings to apply it.
3. State in three sentences what your log's hindrance pattern says
   about your practice (which sittings the hindrances visit, and what
   that suggests about time, place or posture).
4. Name the five aggregates in your own words and match each to one
   moment in one of your own logged sittings (form: the body's posture
   and breath sensations; feeling: the pleasant or unpleasant tone;
   perception: the labels that arose; fabrications: the intentions and
   thought-constructs; consciousness: the knowing of all this) — or, for
   an aggregate your log cannot show, record that plainly. Honest
   absence is a finding; an invented entry is not.

## Instruction sequence

### Step 1 — The teacher (~25 min, video, whole talk)

**Thanissaro Bhikkhu, "Hindrances" (evening Dhamma talk; "Dhamma
Talks by Thanissaro Bhikkhu" channel; the talk's own recording date
was not established on the opened page and is not claimed),
https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=PcR_fI6p9k8, 0:25:00.** The talk
presents the traditional list of five hindrances and discusses
recognizing them, seeing their drawbacks, and using meditation,
especially comfortable breathing, to keep them from taking over the
mind. Free; the description mentions a Creative Commons license, but
the truncated license line does not establish its terms or version. The
speaker's credentials were opened on the buddho.org biography page in
b1 (Thai forest kammatthana tradition; student of Ajahn Fuang Jotiko;
Abbot of Metta Forest Monastery since 1993).

Activity: keep the talk's list beside your sitting log; mark every log
line a hindrance names.

### Step 2 — The canonical text (~40 min, reading)

**Nyanaponika Thera, The Five Mental Hindrances and Their Conquest:
Selected Texts from the Pali Canon and the Commentaries (The Wheel,
No. 26, Buddhist Publication Society, 1993; freely redistributable on
Access to Insight, https://accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/nyanaponika/wheel026.html).**
The compilation gives the canonical texts on each hindrance and the
commentaries' countermeasures. The text is a standard Buddhist
Publication Society Wheel publication, freely redistributable under
the license stated on the opened page. The module makes no
biographical claims about the author beyond what that page states.

Read the sections for your two most-visited hindrances closely; skim
the rest. Activity: the countermeasure table for outcome 2.

### Step 3 — The log as laboratory (~30 min)

Reread your full sitting log (b4 onward). Mark the hindrance-named
lines. Write the three-sentence pattern statement (outcome 3). Then
set up the countermeasure experiment for your next three sittings
(outcome 2) and add those sittings to the log.

### Step 4 — The aggregates, as activities in your sitting (~30 min, reading + one sitting review)

**Thanissaro Bhikkhu, "The Five Aggregates: A Study Guide" (Metta
Forest Monastery; Access to Insight BCBS Edition, ©2010 Metta Forest
Monastery, first published on ATI 2002, last revised 30 November 2013;
the page states a Creative Commons Attribution-NonCommercial 4.0
International license),
https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/study/khandha.html.** Read two
sections: the Introduction (about 2,600 measured words, from "The Buddha's
Awakening gave him, among other things, a new perspective on the uses
and limitations of words." to "As long as you're still alive, you can
employ the khandhas as needed for whatever skillful uses you see
fit.") and "The Questions" (about 600 measured words, from "There are
some cases in which a person overcome with pain, his mind exhausted,
grieves, mourns, laments, beats his breast, & becomes bewildered." to
"Precisely this Noble Eightfold Path: right view, right resolve, right
speech, right action, right livelihood, right effort, right
mindfulness, right concentration."). The guide's framing is the
one this module keeps: the aggregates are analyzed as activities —
what the tradition's own questions ask is "What is suffering? How is
it caused? What can be done to bring those causes to an end?" — not
as a definition of a person. The
remaining sections (Constructing the Aggregates, Constructing a Self,
Constructing the Path, Deconstruction) are optional for the curious.

Activity: after your next regular sitting (no new sitting required),
take five minutes with its log line and write one line per aggregate
naming where it showed up (outcome 4).

## Practice

The three experiment sittings, logged in the b4 format with the
countermeasure named and one observation each.

## Assessment

1. The hindrance-to-log matching (outcome 1).
2. The countermeasure table + experiment sittings (outcome 2).
3. The three-sentence pattern statement (outcome 3).
4. The aggregate-to-sitting matching (outcome 4).

Rubric (observable):

| criterion | exemplar of strong work | rejection example |
|---|---|---|
| hindrance matching | each of the five named with a real log line quoted or dated, or an explicit honest "not observed" entry | the five listed with no log reference, or an entry invented to fill the slot |
| countermeasure | the two chosen hindrances with the sources' countermeasures and the three experiment-sitting records logged (dated, with the countermeasure named and one observation each) | generic advice ("be patient") with no source, or an experiment designed but never run |
| pattern statement | three sentences that a reader could check against the log | a statement about Buddhism in general |
| aggregate matching | each of the five named with a real log line or dated moment, or an explicit honest "not observed" entry | the five listed with no log reference, or an entry invented to fill the slot |

Self-assessment: one line on whether naming the hindrances changed
the feel of the sittings. That line is part of the module.

## Boundaries and passing mentions

The hindrances and the aggregates are taught here as practical psychology
of meditation, which is what the canon's meditation and analysis texts
treat them as; the scholastic elaboration (abhidharma systematization)
stays out per the walk's exclusion rule. The aggregates thread (the
scope's second psychology thread, previously flagged as owed) is now
covered here from the opened study guide, taught as activities in the
learner's own sitting; the distinction between aggregates and
clinging-aggregates appears in the guide's own text and is left to the
optional further reading.
