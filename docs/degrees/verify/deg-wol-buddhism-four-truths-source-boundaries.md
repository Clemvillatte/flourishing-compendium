## The coverage problem

The inherited B04 record in `verify/deg-contemplative.md` points to
Georges Dreyfus's first session in a three-part Four Noble Truths series.
The title and event description can suggest a complete introduction.
The primary video's transcript supplies a narrower plan: at 00:17–00:40
Dreyfus assumes familiarity with the four truths and assigns suffering
to the present session, its origin to the next session, and the remaining
two truths to the third. At 1:20:13 he again says that the next meeting
will address the origin of suffering.
([Primary video and its opened transcript](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DLPsIhDWZp4);
[three-session host event page](https://shantidevanyc.org/dreyfus-apr24/).)

Consequently, the first session can support a selected discussion of
dukkha. Its title alone does not establish that a novice has been taught
all four truths and their different practical tasks. This matters for
the degree's no-background entry assumption: a task requiring all four
needs an explicit teaching source that actually supplies them.

The institutional check is independent of the event host. Williams

as Jackson Professor of Religion; the host page supplies the account

the academic role without relying solely on an uploader description.
([Williams Religion catalogue](https://catalog.williams.edu/pdf/rel.pdf);

## A finite primary-text anchor

SN 56.11 supplies the four truths together with their corresponding
tasks. In Bhikkhu Sujato's translation the learner encounters suffering,
its origin, its cessation and the path, followed by what is to be
understood, given up, realized and developed. The path includes ethical
conduct through right speech, right action and right livelihood, as well
as the other path factors.
([Opened primary text, Sujato translation](https://suttacentral.net/sn56.11/en/sujato?lang=en&layout=linebyline&reference=none&notes=none&highlight=false&script=latin).)

A possible future excerpt can begin with the middle-way paragraph and
end after the four tasks have each been stated. That bounds the reading
around an intelligible practical sequence. The original text's religious
frame should remain visible: its account of craving refers to future
lives, and its closing narrative includes divine beings. The existing
degree scope permits passing contextual mentions where practice needs
them; neither a cosmology unit nor an empirical claim about rebirth is
being proposed.
([Sujato's complete translated discourse](https://suttacentral.net/sn56.11/en/sujato?lang=en&layout=linebyline&reference=none&notes=none&highlight=false&script=latin).)

Peter Harvey's independently opened translation and commentary provide
a useful terminology check. His glossary distinguishes demanding
craving, taṇhā, from every possible desire; it notes that chanda can
include wholesome desire to act. The lesson should therefore avoid
turning the second truth into a blanket command to abandon caring,
purpose or every wish to do something.
([Harvey translation, “Craving” glossary entry](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn56/sn56.011.harv.html).)

This is a candidate substitution for a specific teaching need. It does
not add two whole translations or all three lectures to required study,
and it does not settle how different living Buddhist schools interpret
the path.

## Interpretation and evidence boundaries

Dreyfus introduces a homeodynamic-system account around 09:55 and
clarifies at 1:01:04 that this is his explanatory vocabulary rather
than a Buddhist word. His incidental physiological illustration at
11:15–11:21 also makes survival-time claims. This verification has not
established those claims from physiological research; they should not
be reused as scientific facts or health guidance on the strength of
the Buddhist lecture.
([Primary video's opened transcript](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DLPsIhDWZp4).)

For later excerpt selection, keep the distinction between the translated
canonical discourse, Dreyfus's interpretive analogy and empirical
psychological evidence. A learner's account of an ordinary difficulty
may illustrate their understanding of the four practical tasks; it
cannot by itself demonstrate the clinical efficacy of a Buddhist path.
These are source-use limits, with no additional safety gate or prescribed
practice introduced.
