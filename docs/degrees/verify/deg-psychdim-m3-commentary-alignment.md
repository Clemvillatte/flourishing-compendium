## Source boundary the lesson must retain

Ehring's experimental-evidence paragraph says induced rumination has
been linked experimentally to negative thinking, poor problem solving,
inhibited instrumental behaviour, biased information processing and
impaired interpersonal functioning. It cites other works for those
claims rather than reporting a single experiment in that paragraph.
([Paragraph beginning “Converging evidence comes from experimental
research”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8429319/).)

The selected M3 excerpt must include that paragraph if the task asks
about its experimental claim. A learner should not be asked to appraise
an argument omitted from the assigned reading. The existing PD-V08 M3
variant already allows the small addition; a full extra paper is not
needed to teach this limited source-appraisal skill.

The commentary characterises repetitive negative thinking by process
features rather than by the particular topic of a thought. It also
describes a distinction between abstract, decontextualised processing
and concrete, context-specific processing; that synthesis is not a
license to diagnose a learner or prescribe treatment.
([RNT definition and processing-mode discussion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8429319/).)

## Proposed answer frame and feedback

The production owner can use this short frame without expanding the
scope or claiming that it is a validated assessment instrument.

| Question | Adequate response at this source-limited level |
|---|---|
| What kind of source have you read? | A commentary synthesising research, not the methods and results of one experiment. |
| What claim about experimental evidence does it make? | It reports experimental work in which induced rumination was associated with the adverse outcomes it names; attribute that summary to the commentary rather than inventing a new result. |
| Which details would you need to inspect in a primary report? | Participants and setting; assignment and comparison conditions; how rumination and outcomes were measured; effect estimates and uncertainty; and limitations relevant to the proposed application. |
| Which details can you verify from this paragraph? | The paragraph's summary and references. The listed experiment-specific details are not reported here, so the appropriate answer for those details is “not reported/cannot assess from this source.” |
| What does this help you decide in ordinary life? | It gives a reason to notice the process of recurring thought, while keeping an interpretation of one's own case separate from a clinical diagnosis or a treatment claim. |

**Proposed pass response:** “I read a commentary, not an experiment
report. Ehring summarises experimental evidence suggesting that induced
rumination can interfere with problem solving and other functions. This
paragraph does not give me one experiment's participants, assignment,
comparison condition or numerical effect. I therefore cannot audit that
experiment's causal strength or judge its practical size here. I would
follow the cited research to inspect those details. That is a limit on
what I can assess from this source, not proof that no experimental
evidence exists.” ([Claim being appraised](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8429319/).)

**Proposed reject response, deliberately fabricated:** “Ehring randomly
assigned 60 people to rumination or relaxation and found a large effect,
so this proves that my worrying causes depression.” Reject it because
the source does not report those study details, an effect-size label is
invented, and a personal clinical conclusion does not follow from this
commentary. The invented sample and comparator are rejection examples,
not assertions about a real study.

Credit “not reported” only when the learner names the missing detail and
explains why it matters. Do not credit a blank answer or a blanket claim
that commentaries are useless. A synthesis can orient a reader to an
evidence base without providing enough detail to audit an individual
experiment.
