## Additional hold reason: the numerical ratio

The 2013 correction explicitly withdraws the modeling element and the model-based predictions about ratios of 2.9 and 11.6, while leaving other elements of the original article unaffected ([freely readable correction abstract](https://psycnet.apa.org/record/2013-32937-001)). APA's own public account also describes the withdrawal as applying to the modeling portion of the paper ([publisher's explanation](https://www.apa.org/monitor/2013/12/model-emotions)).

A replacement must therefore avoid teaching 3:1 as an established minimum for a worthwhile or flourishing life. This correction does not warrant rejecting all positive-emotion research, and the receipt makes no such claim.

## Mixed-emotions evidence

Berrios, Totterdell and Kellett's meta-analysis includes **63 experimental studies and 7,157 participants**, finding evidence that mixed emotions can be elicited using different measures and induction procedures ([2015 meta-analysis, Results](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4397957/)). Its sample comprises nonclinical experimental research, and the authors explicitly limit inference about stable traits and everyday conditions ([Methods and Limitations](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4397957/)).

The paper distinguishes continuous, simultaneous reports from retrospective or aggregate indicators; its table includes eight studies using the simultaneous measure ([Measures of mixed emotions and Table 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4397957/)). It also treats genuine co-activation versus rapid succession as an unresolved mechanism question and discusses memory bias in other measures ([Discussion, “Implications for models of affect”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4397957/)). The appropriate correction therefore preserves both the evidence for mixed experience and the measurement question.

A later study of 27 participants found consistent neural patterns associated with reported mixed feelings in some cortical regions, while results differed across regions ([Vaccaro and colleagues, 2024, Abstract and Results](https://academic.oup.com/cercor/article/34/4/bhae122/7639057?login=false)). Participants labeled their experience during a second viewing after scanning, and the authors identify cultural context and personal relevance as matters for further work ([Procedure and Discussion](https://academic.oup.com/cercor/article/34/4/bhae122/7639057?login=false)). This supplies a further reason to avoid the lecture's categorical claim, without treating one small neuroimaging study as a final account of all mixed emotions.

## What a replacement must preserve

A replacement can teach the broaden-and-build proposal while keeping its claims and measurements distinct. It should explain what a particular study measured, what it found and what remains an inference before asking the learner to use the idea in a life situation.

For this specific problem, a useful comprehension check would present an invented person who feels gratitude for support while still grieving a loss. Ask whether the presence of gratitude alone establishes that grief has vanished, and what evidence would be needed to assess a change in physiological recovery or coping.

A satisfactory answer allows the reported mixed experience and avoids inferring that one emotion must erase another. The task is an original teaching suggestion; no claim is made that it is a tested intervention or that it completes the broader positive-emotion row.

I do not recommend rescuing an isolated fragment from the held full lecture in this receipt. The adjacent claims about broadening, cardiovascular recovery and resilience require their own qualification, and a short splice would need a fresh teaching-continuity review.
