## Evidence and scope limits

I do not recommend importing chapter 6 wholesale: it moves into hypnosis, recovered-memory controversy and flashbulb memories, whereas the proposed learning task concerns ordinary evidence-checking ([Yale transcript, chapter 6](https://oyc.yale.edu/psychology/psyc-110/lecture-8)). In a separately opened interview, Loftus explicitly distinguishes demonstrating false memories from disproving the existence of repressed memories; the interviewer offers the stronger inference and Loftus corrects it ([APA interview transcript](https://www.apa.org/news/podcasts/speaking-of-psychology/memory-manipulated)). That interview is a verification aid here, with no teaching-family admission or degree hours claimed.

Avoid turning the lecture's caution about certainty into “confidence tells us nothing.” A later survey of 76 eyewitness-memory experts distinguishes confidence obtained immediately after a properly administered identification from confidence expressed later; its authors also explicitly say that they measured expert opinion rather than adjudicating the validity of every opinion ([study methods, current-study statement and discussion](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12408934/)). This is a recency check for the verifier, not an added degree assignment or a new primary-text search.

The APA transcript dates its interview to the 2018 convention, with page creation in October 2019 and an August 2023 update; it is not a 2026 interview ([APA transcript and footer](https://www.apa.org/news/podcasts/speaking-of-psychology/memory-manipulated)). Loftus's independent UCI biography confirms her professorships and expertise in memory and eyewitness testimony ([UCI faculty profile](https://www.law.uci.edu/faculty/full-time/loftus/)).

## Proposed learner task

This is an original curriculum-design proposal, not a task taken from either lecturer. It remains for the module owner and independent reviewer to assess.

Choose a short, unfamiliar paragraph and write a plain-language explanation of its main idea. Later, reconstruct it without looking and compare your version with the paragraph, marking omissions and additions. Then write two questions about a fictional disagreement over what happened: one that invites an account and one that presupposes a disputed detail. Explain what the second question introduces.

A satisfactory response separates the supplied evidence from the learner's added interpretation and identifies the assumption built into the leading question. Reject a response that treats a vivid recollection as proof, or that concludes that another person's recollection must be false merely because memory can be influenced.
