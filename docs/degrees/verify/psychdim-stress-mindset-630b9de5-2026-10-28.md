## What is usable

Stanford's full interview transcript gives a worked sequence for responding to stress around a presentation: notice the response, identify the value at stake, and choose behavior that serves that value ([Stanford GSB interview](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/mindset-matters-how-embrace-benefits-stress)). Crum distinguishes a person's appraisal of a particular demand and their resources from their broader assumptions about stress itself ([interview transcript](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/mindset-matters-how-embrace-benefits-stress)).

I read the complete English transcript, including the later parenting caveat and closing communication discussion. The useful sequence begins with Abrahams's question about an upcoming presentation and ends with Crum's explanation of using stress to address the underlying purpose; the subsequent caveat expressly rejects deliberately seeking more stress or imposing excess chores and work ([full transcript](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/mindset-matters-how-embrace-benefits-stress)).

The accompanying SPARQ instructions make the sequence concrete: step 4 separates emotional, behavioral and physiological responses, step 5 asks what matters to the learner, and step 6 asks whether their usual response helps or harms that aim ([toolkit instructions, steps 4–6](https://sparqtools.org/rethinkingstress-instructions/)). This is a reason to consider the material as instruction, rather than relying on the landing page's general promises about stress.

## Scope and safety

The practical use would be reflection on an ordinary challenge, such as a presentation or difficult conversation. The proposed learning outcome is to explain how a chosen response serves a value, while identifying when reducing the demand or asking for help would be appropriate.

The landing page makes broad improvement claims about work, mental health and physical health; this receipt verifies the instructional sequence without independently establishing those outcomes ([SPARQ landing page](https://sparqtools.org/rethinkingstress/)). The interview itself explicitly cautions against adding stress, so a summary that tells learners to seek more hardship would misrepresent it ([Stanford transcript](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/mindset-matters-how-embrace-benefits-stress)).

Do not present a change in mindset as treatment for illness, as proof that a dangerous situation is safe, or as a substitute for changing harmful conditions. Do not require a learner to reinterpret trauma positively.
