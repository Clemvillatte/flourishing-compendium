## Claim 1 — Moulds: repetition, abstract processing, difficulty disengaging. VERIFIED (audio ~05:59–06:02)

Moulds, defining RNT for the panel:

> "repetitive negative thinking is a term that we use to describe different types of repetitive thinking across different disorders. And I guess the main features are really just that repetition. So our clients tell us they perseverate their thinking on a particular theme and can't shake that. So the thinking just goes on and on and has that very repetitive, you know, recycling kind of quality."

> "Another really important aspect of repetitive negative thinking is that it has a very abstract flavor. So often our clients when they're worrying or they're dwelling on the past, they're thinking in very abstract terms. Why did I do that? What's wrong with me? What if that happens? So rather than being focused on specific and very concrete details, there's really this flavor of sort of analysing that these very abstract and overgeneral."

> "And I think another key feature is I guess what I call the stickiness of repetitive thinking. So our clients have difficulty [dis]engaging [ASR: "dips engaging"]. So once they're on that train and once they're thinking and they're worrying and that thinking has got rolling, it's very hard to move away from it. So there's a real stickiness."

All three elements of the claim — repetition, abstract processing, difficulty disengaging — are present in Moulds' own words, plus a fourth feature she names "stickiness".

## Claim 2 — Sethi supplies the stuck-radio illustration. VERIFIED (audio ~06:23–07:26)

Newall asks Sethi how she describes RNT to clients; Sethi answers:

> "the way that I tend to talk about this for the clients is I say, like, if you can imagine your mind as being like a radio and radios have different stations, you can be listening to it at any point. It's a question of like, which radio station might I be listening to right now? Or for the last few hours, if you happen to have been stuck to a particular channel."

> "So for example, if you're on the dwelling or rumination channel, it's exactly what Michelle was describing. It has that kind of flavor [ASR: "flavor by me"] … And same with worry. So it's all the sometimes, you know, people might call like their what if channel or the cat[astrophizing] channel … It's just this sense of again, getting stuck in that cycle and having a really hard time disengaging from it."

The stuck-radio (stuck-channel) illustration is Sethi's, as claimed.

## Claim 3 — Moulds: past/future emphasis vs shared transdiagnostic process. VERIFIED (audio ~07:48–09:02)

Moulds, answering "what distinguishes worry from rumination?":

> "I think that distinction has really been very much based on theoretical models and the way we think about particular disorders. So we've the literature on rumination very much developed in the context of depression … on the other side, we've thought about worry in the context of anxiety, particularly GAD … So I think that distinction very broadly has often been on rumination being about the past and worry being about the future. But I think it's more recently, there's really been a shift in our thinking that coincides with the trans-diagnostic movement to focus less on the content and more to look at the process."

> "So yes, that traditional distinction has been around the temporal focus. But I think more and more there's this acknowledgement of it being a process that runs across disorders and diagnoses. Well, mental health disorders."

The module's distinction claim is confirmed: Moulds first states the traditional past/future framing, then pivots to the shared transdiagnostic process view.
