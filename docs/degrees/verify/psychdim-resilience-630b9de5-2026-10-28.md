## Recommendation

Consider George Bonanno's Columbia lecture, “The Resilience Paradox,” as the stronger of the two resilience lectures inspected in this pass. It explains his outcome-based definition of resilience, distinguishes several trajectories after adversity, and teaches a sequence of attending to context, choosing a response and checking whether it helps ([lecture and timed transcript](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Lp5Q4w-_DU)).

This has a direct Walk-of-Life use: a learner can examine whether a response suits the difficulty they face and decide what to revise. The learning task proposed below asks for that reasoning without asking learners to diagnose themselves or predict their response to trauma.

## Evidence limits that should survive editing

Bonanno defines resilience here as a relatively stable trajectory of healthy functioning following adversity, distinguishes it from a recovery trajectory, and explicitly allows an initial period of distress ([lecture transcript, 02:49–07:15](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_Lp5Q4w-_DU)). The module should attribute this operational definition to his research approach, rather than imply that it exhausts all uses of the word.

The lecture's population-level observations should not become predictions about a particular learner. The authors' openly available review explicitly states that resilience is never guaranteed, describes regulatory flexibility as a proposed likely pathway, and calls for further prospective and real-time research ([review, end of the training discussion and “Summary and future directions”](https://www.tc.columbia.edu/faculty/gab38/faculty-profile/files/2023_Bonanno-et-al._NATURE-REVIEWS-PSYCHOLOGY.pdf)).

That review also says that broader training combining the sequence and mindset remained to be developed; the 2025 APA interview describes Bonanno's training work as being developed and tested ([review, “Summary and future directions”](https://www.tc.columbia.edu/faculty/gab38/faculty-profile/files/2023_Bonanno-et-al._NATURE-REVIEWS-PSYCHOLOGY.pdf), [APA interview](https://www.apa.org/news/podcasts/speaking-of-psychology/building-resilience)). Neither source establishes the effectiveness of this receipt's original classroom task.

The review was inspected for those qualifications, and the APA transcript was read in full. Both serve verification here; neither is added as an assignment or counted as an independent teaching source. The review and interview share the lecturer's research perspective, so they do not constitute independent adjudication of his theory.

## Proposed application and assessment

Use a fictional case in which someone has tried the same response to a manageable difficulty several times and remains stuck. Ask the learner to identify the immediate problem, propose two available responses, and state what observation would tell them to continue, stop or change course.

A strong answer names the situational constraint, explains why each response might help, and defines feedback relevant to the goal. An inadequate answer calls the person weak, promises that a positive attitude guarantees recovery, or recommends repeating a failed strategy without considering the result.

This is an original educational exercise, with no validated treatment claim. Learners should be free to use a fictional case, and persistent distress should not be graded as a failure to learn.
