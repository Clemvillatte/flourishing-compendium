## A narrowly useful teaching candidate

Geoffrey Cohen and David Sherman explain how a threat to someone's sense of adequacy can shape their response to health information, then introduce values affirmation as a way of examining that response ([Annual Reviews lecture, 01:20 to 10:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AQ06XYhuQF8)). I recommend considering this passage for the personal question: how can I examine unwelcome information without making my whole worth depend on what it says?

The lecture treats values affirmation as reflection on something personally important, such as relationships or creativity, and repeatedly qualifies its effects by circumstances ([lecture, 04:07 to 10:22](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AQ06XYhuQF8)). A suitable lesson would explain that account and test the learner's reasoning about information, identity and available action. It would leave diagnosis, treatment and a broad survey of health services outside this assignment.

## Qualifications that affect the lesson

The coffee example concerns how participants evaluated a threatening article; the lecture does not establish the article's medical claim as current advice ([lecture, 07:34 to 08:31](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AQ06XYhuQF8)). A lesson should discuss the response to information without repeating the example's health claim as a fact.

The lecturers describe values affirmation as a catalyst whose effects depend on resources and opportunity, and they give examples of unwelcome effects ([lecture, 17:35 to 19:38 and 26:30 to close](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AQ06XYhuQF8)). That qualification belongs alongside the proposed mechanism. Learners should not be asked to accept a health message merely because they feel less defensive; its evidence still needs examination.

The publicly accessible abstract of Sweeney and Moyer's 2015 meta-analysis reports small pooled effects on intentions and behavior, while warning that the available findings do not establish a causal intention-to-behavior pathway ([SUNY-hosted abstract](https://researchconnect.suny.edu/en/publications/self-affirmation-and-responses-to-health-messages-a-meta-analysis/)). I read the complete abstract, not the full paper, and do not reproduce the displayed meta-regression confidence interval because its formatting is ambiguous.

A later meta-review reports heterogeneity in the self-affirmation review it included and classifies that review as lower quality under its own AMSTAR 2 scoring scheme; the authors acknowledge arbitrary category cutoffs and limitations in judging reporting quality ([meta-review, “Assessment of Meta-Analysis Quality,” “Self-affirmation,” and “Limitations and Future Directions”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7571594/)). ## Proposed teaching task

Give a fictional case: someone receives a credible warning that one of their habits may be harmful and hears it as a verdict that they are an inadequate person. Ask the learner to separate the message's claim, its evidence and the judgment about the person's worth.

Then ask how reflection on a valued relationship might change the person's willingness to examine the warning. A good answer explains the proposed self-affirmation account, preserves the need to evaluate the warning's evidence, and names a resource or action that reflection alone cannot supply.

For feedback, contrast that answer with: “If I affirm myself, the warning must be right and I will change.” The learner should identify both unsupported steps and explain why openness, intention and behavior need to be assessed separately.
