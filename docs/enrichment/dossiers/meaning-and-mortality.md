# Meaning & Mortality

> What does being mortal do to the meaning of a life?

I make plans as though my time were open. The ending arrives on other people's schedules — a funeral, a diagnosis, a phone call at night — and for a while the plans look different. Then the old proportions return. What I want to know is what the fact of ending does to the meaning of the life it ends: whether it shapes what a life can mean, or merely cuts the life off.

Part of that question belongs to fear, and I hold that part elsewhere. My Fear material carries the affective response to dying — the fear itself, its clinical study, the Epicurean argument against it, Ivan Ilyich's dying. What I hold here is death as a shaper of meaning, gratitude and choice: the remembrance practices, the analyses of finitude, and the experiments that test what confrontation actually does to people.

The traditions I read agree on the observation that a life is finite and much of living proceeds as though it were not, and they part company on the direction to take from there. The existential analyses treat death as the horizon that gives choices their weight, and I start there because the question is theirs in its sharpest form. The classical practices treat the remembrance of death as a clarifier of conduct, and I reach them through the analysis, since each one answers a conduct question. The research programmes test what reminders of dying actually do to people, and their findings are more mixed than the classical confidence. I want all three in front of me, with their disagreements preserved rather than settled in advance.

## What the ending does to a life

### The possibility that is mine alone

Heidegger gives the sharpest version of the observation. For him, [being-toward-death](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Being-toward-death) is death treated as the one possibility that is mine alone and cannot be outstripped. The anticipation of it, rather than brooding over it, is the mode he prescribes, and flight into the conformist everyday is the alternative he names. The definition, as the encyclopedia entry renders it:

> "the possibility of the absolute impossibility of Dasein" ([Heidegger, SZ 250, as quoted in Wrathall's SEP entry](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/))

In [E043 — Practise being-toward-death (Sein-zum-Tode)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=E043%20%E2%80%94%20Practise%20being-toward-death%20%28Sein-zum-Tode%29) the analysis becomes a practice, with the line "as soon as man comes to life, he is at once old enough to die" ([Heidegger 1962, H. 255, as quoted in the Heideggerian terminology article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heideggerian_terminology)).

[E054 — Practise finitude as gift](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=E054%20%E2%80%94%20Practise%20finitude%20as%20gift) works the same ground from the side of choice: death as "Dasein's ownmost possibility—non-relational, certain and as such indefinite, not to be outstripped" ([Being and Time, Macquarrie and Robinson trans.](https://dhspriory.org/kenny/PhilTexts/Heidegger/BeingTime07.doc)), a limit that belongs to one's own existence rather than an event happening to others, the facing of which discloses the possibilities one can actually choose.

[Finitude](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Finitude) gathers the same analysis as a condition rather than a doctrine: being limited in time, possibility and knowledge. The encyclopedia entry states the cost directly: "Because I am temporally finite, each choice comes at a cost—to pursue one possibility necessarily involves forgoing other possibilities", and "Anxiety is the mood in which I experience immediately the finitude of my future horizon of possibilities" ([SEP, Heidegger](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/)). Thrownness names the limit no one chose: the entry says we find ourselves with preferences, dispositions and traits "that we did not choose" ([SEP, Heidegger](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/)).

### Would an unending life be better?

That analysis makes death the shaper of choice, but it does not settle whether the ending is a benefit or a loss, and the traditions split on that too. Bernard Williams says a life without death would be meaningless, using Karel Čapek's play about Elina Makropulos, who has lived 342 years on an elixir:

> "Immortality, or a state without death, would be meaningless, I shall suggest; so, in a sense, death gives the meaning to life." ([Williams, "The Makropulos Case: Reflections on the Tedium of Immortality"](https://123philosophy.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/williams-immortality.pdf))

His diagnosis of her state is boredom, "a boredom connected with the fact that everything that could happen and make sense to one particular human being of 42 had already happened to her" ([Williams, "The Makropulos Case"](https://123philosophy.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/williams-immortality.pdf)). Thomas Nagel reads the loss from the other side:

> "Viewed in this way, death, no matter how inevitable, is an abrupt cancellation of indefinitely extensive possible goods." ([Nagel, "Death"](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil150/Nagel.pdf))

He adds that we can conceive of what it would be for a person "to go on existing indefinitely", and that this possibility "is still that of the continuation of a good for him, if life is the good we take it to be" ([Nagel, "Death"](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil150/Nagel.pdf)). The two positions are not strict opposites: Williams measures a whole unending life against itself, Nagel measures a death against the goods it removes. A death can be a loss, as Nagel says, and an endless life can still be a trap, as Williams says; the claims are compatible. What the comparison genuinely leaves open is the stronger claim that mortality is itself the source of meaning, which Williams argues in a qualified form and Nagel's essay does not argue at all.

### What the remembrance practices change

The classical practices are more directive than the analyses, and what they aim at first is conduct. The Buddhist [B055 — Contemplate mortality (maranasati)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B055%20%E2%80%94%20Contemplate%20mortality%20%28maranasati%29) recollects the Upajjhatthana Sutta's five facts, death third among them, often; the sutta's own reason is therapeutic: "Because of that intoxication with life, they conduct themselves in a bad way in body... in speech... and in mind", and often reflecting on death either entirely abandons the intoxication or weakens it ([AN 5.57, Thanissaro](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an05/an05.057.than.html)). [B193 — Live with death lightly in view](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B193%20%E2%80%94%20Live%20with%20death%20lightly%20in%20view) keeps the same five facts as a repeated practice rather than a mood, set beside the Dhammapada's verses: "Heedfulness: the path to the Deathless. Heedlessness: the path to death." ([Dhp 21–24, Thanissaro](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/dhp/dhp.02.than.html))

Marcus Aurelius wrote the reminder to himself as a rule for each act. In [S089 — Remember that you will die](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=S089%20%E2%80%94%20Remember%20that%20you%20will%20die) the possibility of departing from life this very moment regulates every act and thought, and [S104 — Practise the contemplation of mortality](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=S104%20%E2%80%94%20Practise%20the%20contemplation%20of%20mortality) gives the instruction its plainest form: "Do not act as if thou wert going to live ten thousand years. Death hangs over thee. While thou livest, while it is in thy power, be good." ([Meditations IV.17, Long trans.](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/15877/pg15877.txt))

The Aztec songs turn the same arithmetic on the use of time. The poem behind [M037 — Number your days; we only borrow our time](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=M037%20%E2%80%94%20Number%20your%20days%3B%20we%20only%20borrow%20our%20time) comes from the Cantares Mexicanos: "Is it nelli [rooted, true, authentic] one really lives on the earth? Not forever on earth, only a little while here. Though it be jade it falls apart, though it be gold it wears away, though it be quetzal plumage it is torn asunder." ([Cantares mexicanos, fol. 17r, trans. León-Portilla, in Maffie's IEP article](https://iep.utm.edu/aztec-philosophy/)) Seneca refuses the complaint behind all of it in [P106 — Let mortality give shape](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=P106%20%E2%80%94%20Let%20mortality%20give%20shape): life is long enough if you know how to use it, and the waste is in the arrangement.

> "Why do we complain of Nature? she has dealt kindly with us." ([Seneca, On the Shortness of Life, Stewart trans., Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/64576/pg64576.txt))

The Daoist entry changes the target from conduct to resistance itself. Zhuangzi's True men of old, the subject of [D090 — Accept death as transformation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=D090%20%E2%80%94%20Accept%20death%20as%20transformation), are described in Legge's translation: "The True men of old knew nothing of the love of life or of the hatred of death. Entrance into life occasioned them no joy; the exit from it awakened no resistance." ([Zhuangzi ch. 6, Legge trans.](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf))

### What reminders of dying do to people

The practices ask for deliberate confrontation, and the experiments tested what reminders of dying actually do. The terror-management paradigm behind [Mortality salience](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Mortality%20salience) predicted defense of worldview and self-esteem, and its meta-analysis reported exactly that across 164 articles and 277 experiments:

> "MS yielded moderate effects (r = .35) on a range of worldview- and self-esteem-related dependent variables (DVs)" ([Burke, Martens and Faucher 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20097885/))

A 2022 attempt across 17 labs and 1,550 participants to repeat one classic result from the same theory did not reproduce it:

> "Our results were non-diagnostic of whether original author involvement improves replicability because we were unable to replicate the finding under any conditions. This suggests that the original finding was either a false positive or the conditions necessary to obtain it are not fully understood or no longer exist." ([Klein et al., Many Labs 4, Collabra: Psychology 2022](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/8/1/35271/186959/Many-Labs-4-Failure-to-Replicate-Mortality-Salience))

Death reflection, the construct on my [Death reflection](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Death%20reflection) card, uses a different induction from the abstract reminder: a specific and vivid contemplation of one's own death. In the apartment-fire scenario participants imagined waking on the "20th floor of an old, downtown building" to "sounds of screams and the choking smell of smoke" ([Frias et al. 2011](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Frias-DeathGratitude.pdf)). These studies measured several outcomes: conventional mortality-salience manipulations have been shown to produce "increased greed, racism, and ethnocentrism" (Koole, Greenberg and Pyszczynski 2006, as cited in [the same article](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Frias-DeathGratitude.pdf)), while "death reflection appears to produce salubrious results, such as increased intrinsic behavior and less greed" (Cozzolino 2006 and Cozzolino et al. 2004, as cited there). On gratitude, the study's own conclusion:

> "In conclusion, this study found that when one confronts their own death in a specific and vivid manner, their feelings of gratitude tend to increase." ([Frias et al. 2011, Death and Gratitude](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Frias-DeathGratitude.pdf))

The pattern of means in that study followed the same order: the death-reflection induction showed the greatest increase in state gratitude, followed by the traditional mortality-salience treatment and the controls ([Frias et al. 2011](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Frias-DeathGratitude.pdf)). So the gratitude result is not an alternative to the defensive findings; it is a result about a different outcome, and the two records sit side by side here rather than in a contest.

The [Death acceptance](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Death%20acceptance) card holds Wong's DAP-R, which rounds out the stances toward death with a measure rather than a practice: fear of death and death avoidance on one side, and three acceptance types on the other, each making death acceptable for a different reason — its status as biology, a belief about what follows it, or relief from suffering ([Wong et al., as listed in Jansen et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6740004/)).

### Wisdom as meditation on life

One tradition runs the emphasis the other way. [SP068 — Think least of death; wisdom meditates on life](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=SP068%20%E2%80%94%20Think%20least%20of%20death%3B%20wisdom%20meditates%20on%20life) carries Spinoza's line, which the card itself sets beside the memento mori family for comparison:

> "A free man thinks about death less than he thinks about anything else; his wisdom is a meditation on life, not on death." ([Spinoza, Ethics IV P67, Bennett trans.](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/spinoza1665.pdf))

The demonstration opens by saying that a free man "isn't led by fear of death" but "wants the good directly" ([Ethics IV P67 demonstration](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/spinoza1665.pdf)). Marcus holds that death is not to be feared and so can be kept in mind as a lever; Spinoza holds that a mind guided by reason has no need to dwell on it. Whether the wise should keep death in view or think of it least is one of the questions below.

## Where the traditions genuinely disagree

### Is death bad for the one who dies?

Whether a person's own death can harm that person, given that the person is not there to be harmed, is the question the arguments keep returning to. Epicurus says it cannot:

> "Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not." ([Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, Hicks trans., Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Epicurus/menoec.html))

His reason is stated just before: "good and evil imply awareness, and death is the privation of all awareness" ([Letter to Menoeceus](https://classics.mit.edu/Epicurus/menoec.html)). Nagel replies that the harm is a loss, so it does not need to be felt:

> "If death is an evil at all, it cannot be because of its positive features, but only because of what it deprives us of." ([Nagel, "Death"](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil150/Nagel.pdf))

He also denies that harm requires experience: a man "is the subject of good and evil as much because he has hopes which may or may not be fulfilled, or possibilities which may or may not be realized, as because of his capacity to suffer and enjoy" ([Nagel, "Death"](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil150/Nagel.pdf)).

Both accept that a dead person feels nothing, and they part on whether feeling is the only place harm can occur. Nagel does not claim a full victory. He grants that the Epicurean's companion argument from Lucretius, that nobody minds the time before birth, still awaits an answer in his essay, and says that answering it needs a general account of how we treat past and future in our own lives.

I find Nagel's deprivation reading the more natural description of why a death at thirty feels worse than one at ninety, and Epicurus's argument the better reply to dread of being dead, as opposed to grief at ceasing to live. The two answers fit different emotions. One letter and one essay, with Nagel read from the PDF text and Epicurus in Hicks's translation; the later literature on deprivation and on the time before birth is not opened here.

### Should the wise keep death in view or think of it least?

Whether turning attention to one's own death makes action better is the practical version of the question, and neither thinker wants fear to rule.

Marcus Aurelius makes the reminder a rule for each act:

> "Since it is possible that thou mayest depart from life this very moment, regulate every act and thought accordingly. But to go away from among men, if there are gods, is not a thing to be afraid of" ([Marcus Aurelius, Meditations II.11, Long trans.](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.2.two.html))

Spinoza gives the opposite instruction in a proposition about the free man:

> "A free man thinks about death less than he thinks about anything else; his wisdom is a meditation on life, not on death." ([Spinoza, Ethics IV P67, Bennett trans.](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/spinoza1665.pdf))

The proof opens by saying that such a man "isn't led by fear of death" ([Ethics IV P67 demonstration](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/spinoza1665.pdf)). So they agree that fear should not govern. Marcus holds that death is not to be feared and so can be kept in mind as a lever. Spinoza holds that a mind guided by reason has no need to dwell on it. I hold the first as [Remember that you will die](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=S089%20%E2%80%94%20Remember%20that%20you%20will%20die) and the second as [Think least of death](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=SP068%20%E2%80%94%20Think%20least%20of%20death%3B%20wisdom%20meditates%20on%20life).

A bounded, scheduled reminder fits Marcus, and the rest of the month fits Spinoza; that compromise is my own, and neither author proposes it. I read one proposition from each. Spinoza's claim depends on his account of freedom and the affects, and I have not examined how the Stoics' wider doctrine on emotion shapes Marcus's use of the reminder.

### Does dying young take more away?

Marcus makes a stronger claim than the reminder to use the day. In Book II.14 he says that a long life and a short one lose the same thing at death:

> "the longest liver and he who will die soonest lose just the same. For the present is the only thing of which a man can be deprived" ([Meditations II.14, Long trans.](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.2.two.html))

The condition matters: for Marcus, only the present is possessed, so a future not yet possessed cannot be taken away ([Meditations II.14](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.2.two.html)). Nagel counts the possible continuation instead. Comparing Keats's death at twenty-four with Tolstoy's at eighty-two, he writes:

> "Keats' death deprived him of many years of life which were allowed to Tolstoy; so in a clear sense Keats' loss was greater" ([Nagel, "Death", p. 7](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil150/Nagel.pdf))

Nagel immediately adds that this does not make Tolstoy's loss insignificant ([Nagel, "Death", p. 7](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil150/Nagel.pdf)). The disagreement is about what can be lost and how losses compare, not about how long the dead remain nonexistent. Marcus restricts loss to present possession; Nagel includes valuable possibilities that death prevents.

I find Nagel's account more convincing here. The fact that I do not yet possess tomorrow does not, to me, make its cancellation count for nothing. I can use Marcus's reminder to return to this day without accepting his equality claim as a measure of what a death takes away. I have compared one passage from each text, not established a general ranking of younger and older deaths.

## Practice: variation, selection, retention

::Variation:: What tends to need varying on this theme is the lived-as-immortal default; the practices meet it with deliberate, bounded confrontation rather than brooding, the direction the remembrance family above shares.

::Selection:: The practices I have selected as sources of that variation:

1. [K222 — Mortality reflection](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K222%20%E2%80%94%20Mortality%20reflection): monthly, ten minutes, written against the prompt I am mortal, the timing is unknown, what does this make urgent, closing with one thing the month will actually change. Notice whether the writing lands as clarification or as dread; the distinction is the practice's own feedback.

2. [K226 — The deathbed test](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K226%20%E2%80%94%20The%20deathbed%20test): for significant decisions, the question of which choice the eighty-year-old self would endorse, the card naming the future-self research beneath it and the deathbed framing as editorial extension. Notice whether the far-end perspective changes the ranking of the options, not only the final pick.

3. [K316 — Mortality-to-action translation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K316%20%E2%80%94%20Mortality-to-action%20translation): five minutes of writing, then one insight translated into one action this week with a day and time attached. Watch whether the action happens; the writing without the action is the part that fades.

::Retention:: Retention is what people keep because it works for them. The cards state plainly that the evidence supports the direction of these practices rather than the exercises as scheduled here: the studies measured attitudes, discounting and gratitude, and the specific exercises, their wording and their schedules are the kernels' editorial adaptations of that evidence. A reader who keeps one monthly reflection or one deathbed question has what the theme offered.
