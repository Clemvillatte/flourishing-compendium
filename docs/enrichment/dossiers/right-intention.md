# Right Intention

> What is worth resolving on, and what does a resolved life look like?

Intention is where a life's autopilot gets interrupted: renunciation instead of craving, goodwill instead of ill will, harmlessness instead of cruelty. The resolve is a standing direction rather than a mood, and the traditions are honest that it fights the current of wanting, disliking and harming. A life cannot be lived without aims; which aims deserve my commitment is the question this page holds.

## Nearby terms

- Right resolve, right thought: other renderings of the same Pali factor, sammā saṅkappa; my card is [Right intention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Right%20intention), and nothing is split between the names.
- Intention: the general material on intending, and on the gap between intending and doing, lives in the sections below ([The intention-behavior gap](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20intention-behavior%20gap), [Implementation intentions](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Implementation%20intentions)); the factor is the path's own discipline of aim, not intending in general.
- Aspiration: the measured family of life goals ([Aspiration index](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Aspiration%20index), [Extrinsic aspirations](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Extrinsic%20aspirations)) answers what people aim at; it sits on the aim side of this page.
- Renunciation: the first resolve's material; the boundary against the Motivation and Goals dossiers is that those hold the general science of wanting and pursuing, while this page holds the path's discipline of aim.

## What the factor is

The [Magga-vibhanga Sutta](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn45/sn45.008.than.html) states the definition plainly:

> "And what is right resolve? Being resolved on renunciation, on freedom from ill will, on harmlessness: This is called right resolve." ([SN 45.8, Thanissaro translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn45/sn45.008.than.html))

[Right intention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Right%20intention) is the second factor of the [Eightfold Path](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Eightfold%20Path).

[The three trainings](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20three%20trainings) group it with [right view](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Right%20view) under wisdom.

SN 45.8 names right view, then right resolve, then [right speech](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Right%20speech) and [right action](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Right%20action). Translations differ (right resolve, right intention, right thought), but the three components are the same across them.

The factor is also where practice touches what [karma](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Karma) is, because the Buddha's definition of karma makes intention the hinge of action. The [Nibbedhika Sutta](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an06/an06.063.than.html) says it directly:

> "Intention, I tell you, is kamma. Intending, one does kamma by way of body, speech, & intellect." ([AN 6.63, Thanissaro translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an06/an06.063.than.html))

And like right view, the resolve is graded in two forms. The [Mahācattārīsaka Sutta](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.117.than.html) separates a resolve with effluents from a transcendent one:

> "And what is the right resolve with effluents, siding with merit, resulting in acquisitions? Resolve for renunciation, resolve for freedom from ill will, resolve for harmlessness. This is the right resolve with effluents... And what is the right resolve that is noble, without effluents, transcendent, a factor of the path? The thinking, directed thinking, resolve, mental fixity, mental transfixion, focused awareness, & verbal fabrications in one developing the noble path whose mind is noble, whose mind is without effluents, who is fully possessed of the noble path." ([MN 117, Thanissaro translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.117.than.html))

## The first resolve: renunciation

My positive rendering of the first resolve is simplicity. The first thing the resolve puts in question is what happens to wanting itself, and two accounts answer from different sides. [The insatiability of craving](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20insatiability%20of%20craving) is Schopenhauer's structural claim about desire: wanting does not end with getting, because wanting keeps regenerating itself. [The craving-to-addiction model](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20craving-to-addiction%20model) reads craving as a learned trigger-response loop that mindfulness can unwind (Brewer).

What replaces the wanting is answered differently. [Contentment with sufficiency](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Contentment%20with%20sufficiency) is the Daodejing's teaching on knowing when enough is enough; its chapter 44 prices the question against life itself. Epictetus contributes [contentment in station](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Contentment%20in%20station), the role-playing counsels from the Discourses.

The Indian traditions add release rather than contentment. [Vairagya](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Vairagya) is dispassion in Patañjali's Yoga Sūtra, the release of the grip on outcomes once they are seen as impermanent; [detachment from outcomes](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Detachment%20from%20outcomes) is the Bhagavad Gita's instruction to act from duty while releasing ownership of the results (2.47).

Psychology has its own construct for release: [nonattachment](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Nonattachment) names release from mental fixations, a flexible relationship with experience; [Sahdra, Shaver and Brown](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Non-attachment) built the measure for the Buddhist concept, distinguished from interpersonal attachment styles. In the Christian mystical register, [Gelassenheit](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Gelassenheit) is Eckhart's releasement, something a person actively cultivates.

The standard the renunciation-thought is held to comes from the Buddha's own account in the Two Sorts of Thinking discourse:

> "And as I remained thus heedful, ardent, & resolute, thinking imbued with renunciation arose in me. I discerned that 'Thinking imbued with renunciation has arisen in me; and that leads neither to my own affliction, nor to the affliction of others, nor to the affliction of both. It fosters discernment, promotes lack of vexation, & leads to Unbinding." ([MN 19, Thanissaro translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html))

## The second resolve: freedom from ill will

My positive rendering is goodwill, and here the Buddhist practice has a name of its own. [Metta (loving-kindness)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Metta%20%28loving-kindness%29) is the first of the [four immeasurables](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Four%20immeasurables): loving-kindness, [compassion](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Compassion), sympathetic joy and equanimity.

The neighbors give goodwill different foundations. For Hume, [benevolence](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Benevolence) is a natural sentiment treated in the second Enquiry.

The Christian materials are [agape](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Agape), the Pauline love given without regard to worth or return, and [enemy-love](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Enemy-love), the Gospel's instruction to love the enemy (Matthew 5).

Śāntideva's argument turns the enemy into an occasion: [enemy as teacher](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Enemy%20as%20teacher) is his case that the adversary provides the practice of forbearance. [The free person loves all](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20free%20person%20loves%20all) is Spinoza's version, the person living by reason desiring the good for everyone.

For an unskillful state that has taken hold, [the counter-affect](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20counter-affect) is the early discourses' own method: attention directed toward a theme or quality that counters the state (MN 20). In ethics, [the expanding circle](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20expanding%20circle) is Singer's name for the history and task of widening moral concern.

The social science of where ill will between groups comes from is [realistic conflict theory](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Realistic%20conflict%20theory), and the finding that structured [intergroup contact](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Intergroup%20contact) reduces it.

The training the discourse prescribes for the ill-will-thought is stated plainly, it is abandoned:

> "Whenever thinking imbued with ill will had arisen, I simply abandoned it, dispelled it, wiped it out of existence." ([MN 19, Thanissaro translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html))

## The third resolve: harmlessness

My positive rendering is refusing to add suffering. The Stoic and modern entries here are grouped by editorial connection: the path factor names a resolve, the neighbors answer kindred questions about harm, and I link them without claiming equivalence.

[Ahimsa](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Ahimsa) is the Indic principle of non-harm; I read it as a starting floor: whatever else a practice does, it begins by doing no injury. Two modern practices take non-harm as their subject: [nonviolent action](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Nonviolent%20action) is Gene Sharp's theory of power and his documented methods, and [nonviolent communication](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Nonviolent%20communication) is Rosenberg's process for reducing conflict in everyday interactions.

The Stoic contribution is a claim about disturbance rather than harm itself: [judgment adds the harm](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Judgment%20adds%20the%20harm), the position that events do not disturb people, the opinions about the events do (Enchiridion 5), a claim I connect editorially to the harmlessness resolve. And [the inner friend](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20inner%20friend) is Hecato's line via Seneca on making peace with oneself.

## How the resolve is trained

The factor is a resolve, and the discourses train it as a discipline of thought, which is what the translation "right thought" is reaching for. The Buddha's own method, before his awakening, was to sort his thinking:

> "So I made thinking imbued with sensuality, thinking imbued with ill will, & thinking imbued with harmfulness one sort, and thinking imbued with renunciation, thinking imbued with non-ill will, & thinking imbued with harmlessness another sort." ([MN 19, Thanissaro translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html))

The mechanism the discourse names is practice bending the mind:

> "Whatever a monk keeps pursuing with his thinking & pondering, that becomes the inclination of his awareness." ([MN 19, Thanissaro translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html))

The counter-affect has its own instruction in MN 20: attend to another theme, apart from the unskillful one, connected with what is skillful.

The modern research on intention is less about training the thought than about closing the gap between aim and act. [Akrasia (weakness of will)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Akrasia%20%28weakness%20of%20will%29) is the standing problem that knowing the aim does not secure acting on it. [The intention-behavior gap](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20intention-behavior%20gap) is Sheeran's measurement of how little intentions predict what people then do, and [implementation intentions](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Implementation%20intentions) are Gollwitzer's if-then plans, advance decisions that link a situation to a response.

The everyday scale of the discipline is [morning intention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Morning%20intention), the daily setting of one sentence of aim. [Intentionality](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Intentionality) is Brentano's revival of the scholastic term for the mind's directedness at its objects, a different sense of intention from the aiming of a resolve.

## What a resolved life aims at

The resolve needs an object, and the traditions and the research both ask what. [Purpose](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Purpose) is Frankl's account of purpose as the thing that lets a person bear almost any how. [Purpose-linked practice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Purpose-linked%20practice) is the self-transcendent purpose research on why people keep working at tedious tasks that matter (Yeager and colleagues). The Mahayana extension is [the bodhisattva vow](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20bodhisattva%20vow), the commitment to work for the liberation of all beings rather than one's own alone.

The measurement side asks the same question with instruments. The [Aspiration index](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Aspiration%20index) measures what people aim at, intrinsic versus extrinsic, and [extrinsic aspirations](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Extrinsic%20aspirations) is the finding that wealth, fame and image goals relate to lower well-being, and that a one-year study found attaining them showed no benefit and some links to ill-being.

The fit of aim and person has its own theory: [self-concordance theory](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Self-concordance%20theory) holds that goals fitting developing interests and core values sustain effort and make attaining more rewarding. Self-determination theory draws the line between the few innate needs whose satisfaction grows a person and the wants that can crowd them out in [distinguishing needs from wants](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Distinguishing%20needs%20from%20wants%20%28SDT%29), and [eudaimonic motivation, intrinsic goals](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Eudaimonic%20motivation%2C%20intrinsic%20goals) is my label for the pull toward intrinsic aspirations, affiliation, growth and contribution; the source paper frames the distinction as intrinsic versus extrinsic aspirations.

The path itself then moves from resolve to conduct, which is where this page ends: [action over intention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Action%20over%20intention) is the label for the right action factor, which comes fourth on the path, after right speech.

## Related questions and parallels

The questions below gather passages that sit side by side; whether the traditions disagree about them is an open research question. The last two hold the strongest pairings I could substantiate from opened carriers, and both stop short of a stated disagreement; what would settle each is a source on one side affirming what a source on the other denies.

### Is the resolve an abstention or a cultivation?

The proposition: freedom from ill will is the whole of the second resolve. The Magga-vibhanga Sutta states the resolve as an absence, being resolved on freedom from ill will. The Karaṇīya Metta Sutta describes goodwill as something cultivated without limit:

> "As a mother would risk her life to protect her child, her only child, even so should one cultivate a limitless heart with regard to all beings. With good will for the entire cosmos, cultivate a limitless heart: Above, below, & all around, unobstructed, without enmity or hate." ([Sutta Nipāta 1.8, Thanissaro translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/snp/snp.1.08.than.html))

Whether the two suttas disagree is the open question. Each can be read as answering the other's need: the resolve names the absence, the metta instruction names the cultivation, and nothing in either text rejects the other. The Two Sorts of Thinking discourse sits between them, treating non-ill will as a thought that is cultivated and kept; which reading the factor itself commits to is left open.

### Less wanting or better wanting?

The proposition: wanting is best cured by aiming it better. The path's instruction contrasts the sensuality-thought with the renunciation-thought, and warns that pursuing the former bends the mind:

> "If a monk keeps pursuing thinking imbued with sensuality, abandoning thinking imbued with renunciation, his mind is bent by that thinking imbued with sensuality." ([MN 19, Thanissaro translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html))

The motivation science studies what people aim at and what aiming does to them. Ryan and Deci, summarising Kasser and Ryan's aspiration studies:

> "They found, first, that placing strong relative importance on intrinsic aspirations was positively associated with well-being indicators such as self-esteem, self-actualization, and the inverse of depression and anxiety, whereas placing strong relative importance on extrinsic aspirations was negatively related to these well-being indicators." ([Ryan & Deci 2000, Self-Determination Theory and the Facilitation of Intrinsic Motivation, Social Development, and Well-Being](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_SDT.pdf))

The two findings run alongside each other: the aspiration studies report associations between what people emphasise and their well-being, and the path grades the aims it names. The question whether the resolve calls for less wanting or better wanting has a direct comparison inside the tradition's own commentary. Bodhi, writing on the renunciation resolve, marks desire for abandonment:

> "The Buddha's message of renunciation states exactly the opposite: the pull of desire is to be resisted and eventually abandoned. Desire is to be abandoned not because it is morally evil but because it is a root of suffering." ([Bhikkhu Bodhi, The Way to the End of Suffering](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/bodhi/waytoend.html))

Thanissaro, writing on the effort the path requires, marks desire as its working part:

> "It's worth noting here the centrality of desire in right effort. As AN 10:58 observes, all phenomena are rooted in desire. This observation applies to skillful as well as to unskillful phenomena. Without skillful desire, it would be impossible to develop the path (SN 51:15)." ([Thanissaro Bhikkhu, Right Mindfulness](https://accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/thanissaro/rightmindfulness.pdf))

The word desire is doing different work in the two passages. Bodhi's target is the desire that runs toward gratification, which he names a root of suffering; his own chapter on right effort distinguishes the energy behind practice from that pull, writing that the same factor "fuels desire, aggression, violence, and ambition on the one hand, and generosity, self-discipline, kindness, concentration, and understanding on the other" ([Bhikkhu Bodhi, The Way to the End of Suffering](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/bodhi/waytoend.html)). Thanissaro's skillful desire is the wish that moves practice. Read together, the two passages mark the same divide from two sides, craving to be abandoned and skillful wanting to be trained. What neither passage settles is whether the skillful wanting is itself relinquished when the work is done; that question stays open.

### Is a good intention worth anything on its own?

Kant's answer is that the good will is good in itself, whatever it manages to do:

> "A good will is good not because of what it performs or effects, not by its aptness for the attainment of some proposed end, but simply by virtue of the volition; that is, it is good in itself." ([Kant, Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals, Abbott translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-h/5682-h.htm))

> "Even if it should happen that, owing to special disfavour of fortune, or the niggardly provision of a step-motherly nature, this will should wholly lack power to accomplish its purpose... then, like a jewel, it would still shine by its own light, as a thing which has its whole value in itself." ([Kant, Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals, Abbott translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/5682/5682-h/5682-h.htm))

The path's texts test a thought by what it does. In MN 19 the Buddha grades each thought as it arises by its fruit:

> "Thinking imbued with sensuality has arisen in me; and that leads to my own affliction or to the affliction of others or to the affliction of both. It obstructs discernment, promotes vexation, & does not lead to Unbinding." ([MN 19, Thanissaro translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html))

> "Thinking imbued with renunciation has arisen in me; and that leads neither to my own affliction, nor to the affliction of others, nor to the affliction of both. It fosters discernment, promotes lack of vexation, & leads to Unbinding." ([MN 19, Thanissaro translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.019.than.html))

The two passages are held side by side; whether they disagree is open. Kant asks what makes a will worthy of esteem, the sutta asks which thoughts to cultivate on a path, and neither addresses the other; a person could hold both answers without contradiction. What differs is where each looks for an intention's worth: for Kant inside the willing itself, in the sutta in what the thought does.

Mill adds a third position that helps mark the boundary. He locates the rightness of an act in what it tends to do, not in the purity of the will:

> "The creed which accepts as the foundation of morals, Utility, or the Greatest Happiness Principle, holds that actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness." ([Mill, Utilitarianism, chapter II](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/11224/11224-h/11224-h.htm))

> "He who saves a fellow creature from drowning does what is morally right, whether his motive be duty, or the hope of being paid for his trouble." ([Mill, Utilitarianism, chapter II](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/11224/11224-h/11224-h.htm))

For Mill the act's rightness is decided by its tendency and the agent's worth by the motive — a division of the question rather than an answer to it, which is why this stands as a parallel rather than a dispute.

### Must the holy life mean leaving the household's duties?

MN 36 has the Buddha recall the reflection that started his path:

> "Household life is confining, a dusty path. Life gone forth is the open air. It isn't easy, living in a home, to practice the holy life totally perfect, totally pure, a polished shell. What if I, having shaved off my hair & beard and putting on the ochre robe, were to go forth from the household life into homelessness?" ([MN 36, Thanissaro translation](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN36.html))

> "So at a later time, when I was still young, black-haired, endowed with the blessings of youth in the first stage of life, having shaved off my hair & beard—though my parents wished otherwise and were grieving with tears on their faces—I put on the ochre robe and went forth from the home life into homelessness." ([MN 36, Thanissaro translation](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/MN/MN36.html))

The Bhagavad Gita, for its part, tells a warrior who wants to lay down his weapons that abstaining from the allotted work attains nothing:

> "Not by merely abstaining from work can one achieve freedom from reaction, nor by renunciation alone can one attain perfection." ([Bhagavad Gita 3.4](https://www.bhagavad-gita.us/bhagavad-gita-3-4/))

> "You have a right to perform your prescribed duty, but you are not entitled to the fruits of action." ([Bhagavad Gita 2.47](https://www.bhagavad-gita.us/bhagavad-gita-2-47/))

The two passages sit on either side of the question whether the holy life requires walking out of the duties a life was born into, and neither addresses the other: one tells of a departure that grieves a household, the other of a refusal to let a warrior abandon his. Whether the traditions disagree turns on a difference of target: the Gita's renunciation is the abandonment of action, the Buddhist going-forth is the abandonment of the home, and each could say the other has misdescribed what it is renouncing. The question left to the reader is whether the duties of a life are obstacles to be left behind or the very material of the practice.

### Is pleasure the aim of a well-led life?

Epicurus calls freedom from pain and disturbance pleasure, and makes it the aim:

> "For this reason we call pleasure the alpha and omega of a happy life." ([Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, Hicks trans., Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Epicurus/menoec.html))

> "By pleasure we mean the absence of pain in the body and of trouble in the soul." (same letter)

The Dhammapada verses turn away from sensual and heavenly pleasures toward the ending of craving:

> "There is no satisfying sensual desires, even with the rain of gold coins. For sensual pleasures give little satisfaction and much pain. Having understood this, the wise man finds no delight even in heavenly pleasures. The disciple of the Supreme Buddha delights in the destruction of craving." ([Dhammapada 186-187, Buddharakkhita trans.](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/dhp/dhp.14.budd.html))

The passages invite comparison, but they do not establish opposing answers about Epicurus's kind of pleasure. Epicurus keeps the word pleasure for the quiet condition that follows when pain and disturbance are gone, and the verses do not address that condition; what they turn away from is sensual and heavenly delight, which Epicurus also rejects. Whether the quiet condition Epicurus calls pleasure is the same thing the verses point to when they speak of the ending of craving is a question these two texts leave open.

Limits: one letter and two verses. The Letter to Menoeceus is a summary Epicurus wrote for a student, and the page extract I used garbled part of a longer passage, so I quote only the lines that read cleanly. I have not opened the Buddhist discourses that grant sensual pleasure a real gratification before listing its drawbacks.

## Where the traditions genuinely disagree

### Is anger ever fitting?

Aristotle includes appropriate anger in good temper and criticizes its absence when warranted. The chapter on good temper praises the man angry at the right things, and counts those who never anger at what should anger them as fools, the endurance of insult as slavish:

> "The man who is angry at the right things and with the right people, and, further, as he ought, when he ought, and as long as he ought, is praised." ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.5, Ross translation, Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.4.iv.html))

> "For those who are not angry at the things they should be angry at are thought to be fools... and to endure being insulted and put up with insult to one's friends is slavish." ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.5, Ross translation, Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.4.iv.html))

The Akkosa Sutta depicts the liberated person as free from anger even under insult ([SN 7.2](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn07/sn07.002.than.html)). A brahman insults the Buddha to his face; the Buddha replies that the insult belongs to its giver:

> "In the same way, brahman, that with which you have insulted me, who is not insulting; that with which you have taunted me, who is not taunting; that with which you have berated me, who is not berating: that I don't accept from you. It's all yours, brahman. It's all yours." ([SN 7.2, Thanissaro translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn07/sn07.002.than.html))

The Buddha then asks:

> "Whence is there anger for one free from anger, tamed, living in tune — one released through right knowing, calmed & Such." ([SN 7.2, Thanissaro translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn07/sn07.002.than.html))

Their ideals differ over whether excellent character includes appropriate anger ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics IV.5](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.4.iv.html); [SN 7.2](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn07/sn07.002.than.html)). The passages leave unresolved whether a firm, anger-free response can perform the protective role Aristotle associates with anger.

### Can the right inner state make killing in a duty-bound war blameless?

The shared question: whether a warrior who fights because duty requires it, and who is killed or kills, has done wrong and faces a bad destination. Krishna answers no, and says so twice in the second chapter of the Gita. A warrior who refuses the fight is the one who sins:

> "If, however, you do not perform your religious duty of fighting, then you will certainly incur sins for neglecting your duties and thus lose your reputation as a fighter." ([Bhagavad Gita 2.33](https://www.bhagavad-gita.us/bhagavad-gita-2-33/))

> "O son of Kunti, either you will be killed on the battlefield and attain the heavenly planets, or you will conquer and enjoy the earthly kingdom. Therefore get up and fight with determination." ([Bhagavad Gita 2.37](https://www.bhagavad-gita.us/bhagavad-gita-2-37/))

The next verse names the inner condition: "Do thou fight for the sake of fighting, without considering happiness or distress, loss or gain, victory or defeat—and by so doing you shall never incur sin" ([Bhagavad Gita 2.38](https://www.bhagavad-gita.us/bhagavad-gita-2-38/)). All three quotes come from one English rendering on the same site, so the wording is the translator's, and I have not compared it with the Sanskrit beyond the verse transliterations printed beside it.

In the Pali collection, a warrior chief asks the Buddha about the claim that a warrior killed while striving in battle is reborn among the gods of the fallen. The Buddha declines twice and answers on the third request:

> "When a warrior strives and struggles in battle, their mind is already low, degraded, and misdirected as they think: 'May these sentient beings be killed, slaughtered, slain, destroyed, or annihilated!' ... when his body breaks up, after death, he's reborn in the hell called 'The Fallen'." ([SN 42.3, Sujato trans.](https://suttacentral.net/sn42.3/en/sujato))

He calls the heaven claim "your wrong view" (same sutta). The Dhammapada states the rule without a battlefield exception: "Putting oneself in the place of another, one should not kill nor cause another to kill" ([Dhammapada 129, Buddharakkhita trans.](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/dhp/dhp.10.budd.html)).

Both sides ask what a soldier's inner state does to the moral weight of the act. The Gita holds that equanimity about outcomes keeps the fighter clear of sin. The Pali discourse holds that the state of mind in battle is hostile by its nature, so the heaven the old teachers promised is a mistaken view. Strictly, the two may be describing different warriors: Krishna's fighter has dropped attachment to results, while the Buddha's thinks of others as things to be destroyed. The disagreement that survives is whether such a detached fighter exists and whether detachment settles the question, and the Dhammapada verse draws no exception for it.

My lean, which is mine: for an essay on the resolve of harmlessness, the Buddha's answer is the stronger one, because the dossier's concern is what the intention does to the person holding it. I hold the Gita's position as the best statement of the opposing case, and I do not claim to have settled whether its detached warrior is psychologically possible.

Limits: three Gita verses and two Pali passages, no commentary on either side. Later Buddhist traditions and Indian readers of the Gita treat these questions at length, and I have not opened that material.

### Is freedom from pain itself a good?

Epicurus defines pleasure by what it removes rather than what it adds, and makes that condition the whole of the happy life:

> "When we say, then, that pleasure is the end and aim, we do not mean the pleasures of the prodigal or the pleasures of sensuality, as we are understood to do by some through ignorance, prejudice, or willful misrepresentation. By pleasure we mean the absence of pain in the body and of trouble in the soul." ([Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, Hicks trans., Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Epicurus/menoec.html))

> "For this reason we call pleasure the alpha and omega of a happy life. Pleasure is our first and kindred good." (same letter)

Seneca, writing to Lucilius on the same question, refuses the condition that rank. He considers health, rest and freedom from pain, the very components of Epicurus's definition:

> "'What, then,' comes the retort, 'if good health, rest, and freedom from pain are not likely to hinder virtue, shall you not seek all these?' Of course I shall seek them, but not because they are goods — I shall seek them because they are according to nature and because they will be acquired through the exercise of good judgment on my part. What, then, will be good in them? This alone — that it is a good thing to choose them." ([Seneca, Moral Letter 92, On the Happy Life, Gummere trans.](https://monadnock.net/seneca/92.html))

> "Now if we are agreed on this point, it is natural that we shall be agreed on the following also — namely, that the happy life depends upon this and this alone: our attainment of perfect reason." (same letter)

Both writers would choose freedom from pain. Their disagreement concerns what makes that choice worthwhile. Epicurus calls the absence of pain part of pleasure, his first and kindred good. Seneca refuses the painless condition that status: what is good, he says, is choosing it well. Agreement about what to seek does not amount to agreement about what is good.

Seneca also recalls Epicurus, tortured on one occasion by strangury and on another by the incurable pain of an ulcerated stomach, calling such days the happiest of all, and turns the report to his own argument: if a man can call such days happy, happiness does not rest on the absence of pain. That is Seneca reading Epicurus for his own case; Epicurus is not there to answer.

My lean, which is mine: Seneca's position reads as the sharper edge of the renunciation resolve, because it questions the wanting inside the remedy itself; Epicurus's reads as the more liveable default, and I notice I cannot prove from these letters which life actually lands further from suffering.

Limits: two letters, one on each side. The Internet Classics page for the Epicurus letter prints a garbled sentence inside its passage on prudence, so I quote only the lines that read cleanly. I have not opened the Epicurean texts Seneca read, nor Seneca's longer dialogue On the Happy Life.

## Practice: variation, selection, retention

::Variation:: What tends to need varying on this theme is the aim's default setting: wanting inherited rather than chosen, ill will rehearsed until it feels like seeing, harm done by habit rather than decision. The adaptive direction is the resolve itself, aims examined and chosen, ill will noticed as a thought that can be abandoned, harmlessness kept as a floor under whatever else is undertaken.

::Selection:: The kernels I have selected as sources of that variation:

1. [K156 — Morning intention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K156%20%E2%80%94%20Morning%20intention): write or speak one sentence of intention for the day: what you want to stand for, what you want to practice, what matters today.

2. [K149 — Values clarification](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K149%20%E2%80%94%20Values%20clarification): what you want your life to stand for, written in three to five domains, values as directions rather than goals.

3. [K150 — Values card-sort](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K150%20%E2%80%94%20Values%20card-sort): a deck of values sorted by importance until the top ten stand out.

4. [K153 — Implementation intentions](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K153%20%E2%80%94%20Implementation%20intentions): the if-then plan that specifies situation and response in advance, reducing reliance on in-the-moment decision-making.

5. [K154 — Mental contrasting (WOOP)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K154%20%E2%80%94%20Mental%20contrasting%20%28WOOP%29): wish, best outcome, main obstacle, plan; the contrast that links aspiration to resistance.

6. [K155 — Enough lines](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K155%20%E2%80%94%20Enough%20lines): sufficiency defined in advance for each domain, while the judgment is still cold.

7. [K161 — Loving-kindness meditation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K161%20%E2%80%94%20Loving-kindness%20meditation): phrases of goodwill extended from yourself to a difficult person and all beings.

8. [K247 — Motivational interviewing (self-directed)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K247%20%E2%80%94%20Motivational%20interviewing%20%28self-directed%29): the ruler question asked of a change you are ambivalent about.

9. [K227 — Time-affluence audit](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K227%20%E2%80%94%20Time-affluence%20audit): a periodic review of whether the trades of time for money can be reversed.

::Retention:: A resolve proves itself in what a life starts doing differently, not in how the sentence of intention reads. The morning sentence, the enough line or the goodwill phrase has earned its place when, a week on, the aim still names what you actually did; what stays is a matter of fit between the resolve and the life it enters.

## Story: the stories that carry the theme

The stories that best carry the theme's aspects, each retold in a line; the full telling lives on the card.

### The three resolves in narrative

[Meghiya Finds the Right Grove and the Wrong Thoughts](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST376%20%E2%80%94%20Meghiya%20Finds%20the%20Right%20Grove%20and%20the%20Wrong%20Thoughts): Meghiya gets the grove he wanted and, alone under the tree, meets thoughts of sensuality, ill will and harm; wanting the place to practise was not the same as knowing what to cultivate.

[The Cowherd After the Harvest](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST352%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Cowherd%20After%20the%20Harvest): the Buddha's own two pictures of watching the mind, the cowherd guarding ripening crops with a stick, then resting in the shade simply mindful of the cows.

### Choosing what to aim at

[The Aspiration Index](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST301%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Aspiration%20Index): Kasser and Ryan measure what people value and find the relative priority of money, image and fame, controlling for actual income, predicts worse outcomes.

### Testing the aim

[The Kālāmas Test It](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST066%20%E2%80%94%20The%20K%C4%81l%C4%81mas%20Test%20It): teachers contradict each other in the Kālāmas' town, and the Buddha tells them to know for themselves which qualities, adopted and carried out, lead to harm and suffering and which to welfare and happiness.

### Ill will and its costs

[The Quarrel at Kosambī](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST085%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Quarrel%20at%20Kosamb%C4%AB): monks at Kosambī quarrel over a point of monastic rule and harden into two factions; the Buddha's attempts to reconcile them fail and he leaves, and the episode's teaching is the founder's limits, reconciliation could not be imposed.

### Harmlessness at the far end

[The Bodhisattva and the Tigress](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST086%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Bodhisattva%20and%20the%20Tigress): the bodhisattva finds a tigress too weak from hunger to feed her cubs and lies down where she can reach him. The tradition places this extreme case at the edge of the map as the far end of harmlessness; it is a story of what compassion can cost, not an instruction to readers.
