# Freedom

> What is a person fleeing when she flees her own freedom, and what does she exercise when she takes it back?

The freedom material gathered here arrives with a cost built in: to be free is to be the one who has to choose, and much of what people do with their freedom is look for ways to give it back. My reading is that the accounts divide on exactly that point. One wing asks why the flight happens, and its answer runs from Fromm's automaton conformity to Sartre's waiter, whose every gesture is a little too exact. The other wing refuses the flight outright: Sartre's radical freedom holds that giving up freedom is itself a choice, and the accounts around it, Rousseau's chains, the Cynic's poverty, Beauvoir's interdependence, are about what exercising the thing actually involves.

I hold the boundary with two neighbours deliberately. The agency-side of freedom, the capacity to act from one's own understanding, Berlin's positive freedom and Frankfurt's second-order desires, is held by Agency. The felt side, Kierkegaard's dizziness before possibility, is held by Anxiety. What neither holds is the flight-and-will wing, the analyses of why freedom is given away and the accounts of freedom as an exercise rather than a status, and that is this dossier's territory.

### Nearby terms

- [Positive freedom](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Positive%20freedom): Berlin's wish to be one's own master; the agency-side word for much of this territory, mapped by Agency.

- [The dizziness of freedom](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20dizziness%20of%20freedom): Kierkegaard's image for anxiety before possibility; mapped by Anxiety, whose dossier holds the Kierkegaardian wing.

- [Second-order desires](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Second-order%20desires): Frankfurt's endorsement structure, the person as the one who can disown a desire; mapped by Agency.

- [Autonomy](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Autonomy): the capacity-side word for the same territory; see Agency.

- [Bad faith](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Bad%20faith): Sartre's name for the flight; the story section carries its most famous example.

## The flight: why freedom is given away

Fromm's diagnosis is the clinical entry point, [escape from freedom](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Escape%20from%20freedom) as he names the modern problem. In modern society the majority find a solution in conformity: the person who gives up the individual self and becomes an automaton, identical with millions of other automatons, need not feel alone and anxious any more. His description of it is blunt:

> "To put it briefly, the individual ceases to be himself; he adopts entirely the kind of personality offered to him by cultural patterns; and he therefore becomes exactly as all others are and as they expect him to be. ... But the price he pays, however, is high; it is the loss of his self." ([Fromm, Escape from Freedom, panarchy excerpt](https://www.panarchy.org/fromm/escapefreedom.html))

The broader frame is that the individual self emerged into a world it was unable to realize its freedom in, overwhelmed by anxiety, doubt and a feeling of powerlessness, and so reaches for secondary bonds. Sartre's café waiter is the same flight in a single body. [ST269 — The Waiter's Dance](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST269%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Waiter%27s%20Dance) describes movements too exact, too quick, too waiter-like: he is playing at being a waiter, and the excess is the giveaway, the attempt to become the role in the way a table is a table and thereby escape the freedom of being a person for whom the role is a choice. The card's diagnostic is portable: a role performed with too much precision is usually such a flight, and the remedy is catching oneself mid-gesture.

The measurable face of the flight-and-will line is [psychological reactance](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Psychological%20reactance), Brehm's motivational state when a freedom is threatened: the boomerang effect, the anti-drinking message that produces a drink, and the sideways restorations, talking up the liking of what was threatened, derogating the source, denying the threat. Where Fromm and Sartre describe giving freedom away, reactance describes defending it, sometimes past the point of sense.

Fromm and Sartre read the same conformity with different emphases. Fromm describes conformity as relief, a solution the majority find for anxiety and powerlessness. Sartre denies that circumstances determine a single response, so on his reading the conformity is still a chosen significance. These passages do not establish a contradiction between the accounts. I read the two as layers of one account: the Frommian description of the machinery is the more useful description, and the Sartrean refusal of the excuse is the more useful demand.

## The exercise: freedom as what a person does

[Radical freedom](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Radical%20freedom) is Sartre's refusal of the flight at the level of theory: a person's situation never determines their response to it, and the Stanford Encyclopedia's summary of the account is categorical:

> "No factual state, whatever it may be, can cause consciousness to respond to it in any one way. Rather, we make a choice (usually pre-reflective) about the significance of that factual state for us, and the ends and motives that we adopt in relation to it." ([Reynolds & Renaudie, SEP, Sartre](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sartre/))

The formula the tradition carries is being condemned to be free, no excuses behind us nor justifications before us. The point is not freedom as power, not the freedom to do or achieve anything: the person, in Sartre's vocabulary the for-itself, is always in a factical situation, and what is chosen, usually pre-reflectively, is the significance that situation has. The affective signature is [anguish (angoisse)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Anguish%20%28angoisse%29): the vertigo at the cliff edge, fear not of falling but of throwing oneself over, the disclosure that no past resolution and no external constraint stands between the person and the act.

The account is kept honest by [situated freedom](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Situated%20freedom), the correction the material carries inside itself: freedom exercised within limits not chosen by the chooser, with the constraint-provision insight that the bodies, habits and situations we chafe against are the very things that give weight and traction to a free act. Nothing is done all by itself, and neither is anything done all alone. On this dossier's reading the two are held together on purpose: the situated critique is the card's own correction of the radical account, and the correction is part of the account, not a rival to it.

## The political edges

Rousseau frames the double question. [Natural liberty versus the chains](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Natural%20liberty%20versus%20the%20chains) opens with the famous diagnosis and closes the loop with the famous definition:

> "Man is born free; and everywhere he is in chains. One thinks himself the master of others, and still remains a greater slave than they." ([Rousseau, The Social Contract, Book I, ch. 1, Cole trans., Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/46333/46333-h/46333-h.htm))

> "for the mere impulse of appetite is slavery, while obedience to a law which we prescribe to ourselves is liberty." ([Rousseau, The Social Contract, Book I, ch. 8, Cole trans., Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/46333/46333-h/46333-h.htm))

The chains are not all removed at once, and the liberty restored is a self-binding one, obedience to law one prescribes to oneself. [The Cynic's freedom](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20Cynic%27s%20freedom) is the ancient form of the same question: freedom in three forms, eleutheria, autarkeia and parrhesia, with the poverty that frees one from having to pay court to a ruler. The exchange that carries the whole stance runs two lines: "Ask of me any boon you like." "Stand out of my sunlight." ([Diogenes, quoted at Philoscopia](https://www.philoscopia.com/en/quotes/diogenes/))

Beauvoir widens the exercise past the single person. [The interdependence of freedoms](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20interdependence%20of%20freedoms) argues that a project has meaning only in a world other people have taken up, so the wish to be free already contains the wish that others be free:

> "To will oneself free is also to will others free. This will is not an abstract formula." ([Beauvoir, The Ethics of Ambiguity, Part II, ch. 1](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/ethics/de-beauvoir/ambiguity/ch02.htm))

## Where the traditions genuinely disagree

### Is the liberty that binds still liberty?

Rousseau's answer does not deny the compulsion; he re-describes it. The general will is the citizen's own will at its truest, so the body's compulsion of the dissenter is presented as freedom-preserving:

> "Whoever refuses to obey the general will shall be compelled to do so by the whole body. This means nothing less than that he will be forced to be free; for this is the condition which, by giving each citizen to his country, secures him against all personal dependence." ([Rousseau, The Social Contract, Book I, ch. 7, Cole trans., Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/46333/46333-h/46333-h.htm))

Constant, writing in 1819, answers from the other side of the Revolution. He saw its leaders as "steeped in ancient views which are no longer valid", among the ancients "the individual, almost always sovereign in public affairs, was a slave in all his private relations", and he read Rousseau as having "furnished deadly pretexts for more than one kind of tyranny": "by transposing into our modern age an extent of social power, of collective sovereignty, which belonged to other centuries, this sublime genius, animated by the purest love of liberty, has nevertheless furnished deadly pretexts for more than one kind of tyranny" ([Constant, The Liberty of the Ancients Compared with that of the Moderns, 1819 speech, text as published in Cours de politique constitutionnelle, 1820, Internet Archive](https://archive.org/download/benjamin-constant-the-liberty-of-the-ancients-compared-with-that-of-the-moderns-1820/Benjamin%20Constant%20-%20The%20Liberty%20of%20the%20Ancients%20compared%20with%20that%20of%20the%20Moderns%20%5B1820%5D_text.pdf)).

The disagreement turns on what the binding law is. For Rousseau the self-prescribed law is the one form of obedience that is liberty, appetite being slavery; for Constant the same self-binding, once it belongs to a collective rather than a person, rides over the private independence it was meant to secure. My own lean is Constant's, on this political question: the part of liberty a modern person cannot do without is the guarantee of private life against the social body, and the excerpt gives no account of what would stop the general will there. The practice section keeps a personal-scale analogy to Rousseau's self-binding, binder and bound being one person, but that is an analogy, not his political claim.

### Is choice an explanatory starting point?

Skinner's behaviorism is the strongest opposition the exercise wing faces, and its target is precise: not the existence of persons but the treating of choice as an uncaused cause, a place where explanation stops.

> "What is being abolished is autonomous man – the inner man, the homunculus, the possessing demon, the man defended by the literatures of freedom and dignity. His abolition has long been overdue. Autonomous man is a device used to explain what we cannot explain in any other way. He has been constructed from our ignorance, and as our understanding increases, the very stuff of which he is composed vanishes. Science does not dehumanize man, it de-homunculizes him." ([Skinner, Beyond Freedom and Dignity, pp. 199-200, quoted in the Cambridge University Press excerpt](https://assets.cambridge.org/97811089/27628/excerpt/9781108927628_excerpt.pdf))

The other side of the comparison is already on the page: the SEP's summary of Sartre holds that no factual state, whatever it may be, can cause consciousness to respond to it in any one way, and Frankl's logotherapy names the choice of one's attitude in any given set of circumstances as the last of the human freedoms, the one thing that cannot be taken ([Reynolds and Renaudie, SEP, Sartre](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sartre/); [Frankl, quoted in Wikipedia, Logotherapy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logotherapy)).

The disagreement turns on what the word choice explains. Skinner rejects an autonomous chooser as the explanation of behavior and directs the search toward its causes; Sartre's account denies that a factual situation determines one response to it. Frankl's camp testimony, that the choice of attitude remained when everything else was taken, corroborates the Sartre side as a witness without settling the causal question.

The dispute is not resolved here, and I do not think the sources resolve it. My tentative editorial position is that Skinner is right that unexplained is not uncaused while Sartre is right that no fact specifies its own meaning, which leaves the exercise wing its meaning-level claim while conceding the mechanism-level one; but that is a way of stating the two positions side by side, not a refutation of either, and the causal question stays open.

### Is the other's freedom a threat or the medium of mine?

Sartre's account of the Look is the hardest case for shared freedom. In Being and Nothingness the Other's gaze fixes me as an object, and the encyclopedia's summary of the account is categorical:

> "It is useless for human reality to seek to escape this dilemma: one must either transcend the Other or allow oneself to be transcended by him. The essence of the relations between consciousness is not the Mitsein (being-with); it is conflict." ([Sartre, Being and Nothingness, p. 429, quoted in Reynolds and Renaudie, SEP, Sartre](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/sartre/))

Beauvoir, Sartre's contemporary and critic, turns the relation the other way. The Ethics of Ambiguity holds that others' freedom is a condition of realizing one's own:

> "The ends which it gives itself must be unable to be transcended by any reflection, but only the freedom of other men can extend them beyond our life. ... Only the freedom of others keeps each one of us from hardening in the absurdity of facticity." ([Beauvoir, The Ethics of Ambiguity, Part II, ch. 1](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/ethics/de-beauvoir/ambiguity/ch02.htm))

The disagreement turns on what the other's freedom does to mine. Sartre's account in Being and Nothingness has the other's freedom compete with mine at the root: to be seen is to be fixed, and every relation between two consciousnesses re-enacts the contest. Beauvoir holds that without others' freedom mine has nothing to work on: projects are defined only by their interference with other projects, and "no man can save himself alone" ([Beauvoir, The Ethics of Ambiguity, Part II, ch. 1](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/ethics/de-beauvoir/ambiguity/ch02.htm)).

Andrew reads Beauvoir as departing from the conflictual account of relations between freedoms in Being and Nothingness: "Contrary to Sartre's idea that two freedoms are always in conflict, Beauvoir emphasizes that individuals have an important alliance and affinity through their mutual recognition" ([Andrew, in The Cambridge Companion to Simone de Beauvoir, ed. Card, Cambridge UP sample](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805217/90963/sample/9780521790963ws.pdf)). Kirkpatrick cautions that construing Beauvoir and Sartre as disagreeing remains disputable, because the texts support both compatible and incompatible readings ([Kirkpatrick, "Beauvoir and Sartre's 'disagreement' about freedom", Philosophy Compass](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:d1561d16-c16c-4d1c-be62-9aaa9cd8e53c)).

My lean is Beauvoir's for the exercise wing, and the dossier's own interdependence card carries that lean; but Sartre's conflict thesis is not answered by it, because the Look is a real hazard, and any account of shared freedom has to say what it does with being seen.

### Does a free act have to escape the causal chain?

Hume's section "Of Liberty and Necessity" in the [Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/9662/pg9662.txt) defines liberty so that it survives determinism. Liberty is the ability to act on one's will, and he treats the old quarrel as a verbal one:

> "By liberty, then, we can only mean a power of acting or not acting, according to the determinations of the will; that is, if we choose to remain at rest, we may; if we choose to move, we also may. Now this hypothetical liberty is universally allowed to belong to every one who is not a prisoner and in chains."

He adds that he hopes "to make it appear that all men have ever agreed in the doctrine both of necessity and of liberty, according to any reasonable sense, which can be put on these terms; and that the whole controversy has hitherto turned merely upon words."

Kant, in the [Critique of Practical Reason](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5683/pg5683.txt) (Abbott's translation), rejects the kind of move Hume makes. He describes the view that actions are free because their causes lie in the agent's own ideas and desires, and answers:

> "This is a wretched subterfuge with which some persons still let themselves be put off, and so think they have solved, with a petty word- jugglery, that difficult problem, at the solution of which centuries have laboured in vain."

His reason is that a cause which lies in the antecedent state is "no longer in his power" when the subject has to act. Such freedom, he says, would be "nothing better than the freedom of a turnspit, which, when once it is wound up, accomplishes its motions of itself." Kant does not name Hume in this passage, so the pairing is mine; the target is the general "comparative" notion of freedom, which Hume's definition fits.

The disagreement turns on what freedom has to deliver. For Hume, acting as one chooses is all that the word can reasonably mean, and the rest is confusion; he even says the doctrines of necessity and liberty are "absolutely essential" to morality. For Kant, a moral law that holds people responsible needs independence from the time-series of causes, which he calls transcendental freedom, and anything weaker leaves "no room" for it.

My lean, which is mine and not the sources', is that Hume's definition is the one the exercise wing can use, since a person's practice of choosing within constraint is what the dossier tracks. Kant's objection still stands as a question about responsibility that the practical definition does not answer by itself.

Limits: Hume is read from one section and Kant from one passage. Neither translation is checked against the original language, and later compatibilist and incompatibilist literature is not covered here.

## Practice: the constraint, and what remains inside it

::Variation:: What tends to need varying on this theme is the lived-as-determined default, the reading of one's own situation as fixed fact end to end. The practices meet it by shrinking the frame to what is genuinely not up to you and enumerating what remains.

::Selection:: Choose one exercise and run it on one constraint, the one that will not move this year.

1. [K315 — Freedom in constraint](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K315%20%E2%80%94%20Freedom%20in%20constraint): name the constraint in one sentence and mark what is genuinely not up to you about it, grieve or curse it for a minute and stop arguing with it, then write every choice that remains yours inside the frame, pick three, and make one concrete move in each this week. The payload is the difference the constraint makes while the moves are being made.

::Retention:: This is an editorial combination rather than a validated protocol. The kernel's warrant is Frankl's, the last of the human freedoms as the choice of one's own way inside circumstances that take everything else, and its measured translation is psychological flexibility, the response repertoire that stays healthy when the situation does not change. The cards state the evidence for the direction, not for the exercise itself. What people keep is the enumerated frame and the diagnostic: catching oneself mid-gesture in a role performed too exactly.
