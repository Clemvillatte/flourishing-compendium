# Anxiety

> Is anxiety a fault to be removed, a signal to be read, or a cost of being a creature that can foresee, and what actually loosens it?

Anxiety is the tax a creature pays for being able to foresee, and the bill arrives whether or not the foreseen thing happens. Whether that tax is a fault to be removed, a signal to be read, or simply a cost of foresight is a live disagreement between the traditions and inside the research. What helps is mapped better than the question of what it is: the practices that ease unnecessary suffering exist, and they work without pretending that all unease is a malfunction.

A caution that belongs at the start. Nothing on this page is clinical advice. Where a card touches diagnosis, it says so itself, and a person in distress is better served by a clinician than by a wiki.

### Nearby terms

The words around anxiety are used loosely, so I keep them apart unless a source joins them. What each source does say, in the sources' own words:

Stress: Lazarus and Folkman define it as a relationship, not a feeling: "psychological stress is a particular relationship between the person and the environment that is appraised by the person as taxing or exceeding his or her resources" ([Lazarus and Folkman, quoted in a PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3110961/)). I hold it under [Stress appraisal](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Stress%20appraisal) and the stress cards in the group below. The sources opened for this page do not say that stress is a form of anxiety, and this page does not say so either.

Fear: Öhman and Mineka propose "an evolved module for fear elicitation and fear learning" ([Öhman and Mineka 2001, abstract](http://people.whitman.edu/~herbrawt/classes/390/Ohman.pdf)). Kierkegaard sets anxiety apart from fear by its object, a distinction taken up in the first divergence below. Fear and courage are held in their own group.

Worry: Borkovec and colleagues describe it as "a predominance of negatively valenced verbal thought activity" ([Borkovec, Ray and Stöber 1998](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/19832/1/Borkovec%20Ray%20St%C3%B6ber%20%281998%29%20COTR.pdf)). It is held under [Worry research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Worry%20research) and [Worry theory cognitive avoidance Borkovec](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Worry%20theory%20cognitive%20avoidance%20Borkovec). Whether worry is a part of anxiety is not settled by what is quoted here.

Angst: Heidegger's term; the Stanford Encyclopedia quotes him: "Anxiety is the mood in which I experience immediately the finitude of my future horizon of possibilities" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Heidegger](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/)). Held by [Angst](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Angst) and [Das Nichts](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Das%20Nichts).

Dread and apprehension: I have no card titled for either, so no separate entry is mapped.

## The map: the entries on anxiety

### What anxiety is, in philosophy

[Anxiety about the future](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Anxiety%20about%20the%20future): Kierkegaard's Concept of Anxiety (1844) locates anxiety in possibility: the felt pull of possibilities, in which we are both drawn toward and repelled by one.

[The dizziness of freedom](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20dizziness%20of%20freedom): Kierkegaard's image for anxiety: fear has a definite object, while anxiety responds to possibility itself.

[Facing the fear](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Facing%20the%20fear): the thread Kierkegaard pulled through The Concept of Anxiety, written under the pseudonym Vigilius Haufniensis.

[Facing the monster](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Facing%20the%20monster): the monster is what one is drawn toward and repelled by at once, a description Kierkegaard takes from children's play.

[Angst](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Angst): the fundamental mood in which the ground of things slips away; for Heidegger, the disclosure of finitude as such.

[Das Nichts](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Das%20Nichts): Heidegger's 1929 lecture claims that the nothing is met in a particular mood, anxiety.

[Existential confrontation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Existential%20confrontation): facing the basic conditions of human life, death, freedom, isolation and meaninglessness, instead of avoiding them.

[The uncanny](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20uncanny): a specific kind of fright that comes from the familiar and not from the alien.

### Fear and courage

[Fear](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Fear): the emotion that mobilizes defense against threat; for Öhman and Mineka, the central motive state of an evolved module.

[The classification of fears](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20classification%20of%20fears): Epicurus's Letter to Menoeceus addresses the two fears he treats as the main sources of disturbance: fear of the gods and fear of death.

[The examination of fear](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20examination%20of%20fear): Epicurus's method: take a fear, hold it up to reasoning, and watch it dissolve or shrink to its real size.

[Epicurean argument against the fear of death](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Epicurean%20argument%20against%20the%20fear%20of%20death): death is the privation of all sentience, so it cannot harm the person who no longer exists, and fearing it is a groundless pain.

[Courage](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Courage): one of the four cardinal virtues: acting well in the face of fear.

[Courage — action despite fear](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Courage%20%E2%80%94%20action%20despite%20fear): Aristotle's definition: the brave person does not lack fear but fears the right things.

[Authentic courage without props](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Authentic%20courage%20without%20props): Aristotle's distinction between courage proper and the things that merely look like it.

[Courage built gradually](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Courage%20built%20gradually): Aristotle describes courage as formed through habituation.

[Moral courage research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Moral%20courage%20research): Hannah, Avolio and Walumbwa's working definition of the research field.

[The fearless mind](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20fearless%20mind): the composure the Buddha shows in the Abhayaraja-kumara Sutta (MN 58).

[Metacognitive model of anxiety and depression (Wells)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Metacognitive%20model%20of%20anxiety%20and%20depression%20%28Wells%29): positive and negative metacognitive beliefs maintain worry and rumination.

[Metacognitive therapy (Wells)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Metacognitive%20therapy%20%28Wells%29): treating beliefs about thinking, such as "worrying is useful", instead of the content of thoughts.

[Intolerance of uncertainty](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Intolerance%20of%20uncertainty): a dispositional incapacity to endure the aversive response triggered by uncertain situations.

[Triple Vulnerability Model](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Triple%20Vulnerability%20Model): Barlow's model of the origins of anxiety and mood disorders through three separate but interacting vulnerabilities.

[Distress tolerance (DBT)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Distress%20tolerance%20%28DBT%29): Leyro and colleagues distinguish the perceived capacity to withstand aversive states from the behavioural act of withstanding them.

## Where the traditions genuinely disagree

### Is anxiety the same as fear?

The shared question: does anxiety differ from fear in kind, or only in how far off the danger is? Kierkegaard says it differs in kind, and puts the difference in what the feeling is about. The version below comes from a newspaper essay that quotes The Concept of Anxiety:

> "Anxiety may be compared with dizziness. He whose eye happens to look down into the yawning abyss becomes dizzy. But what is the reason for this? It is just as much in his own eyes as in the abyss . . . Hence, anxiety is the dizziness of freedom." ([Kierkegaard, The Concept of Anxiety, as quoted in a New York Times essay](https://web.stanford.edu/~jsabol/existentialism/NYT_Kierkegaard.pdf))

Heidegger takes the same line further and reads the mood as a disclosure of what a person is:

> "Anxiety is the mood in which I experience immediately the finitude of my future horizon of possibilities" ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, Heidegger](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/))

The research tradition treats fear as a system with a defined job and rules of operation. Öhman and Mineka say it is activated by stimuli that are fear relevant in an evolutionary perspective, automatically, and that it is "relatively impenetrable to cognitive control" ([Öhman and Mineka, Psychological Review 2001, abstract](http://people.whitman.edu/~herbrawt/classes/390/Ohman.pdf)). The two accounts are pitched at different levels. One asks what a mood tells a person about their situation; the other asks how a defence circuit is triggered. Whether the first kind of anxiety and the second kind of fear are one thing under two descriptions is not settled in the passages quoted.

### Is the stress response a danger or an adaptation?

The shared question: should a person try to quiet the body's stress reaction, or work with it? McEwen's review gives the physiological case that it is both, by timing:

> "Yet, hormones associated with stress protect the body in the short-run and promote adaptation ('allostasis')." ([McEwen, Physiological Reviews 2007, abstract](https://www.healthandenvironment.org/uploads-old/stress%20adaptation%20brain%20McEwen%2007.pdf))

The same abstract describes the other side: events in daily life that produce "a type of chronic stress and lead over time to wear and tear on the body". Crum, Salovey and Achor add that what a person believes about stress is itself a variable:

> "Together, these 3 studies suggest that stress mindset is a distinct and meaningful variable in determining the stress response." ([Crum, Salovey and Achor, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 2013, abstract](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/crum_salovey_anchor_2013_-_rethinking_stress.pdf))

The first source is about the body over time, the second about belief at the moment of stress. Neither abstract says that believing stress is helpful makes chronic stress harmless, and this dossier does not claim it.

### Is worry preparation or avoidance?

The shared question: when someone goes round and round in worry, are they getting ready or hiding? Borkovec and colleagues offer a functional reading in which worry can be a way of not feeling:

> "In this functional way, worry may be a type of cognitive avoidance to perceived dangers." ([Borkovec, Ray and Stöber, Cognitive Therapy and Research 1998](https://kar.kent.ac.uk/19832/1/Borkovec%20Ray%20St%C3%B6ber%20%281998%29%20COTR.pdf))

The same paper notes that people do believe their worry protects them: "people do attribute avoidant functions to their worrying, and there may be good reasons for these attributions." The intolerance-of-uncertainty model gives those beliefs a formal role:

> "The Intolerance of Uncertainty Model (IUM) of Generalized Anxiety Disorder (GAD) attributes a key role to Intolerance of Uncertainty (IU), and additional roles to Positive Beliefs about Worry (PBW), Negative Problem Orientation (NPO), and Cognitive Avoidance (CA), in the development and maintenance of worry, the core feature of GAD." ([Intolerance of Uncertainty Model, PMC article, abstract](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5088195/))

The two models share a structure: worry feels useful and is kept up for that reason. They differ on what is doing the work, avoidance of images in the first, intolerance of not knowing in the second. The abstracts quoted do not say which is the better account, or whether they describe different people.

### Does reasoning, rehearsal or approach loosen fear?

The shared question: if a fear is out of proportion, what moves it? Epicurus bets on argument:

> "Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not." ([Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, Hicks translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Epicurus/menoec.html))

Seneca bets on rehearsal, and says why:

> "Therefore, nothing ought to be unexpected by us. Our minds should be sent forward in advance to meet all problems, and we should consider, not what is wont to happen, but what can happen." ([Seneca, Moral Letters to Lucilius, Letter 91, Gummere translation](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_91))

Clinical practice bets on approach. Craske and colleagues write:

> "Exposure therapy, or repeated approach toward fear provoking stimuli, has been a mainstay of cognitive behavioral therapy for anxiety disorders since its inception." ([Craske and colleagues, Behaviour Research and Therapy 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4114726/))

The three methods target different parts of a fear. Argument works on a belief, rehearsal on surprise, approach on avoidance. The Öhman and Mineka abstract above says the fear module is relatively impenetrable to cognitive control, which is a reason to doubt that argument alone will do the whole job. The sources held do not compare the methods head to head.

## Practice: variation, selection, retention

::Variation:: What tends to need varying is the relationship to the feeling, not the feeling itself. The look-alikes of calm are avoidance, distraction and reassurance-seeking, which relieve anxiety in the moment and tend to keep it going. The adaptive moves are to ask what the anxiety is about, to test the feared prediction, to approach what was avoided, and to settle the body.

::Selection:: The kernels I have selected as sources of that variation:

1. [Anxiety-as-signal](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K314%20%E2%80%94%20Anxiety-as-signal): before trying to reduce anxiety, ask what it is about and whether it points to something that needs attention.

2. [Decatastrophizing](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K130%20%E2%80%94%20Decatastrophizing): ask what the worst outcome is, how you would cope, what is most likely, and what is best.

3. [Catastrophe testing](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K272%20%E2%80%94%20Catastrophe%20testing): ask the Stoic questions: is this really terrible, has anyone endured worse, will it matter in a year.

4. [The downward arrow](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K408%20%E2%80%94%20The%20downward%20arrow): follow a painful thought down by asking what would be bad about it, and then about the answer.

5. [Behavioral experiment](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K183%20%E2%80%94%20Behavioral%20experiment): test a belief through action.

6. [Graded exposure](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K117%20%E2%80%94%20Graded%20exposure): list avoided situations from least to most difficult, enter the easiest, stay, and move up.

7. [Interoceptive exposure](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K118%20%E2%80%94%20Interoceptive%20exposure): induce feared bodily sensations in a safe context and stay with them.

8. [Sitting with uncertainty](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K122%20%E2%80%94%20Sitting%20with%20uncertainty): let not-knowing be present instead of resolving it early.

9. [Implementation intentions](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K153%20%E2%80%94%20Implementation%20intentions): an if-then plan, such as three slow breaths if anxious before a meeting.

10. [Slow breathing (5 to 6 breaths per minute)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K194%20%E2%80%94%20Slow%20breathing%20%285%20to%206%20breaths%20per%20minute%29): breathe at about five to six breaths a minute for five minutes.

11. [Box breathing](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K413%20%E2%80%94%20Box%20breathing): pace the breath in four equal phases of four seconds.

12. [The Three-minute Breathing Space](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K392%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Three-minute%20Breathing%20Space): a three-step mini-meditation of becoming aware, gathering on the breath, and expanding.

13. [Breath anchoring](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K100%20%E2%80%94%20Breath%20anchoring): rest attention on the breath, note where it went, return.

14. [Five-senses grounding](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K106%20%E2%80%94%20Five-senses%20grounding): name five things you see, four you hear, three you touch, two you smell, one you taste.

15. [Comfort recalibration](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K277%20%E2%80%94%20Comfort%20recalibration): introduce small deliberate discomforts into a routine.

16. [Compassion for a struggling version of yourself](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K379%20%E2%80%94%20Compassion%20for%20a%20struggling%20version%20of%20yourself): settle into a compassionate stance and bring to mind a situation where you struggled.

The cards carry their own evidence notes, and those notes matter here. Exposure and the cognitive exercises descend from treatments studied in clinical settings, where they are usually delivered with a clinician. The breathing and grounding practices are held as simple self-regulation tools, and a person with panic, trauma or persistent symptoms should not treat any of these as a substitute for care.

::Retention:: Retention is what people keep because it works for them. A reader who keeps the habit of asking what the anxiety is about, or the slow breath before a hard meeting, or the small step toward what they have been avoiding, has what the theme offered; what stays is a matter of fit between the practice and the life it enters.

## Story: the stories that carry the theme

The stories that best carry the theme's aspects, each retold in a line; the full telling lives on the card.

### Anxiety as the vertigo of possibility

[ST273 — The Dizziness of Freedom](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST273%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Dizziness%20of%20Freedom): standing at the edge of one's own possibility, nothing determines the choice; Adam's anxiety in the garden, Kierkegaard notes, was not about the apple but about the possibility of possibility.

[ST272 — Being-toward-Death](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST272%20%E2%80%94%20Being-toward-Death): Heidegger's analysis: death is not an event at the end of a life but the possibility each person is, which cannot be outstripped and which no one can take over.

### The fear that has no name

[ST275 — Ivan Ilyich's Fear](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST275%20%E2%80%94%20Ivan%20Ilyich%27s%20Fear): Ivan Ilyich's dying is marked above all by fear, not of death in the abstract but of the wrongness of a life everyone around him insists was correct; his scream lasts three days and stops when he stops resisting.

[ST221 — The Premeditation of Evils](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST221%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Premeditation%20of%20Evils): each morning the Stoic rehearses the day's possible losses, so that the loss, when it arrives, meets someone who has already sorted it, and the surprise, which is half the suffering, is removed.

### The second arrow and the tied monkey

[ST078 — The Two Arrows](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST078%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Two%20Arrows): a well-taught person struck by an arrow feels one arrow; an untaught person feels two, the second being the story of why me and what will become of me.

[ST075 — The Monkey Tied to the Post](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST075%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Monkey%20Tied%20to%20the%20Post): a monkey ranges across a whole forest until it is tied to a post, and then stays by the post; the training is not to cage the mind but to give it a post worth staying near.

[ST331 — The Hungry Tiger](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST331%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Hungry%20Tiger): you take in a hungry baby tiger and feed him; every feeding calms him and makes him bigger, until feeding him sooner seems the only safe move, as relief-seeking can grow around a painful feeling.

### The same storm, a different story

[ST207 — The Sailors and the Storm](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST207%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Sailors%20and%20the%20Storm): in a storm at sea the passengers panic and the sailors work; the storm is identical for both, and the difference is the story each has about it.

[ST375 — John Muir Rides the Storm in a Treetop](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST375%20%E2%80%94%20John%20Muir%20Rides%20the%20Storm%20in%20a%20Treetop): John Muir rides out a Sierra windstorm near the top of a tree, moving with it as it bends and circling, having chosen the tree carefully and trusted its elasticity.

### Staying calm when it is real

[ST342 — The Wild Boars Sit Still](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST342%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Wild%20Boars%20Sit%20Still): twelve boys and their assistant coach, who had trained ten years as a monk, wait out the flooded Tham Luang cave in 2018; he taught them to meditate, and the video of their finding shows them sitting calmly.
