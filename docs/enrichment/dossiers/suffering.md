# Suffering

> What is the difference between pain and suffering, and what does suffering ask of the person carrying it?

When something hurts, I am usually hurt twice. The Sallatha Sutta gives the image: an uninstructed person touched by a painful feeling sorrows, grieves and laments, and so feels two pains, physical and mental, while a well-instructed one feels a single pain, physical only ([SN 36.6, Thanissaro translation, dhammatalks.org](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN36_6.html)). [The second arrow](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20second%20arrow) is the distress that grows around the hurt: the story about why me, the rehearsal of what comes next. The sutta does not say the first arrow is unreal, and it does not present the second as a free choice either; it describes what trained and untrained minds do with the same event, and its teaching is addressed to the training. I use the distinction without treating grief or persistent distress as a failure of will.

Some situations do not fit the image of an added layer. Jaspers's [limit situations](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Limit%20situations%20%28Grenzsituationen%29) are, in the words of the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, moments usually accompanied by dread, guilt or acute anxiety in which the mind confronts the restrictions of its own forms ([SEP, Karl Jaspers](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/jaspers/)). Inside such a situation the distinction between the event and my response to it still holds; what is missing is the suggestion that the suffering could be dropped with the right technique. So the theme holds two different questions. When the distress is the added layer, the question is how the addition works and whether it can wear out. When the wound is the situation itself, the question is what a person can still do inside it.

There is a charter for the whole approach, and it is a refusal. The poisoned arrow story ([ST059](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST059%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Poisoned%20Arrow)): a man shot wants to know who shot him and of what wood the bow was made before he will let anyone treat him, and he will be dead before the interrogation ends. The Buddha tells it to a monk demanding metaphysical answers before he will practise. I take the point as a personal discipline: before asking what suffering means, ask what would remove it. What remains after that question is answered is the material of this theme.

### Nearby terms

- [Dukkha](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Dukkha): the Buddhist umbrella, wider than suffering. It names a background unsatisfactoriness built into conditioned experience, of which felt suffering is the loud part.

- [The avoidance of suffering](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20avoidance%20of%20suffering): the reflex of fleeing unsatisfactory experience, a cause of further suffering rather than its name.

- Misfortune: what happens to a person. Suffering, as treated here, is what the person adds or what the situation draws out; the difference between the two is the whole question.

- Pain: the physical event, the first arrow, kept distinct here from everything the mind does with it.

## What suffering is made of, and what it asks

### The arrow that is added

The Buddhist diagnosis starts wide. The first Noble Truth does not only name catastrophes:

> "Birth is stressful, aging is stressful, death is stressful; sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, & despair are stressful; association with the unbeloved is stressful, separation from the loved is stressful, not getting what is wanted is stressful. In short, the five clinging-aggregates are stressful." (SN 56.11, [Thanissaro translation, dhammatalks.org](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN56_11.html))

That is [dukkha](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Dukkha) in one sentence: ordinary experience carries a structural ache, because everything it runs on is conditioned and everything conditioned shifts. The claim concerns the structure of experience and says nothing about how miserable any particular life is. The [Four Noble Truths](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Four%20Noble%20Truths) are often read as propositions to believe; the sutta's own verbs are tasks, comprehended, abandoned, realized, developed.

What is the ache made of? The second truth names craving, [tanha](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Tanha), reaching for sensuality, for becoming, for non-becoming. [Stuckness as suffering](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Stuckness%20as%20suffering) reads the origination formula as a mind that cannot stop reaching for what feeds the sticking, and the reaching is what keeps the sticking alive. This is why the tradition treats [the avoidance of suffering](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20avoidance%20of%20suffering) as part of the machinery rather than its cure: aversion is one of the unwholesome roots, and fleeing the unpleasant is still reaching, in the other direction.

The distinction also has practical rules attached to it. One says not to fire the second arrow at the moment of pain ([B069 — Don't shoot the second arrow](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B069%20%E2%80%94%20Don%27t%20shoot%20the%20second%20arrow%20%28pain%20vs.%20suffering%29)); one says to face suffering directly instead of fleeing it ([B001 — Face suffering directly](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B001%20%E2%80%94%20Face%20suffering%20directly)); one names the engine that keeps the loop running, craving itself ([B006 — Craving is the engine of suffering](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B006%20%E2%80%94%20Craving%20is%20the%20engine%20of%20suffering%20%28tanha%29)).

The daily form matters because the arrows of an ordinary week are small enough to practise on. [B182 — Separate pain from suffering — daily](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B182%20%E2%80%94%20Separate%20pain%20from%20suffering%20%E2%80%94%20daily) runs the naming on stubbed toes and hard conversations, so the habit exists before a crisis asks for it.

### The judgment that adds the harm

The Stoics drew the same line. [Judgment adds the harm](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Judgment%20adds%20the%20harm) is Epictetus, Enchiridion 5, and the wording is worth having exactly:

> "Men are disturbed not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things: for example, death is nothing terrible, for if it were, it would have seemed so to Socrates; for the opinion about death, that it is terrible, is the terrible thing." (Epictetus, [Enchiridion 5, Perseus translation](https://scaife.perseus.org/reader/urn:cts:greekLit:tlg0557.tlg002.perseus-eng3:5/?right=perseus-eng4); same wording in the [George Long 1877 text on Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Discourses_of_Epictetus;_with_the_Encheiridion_and_Fragments/The_Encheiridion_or_Manual))

The two traditions agree on the line and differ on the work. The Buddhist version works on craving and the reflexes of the mind; the Stoic version works on assent, the moment I sign off on the reading of an event. A person can try each and keep the one that fits their case.

### The suffering that is not added

Then the diagnosis runs out. Some of what happens cannot be traced to opinions about it, and pretending otherwise is its own cruelty: telling someone whose child has died that their grief is a judgment problem. Jaspers called the remaining territory [limit situations (Grenzsituationen)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Limit%20situations%20%28Grenzsituationen%29), and his account of the response is not a solution but a new self-consciousness beyond the old forms ([SEP, Karl Jaspers](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/jaspers/)). [ST289 — Jaspers's Shipwreck](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST289%20%E2%80%94%20Jaspers%27s%20Shipwreck) gives Jaspers's own metaphor: we are always in situations, we cannot live without struggling against them, and we cannot fundamentally change them.

Job's story holds the same refusal of easy explanation. Job loses everything, refuses his friends' explanations, and demands an answer; God answers from the whirlwind with questions, and the friends are the book's real targets. What a person does inside that silence is [Frankl](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=T019%20%E2%80%94%20Frankl%2C%20Viktor)'s subject ([The meaning of suffering](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20meaning%20of%20suffering)). His own line is careful: meaning is available even through suffering, provided the suffering is unavoidable; if it is avoidable, the meaningful thing to do is to remove its cause; when a person cannot change the situation, he can still choose his attitude ([Frankl, The Case for a Tragic Optimism](http://fabian.ca/sites/default/files/Frankl%20-%20Search%20for%20Meaning%20%28excerpt%29.pdf)).

[Tragic optimism](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Tragic%20optimism) is his name for the stance: saying yes to life in spite of the tragic triad of pain, guilt and death. The stance is not a command; Frankl explicitly bars commanding optimism, in oneself or in others. [E064 — Practise meaning through the attitude toward suffering](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=E064%20%E2%80%94%20Practise%20meaning%20through%20the%20attitude%20toward%20suffering) is the practice form, and [ST311 — Frankl's Why](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST311%20%E2%80%94%20Frankl%27s%20Why) carries the caution I would keep attached to it: meaning does not remove suffering, it changes the relation to it.

[Amor fati](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Amor%20fati) is the other stance toward the necessary: "a man wishes nothing to be different, either in front of him or behind him, or for all eternity. Not only must the necessary be borne... but it must also be loved" ([Ecce Homo, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/52190/52190-h/52190-h.htm)). The Buddhist path takes the same fact from the other side: the second Noble Truth names craving, wanting, for sensuality, for becoming, for non-becoming, as the origin of suffering and sets abandoning it as the task ([SN 56.11](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN56_11.html)). I read the two as different emphases rather than rivals: the loving helps where a person is at war with what has already happened, and the abandoning helps where the wanting itself is the wound. Whether the two combine, love of fate together with the end of craving, the sources gathered here do not settle.

### The reflex that keeps it going

Between the two halves sits a mechanism the clinical tradition has measured. Avoidance works, briefly: it removes the unpleasant feeling, and the relief reinforces the avoidance, which removes the chance to learn that the feeling was survivable. [AF035 — Recognise that avoidance is negatively reinforcing](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=AF035%20%E2%80%94%20Recognise%20that%20avoidance%20is%20negatively%20reinforcing%20%E2%80%94%20it%20works%20short-term%20and%20maintains%20suffering%20long-term) is the principle: short-term relief, long-term maintenance. The suffering is fed by the very reflex that promises to manage it.

The clinical counterpart of the second-arrow distinction is acceptance, and the research definition is behavior, not mood. [Chronic pain acceptance](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Chronic%20pain%20acceptance) means, in McCracken and Vowles's definition, a behavior pattern of engagement in activity with pain present, without restrictions by pain or attempts to avoid or control it ([definition quoted in the chronic-pain literature](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5032639/)). What follows the event is [meaning-making](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Meaning-making), Park's model: distress arises from the gap between the meaning an event carries and the global meaning it violated, and the work goes to one side of the gap or the other ([Park's meaning-making model](https://www.ehps.net/ehp/index.php/contents/article/download/ehp.v15.i2.p40/1041)).

The findings here are descriptive, not a promise. [Post-traumatic growth](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Post-traumatic%20growth) names positive change experienced as a result of the struggle with highly challenging life circumstances, and Tedeschi and Calhoun, who coined the term, are explicit that growth is not inevitable and that virtually everyone reporting it also acknowledges distress ([their conceptual foundations](https://ptgi.uncc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2013/01/PTG-Conceptual-Foundtns.pdf)). [Benefit finding](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Benefit%20finding) is the narrower cousin: finding good in adversity, as distinct from benefit received from a giver.

The response side of the theme is not only intrapersonal. [Karuna](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Karuna) is the wish to relieve suffering when it is seen, and [B099 — Respond to suffering with the wish to relieve it](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B099%20%E2%80%94%20Respond%20to%20suffering%20with%20the%20wish%20to%20relieve%20it%20%28karuna%20in%20action%29) is its action form. [SE026 — Recognise common humanity](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=SE026%20%E2%80%94%20Recognise%20common%20humanity%20%E2%80%94%20your%20suffering%20is%20not%20unique%20or%20isolating) breaks the isolation that makes suffering worse than it needs to be.

Two principles hold the edges of the territory. [M020 — Accept suffering as a teacher, without seeking it](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=M020%20%E2%80%94%20Accept%20suffering%20as%20a%20teacher%2C%20without%20seeking%20it) carries its own caution in its second half. [SP095 — Convert suffering into understanding at the moment of loss](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=SP095%20%E2%80%94%20Convert%20suffering%20into%20understanding%20at%20the%20moment%20of%20loss) is a moment's work, not a life plan. The preventive side, [Y011 — Prevent suffering before it arrives](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Y011%20%E2%80%94%20Prevent%20suffering%20before%20it%20arrives), closes the loop: some suffering is forestalled by ordinary care, and refusing to romanticize what remains is not fatalism.

### What the body does with the first arrow

The first arrow has its own science. Melzack and Wall's [gate control theory of pain](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Gate%20control%20theory%20of%20pain) changed the field's picture: the brain is an active system that filters, selects and modulates inputs, and the dorsal horns are sites of inhibition, excitation and modulation, not passive relay stations ([Melzack's own retrospective](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10491980/)). His later [neuromatrix theory of pain](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Neuromatrix%20theory%20of%20pain) goes further: a widely distributed neural network whose output pattern evokes pain, integrating sensory, cognitive and emotional inputs. Neither theory makes pain imaginary; both make it a product of the whole system rather than a simple wire from injury to feeling.

That is why the meditation tradition has an instrument for it. The [pain curve](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20pain%20curve) instruction: when pain arises in sitting, observe it a few times, return to the breath, and watch it objectively, knowing it arises from causes and vanishes when they disappear ([Dhammasami's instruction](https://www.wisdomlib.org/buddhism/article/mindfulness-meditation-made-easy/d/doc1640.html)). Montaigne made the same observation from illness rather than doctrine ([the body-mind unity in pain](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20body-mind%20unity%20in%20pain)):

> "Suffering itself less afflicts the senses than the apprehension of suffering." (An old author's saying, quoted in [Montaigne, Essays, Gutenberg vol. 18](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3598/pg3598.html))

The clinical line of the same question is [chronic pain research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Chronic%20pain%20research): what beside the pain itself is associated with disability and distress. In McCracken's 1998 study of 160 adults with chronic pain, greater acceptance of pain came with lower pain intensity, less pain-related anxiety and avoidance, less depression, less physical and psychosocial disability, more daily uptime, and better work status, and the low correlation between acceptance and pain intensity showed acceptance is not simply a function of having little pain. The catastrophizing line studies the mental set a person brings to the pain, an exaggerated negative mental set during actual or anticipated painful experience, with rumination, magnification and helplessness as its parts.

## Where the traditions genuinely disagree

### Is pain itself an evil?

Diogenes Laertius reports that Dionysius of Heraclea, a Stoic, left the school for the doctrine of pleasure because the severity of his ophthalmia meant he "had no longer the nerve to call pain a thing indifferent" ([Diogenes Laertius VII.37, Hicks translation, Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Lives_of_the_Eminent_Philosophers/Book_VII)). The story is an ancient report and shows nothing about pain itself. It does show where the dispute sits. The same book lists pain among the things that "neither benefit nor harm a man", beside death, disease and poverty, and keeps the words good and evil for virtue and vice (VII.101-102, same carrier).

Epicurus says the opposite in plain words: "all pain is an evil and yet not all pain is to be shunned", and he defines the pleasure he recommends as "the absence of pain in the body and of trouble in the soul" ([Letter to Menoeceus, MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Epicurus/menoec.html)). A pain can be worth accepting when it buys more later, and it is still a bad thing while it lasts.

Marcus Aurelius, a Stoic, gives advice that either camp could use. In every pain, he writes, remember that "there is no dishonour in it, nor does it make the governing intelligence worse", and in the same breath he borrows an Epicurean remark, that pain "is neither intolerable nor everlasting" if one remembers its limits and adds nothing to it in imagination ([Meditations, Book 7, MIT Classics](https://classics.mit.edu/Antoninus/meditations.7.seven.html)). The methods overlap and the classification does not. As I read the two lists, the disagreement turns on whether a harm to a living creature counts as a harm to the person, or only a harm to character does.

I take Epicurus's label and Marcus's method. The label is my own preference for severe pain; I would not want to be told that nothing bad is happening to me. The method is the one this dossier already uses on the added layer.

### Should ending suffering govern our aspirations?

The Buddha describes his own teaching as having one subject: "Both formerly & now, it is only stress that I describe, and the cessation of stress" ([SN 22:86, Thanissaro translation, dhammatalks.org](https://www.dhammatalks.org/suttas/SN/SN22_86.html)). The passage puts the ending of suffering at the centre of the teaching's aim; it does not rule on whether suffering can be put to use along the way. The [karuna](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Karuna) line, the wish to relieve suffering, points the same way.

Nietzsche challenges that aspiration. Addressing readers who sympathise with the suffering of others, he writes that they want "to do away with suffering", while he and his kind "would rather have it increased and made worse than it has ever been". "The discipline of suffering, of GREAT suffering", he asks, "know ye not that it is only THIS discipline that has produced all the elevations of humanity hitherto?" ([Beyond Good and Evil, section 225, Zimmern translation, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/4363/pg4363.txt)). Section 225 does not name the Buddha. In The Antichrist he does: he praises Buddhism as "a hundred times as realistic as Christianity", describes it as a religion of struggle with suffering, and still counts it among the nihilistic religions ([The Antichrist, section 20, Mencken translation, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/19322/pg19322.txt)).

[Frankl](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=T019%20%E2%80%94%20Frankl%2C%20Viktor) draws the distinction that matters here. He asks whether suffering is indispensable to the discovery of meaning and answers that it is in no way so. When suffering can be avoided, "the meaningful thing to do is to remove its cause, for unnecessary suffering is masochistic rather than heroic" ([Frankl, The Case for a Tragic Optimism](http://fabian.ca/sites/default/files/Frankl%20-%20Search%20for%20Meaning%20%28excerpt%29.pdf)). His own line separates avoidable from unavoidable suffering: the avoidable kind calls for removing its cause; in the unavoidable kind, meaning is still available, and the person can still choose his attitude toward it. On that account the two aims are not exclusive: ending what can be ended does not decide what to do with the rest. The question is whether eliminating suffering should govern our aspirations, or whether the aspiration to be rid of it is itself the thing Nietzsche doubts.

Nietzsche's claim about "all the elevations of humanity" is a sweeping one, and the passage offers no case that tests it. I follow Frankl: remove what can be removed, and look for meaning in the rest without treating the pain as a requirement. [M020](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=M020%20%E2%80%94%20Accept%20suffering%20as%20a%20teacher%2C%20without%20seeking%20it) holds the same position in its title.

### Does suffering track what a person deserves?

The Book of Job stages this argument between speakers inside one book, not between traditions. Eliphaz asks, "who ever perished, being innocent? Or where were the upright cut off?" ([Job 4:7](https://ebible.org/eng-web/JOB04.htm)), and Bildad says, "God will not cast away a blameless man, neither will he uphold the evildoers" ([Job 8:20](https://ebible.org/eng-web/JOB08.htm), World English Bible). As I read it, the rule comforts the person applying it and burdens the person it is applied to, because it makes his pain evidence about him.

Job rejects the rule on observation. "It is all the same," he says. "Therefore I say he destroys the blameless and the wicked" ([Job 9:22](https://ebible.org/eng-web/JOB09.htm)). Later he asks, "Why do the wicked live, become old, yes, and grow mighty in power?" ([Job 21:7](https://ebible.org/eng-web/JOB21.htm)). The book then sides against the friends: God says to Eliphaz, "you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job has" ([Job 42:7](https://ebible.org/eng-web/JOB42.htm)). The verdict does not say which of Job's statements it endorses, and Job's final speech in the chapter ends in self-abhorrence and repentance, so the ending is not a plain vindication.

The question matters to this dossier because the friends' rule is an explanation of the first arrow, and the [poisoned arrow](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST059%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Poisoned%20Arrow) story warns against making an answer the precondition of treatment. I lean with Job: a rule that fails on observation should not be handed to someone in pain.

## Practice: separating the arrows

::Variation:: If pain and the story about pain have fused into one mass, the variation to make is a separation: learning to feel the first arrow as it is, and to see the second arrow as an addition with its own signature. The maladaptive pattern is the fusion; the adaptive direction is the gap between the two.

::Selection:: Choose one exercise from below and run it at the moments of ordinary pain: stubbed toes, headaches, hard conversations. The point is practice on small arrows, so the skill exists before a large one arrives.

### Name the arrows

1. [B182 — Separate pain from suffering — daily](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B182%20%E2%80%94%20Separate%20pain%20from%20suffering%20%E2%80%94%20daily): at the moment of pain, name the first arrow in one plain sentence, then look for the second. Notice whether naming the second one loosens it.

2. [K397 — Approach, don't avoid (the mouse in the maze)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K397%20%E2%80%94%20Approach%2C%20don%27t%20avoid%20%28the%20mouse%20in%20the%20maze%29): when you notice bracing against a task, a conversation or a feeling, ask which direction you are facing, toward or away. If the answer is away, rewrite the same act in the direction of what you want, do it once, and notice whether the mind feels wider or narrower after.

### Accept what cannot change

3. [K123 — Radical acceptance](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K123%20%E2%80%94%20Radical%20acceptance): accept the reality of a situation you cannot change, as acknowledgment rather than approval. Notice the difference in the body between fighting the fact and admitting it.

4. [K319 — Non-avoidant grief work](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K319%20%E2%80%94%20Non-avoidant%20grief%20work): when grief arises, allow it without rushing to resolve it, and name what has been lost. Keep the engagement time-limited; the practice is deliberate approach, not open-ended rumination.

### Keep the question open

5. [K351 — The meaning question](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K351%20%E2%80%94%20The%20meaning%20question): where the situation cannot be changed, put the question of what this life is for in writing, without answering it, and note what the question does to the day that asks it. If the search turns into rumination, the same movement repeated with no change in living, stop and take the question for a walk instead.

::Retention:: This is an editorial combination rather than a validated protocol. Keep whichever exercise changes what you do at the moment of pain; drop what does not. What people keep is the separation itself, in whatever form worked for them.

## Story: the stories that carry the theme

The theme's two halves have their own stories, and they are not the same story.

### The second arrow

[ST078 — The Two Arrows](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST078%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Two%20Arrows): the well-taught person struck by one arrow feels one; the untaught, struck by the same arrow, feels two. The first arrow, on the sutta's own telling, is not optional.

[ST059 — The Poisoned Arrow](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST059%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Poisoned%20Arrow): a man shot with a poisoned arrow wants to know who shot it and of what wood the bow was made before he will let anyone treat him; he will be dead before the interrogation ends.

### The limit situation

[ST289 — Jaspers's Shipwreck](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST289%20%E2%80%94%20Jaspers%27s%20Shipwreck): Jaspers, a psychiatrist excluded from his university post by the Nazis, describes limit situations as the structures that cannot be avoided or changed, death, chance, guilt and struggle; the response is an illumination, not a solution.

[ST290 — Job and the Whirlwind](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST290%20%E2%80%94%20Job%20and%20the%20Whirlwind): Job loses children, property and health; the friends explain his suffering as punishment; God answers from the whirlwind with questions, and the friends are rebuked.

### Meaning inside it

[ST311 — Frankl's Why](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST311%20%E2%80%94%20Frankl%27s%20Why): the camps memoir grounds its account of survival in the Nietzschean line, he who has a why to live can bear almost any how, and Frankl's use of it is careful: meaning does not remove suffering, it changes the relation to it.
