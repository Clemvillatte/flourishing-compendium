# Leisure

> What is free time for, once it is actually free?

## The time that is left over

One way to treat time off is as maintenance. You rest so you can work again, and you amuse yourself so you can exert yourself again. The treatment works as far as it goes, and it leaves the interesting question untouched. If evenings and weekends are not only fuel for the next shift, what are they for?

[Aristotle](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=T002%20%E2%80%94%20Aristotle) put the ordering bluntly:

> "And happiness is thought to depend on leisure; for we are busy that we may have leisure, and make war that we may live in peace." ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.7, Ross translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.10.x.html))

Read twice, that sentence turns the week around. Work is the means, leisure the end, which is the claim behind [practising scholē as the end of work](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=A111%20%E2%80%94%20Practise%20leisure%20%28schol%C4%93%29%20as%20the%20end%20of%20work). The ancient word [scholē](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Schol%C4%93) did not mean time left over after duties; it named the space where the activities worth having for their own sake could happen. In Aristotle's proposed best state the citizens were to be freed from the fields, a fact taken up below as the start of the tradition's distributive disagreement.

## Amusement is a medicine, not the point

Aristotle does not sneer at rest. He gives amusement a real but limited job:

> "amusement is a sort of relaxation, and we need relaxation because we cannot work continuously. Relaxation, then, is not an end; for it is taken for the sake of activity." ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.6, Ross translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.10.x.html))

In the Politics the same thought gets a medical image. Amusements, he says, "should be our medicines", while "leisure of itself gives pleasure and happiness and enjoyment of life, which are experienced, not by the busy man, but by those who have leisure" ([Aristotle, Politics VIII.3, Jowett translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.8.eight.html)). The card on [distinguishing leisure from amusement](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=A112%20%E2%80%94%20Distinguish%20leisure%20from%20amusement) works out the practical test: an evening of television after a heavy shift is amusement doing its proper job, and two evenings a week spent slowly learning a piece of music, with no deadline, is closer to leisure. The two answer different questions, and on this distinction, being off work does not by itself amount to leisure.

The ordering also runs upward through society: "there must be war for the sake of peace, business for the sake of leisure, things useful and necessary for the sake of things honorable" ([Aristotle, Politics VII.14, Jowett translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.7.seven.html)). Modern psychology studies a cousin of the question under the name of [time affluence](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Time%20affluence), how having command over one's own hours bears on how life goes.

## Leisure is not idleness

The deepest account of what leisure is, as opposed to what it is for, is Josef Pieper's, and it is built against idleness rather than against work:

> "Leisure is a form of that stillness that is the necessary preparation for accepting reality; only the person who is still can hear, and whoever is not still, cannot hear." ([Josef Pieper, Leisure: The Basis of Culture](https://iwcinsb.org/studygroups/2024%20readings/Leisure.pdf))

Pieper insists the stillness is not inactivity: leisure "is not the same as the absence of activity; it is not the same thing as quiet, or even as an inner quiet", and it "lives on affirmation", meaning a fundamental agreement that the world is worth receiving ([Leisure: The Basis of Culture](https://iwcinsb.org/studygroups/2024%20readings/Leisure.pdf)). Idleness, on his account, is not too much rest but a broken relation to reality, an inability to receive it. The page for [leisure as a concept](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Leisure) carries the fuller passages.

This matters for practice because it relocates the failure. In Pieper's account, the capacity for leisure involves more than arranging a schedule. [Resting deeply](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=D113%20%E2%80%94%20Rest%20deeply) and [making rest the root of activity](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=D039%20%E2%80%94%20Make%20rest%20the%20root%20of%20activity) both treat rest as something a person can approach deliberately, and Pieper calls the power to be at leisure one of the basic powers of the human soul.

## Where the traditions differ

### Who gets to have leisure

Aristotle's leisure was bought with other people's labour. He is explicit that the citizens of his best state must not be husbandmen, "since leisure is necessary both for the development of virtue and the performance of political duties" ([Aristotle, Politics VII.9, Jowett translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.7.seven.html)). Russell keeps Aristotle's valuation, leisure over work, and drops the exclusion:

> "The morality of work is the morality of slaves, and the modern world has no need of slavery." ([Bertrand Russell, In Praise of Idleness, Harper's, 1932](https://harpers.org/archive/1932/10/in-praise-of-idleness/))

Russell's case is distributive ([In Praise of Idleness, 1935 text](https://www.idler.co.uk/article/book-of-the-week-in-praise-of-idleness/)). The labours that once bought leisure for the few were valuable, he argues, for the sake of the leisure they bought, and a decent economy would spread that good justly. He adds the sociological sting:

> "The idea that the poor should have leisure has always been shocking to the rich." ([In Praise of Idleness, Harper's, 1932](https://harpers.org/archive/1932/10/in-praise-of-idleness/))

The disagreement is not about whether leisure is the point. It is about whether the point can be universal.

### Contemplation, or display

Pieper says leisure is receptive beholding of reality. Veblen found something else at the bottom of aristocratic leisure: showing that you did not have to work. The "requirement of abstention from productive work" is, on his account, the widest of the demands of emulation, and "labour comes to be associated in men's habits of thought with weakness and subjection to a master", so that not working becomes a mark of rank ([Thorstein Veblen, The Theory of the Leisure Class, ch. 2](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/833/833-h/833-h.htm)). His book names the phenomenon after it, conspicuous leisure.

The two books are not arguing with each other. Pieper describes what leisure is at its best and Veblen what much of it has historically been for, and both can be true of different hours. The uncomfortable question they leave open is which of the two things a given weekend actually is, and [judging a person by where they rest](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Judging%20by%20where%20one%20rests) is one way of asking it.

### Recovery, or an end in itself

Contemporary research on off-job time has a more modest aim than defining leisure: Sonnentag and Fritz built their standard measure to assess how people unwind and recuperate from work during leisure time, and it distinguishes four recovery experiences, psychological detachment from work, relaxation, mastery, and control ([Sonnentag and Fritz, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17638488/)). In the validation studies the four experiences showed moderate relations with measures of psychological well-being, and the research programme behind [recovery experiences](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Recovery%20experiences) and [effort-recovery cycles](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Effort-recovery%20cycles) has built on them since.

The difference from the classical accounts is one of purpose, and the two can coexist. Aristotle calls relaxation a medicine, useful and not an end; Pieper says outright that leisure is not the absence of activity and not quiet, and his own text adds that the power to be at leisure can send a person back "renewed and alive again" into work ([Leisure: The Basis of Culture](https://iwcinsb.org/studygroups/2024%20readings/Leisure.pdf)). The recovery research asks how off-job time repairs people for work, and its findings are worth using for that question. The classical accounts warn against letting that question become the only one, because a life whose free hours are only maintenance is a life without leisure in the classical sense. [Attention that needs recovery](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=AT037%20%E2%80%94%20Understand%20that%20attention%20needs%20recovery%20%E2%80%94%20directed%20attention%20is%20a%20depletable%20resource) and [attention restoration](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Attention%20restoration) sit on the maintenance side of the line, honestly.

### Free time that is not free

Pieper's account leans on a clean line between the world of work and the space of leisure, and Regi reports the sociologist Chris Rojek rejecting exactly that line in an open Anthropology Today essay:

> "Rojek (1995, 2000, 2010) rejects the idea of the modernist 'work versus free time' relational dichotomy (mostly advocated by Pieper 1952 and Dumazedier 1974) to argue that leisure time is not freer than any other time that is not devoted to relaxation." ([Regi, Anthropology Today, 2012](https://www.mursi.org/documents-and-texts/published-articles/regi-tamas/tourism-leisure-and-work/at_download/file))

The continuation is the sharper half: in Rojek's account "leisure often requires more planning, and more physical and mental effort, than work activities", and must conform to cultural and social expectations, since leisure is a politically and economically controlled activity ([Regi, 2012](https://www.mursi.org/documents-and-texts/published-articles/regi-tamas/tourism-leisure-and-work/at_download/file)). Anyone who has booked a family holiday knows the phenomenology. As Regi presents it, the rejection lands on the dichotomy as Pieper is standardly read, and what it targets is free time rather than Pieper's capacity of the soul. Whether the two still quarrel after that correction is a question a reader can take to an actual week, and it is a live one in the field.

### The line drawn for someone else

The classical line between work and leisure assumes the duties are carried by someone. Feminist leisure research asks who, and what the line does to a life that both carries the duties and is described by the dichotomy. An open survey of the field quotes the conclusion of Green, Hebron and Woodward's study of women's leisure:

> "A woman's right to freedom in leisure is circumscribed by her employment status and income level, her family situation and …her lack of status as a woman in a patriarchal society." ([Green, Hebron and Woodward, 1990, quoted in Veal 2015](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/39506/8/2015_Human_Rights_Leisure_Studies_WLJ_Accepted_Veal.pdf))

A later open survey by the same author records an observation reported by Henderson and colleagues, that for many women "their everyday lives tend to be holistic; work and leisure may coexist and be difficult to distinguish" ([Henderson et al., 1996, quoted in Veal, Everyday life and everyday leisure](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10071223/)). What the two passages establish is constrained access to leisure, and interleaved lives. They do not by themselves contradict Aristotle's scholē or Pieper's condition of the soul, since they address access, interleaving and the classification of time use rather than the classical definitions. The field's tools have moved the same way: Veal's time-use taxonomy, set out in that survey, "seeks to widen the scope of the study of leisure from the simple work-leisure binary to encompass eleven categories of time-use" ([Veal, Everyday life and everyday leisure](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10071223/)).

For a life in which work and leisure interleave, the question of what free time is for has no clean subject to address.

### Is free time actually free?

Russell's case for the four-hour day rests on a promise about the freed hours. Modern technique, he writes, has made it possible for "leisure, within limits, to be not the prerogative of small privileged classes, but a right evenly distributed throughout the community" ([In Praise of Idleness, 1932](https://harpers.org/archive/1932/10/in-praise-of-idleness/)), and "the wise use of leisure, it must be conceded, is a product of civilization and education" ([In Praise of Idleness, 1932](https://harpers.org/archive/1932/10/in-praise-of-idleness/)). The promise is conditional: spread the hours, teach their use, and the free time of ordinary people carries the good life.

Adorno's essay reads as a diagnosis of what the promise met. "Free time is shackled to its opposite", and where full employment has become the ideal, free time "is nothing more than a shadowy continuation of labour" ([Adorno, Free Time](http://xenopraxis.net/readings/adorno_freetime.pdf)). The tourism and camping that fill those hours are organized for the sake of profit, and the difference between work and free time is "branded as a norm in the minds of people". The old word leisure, Muße, "denoted the privilege of an unconstrained, comfortable life-style", and his charge is that the modern successor does not deliver what the name promises, since unfreedom keeps annexing the free hours while people feel most at liberty in them.

The two texts are not conducting an argument, and what stands between them is a tension rather than a settled dispute. Russell was writing against a world that withheld free time, Adorno against one awash in it, and the diagnosis names an obstacle to Russell's promise rather than a refutation of it: the social totality that organizes work organizes its opposite as well. What the pair leaves a reader is the sharper test for an evening, not only whether it is free of work, but whether it is free.

### Relaxation, or wholehearted action

Leisure studies has its own version of the fight. Douglas Kleiber, writing in the Journal of Leisure Research, reports a view in the field that treats wholehearted investment in action as the essence of true leisure, following Tinsley and Tinsley, and that in this view relaxation "is clearly neglected and subordinated", taking appreciation, contemplation and peacefulness down with it. Kleiber dissents:

> "I share Pieper's view that leisure is most essentially a position of relaxation, of faithful openness to immediate reality." ([Kleiber, The Neglect of Relaxation, 2000](https://www.nrpa.org/globalassets/journals/jlr/2000/volume-32/jlr-volume-32-number-1-pp-82-86.pdf))

The effort side has its own developed statement in Robert Stebbins's research on amateurs, hobbyists and career volunteers. His serious leisure is the "systematic pursuit of an amateur, hobbyist, or volunteer activity sufficiently substantial, interesting, and fulfilling for the participant to find a (leisure) career there acquiring and expressing a combination of its special skills, knowledge, and experience", with careers in serious leisure resting on "a third quality: significant personal effort" ([Stebbins, LSA Newsletter 79, 2008](https://www.seriousleisure.net/uploads/8/3/3/8/8338986/reflections17.pdf)).

Casual leisure, the rest, play and easy sociability, remains leisure on his account, "immediately, intrinsically rewarding, relatively short-lived pleasurable activity, requiring little or no special training to enjoy it", but it is "too fleeting, mundane, and commonplace for most people to find a distinctive identity there". The distinctive identity and the durable rewards he describes grow around serious pursuits instead, through perseverance, skill built over years, and the social world of the activity ([Stebbins, LSA Newsletter 79, 2008](https://www.seriousleisure.net/uploads/8/3/3/8/8338986/reflections17.pdf)).

The two positions answer different questions, which is why the field can hold both. Kleiber names what leisure is at its essence, a position of relaxation and openness, and Stebbins describes where its distinctive rewards tend to be found, in pursuits that ask for effort. Stebbins's casual leisure is defined as a form of leisure in its own right, immediately and intrinsically rewarding, and Pieper's leisure is expressly not the absence of activity, so the two accounts do not exclude each other.

What remains open is whether an evening of effortless enjoyment and a decade of slowly learning an instrument are the same good or two different ones, and the absorption model Kleiber reports, which the Tuch summary pairs with relaxation among the characteristics of leisure experience, has not settled it ([Tuch, van Schaik and Hornbæk](https://research.tees.ac.uk/ws/files/4260488/620108.pdf)). Kleiber adds that Pieper himself, asked thirty years on whether anything had changed his mind, answered no.

### Distinct types or a continuum?

Stebbins's framework divides leisure into serious, casual and project-based forms, and the serious-casual distinction is its load-bearing wall. He maintained it flatly in a 2022 reflection: "leisure activities can be classified as either serious or casual, with each form offering its participants sharply different experiences and generating sharply different states of mind" ([Stebbins, Leisure Reflections 60, 2022](https://www.seriousleisure.net/uploads/8/3/3/8/8338986/reflections60.pdf)).

Veal, reviewing four decades of the perspective in Leisure Studies, attacks the divide itself. Most leisure activities, he argues, can be joined in either mode, and on that ground "the binary division of leisure activities into distinct casual and serious forms would be more appropriately thought of as a serious leisure-casual leisure (SLCL) continuum, applicable to all leisure activities" ([Veal, A critique of serious leisure as theory, 2021](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/147640/3/A%20critique%20of%20serious%20leisure%20as%20theory.pdf)). The same helping can be a one-off kindness or the start of a volunteer career, and Stebbins's own texts, as Veal reads them, put volunteering in both modes.

Veal's sharpest charge is about the evidence. "Claims concerning the unique qualities of serious leisure have not generally been subject to empirical testing because of the marked lack of comparable research demonstrating the theorised distinctive qualities of casual leisure", while casual leisure, by Stebbins's own estimate, accounts for some 80 per cent of all leisure activity ([Veal, 2021](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/147640/3/A%20critique%20of%20serious%20leisure%20as%20theory.pdf)). He also records that Stebbins once anticipated he would "abandon eventually this primitive categorical terminology for terminology conveying continuousness", a plan that never eventuated, with the responses to continuum proposals ranging from silence to limited acceptance.

The disagreement is genuine, and both sides are carried here by their own texts. Stebbins asserts two forms with sharply different experiences; Veal argues the forms blur into a continuum and that the uniqueness claims have not had the comparative testing their weight deserves. Veal's challenge is to the evidential basis, not a demonstration that the rewards are absent. For the relaxation-versus-effort question above, that means the two categories cannot settle it by themselves, since whether they are categories at all is what these two are arguing about.

### What does the framework explain?

Stebbins presents the perspective as, in his words, "a theoretic framework that synthesizes three main forms of leisure, showing, at once, their distinctive features, similarities, and interrelationships" ([Stebbins, LSA Newsletter 79, 2008](https://www.seriousleisure.net/uploads/8/3/3/8/8338986/reflections17.pdf)). The three forms and their rewards are meant to fit together into one account of free time.

Veal's evaluation in Leisure Sciences asks what has actually been tested. His paper "evaluates the perspective as theory and as a typology", and its verdict on the theory is that "the theory associated with the SLP, concerning social worlds, identification and optimal leisure lifestyles, is found to be generally untested because it has been largely developed in relation to the serious leisure form only" ([Veal, The serious leisure perspective and the experience of leisure, 2017](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/45585/4/ABFBC890-A526-452D-9A94-02E3528ED6AE%20am.pdf)). In a later paper he took up the macro level, judging "the claim that the SLP offers a viable macro-level theoretical framework for the study of leisure is questionable" ([Veal, A critique of serious leisure as theory, 2021](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/147640/3/A%20critique%20of%20serious%20leisure%20as%20theory.pdf)).

The challenge is to the evidence and the explanatory reach, not to the framework's usefulness as a description. An untested theory is still a theory, and Veal's own phrasing keeps the word. What his critique leaves open is whether the perspective explains anything beyond the sorting, and that question waits on comparative research across all three forms.

### Should the framework be replaced?

Veal's 2017 paper ends with a proposal, not just a verdict. Its abstract states it flatly: "It is proposed that the SLP be replaced by a more flexible, open research approach, the Leisure Experience Perspective, which consolidates features of the SLP and other research traditions and a number of other theoretical perspectives" ([Veal, The serious leisure perspective and the experience of leisure, 2017](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/45585/4/ABFBC890-A526-452D-9A94-02E3528ED6AE%20am.pdf)). The typology would fold into something broader.

Stebbins rejects Veal's case for replacement. He records the challenge as the most recent and broadest of the critiques the framework has absorbed, and replies that "four problems seriously undermine his assessment": Veal "bases much of his critique on a textbook", ignores the central idea of core activity, fails to see the theoretic bases of the types in the perspective's schematic typology, and understands poorly its grounded theoretic basis ([Stebbins, Leisure Reflections 47, 2018](https://www.seriousleisure.net/uploads/8/3/3/8/8338986/leisure_reflections_no._47__the_serious_leisure_perspective__past_present_and_future___leisure_studie.pdf)).

The disagreement is direct. Veal proposes a broader successor that combines features of the SLP with other research traditions ([Veal, 2017](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/45585/4/ABFBC890-A526-452D-9A94-02E3528ED6AE%20am.pdf)); Stebbins rejects the grounds offered for that replacement, arguing that Veal overlooks central concepts and the framework's theoretical foundations ([Stebbins, 2018](https://www.seriousleisure.net/uploads/8/3/3/8/8338986/leisure_reflections_no._47__the_serious_leisure_perspective__past_present_and_future___leisure_studie.pdf)). Their disagreement concerns what leisure research should build on, rather than simply whether serious and casual leisure form distinct categories.

### Can uselessness be the point

Zhuangzi ends a story about a huge useless tree with an invitation:

> "There you might saunter idly by its side, or in the enjoyment of untroubled ease sleep beneath it." ([Zhuangzi 1, Legge translation](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf))

His summary line runs: "All men know the advantage of being useful, but no one knows the advantage of being useless" ([Zhuangzi 4, Legge translation](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)). His tree passage and Aristotle's contemplation are not directly opposed: both put the best things outside the economy of usefulness, and sleeping beneath a useless tree does not commit Zhuangzi against worthwhile activity. The divergence is in the shape of the ideal. Aristotle's leisure is an activity, contemplation, "loved for its own sake" ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.7, Ross translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.10.x.html)), the highest and most self-sufficient thing a person can do. Zhuangzi's image is closer to resting in what nothing is harvesting, and [valuing the useless](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=D084%20%E2%80%94%20Value%20the%20useless) draws the lesson for a person's week.

The mesoamerican principle [that idleness is the root disorder](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=M031%20%E2%80%94%20Work%20steadily%3B%20idleness%20is%20the%20root%20disorder) counsels steady work, and its own texts also name rest and laughter as gifts, so its counsel runs against heavy sleeping rather than against rest itself; it is not a third pole in the question. That question remains, whether the best hours of a life are the ones doing something, the ones receiving something, or the ones nothing is harvesting. [Seeing action within rest](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Y112%20%E2%80%94%20See%20action%20within%20rest%20and%20rest%20within%20action) is one classical attempt to refuse the choice.

### Is free time where the good hours are?

A one-week experience-sampling study of 78 adult workers found that most reported flow experiences occurred at work rather than during leisure, and that most measured aspects of the quality of experience were more closely associated with being in flow than with being at work or in leisure, with relaxation and motivation as exceptions ([Csikszentmihalyi and LeFevre, 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2724069/)). The same respondents reported more motivation in leisure than in work. The finding concerns what these participants reported during one week, and it does not test Aristotle's account of scholē, whose leisure is an activity rather than free time. What it does disturb is any assumption that free hours by themselves hold a person's best moments, which is an assumption neither Aristotle nor Pieper held, and which a reader holding it should notice.

## Practice: variation, selection, retention

The unmeasured evening. For two weeks, spend some free hours with every metric off, no step count, no streak, no monetisable output. Vary what fills them. Selection: notice which activities hold attention for their own sake and which need a feed to keep going. Retention: keep the ones that held. This moves motivation and overt behaviour, and it is the Zhuangzi kernel, testing what survives when nothing is measuring you.

The rest audit. For one week, each evening note whether you actually detached from work or only changed rooms while continuing to think about it. Keep the conditions that produced detachment, an end time, a walk, a device boundary. This is the [recovery-as-a-skill](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=PP048%20%E2%80%94%20Practise%20recovery%20as%20a%20skill) kernel and it moves attention. It is maintenance, and knowing that is part of the skill.

One standing sabbath. Put a weekly block of some hours where work and its tools are out of reach and nothing productive is allowed. See what fills it when nothing useful is permitted. This moves the self and overt behaviour, and it is [rest built into the system](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=PP073%20%E2%80%94%20Build%20rest%20into%20the%20system) rather than squeezed in after it. The [Sabbath](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Sabbath) card carries the same boundary as a weekly day of rest.

## Story: the stories that carry the theme

[Tao Yuanming Walks Home After Eighty Days](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST358%20%E2%80%94%20Tao%20Yuanming%20Walks%20Home%20After%20Eighty%20Days): a poet takes a magistrate's post to fill the grain jar, resigns after eighty days, and walks home to his fields, saying his basic nature is spontaneity and cannot be forced.

[Bashō Walks North](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST053%20%E2%80%94%20Bash%C5%8D%20Walks%20North): in middle age Bashō sells his house and walks for months through rain and poor inns; the journey itself is his home, and the poems it yields are small, exact, and empty of commentary.

[Su Shi Walks Through the Rain](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST337%20%E2%80%94%20Su%20Shi%20Walks%20Through%20the%20Rain): exiled and stripped of office, caught out in the rain, he strolls and chants in straw sandals instead of hurrying, and looks back at the storm without rancour.

[Watanabe Finds the Playground](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST215%20%E2%80%94%20Watanabe%20Finds%20the%20Playground): a Tokyo bureaucrat learns he is dying after thirty years of stamping documents, and the one project he pushes through against his whole machine is a playground for other people's children.

[Mead's Play and Game](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST113%20%E2%80%94%20Mead%27s%20Play%20and%20Game): in play a child takes one role at a time, and in the game all roles at once; the self itself, Mead argues, is built in this apparently useless activity, and the [play research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Play%20research) card studies what play does to the adults who keep doing it.
