# Leisure

> What is free time for, once it is actually free?

## The time that is left over

Most advice about time off treats it as maintenance. You rest so you can work again, and you amuse yourself so you can exert yourself again. The treatment works as far as it goes, and it leaves the interesting question untouched. If evenings and weekends are not only fuel for the next shift, what are they for?

[Aristotle](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=T002%20%E2%80%94%20Aristotle) put the ordering bluntly:

> "And happiness is thought to depend on leisure; for we are busy that we may have leisure, and make war that we may live in peace." ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.7, Ross translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.10.x.html))

Read twice, that sentence turns the week around. Work is the means, leisure the end, which is the claim behind [practising scholē as the end of work](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=A111%20%E2%80%94%20Practise%20leisure%20%28schol%C4%93%29%20as%20the%20end%20of%20work). The ancient word [scholē](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Schol%C4%93) did not mean time left over after duties; it named the space where the activities worth having for their own sake could happen. The Greeks could say this because somebody else was carrying the duties, a fact I take up below, because it is the sharpest disagreement in the whole tradition.

## Amusement is a medicine, not the point

Aristotle does not sneer at rest. He gives amusement a real but limited job:

> "amusement is a sort of relaxation, and we need relaxation because we cannot work continuously. Relaxation, then, is not an end; for it is taken for the sake of activity." ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.6, Ross translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.10.x.html))

In the Politics the same thought gets a medical image. Amusements, he says, "should be our medicines", while "leisure of itself gives pleasure and happiness and enjoyment of life, which are experienced, not by the busy man, but by those who have leisure" ([Aristotle, Politics VIII.3, Jowett translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.8.eight.html)). The card on [distinguishing leisure from amusement](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=A112%20%E2%80%94%20Distinguish%20leisure%20from%20amusement) works out the practical test: an evening of television after a heavy shift is amusement doing its proper job, and two evenings a week spent slowly learning a piece of music, with no deadline, is closer to leisure. The two answer different questions, and confusing them is how people who are technically off work spend years without once being at leisure.

The ordering also runs upward through society: "there must be war for the sake of peace, business for the sake of leisure, things useful and necessary for the sake of things honorable" ([Aristotle, Politics VII.14, Jowett translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.7.seven.html)). Modern psychology studies a cousin of the question under the name of [time affluence](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Time%20affluence), how having command over one's own hours bears on how life goes.

## Leisure is not idleness

The deepest account of what leisure is, as opposed to what it is for, is Josef Pieper's, and it is built against idleness rather than against work:

> "Leisure is a form of that stillness that is the necessary preparation for accepting reality; only the person who is still can hear, and whoever is not still, cannot hear." ([Josef Pieper, Leisure: The Basis of Culture](https://iwcinsb.org/studygroups/2024%20readings/Leisure.pdf))

Pieper insists the stillness is not inactivity: leisure "is not the same as the absence of activity; it is not the same thing as quiet, or even as an inner quiet", and it "lives on affirmation", meaning a fundamental agreement that the world is worth receiving ([Leisure: The Basis of Culture](https://iwcinsb.org/studygroups/2024%20readings/Leisure.pdf)). Idleness, on his account, is not too much rest but a broken relation to reality, an inability to receive it. The page for [leisure as a concept](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Leisure) carries the fuller passages.

This matters for practice because it relocates the failure. The person who cannot rest usually has more than a scheduling problem. [Resting deeply](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=D113%20%E2%80%94%20Rest%20deeply) and [making rest the root of activity](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=D039%20%E2%80%94%20Make%20rest%20the%20root%20of%20activity) both treat the capacity to stop as something a person can build, and Pieper calls the power to be at leisure one of the basic powers of the human soul.

## Where the traditions differ

### Who gets to have leisure

Aristotle's leisure was bought with other people's labour. He is explicit that the citizens of his best state must not be husbandmen, "since leisure is necessary both for the development of virtue and the performance of political duties" ([Aristotle, Politics VII, Jowett translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.7.seven.html)). Russell keeps Aristotle's valuation, leisure over work, and drops the exclusion:

> "The morality of work is the morality of slaves, and the modern world has no need of slavery." ([Bertrand Russell, In Praise of Idleness, Harper's, 1932](https://harpers.org/archive/1932/10/in-praise-of-idleness/))

Russell's case is distributive ([In Praise of Idleness, 1935 text](https://www.idler.co.uk/article/book-of-the-week-in-praise-of-idleness/)). The labours that once bought leisure for the few were valuable, he argues, not because work is good but because leisure is good, and a decent economy would spread that good justly. He adds the sociological sting:

> "The idea that the poor should have leisure has always been shocking to the rich." ([In Praise of Idleness, Harper's, 1932](https://harpers.org/archive/1932/10/in-praise-of-idleness/))

The disagreement is not about whether leisure is the point. It is about whether the point can be universal, and each side can point at the other's wreckage.

### Contemplation, or display

Pieper says leisure is receptive beholding of reality. Veblen, reading the same historical record, found something else at the bottom of aristocratic leisure: showing that you did not have to work. The "requirement of abstention from productive work" is, on his account, the widest of the demands of emulation, and "labour comes to be associated in men's habits of thought with weakness and subjection to a master", so that not working becomes a mark of rank ([Thorstein Veblen, The Theory of the Leisure Class, ch. 2](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/833/833-h/833-h.htm)). His book names the phenomenon after it, conspicuous leisure.

The two books are not arguing with each other. Pieper describes what leisure is at its best and Veblen what much of it has historically been for, and both can be true of different hours. The uncomfortable question they leave open is which of the two things a given weekend actually is, and [judging a person by where they rest](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Judging%20by%20where%20one%20rests) is one way of asking it.

### Recovery, or an end in itself

Contemporary research on off-job time has a more modest aim than defining leisure: Sonnentag and Fritz built their standard measure to assess how people unwind and recuperate from work during leisure time, and it distinguishes four recovery experiences, psychological detachment from work, relaxation, mastery, and control ([Sonnentag and Fritz, 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17638488/)). In the validation studies the four experiences showed moderate relations with measures of psychological well-being, and the research programme behind [recovery experiences](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Recovery%20experiences) and [effort-recovery cycles](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Effort-recovery%20cycles) has built on them since.

The difference from the classical accounts is one of definition, and the two definitions can coexist. Aristotle calls relaxation a medicine, useful and not an end; Pieper says outright that leisure is not the absence of activity and not quiet, and his own text adds that the power to be at leisure can send a person back "renewed and alive again" into work ([Leisure: The Basis of Culture](https://iwcinsb.org/studygroups/2024%20readings/Leisure.pdf)). On the recovery account, leisure is defined by its relation to work. On Pieper's, it is defined by its relation to reality. The practical kernels below use the recovery findings, and the classical accounts warn against mistaking them for the whole story, because a life whose free hours are only maintenance is a life without leisure in the classical sense. [Attention that needs recovery](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=AT037%20%E2%80%94%20Understand%20that%20attention%20needs%20recovery%20%E2%80%94%20directed%20attention%20is%20a%20depletable%20resource) and [attention restoration](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Attention%20restoration) sit on the maintenance side of the line, honestly.

### Can uselessness be the point

Zhuangzi ends a story about a huge useless tree with an invitation:

> "There you might saunter idly by its side, or in the enjoyment of untroubled ease sleep beneath it." ([Zhuangzi 1, Legge translation](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf))

His summary line runs: "All men know the advantage of being useful, but no one knows the advantage of being useless" ([Zhuangzi 4, Legge translation](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)). His tree passage and Aristotle's contemplation are not directly opposed: both put the best things outside the economy of usefulness, and sleeping beneath a useless tree does not commit Zhuangzi against worthwhile activity. The divergence is in the shape of the ideal. Aristotle's leisure is an activity, contemplation, "loved for its own sake", the highest and most self-sufficient thing a person can do. Zhuangzi's image is closer to resting in what nothing is harvesting, and [valuing the useless](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=D084%20%E2%80%94%20Value%20the%20useless) draws the lesson for a person's week.

The mesoamerican principle [that idleness is the root disorder](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=M031%20%E2%80%94%20Work%20steadily%3B%20idleness%20is%20the%20root%20disorder) marks a third position, and the choice between these three is not about facts. It is about whether the best hours of a life are the ones doing something, the ones receiving something, or the ones nothing is harvesting. [Seeing action within rest](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Y112%20%E2%80%94%20See%20action%20within%20rest%20and%20rest%20within%20action) is one classical attempt to refuse the choice.

### Is free time where the good hours are?

A one-week experience-sampling study of 78 adult workers found that most reported flow experiences occurred at work rather than during leisure, and that most measured aspects of the quality of experience were more closely associated with being in flow than with being at work or in leisure, with relaxation and motivation as exceptions ([Csikszentmihalyi and LeFevre, 1989](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/2724069/)). The same respondents reported more motivation in leisure than in work. The finding concerns what these participants reported during one week, and it does not test Aristotle's account of scholē, whose leisure is an activity rather than free time. What it does disturb is any assumption that free hours by themselves hold a person's best moments, which is an assumption neither Aristotle nor Pieper held, and which a reader holding it should notice.

## Practice: variation, selection, retention

The unmeasured evening. For two weeks, spend some free hours with every metric off, no step count, no streak, no monetisable output. Vary what fills them. Selection: notice which activities hold attention for their own sake and which need a feed to keep going. Retention: keep the ones that held. This moves motivation and overt behaviour, and it is the Zhuangzi kernel, testing what survives when nothing is measuring you.

The rest audit. For one week, each evening note whether you actually detached from work or only changed rooms while continuing to think about it. Keep the conditions that produced detachment, an end time, a walk, a device boundary. This is the [recovery-as-a-skill](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=PP048%20%E2%80%94%20Practise%20recovery%20as%20a%20skill) kernel and it moves attention. It is maintenance, and knowing that is part of the skill.

One standing sabbath. Put a weekly block of some hours where work and its tools are out of reach and nothing productive is allowed. See what fills it when nothing useful is permitted. This moves the self and overt behaviour, and it is [rest built into the system](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=PP073%20%E2%80%94%20Build%20rest%20into%20the%20system) rather than squeezed in after it.

## Story: the stories that carry the theme

[Tao Yuanming Walks Home After Eighty Days](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST358%20%E2%80%94%20Tao%20Yuanming%20Walks%20Home%20After%20Eighty%20Days): a poet takes a magistrate's post to fill the grain jar, resigns after eighty days, and walks home to his fields, saying his basic nature is spontaneity and cannot be forced.

[Bashō Walks North](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST053%20%E2%80%94%20Bash%C5%8D%20Walks%20North): in middle age Bashō sells his house and walks for months through rain and poor inns; the journey itself is his home, and the poems it yields are small, exact, and empty of commentary.

[Su Shi Walks Through the Rain](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST337%20%E2%80%94%20Su%20Shi%20Walks%20Through%20the%20Rain): exiled and stripped of office, caught out in the rain, he strolls and chants in straw sandals instead of hurrying, and looks back at the storm without rancour.

[Watanabe Finds the Playground](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST215%20%E2%80%94%20Watanabe%20Finds%20the%20Playground): a Tokyo bureaucrat learns he is dying after thirty years of stamping documents, and the one project he pushes through against his whole machine is a playground for other people's children.

[Mead's Play and Game](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST113%20%E2%80%94%20Mead%27s%20Play%20and%20Game): in play a child takes one role at a time, and in the game all roles at once; the self itself, Mead argues, is built in this apparently useless activity.
