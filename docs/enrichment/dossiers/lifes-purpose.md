# Life's Purpose

> What is a life for, and where does a sense of purpose come from?

I come to this question from the side of its absence. People seldom announce that they lack a purpose. The feeling arrives as a question on an ordinary afternoon: what is all this for? I want to know what that question is asking, why it hurts when it has no answer, and what the sources say about where an answer comes from.

Three things hide under the word purpose. One is an aim a person pursues. A second is a sense that a life makes sense as a whole. A third is a sense that it matters to someone or something beyond the person. Researchers have pulled these apart, and I keep them apart here, because an argument about one of them is easily heard as an argument about all three.

The question that organises the rest is whether a purpose is found or made. Frankl says life puts the question to us. Camus says the world gives no answer at all. The constructivists say the building is ours. I do not think the sources settle it, and the practices at the end are meant to work whichever way it falls.

### Nearby terms

- Meaning: the wider field, which the research splits into [comprehension, purpose and mattering](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Existential%20meaning). Purpose is one part of it, and [meaning-making](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Meaning-making) is the process by which people repair it when events break it.
- Goals: purpose is often defined in the vocabulary of goals, but a goal can be reached and a purpose is a direction. [The principle on purpose as a direction](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=PP025%20%E2%80%94%20Clarify%20purpose%20as%20a%20direction), and the goals section below, keep that difference in view.
- Calling and vocation: the work-centred version of the theme, held by [the research on callings](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Calling%20orientation) and [Weber's history of the word](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Vocation%20as%20calling).
- Flourishing: the good life that purpose feeds into. The Genuine Flourishing dossier holds that question; this one holds the aim.
- Flow and absorption: deep involvement is a different thing from significance. [Vital engagement](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Vital%20engagement) is the card that joins them, and the Attention dossier holds the rest.

## What a purpose is

### Three parts of a meaningful life

Michael Steger's account of meaning in life rests on two pillars. The first is comprehension, which he defines this way:

> "Comprehension encompasses people's ability to find patterns, consistency, and significance in the many events and experiences in their lives, and their synthesis and distillation of the most salient, important, and motivating factors." ([Steger, Meaning in Life, The Oxford Handbook of Positive Psychology](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/28153/chapter-abstract/212977120))

The second is purpose, which he defines in the language of goals: "highly motivating, long-term goals about which people are passionate and highly committed." On his account the two work together. People pour effort into their purposes, and the purposes that reward them most are the ones that grow out of how they understand their lives ([Steger's framework](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Existential%20meaning)).

George and Park add a third part, [existential mattering](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Existential%20mattering). In the wording of Ostafin and colleagues, who quote them, mattering consists of individuals feeling that "their existence is of significance, importance, and value to the world" ([Ostafin, Papenfuss and Vervaeke 2022](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/10615806.2021.1994556)). It is a different thing from [interpersonal mattering](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Mattering), the feeling that particular people notice and rely on you.

The split lets a person ask which part is missing: an aim, a sense that things hang together, or a sense of counting for something. Ryff's model of [psychological well-being](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Psychological%20well-being) lists purpose in life as one of six dimensions of positive functioning, next to self-acceptance and personal growth, and it came out of a complaint that the older well-being measures had no theory behind them ([Ryff 1989](https://thefpr.org/wp-content/uploads/Ryff1989_happiness.pdf)). So the research treats purpose as something a person can lack without being ill in any other way. Frankl went further, and that is where the next section starts.

### Why its absence hurts

Frankl made purpose the centre of his psychology. He called the pull toward it the [will to meaning](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20will%20to%20meaning) and set it against Freud's pleasure principle and Adler's striving for superiority:

> "According to logotherapy, this striving to find a meaning in one's life is the primary motivational force in man." ([Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning](https://antilogicalism.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/mans-search-for-meaning.pdf))

That is a claim from a clinician's experience and not a measured result, and I read it that way. What I take from it is the clinical consequence. Frankl described [noogenic neurosis](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Noogenic%20neurosis) as distress that starts in the dimension of meaning: values in conflict, or an aspiration to a meaningful life with nowhere to put it. The practical lesson is a screening question. Before treating a low mood as malfunction, ask whether the person is working well and starving for something to work toward.

His camp account gives the same idea its sharpest form. Any attempt to restore a prisoner's inner strength, he writes, "had first to succeed in showing him some future goal" ([Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning](https://antilogicalism.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/mans-search-for-meaning.pdf)). The [Purpose card](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Purpose) keeps to what that passage supports: a person who is given an aim can bear a great deal. If the aim matters that much, a worry follows at once. Where does a person get one, if the answer cannot simply be decided?

## Where a purpose comes from

### Answering what life asks

Frankl's answer begins by refusing the question as it is usually put. In the camps, he writes, it became clear "that it did not really matter what we expected from life, but rather what life expected from us." His [reversal of the question](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20meaning%20of%20life), in the same passage, is that people should stop asking about the meaning of life and think of themselves as those being questioned by it ([Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning](https://antilogicalism.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/mans-search-for-meaning.pdf)). On this view a purpose is a reply to a situation, given in conduct and not in conversation.

He then names three kinds of reply. [The search for meaning](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20search%20for%20meaning) can run through:

> "(1) by creating a work or doing a deed; (2) by experiencing something or encountering someone; and (3) by the attitude we take toward unavoidable suffering." ([Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning](https://antilogicalism.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/mans-search-for-meaning.pdf))

The third needs a guard, and Frankl supplies it himself. [Attitudinal values](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Attitudinal%20values) are not a recommendation to suffer. He writes that "in no way is suffering necessary to find meaning," that meaning is possible in spite of suffering when the suffering is unavoidable, and that to suffer unnecessarily is "masochistic rather than heroic" ([Frankl](https://antilogicalism.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/mans-search-for-meaning.pdf)). I return to suffering later. The first route, a work or a deed, is the one most people can reach on a Monday, so it comes first.

### Work as a calling

Wrzesniewski, McCauley, Rozin and Schwartz asked how people relate to their work and found three patterns. Most people, they report, see their work "as either a Job (focus on financial rewards and necessity rather than pleasure or fulfillment; not a major positive part of life), a Career (focus on advancement), or a Calling (focus on enjoyment of fulfilling, socially useful work)" ([Wrzesniewski et al. 1997, abstract](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Jobs,-Careers,-and-Callings:-People's-Relations-to-Wrzesniewski-McCauley/eb983573d298ba1b837e5fb2a2867b51b464e8ff)). The pattern did not follow the occupation. A group of 24 college administrative assistants, all doing similar work, spread evenly across the three ([Calling orientation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Calling%20orientation)). The same work can be a job to one person and a calling to the next.

The older meaning of the word runs the other way. Weber traces [Beruf and calling](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Vocation%20as%20calling) to the Reformation, where the word suggested "a task set by God." For Luther the calling was something a person accepted from where they stood, and Weber says the new element in Protestantism was the valuation of everyday duty as the highest form of moral activity ([Weber, The Protestant Ethic, ch. III](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/weber/protestant-ethic/ch03.htm)). So there are two ideas under one word. In the modern research a calling is enjoyable, socially useful work a person feels drawn to. In Weber's history it is a post assigned by something above you.

The difference matters for anyone choosing a path, because it changes who is responsible for the choice. If a calling is assigned, the task is to recognise it. If a calling is enjoyable, useful work a person is drawn to, the task is to choose it and keep choosing it. The Buddhist [right-livelihood](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Right%20livelihood) precept asks a different question of work altogether: whether earning a living causes harm.

Yeager and colleagues studied what keeps people at tedious tasks, and their [self-transcendent purpose](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Purpose-linked%20practice) line joins this section to the next. They define a purpose for learning as "a goal that is motivated both by an opportunity to benefit the self and by the potential to have some effect on or connection to the world beyond the self." In their studies, with more than 2,000 adolescents and young adults, those with more of such a purpose "persisted longer on a boring task rather than giving in to a tempting alternative," and were less likely to drop out of college many months later. Motives that were only self-oriented, such as wanting an enjoyable career, "did not, on their own, consistently produce these benefits" ([Yeager et al. 2014](https://web.stanford.edu/~paunesku/articles/yeager_2014.pdf)). That is evidence about learning, in young people, and I do not stretch it to a whole life.

### Aims that are really yours

Work can be assigned and a calling can be inherited, so the next question is about ownership. Sheldon and Elliot's [self-concordance model](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Self-concordant%20goals) gives a measurable version. A goal is self-concordant when it fits a person's developing interests and core values, and those pursuing such goals "put more sustained effort into achieving those goals and thus are more likely to attain them" ([Sheldon and Elliot 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10101878/)). The same abstract reports that attaining them brings greater well-being, through the satisfaction of needs for autonomy, competence and relatedness.

Kasser and Ryan sort aims by what they serve. [Intrinsic aspirations](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Intrinsic%20versus%20extrinsic%20goals) such as affiliation, personal growth and community contribution are linked to basic need satisfaction. Extrinsic ones such as wealth, fame and image depend on other people's reactions, and may distract from need satisfaction ([Deci and Ryan 2000](https://selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_DeciRyan_PIWhatWhy.pdf)). A test I take from this is to ask of an aim whether it would still matter if no one ever saw it.

Owning an aim also means being able to put it down. Wrosch and colleagues call this [goal disengagement](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Goal%20disengagement): withdrawing effort from a goal that cannot be reached, so as to prevent "repeated goal failure and its associated psychological distress" ([Wrosch et al. 2007](https://www.concordia.ca/content/dam/artsci/wrosch-lab/documents/PSPB_UG_2007.pdf)). People better able to do it reported fewer health problems in their studies, though the authors say the studies do not settle the direction of cause. A purpose is a direction and not a single target. I take that to mean a person can let a particular goal go without losing the direction.

An aim a person owns can still be a small one, though. Frankl's insistence is that it point past the person.

### Pointing beyond the self

Frankl states the claim with unusual force:

> "The more one forgets himself—by giving himself to a cause to serve or another person to love—the more human he is and the more he actualizes himself." ([Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning](https://antilogicalism.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/mans-search-for-meaning.pdf))

The sentence that follows in the book makes self-actualisation a side effect of [self-transcendence](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Self-transcendence) and not something to aim at directly. Psychology uses the same word in a narrower sense. Yaden and colleagues use [self-transcendent experiences](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Self-transcendent%20experiences) for "transient mental states marked by decreased self-salience and increased feelings of connectedness," a family that includes awe, flow and love ([Yaden et al. 2017](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1037/gpr0000102)). Frankl describes an orientation of a whole life, and Yaden describes a state a person passes through. The two are related, and I do not treat one as evidence for the other.

The research on adult life gives the orientation some concrete forms. Erikson's [generativity](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Generativity%20theory), the concern for guiding and providing for the next generation, was turned by McAdams and de St. Aubin into seven measurable facets, from desire to narration ([Rubinstein et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4542587/)). Fox, Tost and Wade-Benzoni review the motive to leave a lasting legacy, and say legacies "can provide a link between life-meaning and pro-social motivation" ([Fox, Tost and Wade-Benzoni 2010, abstract](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/business-ethics-quarterly/article/abs/legacy-motive-a-catalyst-for-sustainable-decision-making-in-organizations/EA4D858EF20AB081B269030657D4806E)). Hans Jonas pushes the same reach to people who do not yet exist: [duties owed to them](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Intergenerational%20responsibility) that they cannot return.

Nietzsche supplies a harder test. In the thought experiment of the eternal recurrence a demon asks whether you would live this life again, innumerable times. [My reading of it](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20eternal%20contribution) turns the question toward what a life leaves behind: what a person gives away, teaches, builds or repairs can answer yes, and what only consumed the hours cannot.

Whether a purpose has to reach beyond the self is disputed, and I return to it in the disagreements. First there is the problem of how a purpose shows itself at all.

### A sign that forbids

Not every purpose arrives as an aim. Socrates told his jurors about an inner sign that had been with him since childhood, "a voice which comes to me and always forbids me to do something which I am going to do, but never commands me to do anything" ([Plato, Apology 31c-d, Jowett translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html)). [The daimon](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20daimon) is a veto and not a map. It tells him what to stay out of, and he found in that a direction.

Heidegger's [call of conscience](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20call%20of%20conscience) has a parallel structure. It comes from the self, summons it out of its absorption in the world, and says nothing. Heidegger writes that conscience "discourses solely and constantly in the mode of keeping silent" ([Heidegger, Being and Time §56, Macquarrie and Robinson](https://dhspriory.org/kenny/PhilTexts/Heidegger/BeingTime08.doc)). I find the pairing useful because both accounts describe a pull that has a direction and no content. Where the content comes from is the next question.

### Purpose as something made

If the inner sign supplies a direction and no content, the content has to be built. The constructivist line says that is true of all meaning. Kelly's psychology of personal constructs holds that events carry no meaning of their own to be read off:

> "Whatever the world may be, man can come to grips with it only by placing his own interpretations upon what he sees." ([Kelly, The Psychology of Personal Constructs](https://www.infoamerica.org/documentos_pdf/kelly02.pdf))

The [constructivist approach](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Constructivist%20approach%20to%20meaning) adds that the building can be done differently. Crystal Park's [meaning-making model](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Meaning-making) describes what happens when it has to be. People hold a global meaning, their general orienting system of beliefs and goals, and appraise each event against it. When the two clash, the discrepancy creates distress, and people revise either the event's meaning or the global meaning until the gap closes ([Park, The Meaning Making Model](https://www.ehps.net/ehp/index.php/contents/article/download/ehp.v15.i2.p40/1041)). The sense of purpose, on this view, is repaired and not only discovered.

The usual form of the repair is a story. McAdams proposed in the 1980s "that what Erikson called an identity is really an inner story or personal myth that integrates a person's life in time (past, present, and future) and social space" ([McAdams, Narrative Identity and the Redemptive Self](https://www.erudit.org/en/journals/nw/2022-v11-nw09057/1108951ar.pdf)). His [life story model](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Life%20story%20model) finds that highly generative adults tell disproportionately many redemption sequences, in which a bad scene turns good. McAdams adds the caveat himself: stories of agency and redemption "tend to be highly favored, at least in American society" ([McAdams 2019](https://web.ics.purdue.edu/~drkelly/McAdamsFirstWeStoriesThenTheyChangedUs2019.pdf)), which is a reason to hold the finding carefully. Ricoeur's account in [Narrative as meaning-making](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Narrative%20as%20meaning-making) is that plot makes a life followable, and Husserl's [constitution of meaning](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Constitution%20of%20meaning) is the older claim that consciousness builds the meaningful world and does not receive it.

That gives two ways to get a purpose: answer what life asks, or build it. Both depend on life being livable long enough to try. The next section is about what happens when it is not.

## When no answer comes

### Suffering and the stance

Frankl's best-known stance is [tragic optimism](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Tragic%20optimism): remaining optimistic "in spite of the 'tragic triad,'" which he lists as pain, guilt and death ([Frankl, The Case for a Tragic Optimism](http://fabian.ca/sites/default/files/Frankl%20-%20Search%20for%20Meaning%20%28excerpt%29.pdf)). He turns each into a possibility: suffering into an achievement, guilt into a chance to change, and life's transience into a reason to act responsibly. He also bars the shortcut. Optimism, he writes, "is not anything to be commanded or ordered" ([Frankl, The Case for a Tragic Optimism](http://fabian.ca/sites/default/files/Frankl%20-%20Search%20for%20Meaning%20%28excerpt%29.pdf)).

The research on [post-traumatic growth](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Post-traumatic%20growth) is the nearest empirical relative. Tedeschi and Calhoun's inventory has five factors: new possibilities, relating to others, personal strength, spiritual change and appreciation of life ([Tedeschi and Calhoun 1996](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8827649/)). Growth is no way around distress. In their later chapter they write that posttraumatic growth is "common but certainly not universal," and that clinicians should never expect every survivor to experience it ([Tedeschi et al., Clinical Applications of Posttraumatic Growth](https://ptgi.uncc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/9/2015/01/Tedeschi-et-al-Joseph-Ch-30-Clinical-applications-of-PTG.pdf)).

[Meaning-centred therapy](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Meaning-centred%20therapy) puts Frankl's ideas into a protocol. Breitbart and colleagues built a group treatment for advanced cancer "to help patients with advanced cancer sustain or enhance a sense of meaning, peace and purpose in their lives, even as they approach the end of life" ([Breitbart et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3648880/)). It has been tested in several trials since, and It is the best-tested attempt I hold to work on purpose late in life.

### The world's silence

Some positions deny that life comes with a meaning attached. Camus names the clash [the absurd](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20absurd): "The absurd is born of this confrontation between the human need and the unreasonable silence of the world." It is a relation, he writes, lying in neither side alone ([Camus, The Myth of Sisyphus, trans. O'Brien](https://rintintin.colorado.edu/~vancecd/phil150/Camus.pdf)). His answer is [revolt](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Revolt): neither ending the confrontation nor papering it over. [Sisyphus](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Sisyphus) is the figure for it, and the essay ends by insisting that the struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart.

Nihilism is the position Camus works against. In the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy's wording, [existential nihilism](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Nihilism) is "the notion that life has no intrinsic meaning or value" ([Pratt, IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/nihilism/)). Beauvoir's reading is that nihilism is "disappointed seriousness which has turned back upon itself" ([de Beauvoir, The Ethics of Ambiguity](https://www.marxists.org/reference/subject/ethics/de-beauvoir/ambiguity/ch02.htm)). Her claim is that the nihilist is a person who wanted something solid, found nothing, and then decided to want nothing.

Jaspers adds the [boundary situations](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Boundary%20situations): death, suffering, struggle, chance and guilt. They cannot be solved, only faced, and he writes that people react "either by obfuscation or, if we really apprehend them, by despair and rebirth" ([Jaspers, Way to Wisdom, quoted at Epimelea](https://epimelea.com/library/jaspers-boundary-situations/)). Frankl, Camus and Jaspers agree that these limits cannot be removed and differ about what a person should do at them, which is the first disagreement below.

## Where the traditions genuinely disagree

### Does life supply the meaning, or do we?

Imagine someone in the middle of a long illness who says that nothing about it makes sense. Frankl would answer that the question is mis-set: life is asking something of this person, and the reply is the meaning. Camus would answer that the silence is the fact, and the honest stance is to stay in it. The constructivists would say the person is already building a meaning whether they notice or not.

Frankl and Camus ask the same thing, whether a human life can be about something, and they part over where an answer would come from. Frankl treats it as waiting to be given. Camus treats the world's silence as the standing condition and revolt as the coherent reply. What the dispute turns on is the status of the experience Frankl describes: whether a felt answerability is a discovery about the world or a stance taken toward it. I lean toward the stance reading, because no source I hold tests the claim, and because the constructivist work shows meanings can be rebuilt. I still find Frankl's reversal useful in practice, since it moves attention from what I expect to what is asked.

### Must a purpose reach beyond the self?

Damon and Malin, who built the best-known definition in the youth literature, say yes. Looking back on how they arrived at it, they write:

> "purpose, in addition to having the component of meaningfulness, also must have the component of engagement in the world beyond the self." ([William Damon and Heather Malin, The Development of Purpose: An International Perspective, 2020](https://coa.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/devel_purpose_intl_persp.pdf))

They add that this engagement is "often, though not always, oriented to serving other people", and that some purposes are driven by "discovery, aesthetic, and/or cosmic motives". The condition is about reach, so it does not demand altruism.

Other researchers object to making it a condition. A review in the Journal of Happiness Studies reports the dispute this way:

> "In fact, some researchers suggest that if one's purpose in life is not 'beyond the self,' then they cannot be classified as having a purpose at all (Damon et al., 2003). However, this definition is fairly contentious, as some state that it should not be in the hands of the researchers to determine whether individuals' purposes are worthy (Burrow et al., 2021), and others have noted that the vast literature focusing on the benefits of purpose has used sense of purpose measures agnostic to an individual's specific purpose orientation (Hill & Pfund, 2022)." ([Pfund, Applying an Allportian Trait Perspective to Sense of Purpose, 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10081294/))

Hill and Burrow press the same objection with a finding attached. The abstract of their paper says that "the prevailing sentiment of some researchers holds that few adolescents have a purpose in life, perhaps reifying negative beliefs about their capacity", and offers "an alternative view that focuses on sense of purpose", which "relies on abundant evidence that adolescents generally perceive themselves to be purposeful" ([Hill and Burrow, Child Development Perspectives 2021, abstract](https://academic.oup.com/cdpers/article/15/4/281/8222299)).

Two definitions met earlier in this dossier sit between the camps. Yeager and colleagues define purpose as a goal motivated by benefit to the self and by connection to the world beyond it, and test that mixed motive. Steger's definition asks only that goals be long-term, highly motivating and strongly committed, with no requirement that they reach anyone else.

The two sides give opposing answers to one question: must a purpose reach beyond the self? On the first view it must. On the second it should not be gated on that requirement: Hill and Burrow's abstract offers a more inclusive portrayal built on sense of purpose, and the Pfund review characterizes this camp's measures as agnostic to an individual's specific purpose orientation.

What the opened passages do not establish is whether this camp would count an aim that stays entirely inside a person's own life as a purpose. The inclusiveness claim and the orientation-agnostic characterization carry the opposition, but that explicit proposition is not in the opened text. I have not opened the full text of Hill and Burrow, so I do not say how they reply to Damon's reasons, and the Pfund review is my only description of how the two camps relate. What the dispute shows me is that a definition of purpose decides who is allowed to have one. For an essay on suffering and flourishing, that matters: a person whose aim is quiet and close to home should not be counted out by a measuring rule.

### Is a coherent life story a way to purpose, or a trap?

McAdams and others find that the stories people tell, and especially redemptive ones, go along with generativity and well-being. Taleb's [narrative fallacy](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20narrative%20fallacy) is the warning on the other side: we cannot look at a sequence of facts without weaving an explanation into them. The passage goes on: "Where this propensity can go wrong is when it increases our impression of understanding" ([Taleb 2007, as quoted in Kunst and Van de Wiel 2016](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5045839/)).

The two answer slightly different questions. McAdams asks what a story does for the person telling it. Taleb asks what the story does to the teller's confidence about causes. I think both are right. A story can orient a life and still mislead about why things happened, so I use it to decide what to do next and I hold it loosely as an account of what caused what.

## Practice: variation, selection, retention

::Variation:: What I would vary on this theme is the author of the aims. A life can read like a list of obligations with no one behind them, with aims that only reach inward, work held purely as a job, and a meaning that is expected to arrive finished. The direction I want is aims a person owns, some of which reach past the person, work that can be entered as a calling, and a meaning treated as built from work, love and stance.

::Selection:: The kernels I selected, grouped by what they do:

### Asking the question

1. [The meaning question](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K351%20%E2%80%94%20The%20meaning%20question): keep "what is this life for?" open as an inquiry and not a settled answer, using Steger's vocabulary of presence and search. Notice how often you want to close it early.
2. [Living the question](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K231%20%E2%80%94%20Living%20the%20question): Rilke's instruction to be patient with what is unsolved and to love the questions themselves. Notice what changes when you stop pressing for an answer you cannot yet live.

### Checking what you serve

3. [Meaning audit](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K223%20%E2%80%94%20Meaning%20audit): once a month, ask of each activity the four reason questions the self-concordance researchers use to separate a chosen goal from an imposed one. Notice which ones turn out to be imposed.
4. [Project alignment](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K313%20%E2%80%94%20Project%20alignment): list the projects that currently structure your life and ask of each whether it expresses who you want to be. Notice which projects you would drop if nobody were watching.
5. [Life chapter mapping](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K332%20%E2%80%94%20Life%20chapter%20mapping): divide your life into chapters and write one sentence on the turning point and one on what it taught. Notice where the story hangs together too neatly.

### Facing the limit

6. [Eulogy exercise](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K151%20%E2%80%94%20Eulogy%20exercise): write the eulogy you would want delivered at your funeral. Notice what it says you stood for.
7. [Mortality-to-action translation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K316%20%E2%80%94%20Mortality-to-action%20translation): spend five minutes on "I will die. The timing is unknown. What does this make urgent?", then turn one insight into one action this week. The kernel notes the finding that confronting death, when it is not defended against, can shift priorities toward intrinsic values.
8. [Absurd-revolt practice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K318%20%E2%80%94%20Absurd-revolt%20practice): for a situation that is truly absurd or unjust and cannot be changed, name it without consolation and then choose the next action inside it. This is an editorial exercise in Camus's spirit and not a tested protocol.

### Giving it away

9. [Service and creation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K225%20%E2%80%94%20Service%20and%20creation): each week, do one act of service or creation that outlasts the week. The cadence and the outlasts-the-week test are the practice's own, and the research behind it is generativity.
10. [Legacy letter](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K224%20%E2%80%94%20Legacy%20letter): write a letter to be read after your death, to one named person, covering what you learned, what you love and what you left unfinished. Notice what you could say now.

The evidence under these varies. The meaning audit rests on the self-concordance goal literature, the legacy letter on the life-review literature it formalises, and the chapter mapping on the life-story studies. Treat the revolt practice as an experiment.

::Retention:: Retention is what people keep because it works for them.

## Story: the stories that carry the theme

These are the stories I would hand someone who asked what a purpose looks like in a life. Each is retold in a line, and the full telling is on the card.

### A life that was never asked

- [ST035 — Ivan Ilyich's Question](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST035%20%E2%80%94%20Ivan%20Ilyich's%20Question): a judge who has lived the life everyone around him approves of falls ill and finds, in his long dying, that it was "not the real thing." He had lived it as others lived theirs, without once asking whether it was his.
- [ST311 — Frankl's Why](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST311%20%E2%80%94%20Frankl's%20Why): Frankl's memoir argues that the will to meaning is the primary human motivation and carries the line that whoever has a why to live can bear almost any how. Meaning does not remove the suffering, it changes the relation to it.

### The stance toward suffering

- [ST036 — Frankl Chooses the Attitude](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST036%20%E2%80%94%20Frankl%20Chooses%20the%20Attitude): the psychiatrist in Auschwitz observes that prisoners who kept something to live for endured differently, and reports a freedom that remained when everything else was taken.
- [ST279 — Frankl's Meaning](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST279%20%E2%80%94%20Frankl's%20Meaning): prisoners who kept a reason to live, a person to return to or a work to finish, endured differently. Frankl limits the claim, because most of those he described died.

### Work as a calling

- [ST057 — Agnes Martin Paints Grids](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST057%20%E2%80%94%20Agnes%20Martin%20Paints%20Grids): decades of hand-drawn grids in solitude in the New Mexico desert, titled "Innocence," "Gratitude," "Happiness." The grid was the trap, and the meaning was what it was a trap for.
- [ST052 — Beethoven Writes the Late Quartets](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST052%20%E2%80%94%20Beethoven%20Writes%20the%20Late%20Quartets): completely deaf, he writes quartets unlike anything before them, at a depth of interior detail that no one writing for the market could reach.
- [ST048 — Yayoi Kusama Paints the Hallucinations](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST048%20%E2%80%94%20Yayoi%20Kusama%20Paints%20the%20Hallucinations): hallucinated fields of dots since childhood, and since 1977 a life lived voluntarily in a psychiatric hospital, walking daily to the studio to paint them at architectural scale.
- [ST005 — Hirayama's Perfect Days](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST005%20%E2%80%94%20Hirayama's%20Perfect%20Days): a Tokyo toilet cleaner waters his plants, photographs light through trees and reads before sleep. Asked why he is content he does not explain, because the days are the answer.

### Purposes beyond the self

- [ST033 — Mandela's Twenty-Seven Years](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST033%20%E2%80%94%20Mandela's%20Twenty-Seven%20Years): twenty-seven years in prison, thirteen of them working the lime quarry on Robben Island and studying his jailers' language and history. Released, he negotiates with the government that jailed him.
- [ST086 — The Bodhisattva and the Tigress](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST086%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Bodhisattva%20and%20the%20Tigress): a bodhisattva finds a tigress too weak from hunger to feed her cubs and lies down where she can reach him, ending his life so that hers and the cubs' continue.

### The long road to a purpose

- [ST092 — Alvin Straight Rides the Mower](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST092%20%E2%80%94%20Alvin%20Straight%20Rides%20the%20Mower): at 73, with eyes too bad to drive and hips too bad to walk, he hitches a trailer to his riding lawnmower and travels 240 miles at five miles an hour to reconcile with his brother. The slowness is the argument.
- [ST089 — Layman Pang Sinks the Fortune](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST089%20%E2%80%94%20Layman%20Pang%20Sinks%20the%20Fortune): a wealthy merchant empties his house and fortune into the river and lives as a layman weaving bamboo sieves with his family. The tradition's proof that awakening does not require monasticism.

### Meaning made rather than found

- [ST312 — McAdams's Redemptive Sequences](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST312%20%E2%80%94%20McAdams's%20Redemptive%20Sequences): the stories people tell about their lives predict well-being, and suffering narrated as leading to growth predicts best of all. The telling is a shaping, not a report.
- [ST280 — Pi's Two Stories](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST280%20%E2%80%94%20Pi's%20Two%20Stories): the same survival told two ways, with a tiger or with a cook. The investigators prefer the tiger, and the story asks what it is to choose the telling a life can be lived inside.

### When life gives no answer

- [ST277 — Sisyphus Is Happy](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST277%20%E2%80%94%20Sisyphus%20Is%20Happy): the gods meant futile labour as the worst punishment, and Camus concludes that the struggle toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart.
- [ST278 — Dr. Rieux Fights the Plague](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST278%20%E2%80%94%20Dr.%20Rieux%20Fights%20the%20Plague): in the quarantined city the physician offers no theory of the plague's meaning. He treats the sick day after day, and his stated reason is plain: it is the only thing a man can do in the face of suffering.
- [ST294 — The Demon's Question](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST294%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Demon's%20Question): Nietzsche's demon offers this life again, innumerable times, every pain and joy in the same sequence. The story leaves the reader a choice between cursing the demon and answering that it is divine.
