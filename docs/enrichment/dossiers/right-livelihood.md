# Right Livelihood

> Can a living be made without harm, and what does work do to the person who does it?

A livelihood is where a life's hours go, so whatever principles a person holds, the hours answer to the work before anything else does. The trade can be reconsidered; the day spent inside it cannot. I want to know whether a living can be made without harm, and what the daily labour does to the person doing it.

The accounts run from the trades the Buddha named outright to the modern study of what work does to a person: burnout, recovery, the fit between a life and its organisation. Underneath the list sits the question already raised in another form: whether a life can be arranged so that its daily labour does not quietly work against it.

### Nearby terms

- Vocation and calling: the tradition that reads work as a task set for a person rather than chosen, held on [Vocation as calling](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Vocation%20as%20calling) with its modern measurement on [Calling orientation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Calling%20orientation); the livelihood question asks what may be done for a living, not only what it means.

- Work aims and goals: the targets a person sets inside work belong to the goals material, for example [Goal setting theory (Locke & Latham)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Goal%20setting%20theory%20%28Locke%20%26%20Latham%29); livelihood keeps to the work itself and the person doing it.

- Life's purpose: a livelihood is one of the places a purpose gets spent; the search for the purpose itself is the [Purpose](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Purpose) material.

- Workaholism: work that takes the person over is its own studied condition, on [Workaholism research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Workaholism%20research); the livelihood question asks about the harm a trade does and the growth it allows, not the hours alone.

- Work engagement: the vigorous, absorbed relation to work, defined on [Work engagement](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Work%20engagement) as a state of mind in its own right rather than the absence of burnout; it is one of the things a livelihood can be judged by, not the ethical floor itself.

## What right livelihood asks of a working life

### What does the path itself say about earning?

The fifth factor of the [Eightfold Path](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Eightfold%20Path) is [Right livelihood](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Right%20livelihood): avoiding and abstaining from wrong livelihood, work that cheats, harms or kills. The Buddha's own words name the trades:

> "Monks, a lay follower should not engage in five types of business. Which five? Business in weapons, business in human beings, business in meat, business in intoxicants, and business in poison. These are the five types of business that a lay follower should not engage in." ([Thanissaro, AN 5.177, the Vanijja Sutta](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an05/an05.177.than.html))

The placement is as instructive as the list. The path groups livelihood with [Right speech](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Right%20speech) and [Right action](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Right%20action) in the virtue division of [The three trainings](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20three%20trainings), so work is an ethical practice field before it is a question of insight.

[Action over intention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Action%20over%20intention) records the same placement logic: conduct gets its own factor rather than being left to trained intention. The grouping says something a modern reader can miss: the question the path asks about work is not first whether the job expresses the person or fulfills them, but whether it is conduct they can stand behind, the same question it asks of a word spoken or a thing taken.

The floor under the factor is [Ahimsa](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Ahimsa), the principle of non-harm that runs through the Indic traditions: whatever else a livelihood does, it begins by doing no injury.

The list is prohibitive, and it is short. [B025 — Practice right livelihood](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B025%20%E2%80%94%20Practice%20right%20livelihood) gives the formation rule in one line: earn a living without causing harm, avoid the trades in weapons, poisons, intoxicants and exploitation, align work with your deepest values.

But as [B152 — Align your work with your values (right livelihood, revisited)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B152%20%E2%80%94%20Align%20your%20work%20with%20your%20values%20%28right%20livelihood%2C%20revisited%29) observes, the list does not describe how to choose among the many livelihoods outside it, and it says nothing about how a practice will feel after a job change. The tradition refuses the harmful trades and leaves the choice among the permitted ones to the person, which is exactly where the calling tradition and the work research begin.

### Is work a calling or an occupation among others?

One answer with a long history is that work is a task set for a person rather than chosen. [Vocation as calling](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Vocation%20as%20calling) holds Weber's account of the Reformation shift that gave everyday worldly activity religious significance:

> "Now it is unmistakable that even in the German word Beruf, and perhaps still more clearly in the English calling, a religious conception, that of a task set by God, is at least suggested." ([Weber, The Protestant Ethic, ch. III](https://www.marxists.org/reference/archive/weber/protestant-ethic/ch03.htm))

The modern measurement tradition keeps the word and drops the theology. [Calling orientation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Calling%20orientation) holds Wrzesniewski, McCauley, Rozin and Schwartz's three orientations to work: a Job focused on financial rewards, a Career focused on advancement, a Calling focused on fulfilling, socially useful work. The finding worth keeping is that the same work can be one or the other to different people doing it, which makes the orientation a property of the person-work relation rather than of the trade.

Dewey refuses both the fixed station and the wage-first reading. [The dignity of labor](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20dignity%20of%20labor) holds his claim in Democracy and Education that work belongs to the educative life of a person, and the chapter's own sentences carry the weight:

> "The dominant vocation of all human beings at all times is living — intellectual and moral growth." ([Dewey, Democracy and Education, ch. 23](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/john-dewey/democracy-and-education/text/chapter-23))

The same chapter adds that "To predetermine some future occupation for which education is to be a strict preparation is to injure the possibilities of present development", and closes the argument with "The only adequate training for occupations is training through occupations" ([Dewey, Democracy and Education, ch. 23](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/john-dewey/democracy-and-education/text/chapter-23)). The sorting of children into laborers and gentlemen is the injury, on his account, and the dignity of the work lies in what it grows. The chapter on the calling describes a conception in which the task is set by God and received in a station; Dewey's chapter argues that predetermining the occupation injures the person being educated. The two accounts give the choice of work different centres, and neither measures the work by its wage alone. The Benedictine [Ora et labora](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Ora%20et%20labora) holds a third arrangement: prayer and work under one rule.

The research tradition asks the fitting question at its own scale. [Person-organization fit](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Person-organization%20fit) holds Kristof-Brown, Zimmerman and Johnson's finding that a person's values matching those of the organization predicts satisfaction, commitment and staying more than performance. [Life-span, life-space career theory](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Life-span%2C%20life-space%20career%20theory) holds Super's stages from growth through exploration, establishment and maintenance to disengagement, each with its career development tasks.

Two further models describe how the path itself forms. [Social cognitive career theory](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Social%20cognitive%20career%20theory) extends Bandura's framework to how interests and choices develop, and [Krumboltz Learning Theory](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Krumboltz%20Learning%20Theory) names the four categories of influence on the path: genetic endowment and special abilities, environmental conditions and events, learning experiences, and task approach skills. What a person can do with the fit question depends on knowing which of these is doing the moving. The models describe how working lives unfold; the question of what work does to the person inside them is where the modern evidence gets darker.

### What does work do to the person doing it?

The clinical tradition has a name for work that hollows the person out. [Burnout](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Burnout) is the WHO's ICD-11 term for a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed: energy depletion, mental distance or cynicism about the job, and reduced professional efficacy. [Maslach burnout model](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Maslach%20burnout%20model) holds the definition researchers use: emotional exhaustion, depersonalization and reduced personal accomplishment among individuals who do people work of some kind.

Two models locate the failure in the exchange rather than the person. [Effort-reward imbalance](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Effort-reward%20imbalance) holds Siegrist's account of occupational stress as a failed social exchange, high cost and low gain between efforts spent and rewards received. [The job demands-resources model](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20job%20demands-resources%20model) holds Demerouti, Bakker, Nachreiner and Schaufeli's two-category account: demands relate to exhaustion, lacking resources to disengagement.

What a person can do about a hollowing job depends on which of these describes it, and the design of the work itself is the worker's ally here: [Job characteristics model](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Job%20characteristics%20model) holds Hackman and Oldham's work design, skill variety, task identity, task significance, autonomy and feedback, the characteristics that make work itself meaningful.

Some of the harm is done through the feelings the work requires. [Impression-management burnout research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Impression-management%20burnout%20research) holds the emotional-labor line: surface acting, faking the required display, relates to impaired well-being across the meta-analysis, while deep acting, actually changing the feeling, is the studied alternative.

Two further entries widen the frame to what the work is for. [The having mode](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20having%20mode) holds Fromm's account of the mode of experience in which the self relates to the world by possession, wanting to make everybody and everything, including itself, its property; the being mode is the alternative the card holds beside it. [The attention economy](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20attention%20economy) holds the market for attention as a factor in working life, and the card itself flags its flagship phone-presence study as influential but contested. [AT059 — Recognise the attention economy — your attention is the product](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=AT059%20%E2%80%94%20Recognise%20the%20attention%20economy%20%E2%80%94%20your%20attention%20is%20the%20product) is the formation principle for work done inside that market: the attention being sold is yours.

### How much effort, and how much rest?

The path itself puts a sixth factor beside the fifth: [Right effort](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Right%20effort), the question of how hard to pull. The classical statement of the measure is Aristotle's mean, held on [The right amount of effort](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20right%20amount%20of%20effort): to feel and to act at the right times, with reference to the right objects, towards the right people, with the right motive and in the right way. [The effort of attention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20effort%20of%20attention) holds William James's claim that the faculty of voluntarily bringing back a wandering attention, over and over, is the very root of judgment, character and will, which is the effort work draws on. Effort spent without recovery, though, is the pattern the recovery research names and the burnout research measures.

The recovery evidence gives the rhythm a research base. [Effort-recovery cycles](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Effort-recovery%20cycles) holds Sonnentag and Fritz's finding that four recovery experiences can be differentiated: psychological detachment from work, relaxation, mastery and control. The rest of the tradition says what the recovery is for.

[Discipline of rest](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Discipline%20of%20rest) holds rest as a practice rather than a remainder: the rule that the recovery is part of the work. [The completeness of rest](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20completeness%20of%20rest) holds rest as something complete in itself rather than the mere absence of work.

[The importance of rest](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20importance%20of%20rest) carries Aristotle's formula from Nicomachean Ethics X.7: we toil that we may rest, and war that we may be at peace. [The rhythm of rest](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20rhythm%20of%20rest) holds the Zhuangzi's sixth chapter: life spent in toil, old age seeking ease, rest at death; what makes my life a good makes my death also a good.

Rest has its own tradition of ends. [Leisure](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Leisure) holds Pieper's account of leisure as the basis of culture: stillness, receptive understanding and affirmation rather than the mere break between shifts. [A111 — Practise leisure (scholē) as the end of work](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=A111%20%E2%80%94%20Practise%20leisure%20%28schol%C4%93%29%20as%20the%20end%20of%20work) is the Aristotelian formation rule: leisure as the end work serves, schole, the condition of contemplation. Confucius gives the rhythm a diagnostic use on [Judging by where one rests](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Judging%20by%20where%20one%20rests): see what a man does, mark his motives, examine in what things he rests, from Analects 2.10, because the resting place is the hardest thing to fake. A livelihood that leaves no honest place to rest is answering a question about output while quietly deciding the shape of the life.

### Where does a livelihood sit inside a whole life?

The hardest cases ask whether a livelihood is worth its terms at all. [The examined economics](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20examined%20economics) holds the Apology read as a pricing problem: offered his life on the condition of silence, Socrates declines, and the sentence he puts on the other side is the unexamined life not worth living, the case where no livelihood is worth its terms. The larger effects have their own standard on [The commonwealth of the good](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20commonwealth%20of%20the%20good): Augustine's two cities, where there is no justice there is no commonwealth, the measure a livelihood's wider effects are held against. And where harm has been done in the course of a trade, [Restorative justice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Restorative%20justice) holds the repair tradition for putting it right.

The question finally returns to the person doing the work. [Spontaneous right action](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Spontaneous%20right%20action) holds Wang Yangming's doctrine of the unity of knowledge and action: there have never been people who know but do not act; those who are supposed to know but do not act simply do not yet know.

### Trade, ownership and acquisition

The Buddhist floor and the Greek household science both reach the question of whether a human being may be an item of economic life, and they reach it from opposite sides. The Buddhist text names the trade: "business in human beings" is one of the five a lay follower should not engage in ([Thanissaro, AN 5.177](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an05/an05.177.than.html)). Aristotle's household science, in the Politics, defends ownership and acquisition as matters of nature:

> "For he is by nature a slave who is capable of belonging to another (and that is why he does so belong), and who participates in reason so far as to apprehend it but not to possess it; for the animals other than man are subservient not to reason, by apprehending it, but to feelings." ([Aristotle, Politics 1.1254b, Rackham translation](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0058%3Abook%3D1%3Asection%3D1254b))

The same work treats the acquiring as legitimate: "The science of acquiring slaves is different both from their ownership and their direction—that is, the just acquiring of slaves, which is akin to the art of war or that of the chase" ([Aristotle, Politics 1.1255b](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0058%3Abook%3D1%3Asection%3D1255b)), and grounds it in nature: "this warfare is by nature just. One kind of acquisition therefore in the order of nature is a part of the household art" ([Aristotle, Politics 1.1256b](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0058%3Abook%3D1%3Asection%3D1256b)).

The Buddhist text prohibits the buying and selling of human beings. The Politics passages defend the owning, acquiring and directing of them. These are different practices, and the passages alone do not establish a direct disagreement about trading people: the sutta says nothing about holding, and the Politics passages quoted here do not evaluate buying and selling people. What the two texts do give a reader is the same underlying question from opposite sides, whether a human being may be an item of economic life at all, and two answers that do not meet. I lean toward the Buddhist side of that question, and I state the limit of the comparison rather than manufacture a cleaner one.

### Monastic and lay obligations about money

The Buddha's rule for monks and his advice to the lay follower Sigala are role-dependent guidance, not opposing answers to a shared question. The monastic rule concerns accepting or arranging acceptance of money, not relinquishing all property:

> "NP 18 forbids a bhikkhu from accepting gifts of money, from getting others to accept them, and from consenting to gifts of money meant for him being placed down next to him." ([Thanissaro, Buddhist Monastic Code I](https://cdn.britannica.com/primary_source/gutenberg/PGCC_classics/lib/modern/thanissaro/bmc1/ch07-1.html))

> "There are people of conviction and confidence, bhikkhus, who place gold and silver in the hand of stewards, saying 'Give the master whatever is allowable.' I allow that whatever is allowable coming from that be accepted. But in no way at all do I say that money is to be accepted or sought for." (same source)

The advice to the lay follower treats wealth as the material of a responsible life:

> "With wealth acquired this way, a layman fit for household life, in portions four divides his wealth: thus will he friendship win. One portion for his wants he uses, two portions on his business spends, the fourth for times of need he keeps." ([Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31, Narada translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/dn/dn.31.0.nara.html))

The monk may not so much consent to money set down beside him; the householder is told to keep a quarter for emergencies and put half back into the business. The two passages assign different obligations to different roles: the rule of the order governs money specifically, and the householder's advice governs wealth generally. These are role-dependent rules, not a demonstrated disagreement about whether the follower of the path must let go of wealth.

## Where the traditions genuinely disagree

### Can anyone be a slave by nature?

The shared question: can anyone rightly be another person's property by nature? Aristotle answers yes and gives the grounds. In the Politics the claim runs from the constitution of living things to a rule over people:

> "For that some should rule and others be ruled is a thing not only necessary, but expedient; from the hour of their birth, some are marked out for subjection, others for rule." ([Aristotle, Politics I.5, 1254a, Jowett translation, Fordham Ancient History Sourcebook](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/ancient/aristotle-politics.asp))

> "It is clear, then, that some men are by nature free, and others slaves, and that for these latter slavery is both expedient and right." ([Aristotle, Politics I.5, 1255a, Jowett translation, Fordham Ancient History Sourcebook](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/ancient/aristotle-politics.asp))

Rousseau names Aristotle and answers no. The Social Contract reports the claim and then reverses it:

> "Aristotle, before any of them, had said that men are by no means equal naturally, but that some are born for slavery, and others for dominion. Aristotle was right; but he took the effect for the cause. Nothing can be more certain than that every man born in slavery is born for slavery. Slaves lose everything in their chains, even the desire of escaping from them: they love their servitude, as the comrades of Ulysses loved their brutish condition." ([Rousseau, Social Contract I.2, Cole translation, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/46333/46333-0.txt))

> "If then there are slaves by nature, it is because there have been slaves against nature. Force made the first slaves, and their cowardice perpetuated the condition." (same source)

Rousseau then closes the argument on the ownership claim itself:

> "So, from whatever aspect we regard the question, the right of slavery is null and void, not only as being illegitimate, but also because it is absurd and meaningless. The words slave and right contradict each other, and are mutually exclusive." ([Rousseau, Social Contract I.4, Cole translation, Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/46333/46333-0.txt))

The two positions answer the same question with opposite rulings. Aristotle holds that some people are marked out from birth for subjection and that slavery is expedient and right for them; Rousseau holds that the very idea of a right of slavery is self-contradictory, and that any slaves by nature exist only because slavery against nature made them. Aristotle's answer grounds a master's claim to the labour of another person; Rousseau's answer denies that such a claim can be legitimate. The disagreement is directly about ownership of a person and the direction of their labour, which is the livelihood question at its sharpest. Rousseau explicitly disputes Aristotle's account; the earlier bounded comparison in this dossier preserved the explicit limit on what the Buddhist trading prohibition establishes, and that limit stands.

I side with Rousseau. I want the test of a livelihood to include whether those doing the work remain free people, rather than somebody else's property.

### Does low burnout mean high engagement?

The research on what work does to a person holds two schools of thought on how [burnout](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Burnout) and [work engagement](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Work%20engagement) relate, and they predict differently about the same working person.

On one account, described by [Schaufeli](https://www.wilmarschaufeli.nl/publications/Schaufeli/414.pdf) as Maslach and Leiter's, the two are the positive and negative endpoints of a single continuum: engagement's energy, involvement and efficacy are the direct opposites of burnout's exhaustion, cynicism and lack of accomplishment, so a person high on engagement is inevitably low on burnout, and vice versa. On the other account, engagement is a distinct concept negatively related to burnout: a positive, fulfilling work-related state of mind characterised by vigour, dedication and absorption, defined in its own right rather than as burnout's mere absence.

The first model places engagement and burnout on one continuum. The alternative defines engagement separately while expecting a negative relationship. I favour asking about both: I want to know how drained someone feels and what still draws them into their work.

### Is work what life is for?

The classical answer runs through Aristotle, and it is blunt: "Happiness is thought to stand in perfect rest; for we toil that we may rest, and war that we may be at peace" ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.7, Chase translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/8438/8438-h/8438-h.htm)). [Pieper](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Leisure), recovering the Greek scholē, gives the same claim its modern defence: the original meaning of leisure has been forgotten in the culture of total work, where "One does not only work in order to live, but one lives for the sake of one's work" — a statement Pieper quotes from Max Weber ([Pieper, Leisure: The Basis of Culture](https://iwcinsb.org/studygroups/2024%20readings/Leisure.pdf)).

Pieper calls that outlook an inversion and sets Aristotle's "We work in order to be at leisure", his rendering of the ancient claim, against it, noting that the modern valuation of work differs so sharply from Antiquity and the Middle Ages that the ancient statement is hard to hear as anything but an attack on the basic principles of society ([Pieper, Leisure: The Basis of Culture](https://iwcinsb.org/studygroups/2024%20readings/Leisure.pdf)). The disagreement turns on whether work is life's governing end or serves something valuable beyond itself. For Pieper, leisure involves stillness, receptive understanding and affirmation; its value extends beyond preparing someone for another shift.

I lean with Pieper here. A livelihood is one of the places a life's hours go, and the hours are for something; I want a livelihood to leave room for things worth doing for their own sake.

### Does being paid wear down interest in the work itself?

The shared question: whether tangible rewards for doing a task reduce the person's own interest in it. This bears on a livelihood directly, since a wage is the standard reward for work a person might otherwise do for its own sake.

Deci, Koestner and Ryan pooled 128 experiments and reported that they did:

> "As predicted, engagement-contingent, completion-contingent, and performance-contingent rewards significantly undermined free-choice intrinsic motivation (d = -0.40, -0.36, and -0.28, respectively), as did all rewards, all tangible rewards, and all expected rewards." ([Deci, Koestner and Ryan 1999, Psychological Bulletin abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10589297/); [article PDF, University of Colorado](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/dahe7472/deci%201999.pdf))

Cameron, Banko and Pierce re-examined the same literature in a new meta-analysis and reached the opposite headline:

> "Our results suggest that in general, rewards are not harmful to motivation to perform a task." ([Cameron, Banko and Pierce 2001, The Behavior Analyst](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2731358/))

Both sets of authors agree on some of the conditions. The 2001 abstract reports negative effects "on high-interest tasks when the rewards are tangible, expected (offered beforehand), and loosely tied to level of performance", and finds that rewards linked to performance level increase intrinsic motivation or leave it unchanged. The 1999 abstract reports that positive feedback enhanced both free-choice behaviour and self-reported interest. They split on the headline reading: whether reward contingencies carry a pervasive cost, or a cost confined to particular conditions and avoidable in practice. Cameron and colleagues frame their paper as an attempt to resolve differences between the two earlier meta-analyses, so the dispute is explicit on their side.

My lean, which is mine: for someone choosing work, the conditional reading is the safer one to plan around. Pay for a task already loved, offered in advance and loosely tied to how well it is done, is the case both abstracts flag as risky. A clean average effect is less useful to a person than knowing which of those conditions their own job meets.

Limits: abstracts only. I have not opened either full paper, the replies that accompanied the 1999 article, or later work. The studies are mostly short laboratory tasks with children and students, which is far from a decades-long working life.

## Practice: variation, selection, retention

::Variation:: What tends to need varying on this theme is the drift into work that is only endured: the trade chosen for its wage and never revisited, the burnout read as a personal failing rather than a failed exchange, the rest postponed until it collapses into illness, and the having-mode relation to work in which the job is a possession to be guarded rather than a use of the person's faculties. The adaptive direction is the periodic revisiting of the livelihood against the floor and the growth standard: does the trade do harm, and does the work still grow the person doing it, with rest kept inside the rhythm rather than deferred.

::Selection:: The practices I have selected as sources of that variation:

1. [B025 — Practice right livelihood](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B025%20%E2%80%94%20Practice%20right%20livelihood): run the floor check before the next job decision, the trade in weapons, poisons, intoxicants or exploitation ruled out and the work aligned with the values you hold. Notice whether the check has ever changed a decision, or whether it only confirms choices already made.

2. [B152 — Align your work with your values (right livelihood, revisited)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B152%20%E2%80%94%20Align%20your%20work%20with%20your%20values%20%28right%20livelihood%2C%20revisited%29): repeat the alignment as its own pass, since the list does not choose among the permitted livelihoods. Notice what the revisiting turns up that the first choice did not consider.

3. [K149 — Values clarification](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K149%20%E2%80%94%20Values%20clarification): write the answer to what you want your life to stand for in three to five domains, values as directions rather than goals, in the Valued Living Questionnaire tradition. Notice which domain the working life touches least.

4. [K320 — Values coherence check](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K320%20%E2%80%94%20Values%20coherence%20check): monthly, read the calendar against the stated values. Notice where the way time is spent contradicts the priorities said out loud, and take one item from that list into the next month.

5. [K200 — Rest as practice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K200%20%E2%80%94%20Rest%20as%20practice): schedule one deliberate do-nothing period daily, ten minutes, guilt-free, rest as the root of activity rather than its absence. Notice what the first days of it show about how much rest was actually being taken.

6. [A111 — Practise leisure (scholē) as the end of work](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=A111%20%E2%80%94%20Practise%20leisure%20%28schol%C4%93%29%20as%20the%20end%20of%20work): treat leisure as the end work serves, schole, the condition of contemplation. Notice whether the leisure is entered on its own terms or used as another place to be productive.

::Retention:: Retention is what people keep because it works for them. A reader who keeps the floor check, or the revisiting habit, or the recovery discipline has what the theme offered; what stays is a matter of fit between the practice and the life it enters.

## Story: the stories that carry the theme

The question of a livelihood becomes concrete when a whole working life is in view: a career left on principle, a trade redesigned, a price refused, a rhythm corrected.

### Work that is one with the life

[ST432 — Jim Forest Washes the Dishes](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST432%20%E2%80%94%20Jim%20Forest%20Washes%20the%20Dishes): the peace activist offers to wash the dishes and Thich Nhat Hanh answers that there are two ways to wash them, to have clean dishes and to wash the dishes; hurrying to get them out of the way is the failure of the first, being incapable of the miracle of life while standing at the sink.

[ST008 — Siddhartha Learns from the River](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST008%20%E2%80%94%20Siddhartha%20Learns%20from%20the%20River): having tried asceticism, pleasure and worldly success, Siddhartha lives beside the river with the ferryman Vasudeva, and the lesson is not a doctrine but a way of hearing the river's many voices.

### The livelihood redesigned

[ST340 — Fukuoka Learns to Do Nothing](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST340%20%E2%80%94%20Fukuoka%20Learns%20to%20Do%20Nothing): the customs-office microbiologist's hospital stay with pneumonia in 1937 changes his view of his own profession; he resigns his research post and returns to the family farm with a question: what happens if you stop?

[ST313 — Jane Goodall's Two Careers](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST313%20%E2%80%94%20Jane%20Goodall%27s%20Two%20Careers): arriving at Gombe in 1960 with no formal training, her discoveries redefine the boundary between human and animal; in 1986, seeing the scale of the destruction, she leaves fieldwork permanently and becomes an activist who travels roughly 300 days a year.

### The price refused

[ST290 — Job and the Whirlwind](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST290%20%E2%80%94%20Job%20and%20the%20Whirlwind): Job, righteous and prosperous, suffers catastrophe after catastrophe; his three friends explain at length that his suffering is a punishment for his sin; God answers from the whirlwind with a speech that is questions, and the friends are rebuked for not speaking of God what is right.

[ST064 — The Lute String](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST064%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Lute%20String): Soṇa strains so hard in meditation that he considers giving up; the Buddha asks the former lute player whether strings sound best too tight or too slack, and says the energy is the same.
