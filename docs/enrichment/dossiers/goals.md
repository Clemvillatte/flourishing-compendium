# Goals

> How does a person pursue what matters, and know when to hold on or let go?

I keep two questions in view whenever I take on an aim: is this worth wanting, and does the way I hold it help or hinder the life around it. The research on goal pursuit is unusually practical. It says what makes aims motivating, what makes them attainable, and what makes their attainment hollow.

The classical traditions add what the science tends to leave alone: whether the aim deserves the wanting, and how to loosen the grip when the aim stops serving the life. Stoic teaching puts the pursuit itself inside a person's control and the outcome outside it, which changes what commitment is for. I want an account of goals that still works when pursuit fails, because failure is where the holding of an aim is tested.

### Nearby terms

- [Intrinsic motivation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Intrinsic%20motivation): motivation concerns why a person acts at all, and intrinsic motivation in particular is defined as doing an activity for its inherent satisfactions rather than for some separable consequence ([Ryan and Deci 2000](https://www.selfdeterminationtheory.org/SDT/documents/2000_RyanDeci_IntExtDefs.pdf)); goals specify what they aim at and how they steer toward it.

- [Purpose](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Purpose): an aim or reason for carrying on, in Frankl's account the why given for a life so a person can bear its how ([Frankl, Man's Search for Meaning](https://archive.org/stream/frankl-viktor-mans-search-for-meaning-1963/FRANKL_Viktor_Man's_Search_For_Meaning-1963_djvu.txt)). I use purpose for the carrying-on itself and goals for the specific pursuits inside it; Frankl's own passage calls the sustaining aim a "future goal", so that boundary is my convention, not his.

- [Commitment](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Commitment): the intent to persist; in the goal-setting literature, commitment is a condition for difficult goals to work ([Locke & Latham 2006](https://www.nj.gov/highereducation/documents/pdf/NewAdvancesinGoalSettingTheory-Locke_Latham2006.pdf)).

- [Self-regulation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Self-regulation): the machinery underneath, guiding behaviour toward goals using feedback about current progress ([Carver and Scheier, On the Self-Regulation of Behavior](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/on-the-selfregulation-of-behavior/14606DD9FB32DA230927C08B331BEF84/listing)); goals are the reference points that machinery steers toward.

## What aims do to a life

### The engineering of aims

The central finding of [Goal setting theory (Locke & Latham)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Goal%20setting%20theory%20%28Locke%20%26%20Latham%29) is that specific, difficult goals produce better performance than vague or easy ones. What the finding leaves open is the aim itself, since the theory takes the goal as given. [Achievement goal theory (Dweck, Elliot)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Achievement%20goal%20theory%20%28Dweck%2C%20Elliot%29) moved the question one step back, asking which goal a person adopts in an achievement situation, since the adopted aim changes what success even means.

The other thing that finding leaves open is where the striving comes from. [Achievement motivation theory](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Achievement%20motivation%20theory), the McClelland and Atkinson account, explains achievement behaviour as arising in situations with a standard of excellence, which locates the motive in meeting a standard rather than in the aim's content. Read together, these theories treat the aim as an instrument: chosen well, it organises effort rather than naming a wish.

How do several aims fit into one life? [Superordinate goals](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Superordinate%20goals), the remedy demonstrated in the Robbers Cave experiment, are aims that outrank a rivalry and cannot be met by one side alone. [Personal projects](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Personal%20projects), Brian Little's unit of analysis for personality, widen the lens to the extended pursuit a person actually cares about. A life holds many aims at once, and the shape of that collection is itself a choice.

### Which aims deserve the wanting

The content of an aim and its fit to the person both matter. On content, [intrinsic versus extrinsic goals](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Intrinsic%20versus%20extrinsic%20goals) is the standing distinction: intrinsic goals predict well-being, and [eudaimonic motivation, intrinsic goals](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Eudaimonic%20motivation%2C%20intrinsic%20goals) sorts goals into these two kinds with different consequences. Where the two kinds come apart is in what happens after attainment, and that is where the harder finding sits.

[Extrinsic aspirations](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Extrinsic%20aspirations) carries it, stated carefully: one longitudinal study found their attainment brought no benefit and some links to ill-being. The [aspiration index](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Aspiration%20index) is the instrument behind that research, asking what people actually want, how likely they think success is, and how much they have attained, so the same person can be scored on both kinds at once.

On fit, a goal is [self-concordant](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Self-concordant%20goals) when it is consistent with the person's developing interests and core values, and such goals are pursued with more sustained [effort over time](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Self-concordant%20goal%20pursuit%20over%20time); [values-concordant goals](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Values-concordant%20goals) name the same match from the values side.

Even a fitting aim can be held too tightly. [The paradox of valuing a goal excessively](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20paradox%20of%20valuing%20a%20goal%20excessively) is that wanting something too much can cost the very thing wanted, which makes the holding of the aim a variable in its own right, separate from the aim's content or fit.

### From aim to action

Steering is a separate skill from choosing, and what steering consists in is a loop. [Self-regulation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Self-regulation) guides behaviour toward goals using feedback about current progress, and [control theory of self-regulation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Control%20theory%20of%20self-regulation) makes the loop explicit as feedback comparing current state to goals.

A second formalisation describes the same loop in engineering terms. [Behavioral self-regulation theory](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Behavioral%20self-regulation%20theory) treats goals as reference values with a comparator measuring the gap, so the aim is the reference point and behaviour is steered against the measured distance from it.

What counts as steering at all has its own tests. [Goal-directed behavior theory](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Goal-directed%20behavior%20theory), developed by Anthony Dickinson and Bernard Balleine, says an action counts as goal-directed only when it meets two: the belief criterion, knowledge of the relationship between the action and its consequences, and the desire criterion, the outcome's current motivational value. A habit that fires regardless of whether the outcome is still wanted fails the second test, which is why the same daily action can be pursuit in one case and habit in another.

The loop needs its decisions made before the moment arrives. [Implementation intentions](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Implementation%20intentions) are if-then plans that link a critical situation to a goal-directed response, carrying the decision into the moment where it would otherwise be made fresh. Reflection has the same structure: [weekly-review implementation intentions](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Weekly-review%20implementation%20intentions) puts the review on the calendar, so it does not depend on remembering to review.

The plan also has to be aimed at something real. [WOOP](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=WOOP%20%28Wish%2C%20Outcome%2C%20Obstacle%2C%20Plan%29), Gabriele Oettingen's four steps, pairs the wish with the obstacle before the plan, because the difficult moment is where that obstacle shows up. Overconfidence has its own named failure: the [planning fallacy](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Planning%20fallacy), the confident belief that a project will run as planned.

Progress, meanwhile, has its own psychology: [the progress principle](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20progress%20principle), Amabile and Kramer's finding from knowledge workers' diaries, is that visible progress feeds a positive inner work life. The environment is not neutral either: [stimulus control theory](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Stimulus%20control%20theory) arranges environmental cues so the target behaviour occurs in their presence, and [situation-sensitivity in self-regulation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Situation-sensitivity%20in%20self-regulation) is the research on matching strategy to context, since no lever fits every situation.

### When aims collide

Aims can fail from the outside or from each other. [Goal conflict research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Goal%20conflict%20research) examines what happens when a person's own goals work against each other, and the interference goes beyond the practical: [unresolved-goal interference](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Unresolved-goal%20interference) is the attentional load open goals keep imposing while they stay open. A familiar failure mode is [escalation of commitment](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Escalation%20of%20commitment), the tendency to keep investing in a course of action that is going badly. Each of these names a way that staying the course can be a problem, which raises the question of when leaving is the better skill.

### Letting go as a skill

The research on exit has its own findings. [Goal disengagement](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Goal%20disengagement) is withdrawing effort and commitment from a goal that cannot be reached. [Goal adjustment capacity](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Goal%20adjustment%20capacity) is the broader ability, to let go of an aim that has become unattainable and take up another. The line between the two cases is attainability, and the two main literatures mark it differently.

The persistence side is the backdrop Miller and Wrosch name: "The notion that persistence is essential for success and happiness is deeply embedded in popular and scientific writings." ([Miller and Wrosch, Psychological Science 2007, abstract](https://www.concordia.ca/content/dam/artsci/wrosch-lab/documents/PSC_2007.pdf)) The goal-setting evidence supports pushing, with conditions:

> "These studies showed that specific, high (hard) goals lead to a higher level of task performance than do easy goals or vague, abstract goals such as the exhortation to ‘‘do one’s best.’’" ([Locke and Latham, Current Directions in Psychological Science 2006](https://www.nj.gov/highereducation/documents/pdf/NewAdvancesinGoalSettingTheory-Locke_Latham2006.pdf))

Locke and Latham add that the difficulty-performance relationship holds so long as a person is committed to the goal, has the requisite ability to attain it, and does not have conflicting goals. The disengagement research concerns the other case:

> "when people are faced with situations in which they cannot realize a key life goal, the most adaptive response for mental and physical health may be to disengage from that goal." ([Miller and Wrosch, Psychological Science 2007, abstract](https://www.concordia.ca/content/dam/artsci/wrosch-lab/documents/PSC_2007.pdf))

The two findings describe different situations, one within reach of ability, one beyond it. The hard part, for a person living through it, is that the sorting of the two cannot be done from outside.

The exit has its own motive correlates. In two studies of autonomous and controlled motives, autonomous motives positively predicted the cognitive ease of reengagement with an alternative goal when the current goal was perceived as unattainable, especially when participants realized the unattainability relatively early during goal striving, and were negative predictors of the cognitive ease of disengagement ([Ntoumanis, Healy, Sedikides, Smith and Duda, Self and Identity 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4104823/)). The abstract's own summary is that "When faced with failure, autonomously motivated individuals are better off realizing early the goal unattainability." These are associations from the studies' measures of cognitive ease, not effects of owning a goal; what they mark is that reengagement and disengagement were measured as two different responses.

### What a commitment is, and what it serves

Underneath holding and letting go sits commitment itself. In relationship research, [commitment](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Commitment) is the intent to persist; in the therapy world, [action commitment](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Action%20commitment) labels what Acceptance and Commitment Therapy calls committed action. Commitment can also be engineered: [commitment devices](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Commitment%20devices%20%28Ariely%20%26%20Wertenbroch%29) are a way of binding the future self, arranging in advance for the wrong choice to become costly or impossible, so the intention held now survives the weakness that will come later.

[Approach-oriented commitment research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Approach-oriented%20commitment%20research) studies experimentally what commitments are made of, while [commitment-consistency dynamics](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Commitment-consistency%20dynamics), Cialdini's principle, warns the other way: people tend to behave in line with their previous actions and obligations, so an old commitment can shape present behaviour without being re-examined.

Above single aims sits purpose. [Purpose](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Purpose) is an aim or reason for carrying on, and [purpose-linked practice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Purpose-linked%20practice) follows the self-transcendent purpose line of research. Where a goal names one pursuit, purpose names what the pursuits are for, which is why a purpose can orient a life that holds many different goals.

The existentialist accounts press a further question: what is at stake in having aims at all? Sartre's [project (projet)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Project%20%28projet%29) is a throwing of oneself toward an end, and Heidegger's [projection (Entwurf)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Projection%20%28Entwurf%29) throws a person ahead of themselves onto their own possibilities. On both accounts, having an aim is never a neutral fact; in pursuing it, the person is at stake.

## Where the traditions genuinely disagree

### Does the criticism of goal-setting make its case?

A published exchange puts the theory's founders against four of its critics. Ordóñez, Schweitzer, Galinsky and Bazerman argue that the very characteristics that make goals work are the ones that make them dangerous: "For decades, goal setting has been promoted as a halcyon pill for improving employee motivation and performance in organizations" ([Columbia Business School, abstract](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/goals-gone-wild-systematic-side-effects-overprescribing-goal-setting), [open copy](http://acornlab.azurewebsites.net/web%20articles/goals%20gone%20wild.pdf)), and in their view it is often these same characteristics that cause goals to go wild.

In their later reply they restate the recommendation: "We reiterate our original claim that goal setting, like a potent medication, can produce both beneficial effects and systematic, negative outcomes... and as a result, it should be carefully prescribed and closely monitored" ([On Good Scholarship, abstract](https://business.columbia.edu/faculty/research/good-scholarship-goal-setting-and-scholars-gone-wild), [open copy](https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/09-122.pdf)).

Locke and Latham, the theory's founders, replied in the same issue. [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/when-academics-go-wild-a-scholarly-scuffle/) reports that they took the critics to task for "an over-reliance on anecdotal evidence and generally poor scholarship", quoting their line that the authors "would do well to abandon their roles as reporters with an axe to grind and embrace good scholarship".

What the disagreement turns on is the standing of the critics' case: they hold that goal setting's benefits have been oversold relative to its harms, and the founders' reported rejoinder challenges the case's evidential basis. The report does not establish the rejoinder's full answer to the critics' concerns. My provisional lean: the critics' prescription wording is usable at face value, and the founders' own conditions (commitment, ability, no conflict) are where a reader would start.

### Should an aim be set on the outcome or on the attempt?

The shared question: when you commit to an aim, what exactly are you committed to, the result or your own conduct in pursuing it? Locke and Latham's account of goals puts the result first:

Locke and Latham describe the setting of goals as first and foremost a process that creates discrepancies: because goals refer to future valued outcomes, setting one implies discontent with the present. In their words, it "implies discontent with one's present condition and the desire to attain an object or outcome" ([Locke and Latham, Current Directions in Psychological Science 2006](https://www.nj.gov/highereducation/documents/pdf/NewAdvancesinGoalSettingTheory-Locke_Latham2006.pdf)).

Cicero's Cato gives the Stoic archer, whose end is the aiming and not the hit:

> "For though if a man were to make it his purpose to take a true aim with a spear or arrow at some mark, his ultimate end, corresponding to the ultimate good as we pronounce it, would be to do all he could to aim straight: the man in this illustration would have to do everything to aim straight, and yet, although he did everything to attain his purpose, his 'ultimate End,' so to speak, would be what corresponded to what we call the Chief Good in the conduct of life, whereas the actual hitting of the mark would be in our phrase 'to be chosen' but not 'to be desired.'" ([Cicero, De Finibus III.22, Rackham translation](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cicero/de_Finibus/3*.html))

Epictetus and Seneca turn the same stance into a way of holding plans. Epictetus advises "Use only the appropriate actions of pursuit and avoidance; and even these lightly, and with gentleness and reservation." ([Epictetus, Enchiridion 2, Carter translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html)) Seneca names the habit:

> "I will set sail unless anything happens to prevent me, I shall... be praetor, if nothing hinders me, my financial operations will succeed, unless anything goes wrong with them." ([Seneca, On Tranquillity of Mind XIII, Stewart translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/64576/64576-0.txt))

The two stances answer different questions, so I do not call either of them refuted. Locke and Latham report what raises task performance, and the passage above is about what a goal is. Cato is describing where the good of a life sits, and the archer's hit is still something to be chosen. They meet on a practical point: whether a person who wants the outcome hard, as hard goals require, can also hold it lightly. My lean, which is mine and not a finding of either source, is that a demanding goal and a reservation about the result can be held together, with the reservation doing work only when the result stops being in the person's reach. What I did not find is any study that tests that combination directly, and I did not read Locke and Latham's longer statements of the theory, only the 2006 article.

### Does picturing the aim help the one who pursues it?

The shared question: does a hopeful view of the future you are aiming at feed the effort or drain it? Taylor and Brown's 1988 review held that hopeful distortions serve effort:

> "Moreover, these illusions appear to promote other criteria of mental health, including the ability to care about others, the ability to be happy or contented, and the ability to engage in productive and creative work." ([Taylor and Brown, Psychological Bulletin 1988](https://web.mit.edu/curhan/www/docs/Articles/biases/Taylor_Brown_Illusion_and_Well-Being.pdf))

Their text adds that these illusions "enhance motivation, persistence, and performance." Oettingen and Mayer's four studies found a split inside positive thinking:

> "Positive expectations (judging a desired future as likely) predicted high effort and successful performance, but the reverse was true for positive fantasies (experiencing one's thoughts and mental images about a desired future positively)." ([Oettingen and Mayer, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 2002, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12416922/), [plain text](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=12416922&rettype=abstract&retmode=text))

The review itself was challenged. Colvin and Block examined its logic and evidence and concluded that the case "failed to substantiate Taylor and Brown's thesis" ([Colvin and Block, Psychological Bulletin 1994, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/8078973/), [plain text](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=8078973&rettype=abstract&retmode=text)).

The sources measure different things. Taylor and Brown review overly positive self-views, exaggerated control and unrealistic optimism, which are beliefs about the self and the odds. Oettingen and Mayer separate a judgment of likelihood from the experience of imagining a pleasant future, and only the imagining predicted lower effort. I do not read the two as a direct contradiction, and neither abstract settles whether an unrealistic belief about the odds helps effort. My lean, which is mine, is that the practical advice differs more than the evidence does: the goals literature gathered here treats the hopeful image as a place to start and the obstacle as the thing to plan around. Limits: I read the abstracts of Oettingen and Mayer and Colvin and Block, not the full articles, and I did not open Taylor and Brown's 1994 reply.

## Practice: choosing, steering, letting go

::Variation:: What tends to need varying is the form of the goal and the way it is held. The look-alikes of commitment are rigidity and a list of things to be seen to do. The adaptive moves are to plan the situation as well as the aim, to check whose goal it is, and to notice early when it is no longer reachable.

::Selection:: The kernels selected as sources of that variation. The implementation-intentions effect is medium-to-large in the meta-analytic record: across 94 independent tests, such plans had "a positive effect of medium-to-large magnitude (d= .65) on goal attainment" ([Gollwitzer and Sheeran 2006, University of Konstanz record](https://kops.uni-konstanz.de/entities/publication/2e749bfb-8533-437c-8203-7e788c910c5f)).

### Make the path concrete

1. [Implementation intentions](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K153%20%E2%80%94%20Implementation%20intentions): write one if-then plan for the foreseeable moment where the goal usually fails, naming the goal, the moment and the specific response. Notice, when the moment arrives, whether the pre-committed response fires or the old one does.

2. [Mental contrasting (WOOP)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K154%20%E2%80%94%20Mental%20contrasting%20%28WOOP%29): name a wish, picture the best outcome, name the main obstacle, and plan what to do when it appears. Notice, when the obstacle shows up, whether the one you planned for is the one that actually arrives.

3. [Deliberate practice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K179%20%E2%80%94%20Deliberate%20practice): structure practice with a clear goal, immediate feedback and repetition at the edge of your ability. Notice which part of the skill keeps failing, and repeat there rather than where you already succeed.

### Keep the thread visible

4. [Small-wins tracking](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K182%20%E2%80%94%20Small-wins%20tracking): at day's end, write one line naming one thing that moved forward, however small. Notice, on days when nothing moved, what blocked it, and write the nearest thing that almost moved.

5. [Social accountability](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K219%20%E2%80%94%20Social%20accountability): tell one person about the goal, send them the written commitment, and give them a short weekly progress report. Notice whether the weekly report changes what actually gets done, not only what gets said.

### Check whose aim it is

6. [Meaning audit](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K223%20%E2%80%94%20Meaning%20audit): once a month, list the recurring activities and ask each one the reason questions the researchers use: is it because somebody else wants you to, because you would feel ashamed or guilty if you did not, because you really believe it is important, or for the fun and enjoyment the striving provides. Notice how much of each activity is for enjoyment or value, and how much is guilt or someone else's want.

The cards carry their own evidence notes. Several of these are proposals built on the research and have not been tested as protocols.

::Retention:: Retention is what people keep because it works for them. A reader who keeps the if-then plan for the one hard moment of the week, or the monthly check on whose goal it is, has what the theme offered; what stays is a matter of fit between the practice and the life it enters.

## Story: aims, endings, beginnings

Pursuit and release read differently when an ending is involved. These stories hold both, without promising that the right choice is the same in every life.

### Which aims are worth having

[ST301 — The Aspiration Index](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST301%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Aspiration%20Index): Kasser and Ryan's research programme measures what people value, and people who prioritise intrinsic goals such as growth, relationships and community report higher well-being than people who prioritise money and image.

[ST392 — Franklin's Speckled Axe](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST392%20%E2%80%94%20Franklin%27s%20Speckled%20Axe): Franklin's proposed daily timetable paired two questions, "What good shall I do this day?" and "What good have I done to-day?"

### Letting go and beginning again

[ST093 — Fern Joins the Nomads](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST093%20%E2%80%94%20Fern%20Joins%20the%20Nomads): Fern loses her husband and, when the US Gypsum plant closes, her town itself; she buys a van and joins the community of older Americans travelling the West for seasonal work.

[ST014 — Marie Kondo and the Spark of Joy](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST014%20%E2%80%94%20Marie%20Kondo%20and%20the%20Spark%20of%20Joy): Kondo's method turns on one question, whether an object sparks joy, and what does not is thanked and let go.
