# Right Mindfulness

> What does it take to know what is happening, while it is happening?

Knowing what is happening while it happens sounds like the simplest of skills and is among the hardest to keep. The seventh factor trains awareness of body, feeling, mind and mental objects as they arise, attention steadied on the actual rather than the assumed. The clinical adaptations carried it into the laboratory, which is both a success story and a boundary dispute: the path factor and the intervention are not the same thing, and the two must not be flattened into one.

### Nearby terms

- Mindfulness: the modern practice family, MBSR and its relatives, a clinical adaptation of a factor that began as [sati](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Sati), remembering what the attention is for.
- Attention: the general science of how experience is selected; the factor is one deliberate training of it, not the science's own account.
- Right concentration: the neighbouring factor that steadies the mind this one keeps honest.
- Meta-awareness: knowing that one is aware; a capacity the factor trains, and one it does not own.

## The entries on right mindfulness

### What the factor is

[Right mindfulness](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Right%20mindfulness) is the seventh factor of the [Eightfold Path](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Eightfold%20Path), the fourth Noble Truth's summary of the training, and it sits inside [the three trainings](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20three%20trainings) as their most compressed form: virtue, concentration and wisdom.

The Pali word behind the factor is sati, and its root is memory. [Sati](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Sati) is the remembering of what the attention is for: keeping something in mind vividly, without commentary.

The practice the factor trains has one flagship form, [anapanasati (mindfulness of breathing)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Anapanasati%20%28mindfulness%20of%20breathing%29), mindfulness established on the breath. The central move is what I call [the returning practice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20returning%20practice): noticing that the mind has wandered and bringing it back, until the returning rather than unbroken attention is understood as the practice. What the returning builds toward is [vipassana (insight)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Vipassana%20%28insight%29), clear-seeing, which Thanissaro treats as a quality of mind rather than a technique, the ability to see events clearly.

The Satipatthana Sutta opens by naming what the practice is for:

> "This is the direct path for the purification of beings, for the overcoming of sorrow & lamentation, for the disappearance of pain & distress, for the attainment of the right method, & for the realization of Unbinding — in other words, the four frames of reference." ([MN 10, Thanissaro translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.010.than.html)).

### The refrain that does the work

For each of the four frames the instruction returns to the same sentence:

> "There is the case where a monk remains focused on the body in & of itself — ardent, alert, & mindful — putting aside greed & distress with reference to the world." ([MN 10, Thanissaro translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/mn/mn.010.than.html)).

The refrain names three qualities at work together. "Ardent" brings the effort; "alert" is sampajañño, clear comprehension of what the mind is doing as it does it; "mindful" is sati itself, the remembering that holds the observation in place. The sutta runs sati "to the extent of knowledge & remembrance", which is why the tradition glosses it as memory rather than as a blank noticing. [Bare attention in mindfulness practice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Bare%20attention%20in%20mindfulness%20practice) carries the first half of that work: attending to what arises before commentary begins.

The fourth phrase, "putting aside greed & distress with reference to the world", is the removal half of the training. The practitioner is not merely observing; the observing itself releases what would pull it into wanting or into hurt. [Meta-awareness](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Meta-awareness) names the wider capacity the refrain trains, knowing that one is aware, and [metacognitive awareness](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Metacognitive%20awareness) is the reportable part of the same skill.

The clinical family gives the same posture other names. [Mindful attention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Mindful%20attention), Kabat-Zinn's operational definition, is attention deliberately trained on present experience; [mindful presence](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Mindful%20presence) is the attended presence itself. [Present-moment awareness theory](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Present-moment%20awareness%20theory) holds that contact with the current moment, rather than the remembered past or imagined future, underpins wellbeing.

[Decentering](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Decentering) and [cognitive defusion](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Cognitive%20defusion) describe the capacity to see thoughts as mental events rather than as the world or the self, and [acceptance](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Acceptance) adds the willingness to let unwanted experience stay as it is.

Two further qualities sit beside the refrain without being named in it. [Beginner's mind](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Beginner%27s%20mind), the Zen practice of meeting experience as if for the first time, describes the freshness the training preserves; [clarity of mind](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Clarity%20of%20mind) is the unclouded state the contemplative traditions converge on. [The lived body](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20lived%20body) is the first frame's home, the body as experienced from within rather than only the body one has.

### The modern family

The clinical adaptations carried the path factor into the laboratory, and that is where the family grew. [Mindfulness](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Mindfulness) is my entry for the modern practice family, and [mindfulness-based stress reduction](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Mindfulness-based%20stress%20reduction) is Kabat-Zinn's clinical programme, its first formal practice the [body scan](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Body%20scan%20in%20MBSR%20%28Kabat-Zinn%29), attention moved systematically through the body.

[Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Mindfulness-based%20cognitive%20therapy) was developed for relapse prevention in depression, and [mindful self-compassion programme](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Mindful%20self-compassion%20programme) is the family's kindness branch.

The family also carries the practice off the cushion. [Walking meditation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Walking%20meditation) is deliberate awareness brought to walking itself, the contact of feet with ground, the movement of the body, the flow of breath. [Dispositional mindfulness](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Dispositional%20mindfulness) is the trait version of the capacity, as it varies between people, and [mindfulness's clarity mechanism](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Mindfulness%27s%20clarity%20mechanism), the Bishop et al. operational definition, is seeing mental events accurately rather than dimly.

The evidence has its own shape, and it is smaller than the path factor's own claim. A 2014 review of 47 randomized trials with 3,515 participants found moderate evidence of improvement in anxiety, depression and pain with mindfulness meditation programs. It found no evidence that meditation programs outperformed active treatments such as drugs, exercise or other behavioral therapies ([Goyal et al. 2014, JAMA Internal Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4142584/)). The reviewers' own conclusion was measured:

> "Clinicians should be aware that meditation programs can result in small to moderate reductions in multiple negative dimensions of psychological stress." ([Goyal et al. 2014, JAMA Internal Medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4142584/)).

### The attention traditions

The factor has neighbours outside its own tradition, and the neighbours give the same skill other names. [The craft of attention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20craft%20of%20attention) and [the discipline of attention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20discipline%20of%20attention) hold Weil's account of attention as the trained capacity to suspend one's own thought so that what is in front of one can appear.

[The effort of attention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20effort%20of%20attention) is James's phrase from the Principles of Psychology, attention doing the work where a thought prevails with effort, and [effortless attention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Effortless%20attention), in Bruya's working definition, is attention or action experienced as effortless.

[Focused-attention meditation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Focused-attention%20meditation), in Lutz et al.'s account, is the closest parallel to the factor's own practice: directing and sustaining attention on a chosen object, noticing when it wanders, returning it. [Attention management](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Attention%20management) is the everyday version, the practice of governing where attention goes, choosing what receives it and protecting it from capture.

[Epoché](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Epoch%C3%A9), suspension, is Husserl's trained posture of attention, the deliberate withholding of an automatic mental act. [Delight in the ordinary](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Delight%20in%20the%20ordinary) carries the freshness the training preserves: the capacity to find sufficiency and freshness in plain, repeated things, the food, clothes, work and people already at hand.

## Where the traditions genuinely disagree


### Is mindfulness bare, non-conceptual awareness?

The shared question: is sati essentially nonconceptual awareness, or can its practice deliberately involve concepts? Gunaratana affirms the first:

> "Mindfulness is nonconceptual awareness. Another English term for sati is ‘bare attention.’ It is not thinking. It does not get involved with thought or concepts. . . . It is, rather, the direct and immediate experiencing of whatever is happening, without the medium of thought. It comes before thought in the perceptual process." ([Bhante Henepola Gunaratana, Mindfulness in Plain English, quoted in Bhikkhu Bodhi, "What Does Mindfulness Really Mean?"](https://ibc-elibrary.thanhsiang.org/files/public/01_mindfulness_canonical_bhikku_bodhi.pdf))

Bodhi, reading the canon and its commentaries against that claim, denies it:

> "In certain types of mindfulness practice, conceptualization and discursive thought may be suspended in favour of non-conceptual observation, but there is little evidence in the Pāli Canon and its commentaries that mindfulness by its very nature is devoid of conceptualization." ([Bhikkhu Bodhi, "What Does Mindfulness Really Mean? A Canonical Perspective"](https://ibc-elibrary.thanhsiang.org/files/public/01_mindfulness_canonical_bhikku_bodhi.pdf))

Bodhi allows nonconceptual observation as a practice. His objection is to making it sati's nature: the phrase bare attention, he argues, conflates sati with manasikāra, the mind's automatic turning to an object, and no act of cognition is ever entirely devoid of factors imparting orientation and meaning. What remains unresolved is where sati's home is: the preconceptual moment of awareness, or the wider field of trained attention, evaluation and recollection the canon gives it.

### Is sati awareness of the present moment, or remembering?

The shared question: whether the core of sati is attending to what is happening now. The clinical definition says yes. Kabat-Zinn's own commentary gives it as an operational working definition:

> "the awareness that emerges through paying attention on purpose, in the present moment, and nonjudgmentally to the unfolding of experience moment by moment." ([Jon Kabat-Zinn, Mindfulness-Based Interventions in Context, 2003, author's site](https://jonkabat-zinn.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/JonKabat-Zinn_MindfulnessBased-InterventionsContext.pdf))

Thanissaro answers from the canon that the Buddha defined it differently:

> "the Buddha himself defined sati as the ability to remember, illustrating its function in meditation practice with the four satipatthanas, or establishings of mindfulness." ([Thanissaro Bhikkhu, "Mindfulness Defined"](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/thanissaro/mindfulnessdefined.html))

He quotes SN 48.10, which describes the faculty of sati as "remembering & able to call to mind even things that were done & said long ago", and he glosses being mindful of the breath as keeping the breath in mind (same essay). Bodhi starts from the same fact and refuses to leave it there:

> "The word sati originally meant 'memory,' but the Buddha ascribed to this old term a new meaning determined by the aims of his teaching." ([Bhikkhu Bodhi, "What Does Mindfulness Really Mean?"](https://ibc-elibrary.thanhsiang.org/files/public/01_mindfulness_canonical_bhikku_bodhi.pdf))

He holds that sati makes the object "stand forth vividly and distinctly before the mind", which takes the form of memory when the object is past and of lucid awareness when it is present (same paper).

The two definitions differ on the core. In Kabat-Zinn's the present moment frames the practice, and in Thanissaro's the core is retaining what matters in mind across time. Bodhi sits between them, accepting both usages in the canon and tying them to one capacity. Thanissaro does not name Kabat-Zinn, so the contrast is between two definitions, with no direct exchange between the authors.

Kabat-Zinn has answered the retention view himself, though in reply to Georges Dreyfus and not to Thanissaro. In a 2011 essay in [Contemporary Buddhism](https://jonkabat-zinn.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/JonKabat-Zinn_SomeReflectionsOriginsMBSR.pdf) he writes that his definitions "were operational definitions, not meant to be definitive statements in absolute accord with the Abhidharma or any other classical teaching," and that retention "did not seem either necessary or useful to feature in a working definition of mindfulness in the West." On his account, then, the present-moment definition is a working one that leaves retention out on purpose.

My lean, which is mine: Bodhi's reading fits what I opened best, since SN 48.10 itself sets the memory definition beside the satipatthana formula. For essays on wisdom development, I would write "keeping the aim in mind while attending to what is present" and avoid making either half the whole.

Limits: I read one essay by each Pali scholar the clinical definition in Kabat-Zinn's 2003 commentary (one sentence), and two sentences from his 2011 essay. I have not read the rest of Kabat-Zinn's commentary or the Pali of SN 48.10, and Thanissaro's own translation of the passage is the only wording I have.

### Is mindfulness a nonjudgmental awareness?

The shared question: whether judging and evaluating belong inside mindfulness or sit outside it. The clinical definition above includes the word nonjudgmentally. Bodhi argues against that reading of sati by pointing to the way the path is built. In MN 117 right view, right effort and right mindfulness accompany each of the other path factors, and the sutta says "Mindfully one abandons wrong intention and mindfully one acquires and dwells in right intention" (as Bodhi quotes it). From this he concludes:

> "This explanation makes problematic the common interpretation of mindfulness as a type of awareness intrinsically devoid of discrimination, evaluation, and judgment." ([Bhikkhu Bodhi, "What Does Mindfulness Really Mean?"](https://ibc-elibrary.thanhsiang.org/files/public/01_mindfulness_canonical_bhikku_bodhi.pdf))

He concedes that "there are certainly occasions when the cultivation of mindfulness requires the practitioner to suspend discrimination, evaluation, and judgment" (same paper). Thanissaro goes further in practical terms:

> "In other words, you don't abandon your powers of judgment as you develop mindfulness. You simply train them to be less judgmental and more judicious, so that they yield tangible results." ([Thanissaro Bhikkhu, "Mindfulness Defined"](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/thanissaro/mindfulnessdefined.html))

Both scholars object to treating judgment as foreign to mindfulness. The disagreement with the clinical wording is about whether that attitude is the essence of mindfulness or one stance within a larger path that includes evaluation. A clinician can reply that nonjudgmental attitude is an instruction for how to relate to experience during practice, and Bodhi himself grants that suspending judgment is sometimes right.

My lean, which is mine: both sides are right about their own settings. A person calming a spiral of self-criticism benefits from the clinical stance, and a person deciding what to do about a harmful habit needs the evaluating function Bodhi describes. The dossier's essays should say which setting they mean.

Limits: Bodhi's MN 117 argument is read in his paper's wording, and I have not opened MN 117 itself. Neither Pali scholar addresses Kabat-Zinn by name in the passages I opened, and the clinical definition is read from a single sentence of Kabat-Zinn's 2003 commentary.

## Related questions and parallels


### How do these definitions differ?

The clinical definition that the modern practice family works from, Kabat-Zinn's, reads:

> "the awareness that emerges through paying attention, on purpose, in the present moment, and non-judgmentally to the unfolding of experience moment by moment." ([Wikipedia, "Mindfulness", quoting Jon Kabat-Zinn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mindfulness)).

Thanissaro, reading the Pali sati, holds that the functions the clinical definition names are not automatically part of sati:

> "Directions such as “bring bare attention to the breath,” or “accept the breath,” or whatever else modern teachers tell us that mindfulness is supposed to do, are actually functions for other qualities in the mind. They're not automatically a part of sati, but you should bring them along wherever they're appropriate." ([Thanissaro Bhikkhu, "Mindfulness Defined"](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/thanissaro/mindfulnessdefined.html)).

From the same essay:

> "meditation isn't just a passive process of being nonjudgmentally present with whatever's there and not changing it at all." ([Thanissaro Bhikkhu, "Mindfulness Defined"](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/thanissaro/mindfulnessdefined.html)).

These excerpts emphasize different things: purposeful, nonjudgmental awareness in the clinical definition, and keeping the practice and its purpose in mind in Thanissaro's account. Whether the two definitions name one capacity or two is a question the sources themselves leave open.

### Has the research earned the claims made for mindfulness?

The 2014 review of meditation programs is cautious in its own words: mindfulness meditation programs "had moderate evidence of improved anxiety... depression... and pain... and low evidence of improved stress/distress and mental health-related quality of life", and the reviewers "found no evidence that meditation programs were better than any active treatment" ([Goyal et al. 2014, JAMA Internal Medicine, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24395196/), [plain text](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=24395196&rettype=abstract&retmode=text)).

Fifteen mindfulness researchers wrote a longer warning a few years later. Their abstract warns of "Misinformation and poor methodology associated with past studies of mindfulness" that "may lead public consumers to be harmed, misled, and disappointed", and the paper takes up the difficulty of defining the word first. They urge scientists, practitioners and the news media "to move away from relying on the broad, umbrella rubric of 'mindfulness'" and toward naming the specific mental states and processes taught and studied ([Van Dam et al. 2018, Perspectives on Psychological Science, abstract and full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5758421/)).

The two papers are not enemies, and both are from inside the field. They differ on how much trust the evidence base can bear: the first counts effects and grades the evidence, and the second asks what each study meant by the word before results are compared. I take the definitional dispute above as part of the same problem, since a trial of a purposeful, nonjudgmental awareness and a trial of Nyanaponika's bare attention are not obviously the same trial. My lean is to treat the measured result as the claim I can carry here, and to say so when the essays lean on mindfulness.

### Is bare attention the core of the practice?

The shared question: what does the work in satipatthana? Nyanaponika Thera makes bare attention the key to it:

> "Bare attention then becomes the key to the meditative practice of satipatthana, opening the door to mind's mastery and final liberation." ([Nyanaponika Thera, The Power of Mindfulness](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/nyanaponika/wheel121.html))

Thanissaro distinguishes sati from the other mental functions involved in these instructions. The functions the modern instructions name are not automatically a part of sati, he holds, but qualities sati can bring along where appropriate:

> "Directions such as “bring bare attention to the breath,” or “accept the breath,” or whatever else modern teachers tell us that mindfulness is supposed to do, are actually functions for other qualities in the mind. They're not automatically a part of sati, but you should bring them along wherever they're appropriate." ([Thanissaro Bhikkhu, "Mindfulness Defined"](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/lib/authors/thanissaro/mindfulnessdefined.html))

The two positions answer the same question and part on what sati itself contributes: for Nyanaponika the bare noticing is the engine of the practice, for Thanissaro it is a function sati can borrow from other qualities and bring along where appropriate. What remains unresolved is whether the borrowing changes the practice enough to count as a different technique, or whether the two accounts describe one discipline with its emphasis placed differently.

## Practice: variation, selection, retention

::Variation:: What tends to need varying on this theme is the default setting of the unattended life: experience passing unexamined, the body noticed only when it complains, attention captured by whatever arrives loudest. The adaptive direction is the factor's own shape, awareness established on some frame of the present, breath, body, feeling or thought, and returned to without drama when it wanders.

::Selection:: The kernels I have selected as sources of that variation:

1. [K102 — Body scan](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K102%20%E2%80%94%20Body%20scan): attention moved deliberately through the body, frame by frame.

2. [K104 — Mindful walking](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K104%20%E2%80%94%20Mindful%20walking): the walk itself taken as the frame of reference.

3. [K415 — Mindful eating](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K415%20%E2%80%94%20Mindful%20eating): one meal attended; the look, smell and changing taste of the food and the body's hunger and fullness signals, noticed.

4. [K398 — Mindfulness bells](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K398%20%E2%80%94%20Mindfulness%20bells): two or three ordinary activities from the day turned into bells, pre-set reminders to stop and attend.

5. [K108 — Meta-awareness check](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K108%20%E2%80%94%20Meta-awareness%20check): a standing question through the day, what is the mind doing right now.

6. [K111 — Interoceptive awareness](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K111%20%E2%80%94%20Interoceptive%20awareness): subtle body signals, heartbeat, breathing, hunger, tension, temperature, tuned into.

::Retention:: Retention is what people keep because it works for them. A reader who keeps the bell, or the evening scan, or the question at the red light has what the theme offered; what stays is a matter of fit between the practice and the life it enters.

## Story: the stories that carry the theme

The stories that best carry the theme's aspects, each retold in a line; the full telling lives on the card.

### What the factor is

[ST069 — The Four Foundations](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST069%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Four%20Foundations): the sutta opens by calling its practice the direct path for the purification of beings, then lays out the four frames of reference, body, feelings, mind and mental qualities.

[ST068 — The Sixteen Steps of the Breath](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST068%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Sixteen%20Steps%20of%20the%20Breath): the Buddha trains mindfulness of breathing step by step, breathing in long or short and discerning it, then breathing sensitive to the entire body, calming bodily fabrication, sensitive to rapture and pleasure, on through the mind to relinquishment; the card reads the progression as the point, nothing skipped, each step verifiable in the body.

### The refrain that does the work

[ST061 — Bāhiya's Single Instruction](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST061%20%E2%80%94%20B%C4%81hiya%27s%20Single%20Instruction): the Buddha's brief instruction to Bahiya, in reference to the seen, there will be only the seen; in reference to the heard, only the heard; the senses received without addition, and Bahiya awakens on the spot.

[ST074 — The Five Hindrances as Thieves](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST074%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Five%20Hindrances%20as%20Thieves): the Buddha catalogues five hindrances that obscure the mind, and the instruction is to know them rather than fight them, noticing when one is present, when absent, and how it arose and passed away.

### The attention traditions

[ST071 — The Wind and the Flag](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST071%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Wind%20and%20the%20Flag): two monks argue whether the flag or the wind is moving; Huineng says it is not the wind or the flag, their minds are moving, the Chan tradition's sharpest statement on where events happen.

[ST023 — Simone Weil on Attention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST023%20%E2%80%94%20Simone%20Weil%20on%20Attention): Weil's reading of attention as the rarest and purest form of generosity, school studies as its training ground.

[ST122 — Augustine's Threefold Present](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST122%20%E2%80%94%20Augustine%27s%20Threefold%20Present): Augustine's account of the present as a triple structure, a present of things past, of things present and of things future, with attention the one of the three that is lived in.
