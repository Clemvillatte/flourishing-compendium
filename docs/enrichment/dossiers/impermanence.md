# Impermanence

> What changes when we take seriously that everything we hold, ourselves included, passes?

I lose things. Not grandly, and death is only one of the ways: a friendship thins, a habit fades, a season ends. The Buddhist tradition has a word for this, [anicca](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Anicca), and puts it first among the three marks of existence: [all conditioned things are impermanent](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/dhp/dhp.20.budd.html). My interest is in what follows from that fact, because almost everything I care about is conditioned.

There is a version of this that sounds like resignation, and another that sounds like an aesthetic of falling petals. Both miss the working question, which is practical: given that the people, plans, bodies and moods I rely on will change, how should I hold them? The traditions I read answer this differently, and the differences are worth having in front of me rather than settled in advance.

### Nearby terms

- Mortality: death is the most consequential instance of impermanence. The clinical and philosophical work on [death anxiety](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Death%20anxiety%20research) and the [Epicurean argument against the fear of death](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Epicurean%20argument%20against%20the%20fear%20of%20death) belongs to my [Fear](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST275%20%E2%80%94%20Ivan%20Ilyich%27s%20Fear) material. I treat mortality here as one thread inside the wider theme of passing.

- Uncertainty: [intolerance of uncertainty](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Intolerance%20of%20uncertainty) concerns the response to not knowing what will happen. Here the fact is settled: things pass. The question is what to do with the passing.

- Grief and loss: the affective response to impermanence. My [grief for what passes](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Grief%20for%20what%20passes) card treats the mourning itself. I borrow Zhuangzi's [drumming on the tub](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2012/entries/zhuangzi/) here only where it teaches a way of holding change, not where it teaches a way of grieving.

- Meaning-making: the question of what makes a life worth living in the light of its ending belongs to my work on [existential meaning](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Existential%20meaning). This page holds the fact of passing; that one holds what I build on top of it.

- Memento mori: the specific Stoic and broader practice of remembering death, which I treat as the theme's most developed practice family rather than as a separate idea.

## What passes, and what that does to a life

### What is actually passing?

The [Dhammapada](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/dhp/dhp.20.budd.html) gives the compressed form: all conditioned things are impermanent, and seeing this with wisdom is the path to purification. Everything conditioned that arises will pass, including moods, plans, bodies, relationships, and this very thought. I want to resist two readings of that at the start, because both flatten it.

One reading treats it as a claim about objects: statues fall, dynasties end. That is true and unhelpful, because the harder cases are the ones I am inside of. A mood that is watched while it fades teaches the next mood less authority, which is the practical point of the [watchfulness](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B066%20%E2%80%94%20Watch%20states%20arise%20and%20pass) the Buddhist sit trains. The fact is not that everything ends eventually, but that everything conditioned is ending now, in the specific sense that nothing I can name is holding still.

The other reading treats it as advice to detach, to want nothing that can be lost. That is a real tradition, and I take it up below, but it is not the only reading. Heraclitus's [doctrine of universal flux](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heraclitus/) is also in this family, and it does not say the same thing: the Stanford Encyclopedia reads the one genuine river fragment as saying that some things stay the same only by changing, so that constancy and change are inextricably connected. The Daoist [return](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/216/216-h/216-h.htm) of Daodejing 16 is in the family too, and it says something else again: all things alike go through their processes of activity, and then we see each of them return to its root.

### What does the passing do to the body I am living in?

There is a way of talking about impermanence that stays abstract, and a way that does not. The body forces the concrete version, because ageing is not a metaphor. The pattern from Hartshorne and Germine's large-sample study is that the abilities do not rise and fall together: some abilities peak and begin to decline around high school graduation, some plateau in early adulthood and begin to decline in the 30s, and still others do not peak until the 40s or later ([Hartshorne & Germine 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4441622/)). My [cognitive ageing](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Cognitive%20ageing) card holds the full pattern. The change is asynchronous and distributed, which makes it easy to ignore until it is not.

This is where I find [wabi-sabi](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Wabi-sabi) useful rather than decorative. The Stanford Encyclopedia treats wabi and sabi separately: wabi is subdued, austere beauty, and sabi is the rustic patina that makes old things beautiful ([SEP, Japanese aesthetics](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/japanese-aesthetics/)). Implements with minor imperfections are often valued more highly, on the wabi aesthetic, than ones that are ostensibly perfect. Must a life look complete to be worth cherishing? I ask that seriously, not rhetorically, because the answer changes what I do with the years I have.

### How do the Stoics meet it: keep the ending in view

The Stoic thread is the most directive. [Seneca](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_101) treats death as the thing most avoided, and the practice is to keep it in view:

> "Therefore, let us so order our minds as if we had come to the very end. Let us postpone nothing. Let us balance life's account every day." ([Seneca, Letters 101](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_101))

The mechanism, in my reading, is contrast rather than fear: against the background of ending, trivial anxieties shrink and postponed living becomes visible. The same letter names the failure the practice repairs, that we schedule our lives as if the schedule were guaranteed. [Marcus Aurelius](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=T014%20%E2%80%94%20Marcus%20Aurelius) carries the cosmic-perspective version, and the Aztec [number-your-days](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=M037%20%E2%80%94%20Number%20your%20days%3B%20we%20only%20borrow%20our%20time) principle from the Cantares Mexicanos holds the same arithmetic in a borrowed-days register.

What I take from the Stoics is the insistence that the practice be deliberate, a kept reminder rather than a passing thought. The [memento mori](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Memento%20mori) kernel works by making the reminder physical: a skull on the desk, a coin in the pocket, something in reach.

### What does the experimental evidence actually show?

Psychology has its own version of the question, and its findings are more mixed than the philosophical confidence suggests. Terror management theory's [mortality salience](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Mortality%20salience) hypothesis says that making death-thought accessible increases worldview and self-esteem defence. Burke, Martens and Faucher's 2010 meta-analysis covered 164 articles and 277 experiments and reported moderate effects, r = .35, with the usual moderator caveats ([Burke et al. 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20097885/)).

The trouble is that the classic Greenberg et al. 1994 mortality-salience finding was tested at scale and did not replicate. A 2022 pre-registered multi-lab replication, 17 labs and 1,550 participants after exclusions, reported:

> "Our results were non-diagnostic of whether original author involvement improves replicability because we were unable to replicate the finding under any conditions. This suggests that the original finding was either a false positive or the conditions necessary to obtain it are not fully understood or no longer exist." ([Klein et al. 2022](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/8/1/35271/186959/Many-Labs-4-Failure-to-Replicate-Mortality-Salience))

I hold both facts together: a large body of theory and results, and a failed replication of the specific 1994 finding. What survives the failure is narrower than the theory, and I am not going to build a practice on a contested foundation without saying so.

There is a sharper construct, though. [Death reflection](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Death%20reflection) distinguishes a specific, vivid contemplation of one's own death from abstract mortality salience. Cozzolino's dual-existential-systems proposal is that we process our existence either via a specific and personalized system or via an abstract and categorical one, with growth-oriented or defense-oriented results ([Cozzolino 2006](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/12454/)). In the studies, mortality salience led highly extrinsic participants to manifest greed, whereas death reflection generated intrinsic, unselfish behaviour, and on gratitude the death-reflection induction showed the greatest increase in state gratitude ([Frias et al. 2011](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Frias-DeathGratitude.pdf)). I scope these findings to the studies that produced them.

### What does finitude do to the shape of a life?

Heidegger's [being-toward-death](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Being-toward-death) takes the question out of the management of feelings and puts it into the structure of a life. Heidegger calls a human life Dasein, and death, in his analysis, is the possibility of the absolute impossibility of Dasein: the one possibility that is mine alone, that no one can take over for me, and that isolates me as the one living my life ([SEP Heidegger](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/)).

The everyday alternative is flight. Inauthentic Dasein flees the experience of nothingness, doubling down on conformist adherence to everyday norms, seeking reassurance that it is living as one is supposed to. Authentic Dasein anticipates, which does not mean brooding over death or pondering when and how the possibility may be actualized. It consists in taking up one's finite existence as finite.

The payoff is freedom. In anticipating death, one can authentically understand and choose among the possibilities lying ahead of that possibility which is not to be outstripped. Every choice comes at a cost, because I am temporally finite and pursuing one possibility necessarily involves forgoing others ([Wrathall, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/heidegger/)). The limits are the condition under which choosing means anything.

[Finitude](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Finitude) as a concept extends beyond the mortal case. It is the condition of being limited in time, in possibilities open to us, and in how we can know things. I take that as the theme's core structure: the limits are what give the choices their shape.

### What is acceptance of death accepting?

Paul Wong's research programme gives the most granular answer to the question of what attitudes toward death are available. The Death Attitude Profile-Revised measures five dimensions, and three of them are types of acceptance:

> "Neutral Acceptance (accepting death as a natural process of life), Approach Acceptance (looking forward to a blessed afterlife) and Escape Acceptance (accepting death as a better alternative to present sufferings)." ([Jansen et al. 2019, reporting the DAP-R](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6740004/))

The three differ in what makes death acceptable: its status as biology, a belief about what follows it, or relief from suffering. I find that distinction useful because it shows that acceptance is not one thing. The [death acceptance](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Death%20acceptance) construct distinguishes attitudes toward death, which is useful when asking what a given practice is actually training.

### What happens when grief is allowed to be grief?

The Chinese material complicates the Stoic-Buddhist frame. Zhuangzi's [drumming on the tub](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/sum2012/entries/zhuangzi/), chapter 18, centres on the acceptance of death as part of the natural processes of Heaven and Earth. The chapter holds the story of Huishi's visit, in which Zhuangzi grieves and then drums on a tub after his wife's death. The SEP entry records the chapter's theme as acceptance of death as part of the natural processes; the narrative itself I do not paraphrase beyond that, because the one sentence I verified does not establish how grief and acceptance relate over time.

I bring it in because the theme needs a case where the passing is met with grief that is allowed to be grief. My [grief for what passes](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Grief%20for%20what%20passes) card is my entry point there, and I treat it as a neighbour rather than absorbing it, because the mourning deserves its own dossier.

## Where the traditions genuinely disagree

### What place should deliberate attention to death have in living well?

Seneca's rule in [Letters 101](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_101) is to keep death in view, to balance life's account every day, and to count each separate day as a separate life. The practice is deliberate and repeated. Spinoza's rule in [Ethics IV P67](https://www.earlymoderntexts.com/assets/pdfs/spinoza1665.pdf) is the opposite in form: a free man thinks about death less than he thinks about anything else; his wisdom is a meditation on life, not on death.

The two prescriptions genuinely conflict about the practice. Seneca says think about death to live better; Spinoza says the better life is what replaces the need for the practice. The disagreement turns on whether the ending is what gives the rest its meaning, or whether the meaning is available without dwelling on the ending.

I lean toward Seneca for my own use, because I find the contrast clarifying rather than morbid. But Spinoza's position is not denial, and I do not read it as a licence to ignore the fact. It is a claim about attention and its objects, and it deserves to be taken seriously rather than dismissed as avoidance.

### Is death the paradigm case of impermanence, or a special case?

Heidegger's [being-toward-death](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Being-toward-death) treats death as uniquely structuring: the ownmost possibility, which cannot be outstripped, which isolates me as the one living my life. That makes mortality the paradigm of impermanence, the case that reveals the structure of all the others.

Epicurus's argument in the [Letter to Menoeceus](http://www.epicurus.net/en/menoeceus.html) reasons that death is the privation of all sentience, so it cannot harm the person who no longer exists to be harmed:

> "Death, therefore, the most awful of evils, is nothing to us, seeing that, when we are, death is not come, and, when death is come, we are not." ([Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus](http://www.epicurus.net/en/menoeceus.html))

The practical payoff, on Epicurus's account, is that a correct understanding that death is nothing to us makes the mortality of life enjoyable, not by adding to life a limitless time, but by taking away the yearning after immortality. On this view, death is not the paradigm of impermanence but a special case, the one change I will not be present for.

I do not resolve this. The disagreement turns on whether the ending is the case that reorganises the rest of a life's choices, or whether it is the one change that deserves the least worry of all.

### What does the practice train: acceptance, urgency, or watchfulness?

The Stoic [memento mori](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Memento%20mori) family trains urgency: keep the ending in view so that postponed living becomes visible. The Buddhist [anicca](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Anicca) training trains watchfulness: watch states arise and pass until the fact of change becomes an observed feature of experience rather than a doctrine. Paul Wong's [death acceptance](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Death%20acceptance) research distinguishes three different things acceptance can mean: biology, what follows, or relief from suffering.

These are not the same practice, and the traditions do not agree about which one to train. The Stoic version can collapse into apathy if misused; the Buddhist version can be read as detachment from things that can be lost; the acceptance research shows that the target itself is plural. I hold that the disagreement is real and not settled by the available evidence, and that a reader choosing a practice should know which of the three they are actually training.

### Can anything be known if everything passes?

Plato's Socrates, in the [Cratylus](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1616/pg1616.txt) in Jowett's translation, takes the flux seriously enough to follow it to its cost. Near the end he tells Cratylus:

> "Nor can we reasonably say, Cratylus, that there is knowledge at all, if everything is in a state of transition and there is nothing abiding; for knowledge too cannot continue to be knowledge unless continuing always to abide and exist."

He adds that if the knower and the known exist always, "then I do not think that they can resemble a process or flux," and he leaves open whether things have "this eternal nature" or whether the truth is what Heraclitus says. [Socrates also reports](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/cratylus.html) that Heraclitus is supposed to say that all things are in motion and nothing at rest, and that you cannot go into the same water twice.

The Dhammapada, in the [Buddharakkhita translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/kn/dhp/dhp.20.budd.html), treats the same fact as the ground of the path:

> "'All conditioned things are impermanent' — when one sees this with wisdom, one turns away from suffering. This is the path to purification."

The disagreement turns on what knowing requires. In Plato's argument knowledge needs an object that abides, so a pure flux would leave nothing to know. In the Dhammapada verse the knowledge in question is seeing that conditioned things are impermanent, and the verses that follow add that all things are not-self. Flux does not undermine that seeing; seeing it clearly is the whole point of the verse.

My lean, which is mine, is that the two are answering different questions. Plato asks what the objects of knowledge must be like; the Dhammapada asks what seeing change clearly does to a person. I am more persuaded by the second for a dossier about living, though Plato's worry stays alive for anyone who wants stable truths about what a life is.

Limits: Socrates in the Cratylus does not commit to either side and calls the question hard to determine. The Dhammapada verse is read in one translation, and neither text is a direct reply to the other.

## Practice: what to do with the fact

::Variation:: If the fact of passing has become either a source of low-grade dread or an excuse for not investing in things that can be lost, begin with the aim. Ask what you would still care about doing if you knew the specific thing you were building would end, and what your present way of living is doing with the time it has.

::Selection:: Choose one practice and try it. The evidence supports the direction of these practices rather than the exercises as scheduled; the studies measured attitudes, gratitude and behaviour, and the specific wording and schedules are the kernels' editorial adaptations of that evidence.

### Keep the fact in reach

1. [Memento mori](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K230%20%E2%80%94%20Memento%20mori): choose one physical reminder of mortality and keep it where you will meet it. Notice whether the reminder sharpens the question of how to spend the time you have, or becomes furniture you stop seeing.

2. [Mortality reflection](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K222%20%E2%80%94%20Mortality%20reflection): once a month, set ten minutes and the prompt: I am mortal, the timing is unknown, what does this make urgent. Write by hand if you can, and do not stop to edit. Notice whether the writing produces a concrete priority or stays abstract.

3. [The deathbed test](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K226%20%E2%80%94%20The%20deathbed%20test): for a significant decision, ask which choice the eighty-year-old you would endorse. Write the answer down before returning to the present-tense considerations. Notice whether the distant self's vote changes the decision or merely confirms it.

4. [Mortality-to-action translation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K316%20%E2%80%94%20Mortality-to-action%20translation): five minutes of writing, then one insight translated into one action this week with a day and time attached. Notice whether the action actually happens, and whether it changes anything about the week.

### Watch the passing directly

5. [Watch states arise and pass](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B066%20%E2%80%94%20Watch%20states%20arise%20and%20pass): in a short sit, note moods and thoughts as rising, passing, rising, passing. Notice whether a mood watched while it fades teaches the next mood less authority.

::Retention:: Retention is what people keep because it works for them. A reader who keeps one monthly reflection or one physical reminder has what the theme offered. Review what the practice changes in your daily actions before deciding whether to continue it.

## Story: lives that meet the fact

These encounters carry the theme's different registers: the analysis that reorganises a life, and the death that forces a reckoning with how a life was lived.

### The analysis

[Being-toward-Death](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST272%20%E2%80%94%20Being-toward-Death): Heidegger's 1927 analysis, death not an event at the end of a life but the ownmost possibility that cannot be outstripped, pulling the rest of a life's possibilities into focus.

### The reckoning

[Ivan Ilyich's Question](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST035%20%E2%80%94%20Ivan%20Ilyich%27s%20Question): Tolstoy's judge, having lived the life everyone around him considered correct, discovers in his long dying that it was not the real thing.
