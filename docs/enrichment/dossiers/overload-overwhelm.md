# Overload and Overwhelm

> When the world gives more than a person can take in, what still works?

Being overwhelmed is what happens when the world's demands outrun a person's capacity to hold them, and it is one of the few experiences that is at once a feeling, a fact about the environment, and a verdict on the self. The last part is the trap: more than one can hold reads as evidence of personal failure. The traditions gathered here answer with rest, structure and steadiness: held that way, the overload becomes information about the environment, something to read and act on.

### Nearby terms

Overwhelm and saturation: held under the information and load groups below.

Burnout: held under the demands and resources group; the stress response itself belongs to a possible future Stress dossier and partly to the Anxiety dossier.

Cognitive load: held under the load group below; attention capture and multitasking are shared with the Attention dossier.

## Where overload sits in a life

### What too much input does

The costs of too much input have been measured in two ways. [Information overload research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Information%20overload%20research) gathers the studies on how excessive information input degrades decision quality, increases stress, and paradoxically reduces knowledge. The related finding that more options degrade satisfaction and decision quality is [choice overload](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Choice%20overload), though later meta-analytic work confines the overload to certain conditions.

Input also arrives in streams that compete. The media-use pattern studied by Ophir, Nass and Wagner, [heavy media multitasking](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Heavy%20media%20multitasking), sits inside the wider question of [media multitasking](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Media%20multitasking), the simultaneous use of multiple information streams, and whether heavy users differ in cognitive control. Whether smartphone notifications and infinite feeds pull attention away from what a person is doing is the question behind [notification and feed research on attention capture](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Notification%20and%20feed%20research%20on%20attention%20capture).

All of it feeds the practical question of [digital well-being](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Digital%20well-being), the impact digital technologies have on a life that is good for a human being.

### What load is, and where it hits limits

Load itself has working definitions. Sweller's theory of working-memory demands in learning and instruction is [cognitive load theory](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Cognitive%20load%20theory), and Lavie's account of selective attention, offered as a resolution to the debate between early and late selection, is the [load theory of attention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Load%20theory%20of%20attention).

The limits those loads run into have names too. Kaplan's name for mental fatigue affecting effortful, voluntarily controlled attention is [directed attention fatigue](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Directed%20attention%20fatigue). The proposal that self-control is a limited resource depleted after a period of exertion is [ego depletion](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Ego%20depletion) — the dispute over that proposal is one of this dossier's disagreements below. Training can also be stepped: the principle of raising the demand on a system in steps so that it keeps adapting is [progressive overload in attentional training](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Progressive%20overload%20in%20attentional%20training).

### What demands do, and how burnout arrives

When the demands are chronic, the outcome is [burnout](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Burnout), the WHO's name for a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed; its standard description through emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and reduced personal accomplishment is the [Maslach burnout model](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Maslach%20burnout%20model).

The conditions that produce it have been modelled too. The model in which working conditions fall into two broad categories, job demands and job resources, is the [job demands-resources model](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20job%20demands-resources%20model), and Hobfoll's 1989 model of stress, presented as an alternative to the phenomenological stress models, is [Conservation of Resources Theory](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Conservation%20of%20Resources%20Theory).

Emotional labour enters through [impression-management burnout research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Impression-management%20burnout%20research), which distinguishes surface acting from deep acting, and the caution from Klimecki and colleagues that excessive sharing of others' negative emotions may be maladaptive is [empathic distress](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Empathic%20distress).

Demand also writes itself into the body. McEwen's term for the cumulative wear the body takes when stress-mediating systems stay chronically over- or under-active is [allostatic load](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Allostatic%20load). The body's coordinated reaction to a perceived threat is the [stress response](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Stress%20response%20%28Lupien%2C%20McEwen%29), and Cohen and Wills's account of how social support touches health is [the stress-buffering model](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20stress-buffering%20model).

### How recovery works

The principle that work draws on a person, and time away from work lets that drain ease, is the [effort-recovery cycle](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Effort-recovery%20cycles), and four ways people recuperate from work during leisure time are [recovery experiences](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Recovery%20experiences).

Sonnentag's research programme on how people unwind from work stress is [recovery research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Recovery%20research), and the occupational-health psychology line on how people recuperate from demands is [recovery science](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Recovery%20science).

Recovery can also be practised deliberately. Completing the stress cycle as a shift from stress toward relief is [stress cycle completion](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Stress%20cycle%20completion%20%28Nagorski%2C%20Sapolsky%29), and the deliberate slowing of breath below the everyday rate is [slow-paced breathing](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Slow-paced%20breathing).

Where the relieving practices ease what is already here, [distress tolerance (DBT)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Distress%20tolerance%20%28DBT%29) is the perceived capacity to endure aversive states, distinguished from the act of withstanding them, and Meichenbaum's method for building coping capacity before stress arrives is [stress inoculation training](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Stress%20inoculation%20training).

### What the traditions say about rest

The classical case for rest is Aristotle's. His argument that rest is what work is for is [the importance of rest](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20importance%20of%20rest), and his treatment of rest in Book X, answering the limit that no human being can work continuously, is [the completeness of rest](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20completeness%20of%20rest).

The rule-bound and the natural follow. The monastic practice of regulating rest by rule is the [discipline of rest](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Discipline%20of%20rest). In the Zhuangzi, nature supports the body through the stages of a life, toil, then ease, then rest at death, in [the rhythm of rest](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20rhythm%20of%20rest).

## Where the traditions genuinely disagree

### Is the capacity to keep going a limited resource?

The shared question: when effort runs out, is something finite being used up? Baumeister and colleagues argued so from a series of experiments, reporting results:

> "These results suggest that the self's capacity for active volition is limited and that a range of seemingly different, unrelated acts share a common resource." ([Baumeister, Bratslavsky, Muraven and Tice, JPSP 1998, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9599441/), [plain text](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=9599441&rettype=abstract&retmode=text))

A 23-laboratory preregistered replication of the standard protocol found almost nothing:

> "Meta-analysis of the studies revealed that the size of the ego-depletion effect was small with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) that encompassed zero (d = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.15]." ([Hagger and colleagues, Perspectives on Psychological Science 2016, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27474142/), [plain text](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=27474142&rettype=abstract&retmode=text))

Later work has not closed the question: a 12-laboratory replication of a different task pair [found a small and significant effect](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8186735/) (d = 0.10), and a reader should treat the resource model of self-control as unsettled.

### Does a larger set of options overload the chooser?

The shared question: past some point, does adding options make a person less likely to choose, or less happy with what they chose? Iyengar and Lepper reported that it does, in three studies:

> "These experiments, which were conducted in both field and laboratory settings, show that people are more likely to purchase gourmet jams or chocolates or to undertake optional class essay assignments when offered a limited array of 6 choices rather than a more extensive array of 24 or 30 choices." ([Iyengar and Lepper, JPSP 2000, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11138768/), [plain text](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=11138768&rettype=abstract&retmode=text))

A meta-analysis of the later literature found something different:

> Their meta-analysis covered "63 conditions from 50 published and unpublished experiments" with 5,036 participants, and they "found a mean effect size of virtually zero but considerable variance between studies." ([Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd, Journal of Consumer Research 2010, abstract](https://academic.oup.com/jcr/article/37/3/409/1827647), [author copy](https://scheibehenne.com/ScheibehenneGreifenederTodd2010.pdf))

A second meta-analysis, built on 99 observations, reached a different overall result once it accounted for the conditions under which overload happens:

> "Finally, we document that when moderating variables are taken into account the overall effect of assortment size on choice overload is significant—a finding counter to the data reported by prior meta-analytic research." ([Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman, Journal of Consumer Psychology 2015, abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1016/j.jcps.2014.08.002), [author copy](https://chernev.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/02/ChoiceOverload_JCP_2015.pdf))

The two meta-analyses disagree on what a zero average means. The 2010 authors report an average near zero with large differences between studies and no condition that reliably produced overload. The 2015 authors name four moderators (choice set complexity, decision task difficulty, preference uncertainty and decision goal) and report that each had a reliable effect on overload. Both can describe the same data if the average hides cases that go in opposite directions, and I cannot judge from abstracts which treatment of the moderators is right.

My lean, which is mine, is that the single claim that "more options overload people" should not be written as a general law in the essays. The more defensible claim is conditional: it holds for hard decisions among complex sets when a person is unsure of their preferences. Limits: I read only abstracts, including the 2015 one as returned in a search listing and not from its publisher page, and I did not open the commentary on the 2010 paper or any study of overload outside consumer choice, such as information overload at work.

### Is rest for the sake of work, or work for the sake of rest?

The shared question: what is rest for? Aristotle gives both answers in the same book. In the discussion of amusement he says:

> "Relaxation, then, is not an end; for it is taken for the sake of activity." ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.6, Ross translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.10.x.html))

A few paragraphs later, on contemplation, the order runs the other way:

> "And happiness is thought to depend on leisure; for we are busy that we may have leisure, and make war that we may live in peace." ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics X.7, Ross translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.10.x.html))

The first treats rest as a tool for further effort; the second treats leisure as the point of effort. The first is the practical answer for someone who is overwhelmed now, since it explains why rest restores work. The second asks what the work was for.

## Practice: variation, selection, retention

::Variation:: What tends to need varying is the pace and the input. The look-alikes of rest are scrolling, more tasks and the next screen. The adaptive moves are to schedule rest as rest, to let attention be held by something that asks nothing of it, and to bring the body down before the mind is asked to think.

::Selection:: The kernels I have selected as sources of that variation:

1. [Rest as practice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K200%20%E2%80%94%20Rest%20as%20practice): schedule one deliberate do-nothing period daily, ten minutes, guilt-free.

2. [Soft fascination](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K107%20%E2%80%94%20Soft%20fascination): spend 20 to 30 minutes in an environment that gently engages attention without demanding it.

3. [Non-sleep deep rest (NSDR)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K409%20%E2%80%94%20Non-sleep%20deep%20rest%20%28NSDR%29): guided practices that take the brain and body into a state of deep relaxation.

The cards carry their own evidence notes. Several of these are proposals built on the traditions and have not been tested as protocols.

::Retention:: Retention is what people keep because it works for them. A reader who keeps the daily ten minutes, or the walk without a phone, has what the theme offered; what stays is a matter of fit between the practice and the life it enters.
