# Wisdom

> What is wisdom doing when it judges, and can it be cultivated or only matured?

Wisdom is what everyone wants to be credited with, and its look-alikes are everywhere: cleverness, accumulated years, confident opinion. The classical accounts make it a capacity of judgment, seeing what is best in the particular case, and the research that tries to measure it mostly measures its neighbours. Whether wisdom can be cultivated or only matured is the open question, and the practices cluster around the answer.

### Nearby terms

- Judgement and insight: held here under the map's judgement and insight groups; the closest anchors are [Reflective thinking](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Reflective%20thinking) and [Insight research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Insight%20research).
- Practical wisdom and phronesis: the same Aristotelian capacity under its English and Greek names, held by [Practical wisdom](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Practical%20wisdom) and [Phronesis](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Phronesis).
- Flourishing: the life wisdom serves; the Genuine Flourishing dossier holds that question, and this one holds the capacity that steers toward it.

## The map: the entries on wisdom

### What wisdom is

[Wisdom](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Wisdom): the knowledge and judgment that experience is supposed to leave behind; the Berlin paradigm treats it as expertise in the fundamental pragmatics of life, and the wise-reasoning programme as metacognitive capacities people apply to social conflict.

[Phronesis](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Phronesis): Aristotle's virtue of practical wisdom: the capacity to deliberate well about what is good for oneself and for living well in general.

[Practical wisdom](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Practical%20wisdom): Aristotle's Book VI account, set beside Confucius: practical wisdom is not knowledge of general principles only, because action is concerned with particular details.

[Sophia](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Sophia): Aristotle's theoretical wisdom, a union of intuitive reason and scientific knowledge of the highest objects.

[Prudence](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Prudence): here Aristotle's practical wisdom, phronesis: a capacity for good deliberation connected with ethical virtue.

[Prohairesis (deliberate choice)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Prohairesis%20%28deliberate%20choice%29): Aristotle's reasoned choice, joining desire for a goal with deliberation about action.

[Wisdom theory Berlin paradigm](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Wisdom%20theory%20Berlin%20paradigm): Baltes's account of wisdom as expert knowledge about the fundamental pragmatics of life: life planning, management and review.

[Wise reasoning](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Wise%20reasoning): Grossmann's programme: wisdom as a set of reasoning strategies people use, or fail to use, situation by situation, and not as a personality type.

[Socioemotional wisdom](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Socioemotional%20wisdom): the strength Erikson places at the end of the life cycle, won from the crisis of integrity versus despair.

[Knowing-how versus knowing-that](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Knowing-how%20versus%20knowing-that): Ryle's contrast between knowledge that shows in ability and knowledge that something is the case.

[Knowing-how](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Knowing-how): the kind of knowledge that shows in ability, in Ryle's The Concept of Mind (1949).

[Knowing-that](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Knowing-that): the propositional side of Ryle's contrast, knowledge that can be true or false, stated and imparted.

[Knowing by acquaintance](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Knowing%20by%20acquaintance): direct knowledge by standing in the experience of a thing, not by holding a description of it.

### Knowing the limits of what you know

[Socratic wisdom](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Socratic%20wisdom): the wisdom Plato's Apology gives Socrates after the Delphic oracle: knowing that you do not know.

[Socratic ignorance](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Socratic%20ignorance): Socrates's profession at his trial that he does not know the things that matter most and, unlike others, does not think he does.

[Intellectual humility](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Intellectual%20humility): the recognition that one's beliefs might be wrong, in Leary and colleagues' 2017 paper.

[Epistemic humility](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Epistemic%20humility): the disposition psychology usually calls intellectual humility: treating one's own beliefs as possibly wrong.

[Humility](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Humility): in Tangney's review, not self-deprecation but an accurate assessment of one's characteristics.

[Expressed humility](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Expressed%20humility): humility as other people can see it and rate it, in organizations (Owens, Johnson and Mitchell).

[Relational humility](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Relational%20humility): Davis and colleagues' model of humility as a judgment other people make about someone.

[Leader humility](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Leader%20humility): expressed humility improves team performance and psychological safety (Owens and Hekman, 2016).

[Openness and not knowing in skilled practice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Openness%20and%20not%20knowing%20in%20skilled%20practice): Ericsson: the key challenge for aspiring experts is to avoid the arrested development associated with automaticity.

[The veil of ignorance](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20veil%20of%20ignorance): Rawls's device in A Theory of Justice for a fair choice of first principles: the parties sit in an original position, stripped of knowledge of their own situation.

## Where the traditions genuinely disagree

### Is there one wisdom, or two?

The shared question: is the wisdom of the contemplative the same excellence as the wisdom of the person who acts well in a hard situation? Aristotle splits them. Sophia knows the highest and unchanging things:

> "Therefore wisdom must be intuitive reason combined with scientific knowledge-scientific knowledge of the highest objects which has received as it were its proper completion." ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.7, Ross translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.6.vi.html))

Practical wisdom works on a different object, and Aristotle uses a plain contrast to say so:

> "any one would say that what is wise is the same but what is practically wise is different" ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.7](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.6.vi.html))

Modern psychology sides with the practical branch. The Berlin paradigm defines wisdom as "an expert knowledge system concerning the fundamental pragmatics of life", covering "knowledge and judgment about the meaning and conduct of life" ([Baltes and Staudinger, American Psychologist 2000, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11392856/)). The classical contemplative ideal of wisdom, knowledge of the highest things, drops out of the research definition. What stays open is whether a life needs both kinds, and whether the contemplative kind steadies the practical one or competes with it for a person's time. The sources above do not settle it.

### Can a clever person be wise without being good?

The shared question: is wisdom a skill for reaching goals, or does it include choosing good goals? Aristotle separates wisdom from mere skill at getting what one aims at:

> "Now if the mark be noble, the cleverness is laudable, but if the mark be bad, the cleverness is mere smartness; hence we call even men of practical wisdom clever or smart." ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.12](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.6.vi.html))

He then ties the two together in both directions:

> "It is clear, then, from what has been said, that it is not possible to be good in the strict sense without practical wisdom, nor practically wise without moral virtue." ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.13](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.6.vi.html))

Baltes and Staudinger keep the link in their own phrase: wisdom as a metaheuristic "that organizes and orchestrates knowledge toward human excellence in mind and virtue, both individually and collectively" ([Baltes and Staudinger 2000, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11392856/)). Grossmann's account of wise thinking, in contrast, names aspects of thinking: "intellectual humility, recognition of uncertainty and change" ([Grossmann 2017, abstract](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691616672066/)). Whether a person who reasons that way but chooses badly would count as wise is a question these abstracts leave open.

### Is wisdom something you have, or knowing that you lack it?

The shared question: does a wise person hold a large store of knowledge, or hold a particular stance toward what they know? Socrates puts the stance first. In Jowett's rendering of the oracle's meaning:

> "He, O men, is the wisest, who, like Socrates, knows that his wisdom is in truth worth nothing." ([Plato, Apology, Jowett translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Plato/apology.html))

The Daodejing says something close, and names the opposite error a disease:

> "To know and yet (think) we do not know is the highest (attainment); not to know (and yet think) we do know is a disease." ([Laozi, Daodejing 71, Legge translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/216/216-h/216-h.htm))

The Berlin paradigm builds uncertainty into the definition as one of five criteria: "recognition and management of uncertainty" ([Baltes and Staudinger 2000, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11392856/)). The traditions and the research agree that knowing one's limits belongs to wisdom. They differ on weight. For Socrates it is close to the whole of human wisdom; for the expertise model it is one criterion beside rich factual and procedural knowledge.

### Is wisdom a trait, or something a situation draws out?

The shared question: when we call a person wise, are we describing them or their circumstances? The Berlin tradition measured it as a feature of the person, scored from think-aloud answers to life problems. Grossmann's review argues the evidence points elsewhere:

> "Empirical evidence indicates that people's ability to think wisely varies dramatically across experiential contexts that they encounter over the life span. Moreover, wise thinking varies from one situation to another, with self-focused contexts inhibiting wise thinking." ([Grossmann, Wisdom in Context, Perspectives on Psychological Science 2017, abstract](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691616672066/))

A specific finding: across three experiments (N = 693), participants reasoned more wisely about another person's problems than their own, and self-distancing removed the gap. The authors add that "contrary to the adage 'with age comes wisdom,' our findings suggest that there are no age differences in wise reasoning about personal conflicts" ([Grossmann and Kross, Psychological Science 2014, abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797614535400)). That result concerns reasoning about personal conflicts, in the measures used, and does not show that life experience adds nothing to wisdom in general. What it does show is that the same person reasons differently in different positions, which is a reason to treat wisdom as partly a matter of where one stands when one thinks.

### Can wisdom be taught, or only grown into?

The shared question: if wisdom matters, what builds it? The neo-Aristotelian answer starts with character:

> "Full virtue and practical wisdom comprise the end of neo-Aristotelian moral development, but wisdom cannot be cultivated straight away through arguments and teaching." ([Sanderse, Journal of Moral Education, abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03057240.2018.1497952))

The abstract goes on to say wisdom "is integrated with, and builds upon, habituation". The experimental answer is that a brief, structured practice can raise wise reasoning. In two preregistered experiments (total N = 555), people who reflected in the third person in a month-long diary showed "a significant increase in wise reasoning about interpersonal challenges" compared with before the intervention ([Grossmann and colleagues, Psychological Science 2021, abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0956797620969170)). In the earlier two-experiment study, cueing people to reason about personal issues from a distanced perspective enhanced wise reasoning, attitudes and behavior ([Kross and Grossmann, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General 2012, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21728454/)). The two claims are about different things. One concerns the formation of a person's character over years; the other concerns scores on a reasoning measure after a short practice. Neither abstract says the other is wrong.

### Does wisdom follow rules, or does it set them?

The shared question: could wisdom be written down as a procedure? Aristotle defines virtue by a standard that involves the wise person:

> "Virtue, then, is a state of character concerned with choice, lying in a mean, i.e. the mean relative to us, this being determined by a rational principle, and by that principle by which the man of practical wisdom would determine it." ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics II.6](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.2.ii.html))

The definition leans on the person of practical wisdom to say what the mean is, so it cannot serve as a rule applied from outside. In Book VI he adds that wisdom is not a craft: "Plainly, then, practical wisdom is a virtue and not an art" ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics VI.5](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.6.vi.html)). Readers sometimes treat the circle in the definition as a flaw. It can also be read as the point: judgment of this kind has no procedure behind it that could stand in for it. How far that is true, and how much of practical judgment can be supported by checklists and prompts, is open in the sources held here.

## Practice: variation, selection, retention

::Variation:: What tends to need varying is the position from which a problem is seen. The look-alikes of wisdom share a shape: cleverness aimed at a bad mark, confident opinion with no sense of its limits, and reasoning that gets worse exactly when the stakes are personal. The adaptive moves are to change viewpoint, slow down, ask, and look back at what happened.

::Selection:: The kernels I have selected as sources of that variation:

1. [Wise-reasoning prompts](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K260%20%E2%80%94%20Wise-reasoning%20prompts): four questions for a troubling situation: how it could unfold, what the perspectives are, how your own interests might bias the view, what a wise friend would advise.

2. [Self-distanced reflection](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K261%20%E2%80%94%20Self-distanced%20reflection): write about a difficult situation in the third person.

3. [Perspective-taking](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K131%20%E2%80%94%20Perspective-taking): step into another position and see what the others conceal.

4. [Perspective-getting](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K218%20%E2%80%94%20Perspective-getting): when you assume you know what someone thinks, ask.

5. [Deliberation timeout](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K264%20%E2%80%94%20Deliberation%20timeout): a mandatory wait of one to three days on decisions above a set threshold.

6. [Impression-judgement-action log](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K266%20%E2%80%94%20Impression-judgement-action%20log): a one-week three-column log separating what appeared, what you judged, and what you did.

7. [Post-action phronesis review](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K263%20%E2%80%94%20Post-action%20phronesis%20review): after a significant action, ask what you intended, what happened, where the gap came from, and what you would do differently.

8. [The mean calibration](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K253%20%E2%80%94%20The%20mean%20calibration): for one virtue, track the excess and the deficiency and rate where each situation landed.

9. [The unity-of-virtue drill](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K256%20%E2%80%94%20The%20unity-of-virtue%20drill): take a decision from this week and ask whether expressing one virtue required suppressing another.

10. [Role-conflict deliberation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K283%20%E2%80%94%20Role-conflict%20deliberation): when two roles conflict, write down what each demands and ask what a person of practical wisdom would do.

11. [Professional phronesis](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K360%20%E2%80%94%20Professional%20phronesis): for a recurring decision, ask when following the rule would produce a worse outcome than using judgement.

12. [The deliberative practice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K363%20%E2%80%94%20The%20deliberative%20practice): in a group decision, hear every voice before the verdict.

13. [Virtue journaling](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K255%20%E2%80%94%20Virtue%20journaling): one sentence each evening on the virtue you practised and how.

14. [Deliberate practice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K179%20%E2%80%94%20Deliberate%20practice): structured practice with a clear goal and immediate feedback.

15. [Life review](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K232%20%E2%80%94%20Life%20review): review the life in chapters, noting turning points and what each taught.

The evidence limits are uneven across the list. The life review (K232) borrows a method tested mostly with older adults and guided by trained facilitators, and its chapter layout is an adaptation. The distancing practices (K261, K260) lean on the experiments quoted above; the prompts in K260 are, by the card's own note, editorial questions informed by the research and not the instrument itself; the professional and role-conflict exercises are proposals and not tested protocols. Each card carries its own evidence note, and those notes are worth reading before relying on any of them.

::Retention:: Retention is what people keep because it works for them. A reader who keeps the evening look back, or the habit of asking before assuming, or the three-day wait, has what the theme offered; what stays is a matter of fit between the practice and the life it enters.

### Knowing the limits of what you know

[ST152 — Socrates and the Oracle](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST152%20%E2%80%94%20Socrates%20and%20the%20Oracle): the oracle at Delphi says no one is wiser than Socrates; he spends years questioning politicians, poets and craftsmen, and finds each believes they know what they do not.

[ST378 — Wang Ni Says How Would I Know That Three Times](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST378%20%E2%80%94%20Wang%20Ni%20Says%20How%20Would%20I%20Know%20That%20Three%20Times): asked whether he knows what everyone agrees on, Wang Ni answers "How would I know that?"; asked whether he then knows nothing at all, he gives the same reply.

[ST261 — Nan-in Overflows the Cup](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST261%20%E2%80%94%20Nan-in%20Overflows%20the%20Cup): a professor visits Nan-in to learn about Zen, and the master keeps pouring tea until the cup overflows: like this cup, you are full of your own opinions.

[ST370 — The Blind Men and the Elephant](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST370%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Blind%20Men%20and%20the%20Elephant): people blind from birth are shown different parts of an elephant, each group describes the whole animal from its part, and the conflicting descriptions end in a fistfight.

### Judging the particular case

[ST158 — Solomon's Judgement](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST158%20%E2%80%94%20Solomon%27s%20Judgement): two women claim one infant; Solomon orders it cut in two, one woman says give it to her and do not kill it, and he awards the child to her.

[ST157 — The Doctor and the Dose](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST157%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Doctor%20and%20the%20Dose): the doctor does not prescribe one regimen to everyone: what is healthful for the athlete may harm the convalescent, so the mean is what is right for this person, in this condition, at this time.

[ST156 — The Navigator Reads the Sea](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST156%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Navigator%20Reads%20the%20Sea): the practically wise person is like a navigator who cannot control the wind or the sea but reads the conditions, adjusts the sails and reaches port anyway.

[ST153 — Aristotle's Craftsman](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST153%20%E2%80%94%20Aristotle%27s%20Craftsman): the doctor does not apply a rule to a body; he brings together knowledge of the patient, the disease, the season and the means, and adjusts.

[ST024 — Wheelwright Pian Chides the Duke](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST024%20%E2%80%94%20Wheelwright%20Pian%20Chides%20the%20Duke): Wheelwright Pian tells Duke Huan that he is reading the dregs of dead men: the pace of the chisel, neither too slow nor too fast, cannot be put into words or taught to a son.

### Wisdom and a whole life

[ST143 — Solon and Croesus](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST143%20%E2%80%94%20Solon%20and%20Croesus): Croesus shows Solon his treasury and asks who is the happiest of men; Solon names an Athenian who died fighting for his city and concludes that no one should be called happy until he is dead.

[ST141 — Socrates Refuses to Escape the Question](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST141%20%E2%80%94%20Socrates%20Refuses%20to%20Escape%20the%20Question): offered an escape from prison, Socrates refuses it: one should never do injustice, not even in return for injustice.

[ST147 — Hercules at the Crossroads](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST147%20%E2%80%94%20Hercules%20at%20the%20Crossroads): the young Hercules is visited by two women, one offering the easy road of pleasure now, the other the steep road of toil and lasting deeds, and he chooses the second.

[ST172 — Thales Falls in the Well](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST172%20%E2%80%94%20Thales%20Falls%20in%20the%20Well): Thales falls into a well while watching the stars and a servant girl laughs at him for being so eager to know the heavens that he cannot see what is at his feet.
