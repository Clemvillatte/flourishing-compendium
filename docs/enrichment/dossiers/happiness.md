# Happiness

> Is happiness a feeling to be had, a condition to be reached, or a by-product of how a life is lived, and what does the research show can be done about it?

Everyone wants it, no one can define it, and aiming at it directly seems to dissolve it: happiness is the one target that behaves worse the harder it is chased. The feeling lifts, adapts to its own causes, and slips away precisely when valued most. The steady question underneath the research is the difference between the passing lift of a good mood and a steadier gladness, and which practices serve the second without chasing the first.

Happiness here means the felt side of a life: pleasure, joy, contentment, the research on adaptation and on subjective well-being. The question of a life that goes well in its whole shape, eudaimonia and the psychological well-being models, belongs to the Genuine Flourishing dossier, and the two meet exactly where a good mood and a good life get confused.

### Nearby terms

- Joy, pleasure and contentment: held as separate entries below, because the sources treat them as different things.
- Subjective well-being: the research name for how people rate their own lives; the cards on adaptation and on sustainability use it.
- Eudaimonia: Aristotle's term for the good life, which the English word happiness often translates; held by [Eudaimonia](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Eudaimonia) and in the Genuine Flourishing dossier.

## Where happiness sits in a life

### What happiness is

For Aristotle, [happiness as activity](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Happiness%20as%20activity) is something we do and not something we have. His claim in Nicomachean Ethics I, that every other good is chosen at least partly for the sake of happiness while happiness is chosen only for itself, is that [everyone desires eudaimonia](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Everyone%20desires%20eudaimonia).

His account of happiness as final and self-sufficient is the [sufficient good](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20sufficient%20good), and Aristotle's term for the highest human good, a life of flourishing rather than a passing state of mind, is [eudaimonia](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Eudaimonia).

In Epicurus's ethics, the pleasure of a settled state rather than of a process is [katastematic pleasure](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Katastematic%20pleasure). Freedom from mental disturbance, tranquillity, untroubledness, or serenity of mind, is [ataraxia](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Ataraxia).

Spinoza's laetitia, defined by a change and not a state, a crossing from a lesser to a greater perfection, is [joy](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Joy). In my Aristotelian frame, the gladness that belongs to a life going well is [spiritual joy](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Spiritual%20joy), and Aristotle's account of the pleasure a good person takes in activity consistent with virtue is [the joy of virtue](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20joy%20of%20virtue).

Kant's term for the pleasure taken in beauty, a satisfaction that neither depends on a desire for the object nor produces one, is [disinterested aesthetic pleasure](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Disinterested%20aesthetic%20pleasure). Mehrabian and Russell's model that locates any emotional state in a space of pleasure, arousal and dominance is the [Pleasure-Arousal-Dominance Model](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Pleasure-Arousal-Dominance%20Model). Oishi and Westgate's proposed aspect of a good life, centred on varied, interesting experiences and distinguished by them from both happiness and meaning, is [psychological richness](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Psychological%20richness).

The first Noble Truth, that experience when grasped is unsatisfactory, is [dukkha](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Dukkha). Schopenhauer's charge that wanting promises a satisfaction it cannot deliver, a failure he takes to be structural, is [the vanity of desire](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20vanity%20of%20desire).

### What sufficiency has to do with contentment

The label I use for the Daodejing's teaching on contentment and sufficiency is [contentment with sufficiency](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Contentment%20with%20sufficiency). The Daoist practice of knowing what is enough, sizing desire to what life already supplies, is [zhi zu (knowing sufficiency)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Zhi%20zu%20%28knowing%20sufficiency%29), and the capacity to find sufficiency and freshness in plain, repeated things, rooted in Daodejing 80, is [delight in the ordinary](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Delight%20in%20the%20ordinary).

Epictetus's answer to what to do with a life one did not choose is [contentment in station](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Contentment%20in%20station). Two decision-making styles that Schwartz and colleagues set against each other in their 2002 studies are [maximizing versus satisficing](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Maximizing%20versus%20satisficing), and the idea that having more options can leave people worse off, not better, is the [paradox of choice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Paradox%20of%20choice).

### Why happiness slips

The hedonic treadmill, in which people adapt to both good and bad changes and return toward a relatively stable baseline of happiness, is [hedonic adaptation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Hedonic%20adaptation). Solomon and Corbit's theory that hedonic states are automatically opposed by mechanisms that reduce their intensity is [Opponent-Process Theory](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Opponent-Process%20Theory).

The longitudinal literature on whether people return to baseline happiness after life events is [adaptation research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Adaptation%20research). Research on how people adjust to major life changes such as marriage, divorce, unemployment, widowhood and disability is [adaptation to life transitions research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Adaptation%20to%20life%20transitions%20research).

The prediction of one's own future emotional reactions is [affective forecasting](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Affective%20forecasting). The finding that negative affect fades faster than positive affect in memory is [fading affect bias](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Fading%20affect%20bias), and the question of whether boosts in happiness from intentional effort can outlast the initial lift is [sustainability of well-being gains](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Sustainability%20of%20well-being%20gains).

### Why aiming at happiness backfires

Sidgwick's finding that pleasure, pursued as a direct target, tends to defeat itself, is the [paradox of hedonism](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20paradox%20of%20hedonism). The claim, in Frankl and before him in Mill, that happiness is not something one can successfully aim at, is [happiness as by-product](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Happiness%20as%20by-product).

The finding that making happiness one's goal can reduce it is the [valuing happiness paradox](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Valuing%20happiness%20paradox). Mauss and colleagues' 2011 paper on whether wanting happiness too much can undermine it is [the hedonic trap of seeking peak experiences](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20hedonic%20trap%20of%20seeking%20peak%20experiences), and the finding that wanting something too much, with happiness as the test case, can cost the very thing wanted is the [paradox of valuing a goal excessively](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20paradox%20of%20valuing%20a%20goal%20excessively).

### What research says helps

The turn in the discipline toward studying the strengths and conditions that let a life go well, alongside its work on repairing damage, is [positive psychology](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Positive%20psychology). Fredrickson's broaden-and-build programme is [positive emotions research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Positive%20emotions%20research), and simple, intentional practices meant to raise a person's happiness, such as expressing gratitude or practicing kindness, are [positive activity interventions](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Positive%20activity%20interventions).

The felt recognition of a benefit received is [gratitude](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Gratitude). The capacity to attend to, appreciate, and amplify positive experience is [savoring](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Savoring), and the mental state of full immersion in an activity, with energized focus, full involvement, and enjoyment in the process, is [flow](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Flow).

Stuart Brown's study of what play does to the adults who keep doing it is [play research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Play%20research), and his research programme on play as a biological state central to development, mood and adaptability is [the play ethos](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=The%20play%20ethos). The paradox Johan Huizinga builds Homo Ludens around, that play is marked off from ordinary life as not serious yet absorbs the player intensely, is [seriousness about the play](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Seriousness%20about%20the%20play).

Spending money to benefit others, with the finding that how people spend their money may be at least as important as how much they earn, is [prosocial spending](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Prosocial%20spending). A family of voluntary, other-directed actions, helping, sharing, donating, co-operating and volunteering, is [prosocial behaviour](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Prosocial%20behaviour).

The subjective sense of having enough time, together with autonomy over its use, is [time affluence](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Time%20affluence). The third of the four brahmaviharas, the capacity to feel glad at another's good fortune as if it were one's own, is [mudita (sympathetic joy)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Mudita%20%28sympathetic%20joy%29).

The Daoist art of taking the world lightly, laughing at grandiosity from below, at oneself, and at the brevity that makes laughter precious, is [Zhuangzi's laughter](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Zhuangzi%27s%20laughter). The Nahuatl difrasismo for art, poetry and metaphor, and by extension the creative activity through which the world was understood, is [flower and song](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Flower%20and%20song).

The finding that how people rate their own lives depends heavily on the social conditions around them, and not only on their own traits or income, is [social determinants of well-being](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Social%20determinants%20of%20well-being). Ryff's 1989 question of what psychological well-being actually is is [holistic well-being](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Holistic%20well-being), and Joshanloo's review of Western and Eastern accounts of happiness, identifying six fundamental differences in conceptions of the good life, is [cross-cultural variations in conceptions of happiness](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Cross-cultural%20variations%20in%20conceptions%20of%20happiness).

### Who the thinkers are

The Greek philosopher whose Nicomachean Ethics gives the lasting account of happiness as an activity of a whole life is [Aristotle](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=T002%20%E2%80%94%20Aristotle). The Dutch philosopher whose Ethics defines pleasure and joy by a change in a person's power is [Spinoza](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=T007%20%E2%80%94%20Spinoza%2C%20Baruch).

The psychologist who began with learned helplessness and, as president of the American Psychological Association in 1998, called for positive psychology is [Seligman](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=T026%20%E2%80%94%20Seligman%2C%20Martin). The Hungarian-American psychologist, co-founder of positive psychology, who named and mapped flow is [Csikszentmihalyi](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=T001%20%E2%80%94%20Csikszentmihalyi%2C%20Mihaly).

### What the stories carry

The story in which Aristotle sorts the competing theories of the good life, where the many say pleasure and he does not dismiss them lightly, is [the Philosopher and the Hedonist](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST169%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Philosopher%20and%20the%20Hedonist). The story of a shy waitress in Montmartre who keeps a catalogue of small pleasures is [Amélie's Small Pleasures](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST305%20%E2%80%94%20Am%C3%A9lie%27s%20Small%20Pleasures).

The story of a Tokyo bureaucrat with less than a year to live who tries the pleasures, and they do not work, is [Watanabe Finds the Playground](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST215%20%E2%80%94%20Watanabe%20Finds%20the%20Playground). The study in which Csikszentmihalyi extends the climbers' finding to ordinary life, with participants paged at random moments, is [Flow in the Everyday](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST138%20%E2%80%94%20Flow%20in%20the%20Everyday).

Fred Bryant's research that distinguishes savoring from positive emotion itself is [Savoring](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST304%20%E2%80%94%20Savoring).

## Where the traditions genuinely disagree

### Is happiness an activity, an untroubled state, or a lift?

The shared question: what kind of thing is happiness, so that one could tell whether one has it? Aristotle answers that it is activity, and that it takes a whole life to show:

> "human good turns out to be activity of soul in accordance with virtue, and if there are more than one virtue, in accordance with the best and most complete. But we must add 'in a complete life.' For one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day; and so too one day, or a short time, does not make a man blessed and happy." ([Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics I.7, Ross translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html))

Epicurus makes it a condition of the body and mind, not an activity, and takes pains to say what he does not mean:

> "By pleasure we mean the absence of pain in the body and of trouble in the soul." ([Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, Hicks translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Epicurus/menoec.html))

Spinoza defines the glad affect by a change, so that joy is a crossing and not a place:

> "Pleasure is the transition of a man from a less to a greater perfection." ([Spinoza, Ethics III, Definitions of the Emotions II, Elwes translation](https://www.sacred-texts.com/phi/spinoza/ethics/eth03.htm))

The three do not use one word for one thing. Aristotle's word is eudaimonia, Epicurus's is pleasure, Spinoza's is laetitia, and the English happiness stretches over all three. Which of them a reader means by happiness decides what counts as getting more of it.

### Can you reach happiness by aiming at it?

The shared question: if happiness is the goal, is it also the target? Mill said no, from his own experience:

> "Those only are happy (I thought) who have their minds fixed on some object other than their own happiness; on the happiness of others, on the improvement of mankind, even on some art or pursuit, followed not as a means, but as itself an ideal end. Aiming thus at something else, they find happiness by the way." ([Mill, Autobiography, ch. 5](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10378/10378-h/10378-h.htm))

Sidgwick named the same effect as a paradox of hedonism:

> "Here comes into view what we may call the fundamental paradox of Hedonism, that the impulse towards pleasure, if too predominant, defeats its own aim." ([Sidgwick, The Methods of Ethics, Book I, ch. IV, §2](https://www.laits.utexas.edu/poltheory/sidgwick/me/me.b01.c04.s02.html))

Mauss and colleagues tested a version of it. Across two studies they found that valuing happiness was linked to disappointment, and they concluded:

> "Paradoxically, therefore, valuing happiness may lead people to be less happy just when happiness is within reach." ([Mauss, Tamir, Anderson and Savino, Emotion 2011, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21517168/), [eutils abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=21517168&rettype=abstract&retmode=text))

The research on intentional activity points the other way, or at least sideways. Lyubomirsky, Sheldon and Schkade propose that happiness has three influences, and that the third can be changed on purpose:

> "changing one's intentional activities may provide a happiness-boosting potential that is at least as large as, and probably much larger than, changing one's circumstances." ([Lyubomirsky, Sheldon and Schkade, Review of General Psychology 2005](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Pursuing_Happiness-_The_Architecture_of_Sustainable_Change.pdf))

The two claims are less opposed than they look. Mauss and colleagues studied the valuing of happiness as a goal and its link to disappointment in two studies of female participants; Lyubomirsky and colleagues describe practices such as kindness and gratitude, which direct attention outward. A reader can hold both: attention fixed on one's own mood tends to spoil it, and attention to what one does may lift it. The sources here do not test the two against each other.

### Does happiness return to a baseline whatever happens?

The shared question: if people adapt to almost everything, is effort to raise happiness wasted? The treadmill model says so, in the words of the review that set out to revise it:

> "According to the hedonic treadmill model, good and bad events temporarily affect happiness, but people quickly adapt back to hedonic neutrality." ([Diener, Lucas and Scollon, American Psychologist 2006, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16719675/), [eutils abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=16719675&rettype=abstract&retmode=text))

The same abstract lists revisions, among them that "well-being set points can change under some conditions" and that individuals differ in how they adapt. Lucas's review of panel studies puts the finding from life events in plain terms:

> "Although inborn factors certainly matter and some adaptation does occur, events such as divorce, death of a spouse, unemployment, and disability are associated with lasting changes in SWB." ([Lucas, Current Directions in Psychological Science 2007, abstract](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/current-directions/j.1467-8721.2007.00479.x/))

So the treadmill describes part of what happens. Adaptation is real and often useful; it is not complete or uniform, and it varies by event and by person. The abstracts do not say how much a deliberate practice can move a person's own level, which is the question the previous section left open.

### Do people know what will make them happy?

The shared question: if happiness is the aim, can we judge in advance what serves it? Wilson and Gilbert's review says that forecasts tend to go wrong in a particular direction:

> "They often display an impact bias, overestimating the intensity and duration of their emotional reactions to such events." ([Wilson and Gilbert, Current Directions in Psychological Science 2005, abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.0963-7214.2005.00355.x), [author manuscript PDF, University of Florida](https://bear.warrington.ufl.edu/brenner/mar7588/Papers/wilson-gilbert-cdips2005.pdf))

Against a purely inward guess, Dunn, Aknin and Norton proposed that a plain, countable choice, whom the money is spent on, mattered:

> "how people spend their money may be at least as important as how much money they earn." ([Dunn, Aknin and Norton, Science 2008, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18356530/), [eutils abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=18356530&rettype=abstract&retmode=text))

The finding has a replication history, and the registered replication report states it with care:

> "Taken together, these studies support the hypothesis that spending money on others does promote happiness, but demonstrate that the magnitude of the effect depends on several methodological features." ([Registered replication report, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32250135/), [eutils abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=32250135&rettype=abstract&retmode=text))

A fair reading is that the direction held up and the size is smaller and less certain than the first paper suggested. The abstracts do not tell a reader how big the effect is in a given life.

### Is happiness the same thing across cultures?

The shared question: does the word name one experience everywhere? Joshanloo's review compares accounts from Western philosophy and psychology with those from Hinduism, Buddhism, Taoism, Confucianism and Sufism:

> "Six fundamental differences in western and eastern conceptualizations of the good life are identified and discussed in the context of broader psychological theory." ([Joshanloo, Journal of Happiness Studies 2014, abstract](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10902-013-9431-1))

The abstract does not list the six differences, and this dossier does not guess at them; the card summarises the review. On the other side, one cross-country study of giving reports:

> "This research provides the first support for a possible psychological universal: Human beings around the world derive emotional benefits from using their financial resources to help others (prosocial spending)." ([Aknin and colleagues, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 2013, abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23421360/), [eutils abstract](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=23421360&rettype=abstract&retmode=text))

The two findings answer different questions. One is about what people mean by the good life; the other is about what lifts mood across the countries studied. Both can be true, and the sources held here do not weigh one against the other.

## Practice: variation, selection, retention

::Variation:: What tends to need varying is where attention rests. The look-alikes of lasting happiness share a shape: a lift that fades, a goal that keeps moving, a mood watched so closely it goes flat. The adaptive moves are to turn attention outward, to mark what is enough, and to notice good things while they are happening.

::Selection:: The kernels I have selected as sources of that variation:

1. [Savoring](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K110%20%E2%80%94%20Savoring): when a pleasant moment occurs, deliberately extend it and notice the sensory details.

2. [Gratitude practice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K164%20%E2%80%94%20Gratitude%20practice): write down three specific things you are grateful for, or thank a person directly.

3. [Gratitude expression](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K212%20%E2%80%94%20Gratitude%20expression): thank a person directly, specific about what they did and what it meant.

4. [Prosocial spending](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K213%20%E2%80%94%20Prosocial%20spending): spend a small amount of money on someone else; the card states the replication history.

5. [Acts of kindness](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K211%20%E2%80%94%20Acts%20of%20kindness): one visible act of help daily; the card says the study counted acts and did not weigh them.

6. [Sympathetic joy](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K166%20%E2%80%94%20Sympathetic%20joy): when someone you might compare yourself to does well, rejoice deliberately.

7. [Flow block](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K190%20%E2%80%94%20Flow%20block): protect 45 to 90 minutes for single-tasked work with a clear goal and a feedback signal.

8. [Play practice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K331%20%E2%80%94%20Play%20practice): once a week, do something with no purpose beyond the doing.

9. [Time-affluence audit](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K227%20%E2%80%94%20Time-affluence%20audit): review whether you are trading time for money in ways that diminish well-being.

10. [Gratitude for sufficiency](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K280%20%E2%80%94%20Gratitude%20for%20sufficiency): each evening, name one thing you already have that is enough.

11. [Sufficiency testing](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K279%20%E2%80%94%20Sufficiency%20testing): for one domain, write down in advance what would be enough, and stop when it is reached.

12. [Enough lines](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K155%20%E2%80%94%20Enough%20lines): for each life domain, define sufficiency in advance, while the judgment is still cold.

13. [Signature strengths in new ways](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K251%20%E2%80%94%20Signature%20strengths%20in%20new%20ways): use one of your top strengths in a new way each day.

Evidence is uneven across the list. The cards themselves flag the limits: the prosocial-spending finding has a replication history, the acts-of-kindness study counted rather than weighed, and the card on sufficiency separates the practice from gratitude journaling, which it says has mixed evidence. Read each card's own evidence note before relying on a practice.

::Retention:: Retention is what people keep because it works for them. A reader who keeps the evening line about what is enough, or the weekly hour of play, or the habit of thanking someone by name, has what the theme offered; what stays is a matter of fit between the practice and the life it enters.

## Story: the stories that carry the theme

The stories that best carry the theme's aspects, each retold in a line; the full telling lives on the card.

### What a happy life looks like

[ST143 — Solon and Croesus](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST143%20%E2%80%94%20Solon%20and%20Croesus): Croesus asks who is the happiest of men, and Solon names Tellus of Athens, who lived in a prosperous city, saw his grandchildren and died fighting for it.

[ST005 — Hirayama's Perfect Days](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST005%20%E2%80%94%20Hirayama%27s%20Perfect%20Days): Hirayama cleans public toilets in Tokyo, waters his plants, photographs the light through the trees and reads before sleep; asked why he is so content, he does not explain.

[ST277 — Sisyphus Is Happy](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST277%20%E2%80%94%20Sisyphus%20Is%20Happy): Camus's Sisyphus pushes the boulder up the hill forever, and the conclusion is that the struggle toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart.

### Pleasure and its traps

[ST169 — The Philosopher and the Hedonist](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST169%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Philosopher%20and%20the%20Hedonist): in Nicomachean Ethics X, Aristotle sorts the theories of the good life: the many say pleasure, and he resolves that pleasure accompanies the end and does not constitute it.

[ST170 — The Lotus-Eaters](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST170%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Lotus-Eaters): Odysseus's scouts eat the lotus and want only to stay eating, and he drags them weeping to the ships and ties them under the benches.

### Small pleasures and savoring

[ST305 — Amélie's Small Pleasures](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST305%20%E2%80%94%20Am%C3%A9lie%27s%20Small%20Pleasures): Amélie keeps a catalogue of small pleasures and secretly arranges small kindnesses for others, and finds she cannot give what she will not receive.

[ST304 — Savoring](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST304%20%E2%80%94%20Savoring): Fred Bryant's research separates savoring, the capacity to attend to and prolong enjoyment, from positive emotion itself.

[ST014 — Marie Kondo and the Spark of Joy](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST014%20%E2%80%94%20Marie%20Kondo%20and%20the%20Spark%20of%20Joy): Marie Kondo has people hold each object and ask whether it sparks joy; if not, thank it and let it go.

### The science of positive emotion

[ST303 — Broaden and Build](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST303%20%E2%80%94%20Broaden%20and%20Build): Fredrickson's theory holds that positive emotions broaden the repertoire of thought and action and build resources over time, while negative emotions narrow attention to the threat.

[ST298 — The Positivity Ratio Falls](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST298%20%E2%80%94%20The%20Positivity%20Ratio%20Falls): a claimed tipping-point ratio of positive to negative emotion falls in 2013 when Brown, Sokal and Friedman show the mathematics behind it was unfounded, and the empirical application was retracted.

[ST138 — Flow in the Everyday](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=ST138%20%E2%80%94%20Flow%20in%20the%20Everyday): Csikszentmihalyi has thousands of people paged at random moments and finds the most satisfying hours were the most absorbed, not the most pleasant.
