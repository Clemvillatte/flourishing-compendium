# Gratitude

> What is gratitude actually about, and why does it resist being a self-help technique?

Gratitude looks like the simplest of the virtues and keeps turning out to be one of the strangest: a benefit received, a debt acknowledged, and a whole anthropology of giving and owing underneath. Both the classical and the scientific traditions have a worked-out account of it, and the two do not match: in the classical treatment gratitude is a virtue with duties, in the research a positive emotion with effects. The theme rewards keeping the two answers visible side by side rather than letting either one absorb the other.

### Nearby terms

- Thankfulness and appreciation: near synonyms, held inside the groups below.
- Indebtedness: the benefit's shadow, the felt obligation to repay; the couples studies found gratitude, unlike indebtedness, predicted connection.
- Reciprocity: the exchange structure gratitude sits in and can exceed, in the classical group below.
- Contentment and sufficiency: gratitude's present-tense cousins, in their own group below.
- Benefit finding: finding good in adversity rather than benefit received from a giver; its own card and evidence.

## Where gratitude sits

### What is a benefit, and what does the receiver owe?

The felt recognition of a benefit received is the anchor, and both traditions begin there. [Gratitude](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Gratitude) carries the classical anchors, Seneca's On Benefits and Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics VIII.14, and the psychological study begins from the same observation that the emotion is about a source. [T013 — Seneca](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=T013%20%E2%80%94%20Seneca) is the Stoic whose Moral Letters and essays carry the account.

Seneca's location of a benefit is in the goodwill behind it rather than the money or object that changes hands. [S037 — Practise beneficence](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=S037%20%E2%80%94%20Practise%20beneficence) names delay, reproach and humiliation as spoilers of the gift. [S084 — Practise gratitude](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=S084%20%E2%80%94%20Practise%20gratitude) carries Marcus Aurelius's first book as a list of what he learned from particular people, each entry naming a giver and a gift, which is what makes it more than a mood.

Aristotle's test of the manner of the gift is [generosity](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Generosity): giving for the sake of the noble, to the right people, amounts and times, and the giver's substance rather than the multitude of gifts. The exchange structure the emotion sits in is [reciprocity](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Reciprocity), with its two demands, help those who have helped you and do not injure them.

[Reciprocity norm theory Gouldner](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Reciprocity%20norm%20theory%20Gouldner) is the 1960 statement of the norm in its universal form, and [reciprocal altruism research](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Reciprocal%20altruism%20research) is Trivers's evolutionary account that lists gratitude among the regulators of the exchange.

Spinoza's definition makes gratitude a desire springing from love, wanting to benefit someone who has benefited us out of love, carried on [SP022 — Practice gratitude as repayment of love](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=SP022%20%E2%80%94%20Practice%20gratitude%20as%20repayment%20of%20love). Hume's [benevolence](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Benevolence) is the sentiment that grounds the social virtues, the giving side of the same bond.

The asymmetry of benefaction is Aristotle's observation that benefactors love those they have benefited more than the reverse. [A070 — Understand the asymmetry of benefaction](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=A070%20%E2%80%94%20Understand%20the%20asymmetry%20of%20benefaction) carries his own account of why: the beneficiary is the benefactor's handiwork and carries the maker's activity in it, so the imbalance is normal and not a ledger to correct.

The contemplative traditions extend the remembrance outward. [K053 — Practice recognizing the kindness you have received](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K053%20%E2%80%94%20Practice%20recognizing%20the%20kindness%20you%20have%20received) carries the lamrim's contemplation: recognize all beings as your mothers, begin with your mother's kindness in this lifetime, and extend the remembrance outward gradually. [K034 — Be grateful to everyone](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K034%20%E2%80%94%20Be%20grateful%20to%20everyone) is the thirteenth lojong slogan, whose gratitude includes the harmful, with Trungpa's commentary that the difficult are presenting the chance to practise. [Thanksgiving as sacrifice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Thanksgiving%20as%20sacrifice) is the Psalm's line, the offering-side of thankfulness.

### What the counting practice works on

The mechanism as the lab frames it is attention itself. [AF063 — Practise gratitude: it recalibrates attention toward what is good](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=AF063%20%E2%80%94%20Practise%20gratitude%20%E2%80%94%20it%20recalibrates%20attention%20toward%20what%20is%20good) holds the practice as directing attention to what is good rather than what is lacking, with the field's pooled verdict on its own scale: small overall increases in well-being, Hedges' g = 0.19 across 145 papers and 28 countries. [PP013 — Practise gratitude with specificity](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=PP013%20%E2%80%94%20Practise%20gratitude%20with%20specificity) carries the Emmons and McCullough experiments as the evidence base, with their limits stated plainly on the card.

Dose matters. The 2005 interventions carried on [PP063 — Dose appropriately](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=PP063%20%E2%80%94%20Dose%20appropriately) found the once-weekly gratitude group improved relative to controls and the three-times-weekly group did not, with the card's own caution that significance in one group and not the other does not itself establish a difference between them.

The Kataññu Suttas frame gratitude as a mark of integrity, with parents as the clearest case and even extreme service not discharging the debt, carried on [B112 — Practice gratitude: recognize your conditions](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B112%20%E2%80%94%20Practice%20gratitude%20%E2%80%94%20recognize%20your%20conditions). [Hedonic adaptation](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Hedonic%20adaptation) is the taking-for-granted treadmill the counting practice counters, in Brickman and Campbell's original image, and [savoring](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Savoring) is its present-tense cousin: attention, not acquisition, stopping the good moment from passing unnoticed.

The adversity-side relation is [benefit finding](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Benefit%20finding), finding good in a stressor, with the meta-analytic finding that it tracks both more well-being and more intrusive thoughts about the stressor. Epicurus's memory as a source of present pleasure, in his own last letter, is carried on [X011 — Bank gratitude for past pleasures, and draw on it in need](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=X011%20%E2%80%94%20Bank%20gratitude%20for%20past%20pleasures%2C%20and%20draw%20on%20it%20in%20need).

Tanabe's philosophy is returned to the world in an act of gratitude, with the daily routine of naming one thing that was given and not earned, carried on [K068 — Practice gratitude as the fundamental stance](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K068%20%E2%80%94%20Practice%20gratitude%20as%20the%20fundamental%20stance). [Fundamental stance](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Fundamental%20stance) is Jaspers's category of settled postures from which a whole life thinks and sees.

### What the bond receives

The receiving side of the bond has its own research. [Active constructive responding](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Active%20constructive%20responding) is meeting another's shared good news with enthusiasm, the capitalization research. Kumar and Epley's finding, carried on [BE055 — Learn to express gratitude behaviourally: thank others](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=BE055%20%E2%80%94%20Learn%20to%20express%20gratitude%20behaviourally%20%E2%80%94%20thank%20others), is that expressers underestimate how positive and overestimate how awkward recipients will feel.

[Mudita (sympathetic joy)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Mudita%20%28sympathetic%20joy%29) is the unadulterated goodness felt when something good happens for someone else, and [four immeasurables](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Four%20immeasurables) holds each as a stance toward a condition: mudita is metta meeting good fortune, joy in another's happiness even when one contributed nothing to it. Śāntideva's closing turn, longing for an enemy like a treasure discovered in the home, because forbearance depends on the hostile intention, is carried on [enemy as teacher](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Enemy%20as%20teacher).

### What sufficiency has to do with it

The Yoga-sutra's one line, that by contentment the acquisition of extreme happiness, with Vyasa's commentary on "extreme," is [santosha](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Santosha). The Daodejing's contentment that needs fear no shame, the knowing-when-to-stop that gratitude shares a border with, is [sufficiency](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Sufficiency).

Marcus Aurelius's placement of the Stoic's self-sufficiency in one's own mind is [S026 — Practise contentment with what you have](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=S026%20%E2%80%94%20Practise%20contentment%20with%20what%20you%20have), and the Daodejing chapter 46 naming of the failure, no disaster greater than not knowing contentment, is [D030 — Contentment is the greatest wealth](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=D030%20%E2%80%94%20Contentment%20is%20the%20greatest%20wealth).

The Quran's pairing, whoever is steadfast, grateful, as the two readings of one condition, is [X016 — Pair patience and gratitude as the two meters of every state](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=X016%20%E2%80%94%20Pair%20patience%20and%20gratitude%20as%20the%20two%20meters%20of%20every%20state). Heidegger's death as a limit that belongs to one's own existence, the facing of which discloses the possibilities one can actually choose, is [E054 — Practise finitude as gift](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=E054%20%E2%80%94%20Practise%20finitude%20as%20gift).

### What is owed to what one stands on

## Related questions and parallels

### Attention or bond: two accounts that do not exclude each other

The counting-blessings experiment and the relationship theory answer different questions about the same emotion. Read side by side, the experiment's own summary keeps both effects in view:

> "The gratitude-outlook groups exhibited heightened well-being across several, though not all, of the outcome measures across the 3 studies, relative to the comparison groups. The effect on positive affect appeared to be the most robust finding. Results suggest that a conscious focus on blessings may have emotional and interpersonal benefits." ([Emmons and McCullough, "Counting Blessings Versus Burdens," JPSP 2003](https://emmons.faculty.ucdavis.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/90/2015/08/2003_2-Emmons_McCullough_2003_JPSP.pdf))

Algoe's theory relocates the function without denying the felt effect, and the couples study reports both sides of the bond:

> "The find-remind-and-bind theory of gratitude posits that the positive emotion of gratitude serves the evolutionary function of strengthening a relationship with a responsive interaction partner (Algoe, Haidt, & Gable, 2008)." ([Algoe, "Find, Remind, and Bind," 2012](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1751-9004.2012.00439.x))

> "However, for both men and women, gratitude from interactions predicted increases in relationship connection and satisfaction the following day, for both recipient and benefactor." ([Algoe, Gable and Maisel, "It's the little things," 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1475-6811.2010.01273.x)). The same abstract adds that "gratitude had uniquely predictive power in relationship promotion, perhaps acting as a booster shot for the relationship," where indebtedness maintained only the external signals of engagement.

Which effect is primary in ordinary life, the feeler's attention or the bond's maintenance, is a question an essayist can take up rather than one the research settles; the practices here split along the same line, attention practices on one side, expression practices on the other.

### What measures the return for a benefit

The two accounts start from different places. Seneca locates the benefit itself in the giver's goodwill: "neither gold, nor silver, nor any of those things which are most highly esteemed, are benefits, but the benefit lies in the goodwill of him who gives them" ([Seneca, On Benefits I.5, Stewart trans., Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_Benefits/Book_I)). A great sum given "neither with discernment nor with good will" is for him no benefit at all (On Benefits I.15), and much of the debt is discharged on the spot: "a benefit is repaid by being acknowledged" (On Benefits I.1).

Aristotle asks a different question, namely what the return should be, and splits the answer by relationship: "It is disputable whether we ought to measure a service by its utility to the receiver and make the return with a view to that, or by the benevolence of the giver." In friendships of utility, "the advantage to the receiver is the measure"; in friendships of virtue, "the purpose of the doer" is "a sort of measure" ([Aristotle, NE VIII.13, Ross trans.](http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.8.viii.html)). Seneca's measure is the giver's will; Aristotle's is the kind of relationship the exchange sits inside. Where the relationship is a friendship of virtue the two measures converge; where it is a friendship of utility the receiver's advantage governs. Whether the two measures can give opposed verdicts on the same act is a question the texts do not settle, and one an essay can take up.

### What full repayment requires

Seneca holds that acknowledgment itself does most of the repaying: "a benefit is repaid by being acknowledged" ([Seneca, On Benefits I.1, Stewart trans., Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_Benefits/Book_I)). The grateful person's first act is to own the debt, and on Seneca's account the goodwill that constitutes the benefit is honored by seeing it.

Aquinas emphasizes a further grateful return. The giver was "deserving of praise, chiefly for having conferred the favor gratis without being bound to do so," so the return must also be gratis, and it is not gratis unless it exceeds the gift: "he does not seem to bestow something gratis, unless he exceeds the quantity of the favor received: because so long as he repays less or an equivalent, he would seem to do nothing gratis, but only to return what he has received. Therefore gratitude always inclines, as far as possible, to pay back something more" ([Aquinas, ST II-II Q106 a.6, English Dominican Province trans., Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Summa_Theologiae/Second_Part_of_the_Second_Part/Question_106)).

The two accounts pull different parts of the act to the center. Seneca puts the acknowledgment at the heart of repayment: the benefit is honored by being owned and seen as goodwill. Aquinas puts the excess there: an equal return gives back what was received and adds nothing gratis. A grateful person can practice both, and the texts differ in which part each treats as the repayment itself.

### What the return to parents is made of

Both texts deny that material service can equal what parents gave. Aristotle: toward gods and parents "no one could ever return to them the equivalent of what he gets, but the man who serves them to the utmost of his power is thought to be a good man," and "there is nothing by doing which a son will have done the equivalent of what he has received, so that he is always in debt" ([Aristotle, NE VIII.14, Ross trans.](http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.8.viii.html)).

The Kataññu Suttas: carrying both parents on the shoulders for a hundred years, or establishing them in "absolute sovereignty over this great earth," does not repay ("you would not in that way pay or repay your parents"), but one class of act does repay: "anyone who rouses his unbelieving mother & father, settles & establishes them in conviction; rouses his unvirtuous mother & father, settles & establishes them in virtue... To this extent one pays & repays one's mother & father" ([AN 2.31–32, Thanissaro trans., accesstoinsight](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an02/an02.031.than.html)).

Both texts measure filial observance against the parental care. Confucius grounds the three years' mourning in the care it answers: "It is not till a child is three years old that it is allowed to leave the arms of its parents. And the three years' mourning is universally observed throughout the empire" ([Confucius, Analects XVII.21, Legge trans., Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/3330/pg3330.txt)). The suttas name a different repayment: no quantity of service, up to a century of carrying them, pays the parental debt; the return they name is the establishing of parents in conviction, virtue, generosity and discernment.

Aristotle sits closest to the suttas in denying that any service reaches equivalence, though he commends service to the utmost of one's ability and does not take up the establishing of parents in virtue.

## Where the traditions genuinely disagree

### Can the child's return equal the parental gift

Aristotle denies that a child can return an equivalent for parental beneficence: toward gods and parents "no one could ever return to them the equivalent of what he gets" ([Aristotle, NE VIII.14, Ross trans.](http://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.8.viii.html)). Seneca: "The question arises, whether children can ever bestow upon their parents greater benefits than those which they have received from them," and his answer runs past equivalence to surplus: "A father gives life to his son; there is something better than life; therefore a father may be outdone" ([Seneca, On Benefits III.29, III.35, Stewart trans., Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_Benefits/Book_III)).

His concrete case is teaching: "supposing that any one were to learn philosophy and teach it to his father, could it be any longer disputed that the son had given him something greater than he had received from him, having returned to his father a happy life, whereas he had received from him merely life?" (On Benefits III.33).

Aquinas later distinguishes two measures: by the effect of beneficence the son cannot make an equal repayment, "but if we consider the will of the giver and of the repayer, then it is possible for the son to pay back something greater to his father" ([Aquinas, ST II-II Q106 a.6 ad 1, English Dominican Province trans., Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Summa_Theologiae/Second_Part_of_the_Second_Part/Question_106)).

The disagreement concerns what should count when benefits are compared: the effect received, or the will with which it is given and returned.

### Can the obligation of gratitude be discharged

Seneca allows grateful intention and acknowledgment to count as repayment of a benefit: "we do not need our wealth but only our intention to set us free from the obligation of it; for a benefit is repaid by being acknowledged" ([Seneca, On Benefits I.1, Stewart trans., Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_Benefits/Book_I)). The obligation is to the goodwill, and the goodwill is honored by being owned.

Kant denies the discharge in principle: "gratitude must also be considered, in particular, a sacred duty... For a moral object is sacred if the obligation with regard to it cannot be discharged completely by any act in keeping with it (so that one who is under obligation always remains under obligation)... But one cannot, by any repayment of a kindness received, rid oneself of the obligation for it, since the recipient can never win away from the benefactor his priority of merit, namely having been the first in benevolence" ([Kant, Metaphysics of Morals, Doctrine of Virtue §32, Gregor trans.](http://debracollege.dspaces.org/bitstream/123456789/508/1/The%20Metaphysics%20of%20Morals%20of%20kant.pdf)).

Seneca allows the acknowledgment to count as repayment; Kant maintains that repayment never completely cancels the obligation of gratitude, because the benefactor retains the distinction of having acted first. The disagreement turns on what the debt is made of: for Seneca it is the goodwill, which the acknowledgment honors; for Kant it is the benefactor's priority in benevolence, which no later act can take away.

### Does unequal rank exclude a claim to gratitude

Seneca answers no: "he who affirms that a slave does not sometimes confer a benefit upon his master is ignorant of the rights of man; for the question is, not what the station in life of the giver may be, but what his intentions are. The path of virtue is closed to no one... whether they be free-born men, slaves or freed-men, kings or exiles" and so "slaves can bestow benefits upon their masters" ([Seneca, On Benefits III.18, Stewart trans., Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/On_Benefits/Book_III)).

The noble morality Nietzsche describes in Beyond Good and Evil answers the other way: "one has duties only to one's equals; that one may act towards beings of a lower rank... just as seems good to one... 'beyond good and evil'" and "the ability and obligation to exercise prolonged gratitude and prolonged revenge—both only within the circle of equals" ([Nietzsche, BGE 260, Zimmern trans., Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/4363/pg4363.txt)). On the same question, whether a benefit received from someone of lower rank creates a claim, the two texts give incompatible answers.

A related extension sits beside this dispute rather than inside it: the lojong slogan "Be grateful to everyone" is taught as gratitude even toward people presenting threats or challenges: "the idea here is to feel grateful that others are presenting us with tremendous obstacles— even threats or challenges. The point is to appreciate that. Without them, we could not follow the path at all" ([Trungpa, Training the Mind, slogan 13](https://terebess.hu/english/coll2.pdf)). What none of the three settles is whether gratitude to one who cannot or will not reciprocate is generosity or self-deception; the counting-blessings evidence, which measures the practitioner, does not speak to it.

## Practice: variation, selection, retention

::Variation:: What tends to need varying on this theme is the taken-for-granted: adaptation flattens repeated benefits, the counting practice meets it with deliberate attention, and the danger the theme itself names is that gratitude becomes a technique, a mood listed for effect. The adaptive direction keeps the benefit's source in view, names the giver and the gift specifically, and lets the acknowledgment reach the person when expression is possible.

::Selection:: The practices I have selected as sources of that variation:

1. [K164 — Gratitude practice](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K164%20%E2%80%94%20Gratitude%20practice): three things, written or expressed directly, with specificity named as what matters: not a friend in general but what the friend did.

2. [K212 — Gratitude expression](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K212%20%E2%80%94%20Gratitude%20expression): the gratitude visit from Seligman's intervention study, reported on the card with its own raw-text check of the paper as the largest immediate effect of the five exercises tested, lasting about a month before it fades.

3. [K280 — Gratitude for sufficiency](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K280%20%E2%80%94%20Gratitude%20for%20sufficiency): each evening, one thing you already have that is enough, targeting the mechanism of desire rather than the mood.

4. [K110 — Savoring](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K110%20%E2%80%94%20Savoring): the present-tense cousin, stopping the good moment from passing unnoticed.

5. [K157 — Evening review](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=K157%20%E2%80%94%20Evening%20review): the last quarter hour of the day, three questions in writing; the card's report that the written form outperformed more practice in the field experiment.

::Retention:: Retention is what people keep because it works for them. A reader who keeps one evening list, one expressed thanks, or the sufficiency naming has what the theme offered; what stays is a matter of fit between the practice and the life it enters.
