# Executive Functions

> What does it cost to hold a plan in mind against distraction, and who or what is doing the holding?

I want to understand what holds a life's plans in place. I care about the difference between a resolution that survives the afternoon and one that does not, and I have noticed that the everyday explanation, a quantity of willpower one has or lacks, does not match what the research actually found. The mind's management suite, the capacities that inhibit, hold, switch and plan, turns out to be real and measurable, and stranger than the muscle picture suggests.

The question has two halves. The first is cost: holding a plan against distraction is effortful, and researchers have asked what, if anything, is spent in the effort. The second is ownership: the psychologists call these functions 'executive', as if a little manager sat in the frontal lobes issuing orders, and they are candid about what the label does and does not explain. William James, reading his own will from the inside a century earlier, described its essential achievement as attention holding a difficult object fast before the mind. Executive-function studies measure performance on control tasks; James describes voluntary effort in terms of attention. Those are different questions, and where the sources genuinely line up against each other, the disagreements are narrower and sharper.

### Nearby terms

- **Willpower**: the everyday name for the whole package, treated as grit one has or lacks. The resource metaphor behind it is now contested ([Executive function theory Miyake](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Executive%20function%20theory%20Miyake)).
- **Working memory**: the holding part: keeping a plan active while other thoughts compete. A capacity with known limits ([Working memory capacity limits](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Working%20memory%20capacity%20limits)), not a virtue ([Working memory model Baddeley](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Working%20memory%20model%20Baddeley)).
- **Cognitive flexibility**: the switching part: releasing one frame when the situation has changed. In the triad below it is the Shifting factor ([Cognitive flexibility](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Cognitive%20flexibility)).
- **Akrasia**: the classical name for knowing the better and doing the worse. The Greeks asked what kind of person fails this way ([A056 — Recognise akrasia — weakness of will](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=A056%20%E2%80%94%20Recognise%20akrasia%20%E2%80%94%20weakness%20of%20will)).
- **Attention**: on James's account, the substance of will itself. Attention is the wider capacity that selects and holds objects; the executive suite is what that holding draws on when the object is a plan competing against other pulls.

## The mind's management suite

### What the laboratory actually measures

One influential study here is deliberately modest about what it assumes. Miyake and colleagues did not take for granted that the mind's control processes form one faculty or many; they tested the question. With latent variable analysis on 137 students, they examined three candidate functions: shifting between tasks and mental sets ([Attentional flexibility (set-shifting)](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Attentional%20flexibility%20%28set-shifting%29)); updating and monitoring the contents of working memory; and inhibiting dominant, automatic responses when they are the wrong ones ([Response inhibition](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Response%20inhibition)) ([Miyake et al. 2000](https://www.ling.ohio-state.edu/research/groups/lacqueys/readings/MiyakeEtAl2000.pdf)).

Their result is the field's touchstone, and its phrasing has a deliberate double edge:

> "Confirmatory factor analysis indicated that the three target executive functions are moderately correlated with one another, but are clearly separable." ([Miyake et al. 2000](https://www.ling.ohio-state.edu/research/groups/lacqueys/readings/MiyakeEtAl2000.pdf))

Moderately correlated, clearly separable. The three functions travel together, so a person good at one tends to be somewhat better at the others, but the analysis keeps them distinct. Complex tasks draw on them in different proportions: card sorting leaned on shifting, the Tower of Hanoi puzzle on inhibition, operation span on updating. The authors' own closing caution is that a simple dichotomy, one faculty or three, "will not suffice, and both aspects must be taken into account" ([Miyake et al. 2000](https://www.ling.ohio-state.edu/research/groups/lacqueys/readings/MiyakeEtAl2000.pdf)).

A later review by the same group extended the picture without breaking it. Executive functions are high-level processes that control lower-level ones in the service of goal-directed behavior; they are robustly correlated but separable; they are not the same as general intelligence or g; and they are highly heritable at the latent level ([Friedman & Miyake 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27251123/)). What the laboratory hands the reader is a correlated family of capacities: shared variance plus specific skills.

A decade of further work sharpened the unity half of that result into something with a strange shape. In the nested-factors model of Miyake and Friedman, all nine executive-function tasks in their battery load onto one Common EF factor, with updating and shifting each adding their own nested factors:

> "there is a Common EF latent variable on which all nine EF tasks load (unity), as well as two 'nested' latent variables on which the updating and shifting tasks, respectively, also load (diversity)." ([Miyake & Friedman 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3388901/))

The surprise is what is missing: inhibition has no factor of its own. "After accounting for the unity (Common EF), there is no unique variance left for inhibition", the authors write; the inhibition factor "happens to correlate virtually perfectly with Common EF, leaving no inhibition-specific variance" ([Miyake & Friedman 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3388901/)). Their current view of what the shared factor reflects is telling for the manager question: "Common EF is about one's ability to actively maintain task goals and goal-related information and use this information to bias lower-level processing" ([Miyake & Friedman 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3388901/)). What travels together across all the tasks, on the authors' account, is the holding of a goal where it can bias what happens next.

### The executive as placeholder

The word 'executive' carries a standing criticism its own architect has accepted. Baddeley, whose working-memory model gave the field the 'central executive', accepts the criticism that it is "a convenient homunculus, a little man in the head who could be used to explain away any awkward findings", and answers that such homunculi can be useful "as a means of acknowledging that a certain area lies beyond the scope of a developing model", provided one treats them as "a convenient reminder of the problem area, not an explanation" ([Baddeley 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11979773/)).

His stated hope is that by explaining, function by function, how the homunculus does its work, "eventually with all the functions explained, the homunculus would be able to retire" ([Baddeley 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11979773/)).

The retirement now has advocates: it has been suggested "it is time for the executive to be retired and replaced with a set of distributed processors that operate without the need for central control, in so doing avoiding the problem of the homunculus" ([Hitch, Allen & Baddeley 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11783980/), citing Logie 2016). The model's own current position retains the executive: "We see the central executive as a separate set of control resources that can interact with the rest of the system in many ways" ([Hitch, Allen & Baddeley 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11783980/)). James describes the essential achievement of voluntary effort as attending to a difficult object and holding it fast before the mind ([James 1890](https://www.yorku.ca/pclassic/James/Principles/prin26.htm)).

### The disputed resource account

The everyday picture of willpower as a fuel tank has a precise scientific ancestor: the strength model of self-control, in which self-control is "a limited resource that becomes depleted after a period of exertion resulting in self-control failure", the state the literature calls ego depletion ([Hagger et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27474142/), [plain text](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=27474142&rettype=abstract&retmode=text); [Ego depletion](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Ego%20depletion)). The model had intuitive pull and a long experimental run. It also had a replication problem, and the problem was measured on a scale the field rarely manages: twenty-three laboratories, 2,141 participants, one preregistered protocol.

> "Meta-analysis of the studies revealed that the size of the ego-depletion effect was small with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) that encompassed zero (d = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.15]." ([Hagger et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27474142/), [plain text](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=27474142&rettype=abstract&retmode=text))

Under that protocol, the effect was indistinguishable from zero. The model's defenders did not accept the verdict: in their reply, Baumeister and Vohs argued that the RRR "was plagued by manipulation failure and therefore did not test ego depletion", and noted that "for two decades, we have conducted studies of ego depletion carefully and honestly... and we find the effect over and over" ([Baumeister & Vohs 2016](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2019-04/baumeister_vohs_2016_perspectives_comment_on_hagger_rrr_misguided_effort_with_elusive_implications_2_0.pdf)). Five years later, Vohs, Schmeichel and colleagues reported a preregistered multilaboratory project, 36 laboratories and 3,531 participants, whose confirmatory result was again one the resource reading cannot rest on: d = 0.06, nonsignificant ([Vohs et al. 2021](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2026-01/vohs-et-al-2021-psci-ego-depletion.pdf)).

The record is not one of settled demolition. A third multilab replication, using a different depleting task, found a small effect that cleared significance (12 labs, N = 1,775, d = 0.10) ([Dang et al. 2021](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/71213306/junhua_dang_et_al_a_multilab_replication_of_the_ego_depletion_effect_publishersversion.pdf)). Taken together, the findings leave the strong fuel-tank version of the model in doubt without settling what self-control effort actually is.

### Where the suite visibly strains: ADHD

ADHD is usually described as an attention deficit. In his 2020 capstone, DeWitt proposes an interest-based account of ADHD. In one study, children at risk for ADHD "performed significantly worse... than non-ADHD children on attention and recall with reading material that was familiar", yet "when presented with a novel story, they performed indistinguishably from their non-ADHD counterparts" ([DeWitt 2020](https://repository.upenn.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/e85d5911-7c45-41b2-8377-2bb30cec785a/content), citing Beike & Zentall 2012).

The synthesis DeWitt draws from the evidence is direct: "we could therefore describe ADHD as a deficit of interest, meaning that when interest is low, ADHD traits tend to be high, but when interest is high, ADHD traits tend to be low" ([DeWitt 2020](https://repository.upenn.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/e85d5911-7c45-41b2-8377-2bb30cec785a/content)). The design implication is environmental: "the brain of an individual with ADHD needs stimulation and interest in order to ameliorate some ADHD traits" ([DeWitt 2020](https://repository.upenn.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/e85d5911-7c45-41b2-8377-2bb30cec785a/content); [AT069 — Recognise ADHD — interest-based attention, not a deficit](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=AT069%20%E2%80%94%20Recognise%20ADHD%20%E2%80%94%20interest-based%20attention%2C%20not%20a%20deficit)).

### James: willing as effortful attention

William James, reading his own volition from the inside, came to a conclusion that sits oddly well beside this controversy. What we call will, he argued, has one observable component:

> "The essential achievement of the will, in short, when it is most 'voluntary,' is to ATTEND to a difficult object and hold it fast before the mind." (James, [The Principles of Psychology, 1890, chapter XXVI](https://www.yorku.ca/pclassic/James/Principles/prin26.htm))

On this account the hard part of keeping a resolution is keeping the chosen object in possession of the field of thought against whatever else is pulling at it. The motor consequences, James says, are a "physiological incident" of the attending ([P068 — Will is the effort of attention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=P068%20%E2%80%94%20Will%20is%20the%20effort%20of%20attention)). Read this way, what holds a plan in place is a grip: attention holding the plan where action can find it.

The Buddhist tradition approaches effort from a different angle. The Buddha's words to Soṇa, a former lute player straining in meditation, take an instrument as their working image of effort:

> "In the same way, Sona, over-aroused persistence leads to restlessness, overly slack persistence leads to laziness. Thus you should determine the right pitch for your persistence, attune the pitch of the [five] faculties [to that], and there pick up your theme." ([Thanissaro, AN 6.55](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an06/an06.055.than.html))

Effort here is a pitch to tune: too taut produces restlessness, too slack produces laziness ([B036 — Tune, don't force — the lute string](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B036%20%E2%80%94%20Tune%2C%20don%27t%20force%20%E2%80%94%20the%20lute%20string)). The sutta says nothing about managers or fuel tanks; it addresses the feel of effort directly, and its instruction is calibration. A companion conclusion follows for practice: if the environment is stronger than willpower, the first move is design, not resolve ([AT045 — Learn to protect attention through environmental design — the environment is stronger than willpower](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=AT045%20%E2%80%94%20Learn%20to%20protect%20attention%20through%20environmental%20design%20%E2%80%94%20the%20environment%20is%20stronger%20than%20willpower)).

## Where the traditions genuinely disagree

### What did the multilab tests settle about ego depletion?

The strength model says self-control is a resource that exertion depletes ([Hagger et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27474142/), [plain text](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=27474142&rettype=abstract&retmode=text)). The multilab record is a genuine dispute about that claim and about whether the tests were fair. Baumeister and Vohs answer the null replication by charging manipulation failure and pointing to two decades of their own positive studies ([Baumeister & Vohs 2016](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2019-04/baumeister_vohs_2016_perspectives_comment_on_hagger_rrr_misguided_effort_with_elusive_implications_2_0.pdf)); their side's preregistered multilab test then came back null as well ([Vohs et al. 2021](https://carlsonschool.umn.edu/sites/carlsonschool.umn.edu/files/2026-01/vohs-et-al-2021-psci-ego-depletion.pdf)); and a third multilab test with a different depleting task found a small significant effect ([Dang et al. 2021](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/71213306/junhua_dang_et_al_a_multilab_replication_of_the_ego_depletion_effect_publishersversion.pdf)).

The James and Miyake material bears on how effort is described, but these are not rival positions the sources force into opposition; the documented dispute here is about the depletion findings and the adequacy of the tests.

### Does self-control practice generalize?

The strength-model tradition treats self-control as trainable in bulk ([Self-control training](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Self-control%20training)). In its laboratory, Muraven, Baumeister and Tice had 69 students spend two weeks on self-control exercises, monitoring posture, regulating mood or recording eating, and the exercisers "showed significant improvement in self-regulatory capacity" compared with a no-exercise control group ([Muraven et al. 1999](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/10457761/), [plain text](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=10457761&rettype=abstract&retmode=text)). On this reading the will is like a muscle in both directions: it fatigues in the short term and strengthens with practice in the long term.

In a six-week program (N = 174) of daily cognitive or behavioral self-control tasks, Miles and colleagues report that "we found no evidence that training led to any improvements in self-control", and conclude that "training self-control through repeated practice does not result in generalized improvements in self-control" ([Miles et al. 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/107549/3/Does%20Self-Control%20Improve%20With%20Practice%20Final%20Manuscript%20with%20Supp%20Refs.pdf); [White Rose abstract page](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/107549/)).

Separately from the empirical dispute, the Sona Sutta gives effort advice rather than a measurement: over-aroused persistence produces restlessness, which is a failure mode in its own right ([Thanissaro, AN 6.55](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an06/an06.055.than.html)). My practice cards mostly side with tuning: protect attention by design, and treat cognitive flexibility as the capacity that lets go of a dead frame ([CO045 — Cultivate cognitive flexibility — the capacity to switch between modes](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=CO045%20%E2%80%94%20Cultivate%20cognitive%20flexibility%20%E2%80%94%20the%20capacity%20to%20switch%20between%20modes)).

### Should working memory retain a separate executive component?

The controller question has a documented dispute inside it: how should working memory represent control? The model's own architects give the retain side:

> "We see the central executive as a separate set of control resources that can interact with the rest of the system in many ways" ([Hitch, Allen & Baddeley 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11783980/)).

The same article reports a retirement proposal, citing Logie (2016):

> "It has been suggested it is time for the executive to be retired and replaced with a set of distributed processors that operate without the need for central control, in so doing avoiding the problem of the homunculus" ([Hitch, Allen & Baddeley 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11783980/), citing Logie 2016).

The retirement case itself rests on a regress worry: on Logie's account, "the concept of an executive controller begs the question of what is controlling the controller and so on, leading to an infinite hierarchy of executives or 'homunculi'", and after two decades of research "executive control might arise from the interaction among multiple different functions in cognition that use different, but overlapping, brain networks" ([Logie 2016](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17470218.2015.1136657)).

The dispute concerns how working memory should represent control: as a separate set of executive resources, or through distributed processors operating without central control. Baddeley's own earlier rationale for using a provisional construct, that the homunculus is "a convenient reminder of the problem area, not an explanation" whose functions could eventually be explained so it "would be able to retire" ([Baddeley 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11979773/)), is a rationale for keeping the label while its component functions remain unexplained, not a third camp between the two positions.

I favour treating the executive as a working description whose component functions still need explaining. I want the label to keep those questions visible.

## Practice: holding a plan without gripping it

::Variation:: Executive capacities vary, between people and within the same person across a day, a mood and a body state. A plan that held easily at nine in the morning can slip at eleven. The practices below are chosen so that the holding does not depend on feeling strong.

::Selection:: I have kept the practices that design around the executive, and the tuning checks that catch the two failure modes, restlessness and slackness, before they become self-blame.

### Design the field before the effort

1. Remove the competing stimuli before starting, so attention does not have to fight them. Put the phone out of the room, close the tabs, put the plan on one page ([AT045 — Learn to protect attention through environmental design — the environment is stronger than willpower](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=AT045%20%E2%80%94%20Learn%20to%20protect%20attention%20through%20environmental%20design%20%E2%80%94%20the%20environment%20is%20stronger%20than%20willpower)). Notice: whether holding the plan takes less effort when there is nothing else in the field.

### Keep the object in front of the mind

2. When a resolution and a wish part ways, do not push the wish down; put the chosen object back in front of attention and hold it there ([P068 — Will is the effort of attention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=P068%20%E2%80%94%20Will%20is%20the%20effort%20of%20attention)). Notice: what happens to the resolution while attention is elsewhere, and what happens when attention returns to it.

### Check the pitch, not just the effort

3. When persistence produces restlessness or laziness, treat it as a pitch problem: retune the pace, the stakes or the rest intervals before pushing harder ([B036 — Tune, don't force — the lute string](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B036%20%E2%80%94%20Tune%2C%20don%27t%20force%20%E2%80%94%20the%20lute%20string)). Notice: which of the two failure modes you default to under pressure.

### Practice letting go of a dead frame

4. Deliberately switch frames, a route, an interpretation, a plan, while a task is live, so that shifting stays a capacity and not a threat ([CO045 — Cultivate cognitive flexibility — the capacity to switch between modes](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=CO045%20%E2%80%94%20Cultivate%20cognitive%20flexibility%20%E2%80%94%20the%20capacity%20to%20switch%20between%20modes)). Notice: the difference between abandoning an aim and abandoning one route to it.

::Retention:: What stays is the holding itself: the work of will is the holding of the chosen object before the mind, and most of the rest, the environment, the pace, the plan's shape, can be arranged so that the holding is brief.
