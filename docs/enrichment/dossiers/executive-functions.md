# Executive Functions

> What does it cost to hold a plan in mind against distraction, and who or what is doing the holding?

I keep losing the thread. I sit down to write with a plan, and twenty minutes later I am three tabs deep in something that mattered to no one. The experience feels like a failure of a muscle, something inside me that was too weak to hold the line. This dossier is about that feeling, and about how much of it survives contact with the evidence. The mind's management suite — the capacities that inhibit, hold, switch and plan — turns out to be real, measurable, and stranger than the muscle picture suggests.

The question has two halves. The first is cost: holding a plan against distraction is effortful, and something is spent in the effort — but what, and how much? The second is ownership: the psychologists call these functions 'executive', as if a little manager sat in the frontal lobes issuing orders.

William James, reading his own will from the inside a century earlier, found no manager at all — only attention, gripping. The traditions do not agree here, and the disagreement is worth keeping in view rather than smoothing over.

### Nearby terms

- **Willpower** — the everyday name for the whole package, treated as grit one has or lacks. The resource metaphor behind it is now contested ([Executive function theory Miyake](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Executive%20function%20theory%20Miyake)).
- **Working memory** — the holding part: keeping a plan active while other thoughts compete. A capacity with known limits, not a virtue ([Working memory model Baddeley](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Working%20memory%20model%20Baddeley)).
- **Cognitive flexibility** — the switching part: releasing one frame when the situation has changed. In the triad below it is the Shifting factor ([Cognitive flexibility](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=Cognitive%20flexibility)).
- **Akrasia** — the classical name for knowing the better and doing the worse. The Greeks asked what kind of person fails this way ([A056 — Recognise akrasia — weakness of will](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=A056%20%E2%80%94%20Recognise%20akrasia%20%E2%80%94%20weakness%20of%20will)).
- **Attention** — on James's account, not a neighbour of will but its substance. The attention dossier holds the general science; this one follows what happens when attention holds a plan.

## The mind's management suite

### What the laboratory actually measures

The modern study of executive functions began with a rejection: the rejection of the "central executive" as an unexplained explainer. The field's most cited paper here is deliberately modest. Miyake and colleagues chose three candidate functions and tested, with latent variable analysis on 137 students, whether they were one thing, three things, or something in between ([Miyake et al. 2000](https://www.ling.ohio-state.edu/research/groups/lacqueys/readings/MiyakeEtAl2000.pdf)). The three: shifting between tasks and mental sets; updating and monitoring the contents of working memory; and inhibiting dominant, automatic responses when they are the wrong ones.

Their result is the field's touchstone, and its phrasing has a deliberate double edge:

> "Confirmatory factor analysis indicated that the three target executive functions are moderately correlated with one another, but are clearly separable." ([Miyake et al. 2000](https://www.ling.ohio-state.edu/research/groups/lacqueys/readings/MiyakeEtAl2000.pdf))

Moderately correlated, clearly separable. The three functions travel together — a person good at one tends to be somewhat better at the others — but they are not one muscle. Complex tasks draw on them in different proportions: card sorting leans on shifting, the Tower of Hanoi puzzle on inhibition, operation span on updating. The authors' own closing caution is that a simple dichotomy, one faculty or three, "will not suffice, and both aspects must be taken into account" ([Miyake et al. 2000](https://www.ling.ohio-state.edu/research/groups/lacqueys/readings/MiyakeEtAl2000.pdf)).

A later review by the same group extended the picture without breaking it. Executive functions are high-level processes that control lower-level ones in the service of goals; they are robustly correlated but separable; they are not the same as intelligence; and they are highly heritable at the latent level ([Friedman & Miyake 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27251123/)). What the laboratory hands the reader is not a muscle but a correlated family of capacities — shared variance plus specific skills.

### The muscle that failed to replicate

The everyday picture of willpower as a fuel tank has a precise scientific ancestor: the strength model of self-control, in which self-control is "a limited resource that becomes depleted after a period of exertion resulting in self-control failure" — the state the literature calls ego depletion ([Hagger et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27474142/)). The model had intuitive pull and a long experimental run. It also had a replication problem, and the problem was measured on a scale the field rarely manages: twenty-three laboratories, 2,141 participants, one preregistered protocol.

> "Meta-analysis of the studies revealed that the size of the ego-depletion effect was small with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) that encompassed zero (d = 0.04, 95% CI [-0.07, 0.15]." ([Hagger et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27474142/))

That is not a small effect confidently measured; it is an effect indistinguishable from nothing, measured well. The resource-depletion model has not been executed — a later multilab replication with a different depleting task found a small but significant effect (12 labs, N = 1,775, d = 0.10) ([Dang et al. 2021](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/71213306/junhua_dang_et_al_a_multilab_replication_of_the_ego_depletion_effect_publishersversion.pdf)) — but the strong version, the fuel tank behind the everyday self-blame, is gone. What remains is a question the muscle picture never had to answer: if holding the line is not a fuel that runs out, what exactly is effortful about it?

### James: no manager, only attention

William James, reading his own volition from the inside, came to a conclusion that fits the post-replication landscape oddly well. What we call will, he argued, has one observable component, and it is not a push:

> "The essential achievement of the will, in short, when it is most 'voluntary,' is to ATTEND to a difficult object and hold it fast before the mind." (James, [The Principles of Psychology, 1890, chapter XXVI](https://www.yorku.ca/pclassic/James/Principles/prin26.htm))

On this account the hard part of keeping a resolution is not summoning force but keeping the chosen object in possession of the field of thought against whatever else is pulling at it. The motor consequences, James says, are a "physiological incident" of the attending rather than a separate push ([P068 — Will is the effort of attention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=P068%20%E2%80%94%20Will%20is%20the%20effort%20of%20attention)). It is a striking inversion: the executive is not a manager issuing orders but a grip — attention holding a plan where action can find it.

The Buddhist tradition, asked about effort, declines both the manager and the muscle in a different direction. The Buddha's words to Soṇa, a former lute player who was straining in meditation, take an instrument as their whole psychology:

> "In the same way, Sona, over-aroused persistence leads to restlessness, overly slack persistence leads to laziness. Thus you should determine the right pitch for your persistence, attune the pitch of the [five] faculties [to that], and there pick up your theme." ([Thanissaro, AN 6.55](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an06/an06.055.than.html))

Effort here is not a quantity to maximize but a pitch to tune — too taut produces restlessness, too slack produces laziness ([B036 — Tune, don't force — the lute string](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B036%20%E2%80%94%20Tune%2C%20don%27t%20force%20%E2%80%94%20the%20lute%20string)). A companion conclusion follows: if the environment is stronger than willpower, the first move is design, not resolve ([AT045 — Learn to protect attention through environmental design — the environment is stronger than willpower](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=AT045%20%E2%80%94%20Learn%20to%20protect%20attention%20through%20environmental%20design%20%E2%80%94%20the%20environment%20is%20stronger%20than%20willpower)).

## Where the traditions genuinely disagree

### Is self-control a resource, a skill, or an act of attention?

The strength model says resource: exertion depletes, failure follows ([Hagger et al. 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27474142/)). The preregistered multilab test of that model found an effect centred on zero, which weakens the resource reading without installing a replacement. James's introspective account says attention: willing is the holding of a difficult object before the mind, and the effort is the holding ([James 1890](https://www.yorku.ca/pclassic/James/Principles/prin26.htm)). The Miyake framework says neither, exactly: what exists is a correlated family of capacities, trainable at the level of specific skills, with no single fuel tank to empty or fill ([Friedman & Miyake 2017](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27251123/)). I lean toward treating the three as descriptions at different levels — a skill is what attention-holding becomes when it has been practised; a resource was what the skill felt like from inside. But the disagreement is real, and the fuel-tank version in particular no longer has the evidence behind it.

### Is there an executive at all?

The psychologists' word 'executive' implies a controller, and the field's own findings make that picture doubtful. The unity in the data is a statistical common factor, not a discovered manager; the diversity is real separable skills ([Miyake et al. 2000](https://www.ling.ohio-state.edu/research/groups/lacqueys/readings/MiyakeEtAl2000.pdf)). James, looking inside, found no issuing authority — only attention gripping an object ([James 1890](https://www.yorku.ca/pclassic/James/Principles/prin26.htm)). The Buddhist account locates the work even less centrally: effort is one faculty among five, and it is tuned by a comparison to a stringed instrument, not commanded ([AN 6.55](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an06/an06.055.than.html)). What 'does the holding' may be a bad question — the holding is what the whole system is doing, in the way a standing wave is what the whole river is doing. I find that deflationary reading the most honest, but it leaves the phenomenology of effort unexplained in a way the manager picture never did.

### Should effort be maximized or tuned?

The self-control tradition often speaks as if more effort is better: stronger inhibition, harder grip, more resolve. The Sona Sutta's whole point is that this is a category error — over-aroused persistence produces restlessness, which is a failure mode, not a success with a cost ([Thanissaro, AN 6.55](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an06/an06.055.than.html)). The laboratory agrees obliquely: inhibition is one separable capacity among three, not the master virtue of the suite, and rigidity is what a strong grip without shifting looks like ([Miyake et al. 2000](https://www.ling.ohio-state.edu/research/groups/lacqueys/readings/MiyakeEtAl2000.pdf)). The practice cards mostly side with tuning: protect attention by design rather than force, treat cognitive flexibility as the capacity that lets go of a dead frame ([CO045 — Cultivate cognitive flexibility — the capacity to switch between modes](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=CO045%20%E2%80%94%20Cultivate%20cognitive%20flexibility%20%E2%80%94%20the%20capacity%20to%20switch%20between%20modes)). The maximizer's reply — that some situations do call for everything one has — is not refuted by the tuning image; it is bounded by it.

## Practice: holding a plan without gripping it

::Variation:: Executive capacities vary, between people and within the same person across a day, a mood and a body state. A plan that held easily at nine in the morning can slip at eleven. The practices below are chosen so that the holding does not depend on feeling strong.

::Selection:: I have kept the practices that design around the executive rather than exhort it, and the tuning checks that catch the two failure modes — restlessness and slackness — before they become self-blame.

### Design the field before the effort

1. Remove the competing stimuli before starting, so attention does not have to fight them. Put the phone out of the room, close the tabs, put the plan on one page ([AT045 — Learn to protect attention through environmental design — the environment is stronger than willpower](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=AT045%20%E2%80%94%20Learn%20to%20protect%20attention%20through%20environmental%20design%20%E2%80%94%20the%20environment%20is%20stronger%20than%20willpower)). Notice: how much of the struggle disappears when there is nothing else in the field.

### Keep the object in front of the mind

2. When a resolution and a wish part ways, do not push the wish down; put the chosen object back in front of attention and hold it there ([P068 — Will is the effort of attention](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=P068%20%E2%80%94%20Will%20is%20the%20effort%20of%20attention)). Notice: the moment the object slips, action does not yet fail — it waits for the attention to return.

### Check the pitch, not just the effort

3. When persistence produces restlessness or laziness, treat it as a pitch problem: retune the pace, the stakes or the rest intervals before pushing harder ([B036 — Tune, don't force — the lute string](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=B036%20%E2%80%94%20Tune%2C%20don%27t%20force%20%E2%80%94%20the%20lute%20string)). Notice: which of the two failure modes you default to under pressure.

### Practice letting go of a dead frame

4. Deliberately switch frames — a route, an interpretation, a plan — while a task is live, so that shifting stays a capacity and not a threat ([CO045 — Cultivate cognitive flexibility — the capacity to switch between modes](https://clemvillatte.github.io/flourishing-compendium/#card=CO045%20%E2%80%94%20Cultivate%20cognitive%20flexibility%20%E2%80%94%20the%20capacity%20to%20switch%20between%20modes)). Notice: the difference between abandoning an aim and abandoning one route to it.

::Retention:: What stays is the grip image: the work of will is the holding of the chosen object before the mind, and most of the rest — the environment, the pace, the plan's shape — can be arranged so that the holding is brief rather than heroic.
