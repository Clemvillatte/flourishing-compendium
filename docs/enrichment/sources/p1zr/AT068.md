## Claims in body (each verified in an opened source)
- Opened source: Killingsworth & Gilbert 2010, "A wandering mind is an unhappy mind", Science 330:932 — full text PDF (Greater Good Berkeley mirror) downloaded and pdftotext-extracted; abstract also retrieved via PubMed efetch (PMID 21071660, found via esearch after an initial wrong-PMID fetch).
- Blockquote — verbatim from the report's conclusion: "A human mind is a wandering mind, and a wandering mind is an unhappy mind. The ability to think about what is not happening is a cognitive achievement that comes at an emotional cost."
- Study design (2,250 adults, random-moment smartphone sampling, happiness/activity/mind-wandering questions) — full text verbatim basis.
- "Mind wandering occurred in 46.9% of the samples and in at least 30% of the samples taken during every activity except making love" — verbatim; on the page softened to "every activity except one" (the study's own list includes making love; the exception wording is accurate).
- "People were less happy when their minds were wandering than when they were not" and "what people were thinking was a better predictor of their happiness than what they were doing" — full text basis (mind wandering explained 10.8%/17.7% of within/between-person happiness variance vs 4.6%/3.2% for activity).
- The final framing paragraph is labelled as the principle's grounding/reading, not as a study finding.
- The time-lag direction claim ("generally the cause, and not merely the consequence, of unhappiness") is kept in the Sources entry only, stated as the authors' own reading ("time-lag analyses strongly suggested").
