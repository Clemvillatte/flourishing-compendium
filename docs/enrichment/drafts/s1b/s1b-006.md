Daoist literary parable · Liezi, Book II, "The Yellow Emperor," in Lionel Giles's 1912 translation.

A sailor is fond of sea-gulls, and every morning he goes into the sea and swims about in their midst, and a hundred gulls and more constantly flock about him ([Liezi, Book II, sailor and sea-gulls passage](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tz%C5%AD/Book_2)). His father says to him: "I am told that sea-gulls swim about with you in the water. I wish you would catch one or two for me to make pets of." ([Liezi, Book II, same passage](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tz%C5%AD/Book_2))

The next day the sailor goes down to the sea as usual, but the usual result does not follow ([Liezi, Book II, same passage](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tz%C5%AD/Book_2)). No capture is attempted in the narration, and the account ends without an apology or reconciliation; its last narrative image is of the gulls refusing to alight ([Liezi, Book II, same passage](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tz%C5%AD/Book_2)):

> "the gulls only wheeled about in the air and would not alight." ([Liezi, Book II, sailor and sea-gulls passage, exact excerpt from Giles](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tz%C5%AD/Book_2))

Giles prints an attached commentary that explains the change through a disturbance in the sailor's mind accompanied by altered outward demeanour ([Liezi, Book II, footnote to the same passage](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tz%C5%AD/Book_2)). Giles identifies the commentary he excerpts as Chang Chan's, so that explanation should be distinguished from the sailor's narrated actions ([Giles, Introduction](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tz%C5%AD/Introduction)).

The compendium reads the parable as a warning about wanting to own the conditions of a good encounter. The closeness was something the birds participated in; a request to catch them as pets changes what is being asked of the relationship, and the next morning the shared encounter is gone.

It is not a trick for hiding one's intentions more effectively, nor proof that planning itself is harmful. Read as a parable, it can question the demand to secure another being's participation without pretending to establish how gull cognition works.

**Principles:**
- [[D133 — Don't dominate — the world can't be controlled]]: the father's request to possess the birds is followed by the loss of their freely shared encounter; the compendium reads this as carrying the "would hold it in his grasp loses it" facet of DDJ 29, as literary analogy rather than demonstration.
