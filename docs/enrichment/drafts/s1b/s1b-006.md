Daoist literary parable · Liezi, Book II, "The Yellow Emperor," in Lionel Giles's 1912 translation.

A sailor swims among sea-gulls every morning, and a hundred or more gather around him ([Liezi, Book II, p. 49](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tzu;_(IA_taoistteachingsf00liezrich).pdf)). After his father asks him to catch one or two as pets, the next encounter changes: the birds remain in the air and will not land ([Liezi, Book II, pp. 49–50](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tzu;_(IA_taoistteachingsf00liezrich).pdf)).

The sailor is described as fond of the birds, and their habitual closeness comes before any request to catch them ([Liezi, Book II, p. 49](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tzu;_(IA_taoistteachingsf00liezrich).pdf)). His father's request changes the terms: birds that had joined him freely are now wanted as possessions ([Liezi, Book II, p. 49](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tzu;_(IA_taoistteachingsf00liezrich).pdf)).

The next day he goes down to the sea as usual, but the usual result does not follow ([Liezi, Book II, p. 49](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tzu;_(IA_taoistteachingsf00liezrich).pdf)). The account ends without a capture, apology or reconciliation; its last narrative image is of the gulls refusing to alight ([Liezi, Book II, pp. 49–50](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tzu;_(IA_taoistteachingsf00liezrich).pdf)):

> "the gulls only wheeled about in the air and would not alight." ([Liezi, Book II, p. 49, exact excerpt from Giles](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tzu;_(IA_taoistteachingsf00liezrich).pdf))

An attached commentary explains the change through a disturbance in the sailor's mind accompanied by altered outward demeanour ([Giles, p. 50](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tzu;_(IA_taoistteachingsf00liezrich).pdf)). Giles identifies the commentary he excerpts as Chang Chan's; that explanation should be distinguished from the sailor's narrated actions ([Giles, introduction, p. 16](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/11/Taoist_teachings_from_the_book_of_Lieh_Tzu;_(IA_taoistteachingsf00liezrich).pdf)).

The compendium reads the parable as a warning about wanting to own the conditions of a good encounter. The closeness was something the birds participated in; trying to turn it into possession changes what is being asked of the relationship. This offers D133 a relational angle rather than another example of an ambitious ruler.

It is not a trick for hiding one's intentions more effectively, nor proof that planning itself is harmful. Read as a parable, it can question the demand to secure another being's participation without pretending to establish how gull cognition works or to substantiate D133's separate research claims.

**Principles:**
- [[D133 — Don't dominate — the world can't be controlled]]: the attempted turn from a freely shared encounter to possession ends with the encounter no longer available on the sailor's usual terms.
