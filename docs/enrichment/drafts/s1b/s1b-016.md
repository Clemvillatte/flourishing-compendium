Contemporary ACT · David Gillanders, The Rope Bridge Metaphor

Gillanders describes a client who wanted to reconnect with her husband after years of distance. She valued the relationship but feared rejection and did not know whether he wanted the same closeness. The therapist pictured a canyon with a rope bridge between her and the place she hoped to reach. The bridge's safety was uncertain ([author's text](https://contextualscience.org/files/The%20Rope%20Bridge%20Metaphor.pdf)).

Standing still and rushing across were possible responses. The client proposed another: place one foot on the bridge to test it, keeping the other on firm ground. In her situation, she chose trying a conversation with her husband. The account stops at that decision; it gives no result for the conversation ([author's text](https://contextualscience.org/files/The%20Rope%20Bridge%20Metaphor.pdf)).

> whilst keeping a foot on solid ground.
>
> Gillanders, The Rope Bridge Metaphor ([author's text](https://contextualscience.org/files/The%20Rope%20Bridge%20Metaphor.pdf)).

For a reader, the bridge can prompt a question about scale: what action would express a chosen direction while leaving room to respond to what happens? An editorial application is to make one limited request, observe the response, then decide whether to continue, revise the request or stop. Overt behaviour supplies the experiment; attention supplies the feedback.

This analogy cannot establish that a real bridge or a relationship is safe. Where there is coercion, violence or another concrete danger, obtaining protection or support may be the appropriate next action. The destination must remain freely chosen.

**Principles:**
- [[P162 — Take committed action — behavior is where values become real]]: a proposed conversation gives a valued relationship an observable next action.
- [[P177 — Set process goals and let outcomes follow]]: the small test offers information for deciding what to do next.
