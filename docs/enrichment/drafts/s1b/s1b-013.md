2014 · ACT experiential exercise · Pia Heppner, Holding a Pen, in Stoddard and Afari

In Pia Heppner's example, a man has spent six months unemployed. Repeated job-search efforts have brought no offer, and he is becoming irritable and despondent. The therapist asks him to grip a pen with the intensity he brings to the search, then halve that effort, then use only enough to keep it held ([Stoddard and Afari, 2014, pp. 47-49](https://mimhtraining.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/The-Big-Book-of-Act-Metaphors.pdf)).

> Now see if you can bring it down even more.
>
> Heppner, Holding a Pen, in Stoddard and Afari, p. 49 ([book text](https://mimhtraining.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/The-Big-Book-of-Act-Metaphors.pdf)).

The proposed application is to continue searching while easing the grip on the desired result. The client tentatively thinks he could try. The example reports no later job offer or improvement ([pp. 48-49](https://mimhtraining.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/06/The-Big-Book-of-Act-Metaphors.pdf)).

An editorial application in conversation: take answering one question honestly as the task. Notice whether rehearsing every possible reply helps that task. Try answering the question while allowing some uncertainty, then check whether you answered it and remained attentive to the other person. Keep the adjustment if it helped; change it if it made listening harder.

This comparison leaves the other person's reaction open. It also makes room for necessary effort: preparing information, choosing a safe time, or ending an unsafe conversation. The pen exercise cannot decide which of those actions a particular situation requires.

**Principles:**
- [[P157 — Accept what cannot be changed while committing to what can]]: the conversation application pairs willingness with a chosen action.
- [[P177 — Set process goals and let outcomes follow]]: keeping the pen held provides a process criterion; the other person's reaction remains open.
