Documented case, in his own words · Andrew Wiles on proving Fermat's Last Theorem, in the PBS NOVA program The Proof, first broadcast October 28, 1997.

Wiles met the problem at ten, in a public library book that told of Fermat's claim to have proved a roughly three-century-old problem whose proof no one had ever seen. "It was such a challenge, such a beautiful problem," he says; from that moment he tried to solve it himself ([NOVA, The Proof](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/transcripts/2414proof.html/)). As a Princeton professor he then worked on it for seven years in complete secrecy, feeling that too many spectators would have destroyed the concentration the problem required ([NOVA, The Proof](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/transcripts/2414proof.html/)). He announced a proof in 1993; a referee found a fundamental error in it, and the repair took more than a year in public view, work he says was not his style and he has no wish to repeat ([NOVA, The Proof](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/transcripts/2414proof.html/)).

The turn came on a Monday morning, September 19, 1994, while he was at his desk: "Suddenly, totally unexpectedly, I had this incredible revelation" ([NOVA, The Proof](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/transcripts/2414proof.html/)). What had held him up turned out to be exactly what resolved his abandoned earlier approach: "So, out of the ashes seemed to rise the true answer to the problem" ([NOVA, The Proof](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/transcripts/2414proof.html/)). His description of the moment runs:

> "It was the most—the most important moment of my working life. It was so indescribably beautiful; it was so simple and so elegant, and I just stared in disbelief for twenty minutes. Then, during the day, I walked around the department. I'd keep coming back to my desk and looking to see if it was still there. It was still there." ([Andrew Wiles, NOVA, The Proof](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/transcripts/2414proof.html/))

Read through P191, Wiles's account offers an example of creative fulfillment: his description dwells on the constructed proof, on its beauty, simplicity, and continued presence on his desk. He also says of the seven years of work, "I loved every minute of it," a hard and private battle; his testimony does not itself distinguish Bergson's pleasure from joy, and that distinction remains this page's editorial lens rather than a claim Wiles makes. In the same revelation passage he adds: "It was a wonderful feeling after seven years to have really solved my problem. I'd finally done it," and "I had this very rare privilege of being able to pursue in my adult life what had been my childhood dream" ([NOVA, The Proof](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/transcripts/2414proof.html/)). One man's retrospective testimony is a single case; it illustrates Bergson's reading rather than proving it.

**Principles:**
- [[P191 — Joy is the felt signature of creation; distinct from pleasure]]: Wiles's own words place the fulfillment on the constructed proof that came to exist, the true answer out of the ashes, described as beautiful and simple, checked and rechecked at the desk; the pleasure-joy distinction is the page's editorial lens, not a claim the testimony makes.
