ACT teaching metaphor · Hayes, Masuda and De Mey, Two Scales

A therapist imagines two scales like stereo controls. One shows the intensity of anxiety; the other represents willingness to experience it. The person has been trying to turn anxiety down. The therapist introduces the second scale and shifts the question toward how openly the person can meet what is already happening ([manuscript, pp. 9-10](https://actmindfully.com.au/upimages/acceptance_and_commitment_therapy_-_an_overview.pdf)).

The dialogue then refuses a comforting prediction. With greater willingness, anxiety might be low or high. Its future level remains unspecified. The second scale therefore cannot honestly be offered as a guaranteed way to turn down the first ([p. 10](https://actmindfully.com.au/upimages/acceptance_and_commitment_therapy_-_an_overview.pdf)).

> it is a choice.
>
> The manuscript's description of willingness, p. 9 ([source text](https://actmindfully.com.au/upimages/acceptance_and_commitment_therapy_-_an_overview.pdf)).

An editorial application is to distinguish two questions before a manageable task: how anxious am I, and what response am I willing to try? Someone might answer one email while allowing some uncertainty, then examine whether that action served their purpose. Keep the experiment if useful; reduce its scale or seek support if it is too much. Affect and overt behaviour supply different observations.

The image should not become an accusation that a distressed person has chosen badly. It offers a question about responding within present capacities. It gives no warrant to withhold treatment, ignore material obstacles or impose an exposure exercise on someone.

**Principles:**
- [[P157 — Accept what cannot be changed while committing to what can]]: openness to an experience is distinguished from determining its intensity.
- [[P156 — Cultivate psychological flexibility — the master skill]]: the editorial application tests a response while retaining the option to revise it.
