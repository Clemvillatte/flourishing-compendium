Documented case, in his own words · William James's opening anecdote for the pragmatic method, from Lecture II of Pragmatism (1907), in the text the principle itself cites.

Returning from a solitary ramble in the mountains, James finds his camping party "engaged in a ferocious metaphysical dispute. The corpus of the dispute was a squirrel--a live squirrel supposed to be clinging to one side of a tree-trunk" ([James, Pragmatism, Lecture II](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5116/pg5116.txt)). They are debating an imagined scene: a man circles the tree trying to see the squirrel; the squirrel keeps the trunk between them. The question: "DOES THE MAN GO ROUND THE SQUIRREL OR NOT?" The party has worn the discussion threadbare, everyone has taken sides, and the sides are even; each appeals to James "to make it a majority" ([James, Pragmatism, Lecture II](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5116/pg5116.txt)).

His answer is a distinction. "Which party is right," I said, "depends on what you PRACTICALLY MEAN by 'going round' the squirrel. If you mean passing from the north of him to the east, then to the south, then to the west, and then to the north of him again, obviously the man does go round him, for he occupies these successive positions. But if on the contrary you mean being first in front of him, then on the right of him, then behind him, then on his left, and finally in front again, it is quite as obvious that the man fails to go round him." So: "You are both right and both wrong according as you conceive the verb 'to go round' in one practical fashion or the other" ([James, Pragmatism, Lecture II](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5116/pg5116.txt)). A couple of hotter disputants call this shuffling evasion, but most find the quarrel assuaged ([James, Pragmatism, Lecture II](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5116/pg5116.txt)).

James offers the story as "a peculiarly simple example" of the pragmatic method, "a method of settling metaphysical disputes that otherwise might be interminable": "What difference would it practically make to anyone if this notion rather than that notion were true? If no practical difference whatever can be traced, then the alternatives mean practically the same thing, and all dispute is idle" ([James, Pragmatism, Lecture II](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/5116/pg5116.txt)).

Read through P020, the anecdote shows the audit in miniature. The party's beliefs about the man and the squirrel are not tested against the world; the words are tested against their practical content, and the cash-value question dissolves the dispute without declaring a winner. What the story does not show is the other half of the principle, the Lecture VI audit of which ideas assimilate, validate, and corroborate in experience; that belongs to beliefs at work, not to a verb with two meanings.

**Principles:**
- [[P020 — Ask for the cash value of your beliefs]]: The camping-party squirrel dispute ends when James traces each side's words to their practical content, the positions occupied or the belly kept turned; the anecdote is his own exhibit of the cash-value question, while the truth-of-ideas audit of Lecture VI stays with the principle's own text.
