Dialogue from the Zhuangzi, ch. 19 · Yan Yuan asks Confucius about a ferryman, and Confucius answers with the archer.

Yan Yuan reports a crossing: "When I was crossing the gulf of Shang-shen, the ferryman handled the boat like a spirit. I asked him whether such management of a boat could be learned, and he replied, 'It may. Good swimmers can learn it quickly; but as for divers, without having seen a boat, they can manage it at once.' He did not directly tell me what I asked - I venture to ask you what he meant" ([Zhuangzi 19, Legge translation, Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/full-understanding-of-life)). Confucius replies: "Good swimmers acquire the ability quickly - they forget the water (and its dangers)" ([Zhuangzi 19, Legge translation, Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/full-understanding-of-life)). The divers go further still — to them the watery gulf is a hillside and a capsized boat is a carriage rolling back ([Zhuangzi 19, Legge translation, Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/full-understanding-of-life)).

Then the reply turns from water to stakes, and the outer begins to weigh. "He who is contending for a piece of earthenware puts forth all his skill. If the prize be a buckle of brass, he shoots timorously; if it be for an article of gold, he shoots as if he were blind" ([Zhuangzi 19, Legge translation, Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/full-understanding-of-life)). The judgment that follows is the chapter's own: "The skill of the archer is the same in all the cases; but (in the two latter cases) he is under the influence of solicitude, and looks on the external prize as most important. All who attach importance to what is external show stupidity in themselves" ([Zhuangzi 19, Legge translation, Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/full-understanding-of-life)). The Chinese gives the line its edge: 凡外重者內拙 — whoever makes the outer heavy makes the inner clumsy ([Zhuangzi 19, Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/full-understanding-of-life)).

Read beside D096, the dialogue adds an angle about reward pressure: the archer's skill remains the same, but concern for the more valuable prize interferes with its use. That is a related warning about attention, not an illustration of every contrast in Daodejing 12; the chapter's own line runs 凡外重者內拙 — whoever makes the outer heavy makes the inner clumsy ([Zhuangzi 19, Chinese Text Project](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/full-understanding-of-life)).

**Principles:**
- [[D096 — Don't let the outer displace the inner]]: The Zhuangzi dialogue carries one angle of the principle — external reward pressure interfering with the use of an unchanged skill — beside the ferryman's spirit-like ease that belongs to those who have forgotten the water.
