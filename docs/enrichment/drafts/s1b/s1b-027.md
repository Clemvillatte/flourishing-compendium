Canonical narrative, translated · selected episodes from Cullavagga VII, a Vinaya account of Devadatta's schism, in the Rhys Davids and Oldenberg translation; with a separate Mahayana reversal from the Lotus Sutra.

Devadatta approaches the Buddha before a great assembly of the brotherhood and asks him to retire. The Buddha is old, Devadatta says, and should now dwell at ease; the order should be handed over, and Devadatta will be its leader ([Cullavagga VII.3 §1](https://www.wisdomlib.org/buddhism/book/vinaya-3-the-cullavagga/d/doc370296.html)). The request is made three times, and three times refused, the last refusal ending: "I would not give over the Bhikkhu-saṃgha, Devadatta, even to Sāriputta and Moggallāna. How much less, then, to so vile and evil-living a person as you" ([Cullavagga VII.3 §1](https://www.wisdomlib.org/buddhism/book/vinaya-3-the-cullavagga/d/doc370296.html)).

Refused, Devadatta turns to force. When the Buddha walks in meditation below Vulture's Peak, Devadatta climbs above him and rolls a great rock down: "two mountain peaks came together and stopped that rock and only a splinter falling from it made the foot of the Blessed One to bleed" ([Cullavagga VII.3 §9](https://www.wisdomlib.org/buddhism/book/vinaya-3-the-cullavagga/d/doc370296.html)). The Buddha looks up and says to him: "Great, O foolish one, is the demerit you have brought forth for yourself, in that with evil and murderous intent you have caused the blood of the Tathāgata to flow" ([Cullavagga VII.3 §9](https://www.wisdomlib.org/buddhism/book/vinaya-3-the-cullavagga/d/doc370296.html)). A fierce, man-slaying elephant, Nālāgiri, is then loosed on the alms road; the Buddha pervades it with the sense of his love, and the animal puts down its trunk and stands still before him ([Cullavagga VII.3 §12](https://www.wisdomlib.org/buddhism/book/vinaya-3-the-cullavagga/d/doc370296.html)). The crowd's verse on the scene ends: "They can be tamed by sticks, and goads, and whips, But the great Sage has tamed this elephant without a weapon or a stick" ([Cullavagga VII.3 §12](https://www.wisdomlib.org/buddhism/book/vinaya-3-the-cullavagga/d/doc370296.html)). After the violence, the text reports, public feeling turns; Devadatta's gain and honour fall off while the Buddha's increase ([Cullavagga VII.3 §13](https://www.wisdomlib.org/buddhism/book/vinaya-3-the-cullavagga/d/doc370296.html)).

Read through B016, the episodes carry two of the three roots directly. The demand for the order runs on grasping; the boulder and the elephant run on hatred. The third root, delusion, is not named by these texts, and an editor may only wonder at it; the story does not demonstrate it. The Lotus Sutra, a separate Mahayana narrative, reverses the frame: the Buddha recalls a past life in which the seer who taught him the Lotus was Devadatta, says his own awakening is due to that good friend, and predicts Devadatta's future Buddhahood as Heavenly King ([Lotus Sutra ch. 12, Watson](https://quangduc.com/a52688/chapter-twelve-devadatta)). The two texts are read together here as an editorial juxtaposition, not as one continuous account.

**Principles:**
- [[B016 — Know the three root poisons]]: Devadatta's bid for the order and his rock and his elephant display grasping and hatred in one man; the Buddha's naming of the act and his loving-kindness toward the elephant show the diagnostic response in action, and the delusion root is flagged as not carried.
