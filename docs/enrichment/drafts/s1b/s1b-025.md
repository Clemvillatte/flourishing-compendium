ACT thought experiment · Hayes, Masuda and De Mey, Polygraph Metaphor

The manuscript imagines a highly sensitive anxiety detector. A person is instructed to stay calm, then threatened with being shot if anxiety appears. In the dialogue, the client finds the imagined situation frightening ([Control is the Problem, pp. 8-9](https://actmindfully.com.au/upimages/acceptance_and_commitment_therapy_-_an_overview.pdf)).

> All you have to do is stay relaxed.
>
> The fictional therapist's initial instruction ([manuscript, pp. 8-9](https://actmindfully.com.au/upimages/acceptance_and_commitment_therapy_-_an_overview.pdf)).

The setup makes the demand for calm part of what is feared. It is a deliberately coercive fiction, not a procedure to enact or evidence about how accurately an actual polygraph measures anxiety. Its usefulness as an image depends on examining a response without treating the dramatic premise as scientific proof.

An editorial use might begin before a manageable presentation. Notice whether checking repeatedly for anxiety helps prepare the next point or adds another demand. Try rehearsing one short passage while allowing some uneasiness, then assess whether the rehearsal helped. Retain a useful adjustment or change it if it disrupted preparation. Affect and overt behaviour provide different observations.

This application concerns ordinary preparation within safe circumstances. It gives no reason to remain exposed to a threat or to reject effective support. Nor does the thought experiment establish that a person can simply choose to feel calm or that every effort to regulate emotion will fail.

**Principles:**
- [[P155 — Make workability your everyday criterion]]: investigate whether the demand for perfect calm helps the actual task.
- [[P157 — Accept what cannot be changed while committing to what can]]: the editorial application permits uneasiness while keeping a limited action available.
