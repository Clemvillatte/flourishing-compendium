Zen biographical account · Hakuin Ekaku (1686–1769), from his autobiographical Tale of My Childhood, translated in Norman Waddell's Hakuin's Precious Mirror Cave.

In his early twenties, after a first taste of awakening, Hakuin travels over the pass at Mount Tomikura to seek out the old teacher Shōju Rōjin at Iiyama ([Hakuin, Tale of My Childhood, ch. 1](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf)). Shōju takes one look at him, asks how he sees Chao-chou's Mu, pushes the end of his nose with his fingers, and in Hakuin's own words, "He had crushed my pride and arrogance as easily as if he were smashing an egg on a large rock." ([same passage](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf)) The training that follows is physical: Shōju once "picked me up and tossed me off the veranda of the temple as if I were a kitten" ([same passage](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf)).

Shōju then assigns him a koan, with a lineage story attached:

> "Long ago, after Hsi-keng Rōshi had once died the Great Death, he bored into this story for four long years, and he eventually emerged as a great shade tree without equal in all of China." ([Shōju, quoted in Hakuin's Tale of My Childhood](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf))

Hakuin gnaws at the koan from the front and from the sides, forgetting food and sleep, and reports that "the tremendous sense of joy I had felt on my previous enlightenment was now all transformed into a boundless agony" ([same passage](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf)). The breakthrough arrives in an unpromising shape: a local madman strikes him about the head with a broom, he topples over unconscious, and on coming to, he finds he has bored through koans "right to their roots and smashed them to smithereens" ([same passage](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf)). Shōju is pleased, then blocks the exit:

> "But don't be satisfied with this minor attainment. The farther you proceed into enlightenment, the harder you must strive. The greater the understanding you achieve, the more effort you must exert. Do not cease striving until the day you die." ([Shōju, quoted in the same passage](https://terebess.hu/zen/cave.pdf))

For B133, the compendium reads this account as the principle's whole shape told from the teacher's side. The collapse, the smashing of the proud self, is over in a moment; what Shōju will not allow is treating that death as the finish. The shade tree is the point of the story: dying the Great Death matters because of what grows afterward, and the return is charged as the arduous half. The tale is a spiritual autobiography, not clinical evidence for ego-dissolution research, and it does not carry that facet of the principle.

**Principles:**
- [[B133 — Let the self-construct fall away (the Great Death)]]: the self-construct is smashed early and decisively, but the teacher's closing charge marks the return, practice that continues after the breakthrough, as the real work; the shade tree, not the collapse, is the image the account leaves standing.
