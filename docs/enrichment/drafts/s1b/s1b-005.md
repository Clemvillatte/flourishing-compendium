Russian literary fiction · Tolstoy, Master and Man, translated by Louise and Aylmer Maude.

Vasili Brekhunov sets out to secure a profitable purchase, becomes lost in a snowstorm, and eventually dies warming the servant he had underpaid ([Master and Man, I and VI–X](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/986/986-h/986-h.htm)). Nikita survives, but the story does not give him back an uninjured body or turn the merchant's last act into an uncomplicated happy ending ([Master and Man, X](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/986/986-h/986-h.htm)).

Brekhunov considers himself Nikita's benefactor while paying about forty rubles a year for work the narrator values at eighty, often in overpriced shop goods ([Master and Man, I](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/986/986-h/986-h.htm)). Stranded, he rides away alone, reasoning that his own life is worth saving; after circling in the storm, he follows the horse back to the sledge ([Master and Man, VI–VIII](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/986/986-h/986-h.htm)).

Nikita is nearly frozen and asks that the wages owed him be given to his family ([Master and Man, IX](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/986/986-h/986-h.htm)). Brekhunov clears away snow, opens his coat and lies over him, acting with the same decisiveness the narrator has associated with making a good purchase ([Master and Man, IX](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/986/986-h/986-h.htm)).

For hours he stays there, concerned with warming Nikita even as his own hands and legs freeze ([Master and Man, IX](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/986/986-h/986-h.htm)). He notices that the horse needs covering but cannot bring himself to leave Nikita; near death, his sense of where his life resides changes ([Master and Man, IX](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/986/986-h/986-h.htm)).

> "Nikita is alive, so I too am alive!" ([Master and Man, IX, Maude translation](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/986/986-h/986-h.htm))

Brekhunov and the horse die; peasants dig Nikita out alive, and three of his toes are amputated ([Master and Man, IX–X](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/986/986-h/986-h.htm)). He lives another twenty years and eventually dies at home among his family ([Master and Man, X](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/986/986-h/986-h.htm)).

The compendium reads the change as an opening: the man previously treated as cheap labour becomes the life Brekhunov acts to preserve. That offers a literary angle on Bergson's distinction between enlarging an existing attachment and changing its kind, including his account of a soul in the process of opening ([The Two Sources, chapter I, pp. 27 and 48 to 50](https://archive.org/stream/twosourcesofmora033499mbp/twosourcesofmora033499mbp_djvu.txt)).

The analogy stops short of declaring Brekhunov a fully open soul. Concern for one employee does not demonstrate love of all humanity, and the neglected horse complicates a reading of unrestricted care; Bergson's open love can extend beyond humanity to animals and nature ([Master and Man, IX and X](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/986/986-h/986-h.htm); [The Two Sources, chapter I, p. 27](https://archive.org/stream/twosourcesofmora033499mbp/twosourcesofmora033499mbp_djvu.txt)).

**Principles:**
- [[P194 — Distinguish closed morality from open morality; the open requires creative effort, not mere expansion]]: Brekhunov's commercial resolve becomes costly care for the person he exploited, illustrating an opening of orientation without establishing fully universal concern.
