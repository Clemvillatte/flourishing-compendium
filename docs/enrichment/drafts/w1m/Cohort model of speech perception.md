The cohort model of speech perception holds that hearing a word is not waiting for it to finish: from the first sounds, every word in the listener's vocabulary that fits the opening is active at once. William Marslen-Wilson states the frame:

> "The process of spoken word-recognition breaks down into three basic functions, of access, selection and integration." ([Marslen-Wilson, 1987, p. 71, abstract](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf))

### Origin

The model grew out of Marslen-Wilson's research on real-time recognition of continuous speech, with Alan Welsh; the 1987 paper notes the approach was "labelled an 'active direct access model' in Marslen-Wilson and Welsh (1978), but now usually referred to as the 'cohort model'" ([1987, p. 78](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf)). The paper appeared in Cognition (25, 71-102), written while Marslen-Wilson was at the Max-Planck-Institut für Psycholinguistik and the MRC Applied Psychology Unit, Cambridge ([1987, p. 71](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf)). It presents the model's first version and its revision in one argument.

### Core claims

The word-initial cohort, in the author's words:

> "Early in the word, when only the first 100-150 ms have been heard, then the recognition devices corresponding to all of the words in the listener's mental lexicon that begin with this initial sequence will become active—thereby meeting the requirement for multiple access." ([1987, pp. 78-79](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf))

In the first version, candidates drop out as they stop fitting sound or context:

> "A mismatch with either source of constraint causes the elements to drop out of the pool of potential candidates. This means that there will be a sequential reduction over time in the initial set of candidates, until only one candidate is left." ([1987, p. 79](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf))

Access, in both versions, is autonomous: "The model prohibits top-down activation of these units in normal word-recognition, so that only the sensory input can activate a unit" ([1987, p. 78](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf)). The revision extends autonomy to selection: context "plays no role in the processes of form-based access and selection" ([1987, p. 71, abstract](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf)). In the revision, frequency tunes activation rather than membership:

> "the response of higher-frequency word-forms appears to be enhanced in some way, such that the level of activation of these elements can rise more rapidly, per unit information, than the activation of less frequent elements" ([1987, p. 93](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf))

### How the cohort computes

The model is a parallel-processing account built around three functions rather than a pipeline of stages: access concerns "a mapping of the speech signal onto the representations of word-forms in the mental lexicon", selection discriminates the best-fitting match, and integration fits the word's syntax and semantics into the utterance ([1987, pp. 72-73](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf)). The functions must run in real time: the system must allow access and assessment "such that the correct candidate can be identified—and begin to be integrated into an utterance-level representation—within about 200 ms of word-onset" ([1987, p. 77](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf)). The model's signature derivable prediction is the recognition point: "This is the point at which, starting from word-onset, a word can be discriminated from the other members of its word-initial cohort, taking into account both contextual and sensory constraints" ([1987, p. 80](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf)). For "trespass" heard in isolation, that point is the /p/, where it separates from "tress" and "trestle"; in context it can come earlier. A scope limit: some words are recognised late, and the model's claim is only that a word is "uniquely discriminated as soon as the available constraints … make it possible", wherever that falls ([1987, p. 81](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf)).

### In everyday life

Someone says "she locked the cap…" and for a fraction of a second captain, capsule and capital are all live; as more sounds arrive, the field narrows until one is left. (Illustrative scenario, not a reported participant or study result.) What the theory hands a curious reader: hearing works from incomplete input, recognition does not wait for the whole word, and what counts as enough depends on the constraints available.
