The cohort model of speech perception holds that hearing a word is not waiting for it to finish. From the first sounds, every word in the listener's vocabulary that fits the opening is active at once, and the field narrows as the signal continues. William Marslen-Wilson states the frame:

> "The process of spoken word-recognition breaks down into three basic functions, of access, selection and integration." ([Marslen-Wilson, 1987, abstract](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf))

### Origin

The model grew out of Marslen-Wilson's speech-shadowing research with Alan Welsh, where people repeated speech back almost as fast as it arrived; the 1987 paper notes the approach was "labelled an 'active direct access model' in Marslen-Wilson and Welsh (1978), but now usually referred to as the 'cohort model'" ([1987, p. 73](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf)). The theory appeared in a special issue of Cognition on spoken word recognition (25, 71-102), written while Marslen-Wilson was at the Max-Planck-Institut für Psycholinguistik, Nijmegen, and the MRC Applied Psychology Unit, Cambridge. The paper describes two versions: the original partially interactive model, and a revised, fully bottom-up one.

### Core claims

The word-initial cohort, in the author's words:

> "Early in the word, when only the first 100-150 ms have been heard, then the recognition devices corresponding to all of the words in the listener's mental lexicon that begin with this initial sequence will become active—thereby meeting the requirement for multiple access." ([1987](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf))

Candidates then drop out as they stop fitting:

> "A mismatch with either source of constraint causes the elements to drop out of the pool of potential candidates. This means that there will be a sequential reduction over time in the initial set of candidates, until only one candidate is left." ([1987](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf))

In the revised version, access is autonomous: only the signal, not context, can activate a word:

> "The model prohibits top-down activation of these units in normal word-recognition, so that only the sensory input can activate a unit." ([1987](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf))

Word frequency tunes activation rather than membership: "the response of higher-frequency word-forms appears to be enhanced in some way, such that the level of activation of these elements can rise more rapidly, per unit information, than the activation of less frequent elements" ([1987](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf)).

### How the cohort computes

The model is a parallel-processing account: each word-form works as an independent pattern-matching device, and recognition runs through access (signal to word-forms), selection (discriminating the best-fitting match) and integration (mapping the word's syntax and semantics into the utterance). It carries a hard real-time constraint: "the correct candidate can be identified—and begin to be integrated into an utterance-level representation—within about 200 ms of word-onset" ([1987](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf)). Its signature derivable prediction is the recognition point: "This is the point at which, starting from word-onset, a word can be discriminated from the other members of its word-initial cohort" ([1987](https://shotam.github.io/LING611_papers/MarslenWilson_1987.pdf)), and for many words it falls well before the word ends. A scope limit the author himself sets: in the revision, context plays no role in form-based access and selection, since context operates at the integration interface, and the model is an account of recognising familiar spoken word-forms, not of learning new ones.

### In everyday life

Someone says "she locked the cap…" and for a fraction of a second captain, capsule and capital are all live; by the word's end one is left standing, often before the sound has finished. This is why you can finish a friend's sentence, why a misheard song lyric feels so certain, and why a rhyme can hijack a moment of listening. (Illustrative scenario, not a reported participant or study result.) The lesson the theory hands a curious reader: perception is an active commitment made on partial evidence and continuously revised, so hearing is already interpreting.
