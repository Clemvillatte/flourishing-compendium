Eternal recurrence (ewige Wiederkunft) is Friedrich Nietzsche's thought that this life, with "every pain and every joy and every thought and every sigh," must be lived "once more, and also innumerable times," with "nothing new in it," all "in the same series and sequence" ([The Gay Science, Book IV, §341](https://americanliterature.com/author/friedrich-nietzsche/book/the-gay-science/book-fourth-sanctus-januarius)). The first published statement, in 1882, is section 341 of The Gay Science, "The Heaviest Burden" (Das grösste Schwergewicht), and Nietzsche stages it as a demon's question rather than a lecture ([SEP, Nietzsche](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nietzsche/)).

### The demon's question

The thought is designed to test whether one's life has been good: the emotional reaction to the prospect of return reveals something about the value of the life ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nietzsche/)). Faced with the demon, one would either "throw thyself down and gnash thy teeth, and curse the demon that so spake," or answer "thou art a God, and never did I hear aught more divine" ([§341](https://americanliterature.com/author/friedrich-nietzsche/book/the-gay-science/book-fourth-sanctus-januarius)). Nietzsche adds that if the thought acquired power over a person it would "transform thee, and perhaps crush thee," and asks how well disposed to oneself and to life one would have to become to long for nothing more ardently than this "last eternal sanctioning and sealing" ([§341](https://americanliterature.com/author/friedrich-nietzsche/book/the-gay-science/book-fourth-sanctus-januarius)).

### Doctrine or test

Whether Nietzsche also meant it as cosmology is disputed. The SEP notes that all Nietzsche's official presentations of the thought in published work are hypothetical, elliptical or metaphorical, that most early readers took him to be offering a cosmological hypothesis about the structure of time or fate, and that problems have been posed for that thesis ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nietzsche/)). In the aftermath of Nehamas (1985), an influential line of readings argues that the thought was never a cosmological claim at all but a practical thought experiment designed to test whether one's life has been good, much as a spouse's question about whether one would marry again evokes an assessment of the marriage ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nietzsche/)).

### In the compendium

E104 states the test directly: "Practise living in a way that you would will the eternal recurrence of every moment."
