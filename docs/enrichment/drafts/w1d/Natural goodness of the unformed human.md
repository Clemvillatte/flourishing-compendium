Natural goodness of the unformed human is Rousseau's claim that the human being arrives in the world good, and that whatever corrupts it comes later, from society's hands. The opening sentence of Emile (1762) states it: "Everything is good as it leaves the hands of the Author of things; everything degenerates in the hands of man" ([Emile, Book I, quoted in the IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/rousseau/); [Wikipedia, Emile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emile,_or_On_Education)). The goodness is of the unformed: the child is born weak, needing strength, assistance and judgment, and the work of education is to form what nature left as potential.

### The two self-loves

The doctrine's psychology turns on Rousseau's distinction between two self-loves. Amour de soi, the natural one, "regards only ourselves" and "is content when our real needs are satisfied"; amour-propre, the comparative one, "is never satisfied, and could not be, because this feeling, by preferring ourselves to others, also requires that others prefer ourselves to them, a thing which is impossible" ([Emile, archive.org full text](https://archive.org/stream/rousseausemileor00rousiala/rousseausemileor00rousiala_djvu.txt)). The sole natural passion "becomes good or bad only by the application made of it and the relations given to it" ([Emile 1979, 92, quoted in the UNT dissertation](https://digital.library.unt.edu/ark:/67531/metadc1011841/m2/1/high_res_d/GROSS-DISSERTATION-2017.pdf)). Alongside self-preservation stands pity, "an innate repugnance to see his fellow suffer" ([Second Discourse, quoted in IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/rousseau/)).

The nuance matters: for Rousseau, humans in the state of nature are amoral, neither virtuous nor vicious; moral goodness is a later achievement ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/rousseau/)). Natural goodness is a starting position, not a finished character.
