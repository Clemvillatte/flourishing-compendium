"Locus of control" is Julian Rotter's generalized expectancy about where reinforcement comes from. The 1966 definition: "If the person perceives that the event is contingent upon his own behavior or his own relatively permanent characteristics, we have termed this a belief in internal control." A reinforcement "not being entirely contingent upon his action" is typically perceived "as the result of luck, chance, fate, as under the control of powerful others, or as unpredictable because of the great complexity of the forces surrounding him" — a belief in external control ([Rotter 1966, Psychological Monographs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jjcurtin/arc_measures/main/LCS/Rotter1966.pdf)).

### What internality does

The construct's core hypothesis is about learning: "if a person perceives a reinforcement as contingent upon his own behavior, then the occurrence of either a positive or negative reinforcement will strengthen or weaken potential for that behavior to recur"; seen as chance, the behavior is less likely to be strengthened ([Rotter 1966](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jjcurtin/arc_measures/main/LCS/Rotter1966.pdf)). The studies' summary: "the individual who has a strong belief that he can control his own destiny is likely to (a) be more alert to those aspects of the environment which provide useful information for his future behavior; (b) take steps to improve his environmental condition; (c) place greater value on skill or achievement reinforcements and be generally more concerned with his ability, particularly his failures; and (d) be resistive to subtle attempts to influence him" ([Rotter 1966](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jjcurtin/arc_measures/main/LCS/Rotter1966.pdf)).

### What it predicts

Later research ties the construct to development and well-being. A 2021 editorial on the construct's descendants reports that internal LOC "acts as a mediator to buffer against the development of depression in young high-risk children from compromised environments," and that children facing chronic disease may "erroneously 'learn' to be more helpless than they actually are" ([Nowicki et al. 2021, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8275955/)). Rotter was careful about what the scale measured: the items "deal exclusively with the subjects' belief about the nature of the world," and "none of the items is directly addressed to the preference for internal or external control" ([Rotter 1966](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/jjcurtin/arc_measures/main/LCS/Rotter1966.pdf)).

### In the compendium

The kernel practices operationalize the sorting: K267's controllability sorting, K271's is-this-up-to-me check, K311's responsibility inventory. The Stoic pairing gives the compendium its philosophical version of the same sorting: S001's distinguishing what is up to you, S053's control-versus-influence. A053's acting voluntarily and R106's situated freedom give it the existential register. The construct is adjacent to, but distinct from, the perceived locus of causality in self-determination theory: Rotter's construct concerns expected outcomes, SDT's concerns reasons for acting.
