"Zu den Sachen selbst," to the things themselves, is Edmund Husserl's maxim for what philosophy should do: stop trading in inherited concepts and return to what is actually given in experience. The formulation stands in the introduction to the second part of the Logical Investigations (1900-01): "Meanings inspired only by remote, confused, inauthentic intuitions...are not enough: we must go back to the 'things themselves'" ([SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/husserl/)). The Sachen are the matters under investigation, taken as they present themselves rather than as prior theory has filed them.

### What the return asks

The method behind the maxim is description. Husserl brackets the "natural attitude," the everyday taking-for-granted of the world, not to doubt the world but to examine how anything shows up in it. As he puts it, "we do not lose the world as a result of the epoché, but from now on it must be considered solely as an intentional correlate." What remains for study is the correlation between experienced objects and their manners of givenness, which he called the correlational a priori and described as the first breakthrough of his work. Consciousness is always consciousness of something; describing an experience means describing what it is of, its object as given. In Ideas I (1913) the same stance becomes the "principle of all principles": everything offered in intuition is to be accepted as what it presents itself as being, but only within the limits in which it is so presented.

### From things to the life-world

The return has a late destination. In his final period Husserl argued that science lives off the life-world, "the world we live and feel at home in," with its cups, chairs and trees, and that scientific method never leaves it: "the world as described by science is not an autonomous world, a world behind or below the manifest world. Rather, it is the same world as that of everyday experience...but now enriched theoretically." The maxim is therefore not an attack on science but a demand that theory stay answerable to the experience it enriches.

### In the compendium

E011 "Practise phenomenological description" cites the maxim directly: describe experience as it is given, before theorising about it, and let the phenomenon show itself from itself. P004 "Start from experience, not from theory" carries the same rule with James and Dewey, and Y026 and A050 train the attention it needs. The compendium also pairs the return with contemplative cousins: K015 and B124 meet suchness, the Bahiya Sutta's instruction to let the seen be only the seen, B048 keeps beginner's mind, and D010, D013 and D099 receive things as they are in the Daoist register. ST259, the apple story, gives the method its front door: the apple's far side is not seen and yet is given as there.
