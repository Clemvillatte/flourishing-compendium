**Heart rate variability theory** is the psychophysiological account of beat-to-beat variation in heart rate as an index of self-regulation. Thayer and Lane's model of neurovisceral integration "posits that cardiac vagal tone, indexed by heart rate variability (HRV), can indicate the functional integrity of the neural networks implicated in emotion–cognition interactions" ([Park & Thayer 2014, Frontiers in Psychology](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4013470/)). The core proposal: "the characteristic beat-to-beat variability in the heart rate (HR) time series—heart rate variability (HRV)—serves not only as an index of healthy heart function... but as an readily available index and measure of inhibitory control, and more specifically, ER capacity" ([Williams et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4354240/)).

### The vagal link

The mechanism is vagal. On the model's account, "the functioning of prefrontal-subcortical inhibitory circuits critical for self-regulation is linked with the heart via the vagus nerve that provides inhibitory inputs to the heart" ([Park & Thayer 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4013470/)). Higher resting HRV marks a system that can both engage and disengage — flexibility rather than mere calm.

### What the evidence shows

Empirical results track the theory, with the usual correlational caveat. "Higher resting HRV is associated with more adaptive and functional top-down and bottom-up cognitive modulation of emotional stimuli, which may facilitate effective emotion regulation. Conversely, lower resting HRV is associated with hyper-vigilant and maladaptive cognitive responses to emotional stimuli, which may impede emotion regulation" ([Park & Thayer 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4013470/)). In a study of 183 undergraduates, "lower resting vmHRV was associated with greater difficulties in emotional regulation, especially a lack of emotional clarity and impulse control" ([Williams et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4354240/)). The study reports an association, not a test of whether raising HRV improves regulation.
