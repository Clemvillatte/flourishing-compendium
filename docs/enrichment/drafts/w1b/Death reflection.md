Death reflection is the experimental construct distinguishing a specific, vivid contemplation of one's own death from abstract mortality salience. The theoretical paper behind it proposes "dual-existential systems": "individuals process their existence either via a specific and personalized existential system, or via an abstract and categorical existential system," with the two ending in "growth-oriented or defense-oriented motivational states and self-regulatory processes" ([Cozzolino 2006, Psychological Inquiry](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/12454/)).

### The manipulation

The induction asks participants to imagine their own death in detail. In the scenario, they "imagined waking up in the middle of the night in a friend's apartment on the '20th floor of an old, downtown building' to the 'sounds of screams and the choking smell of smoke'," then answered four questions — the thoughts and emotions, how they would handle the final moments, "describe the life you led up to that point," and how their family would react ([Frias et al. 2011, describing the Cozzolino et al. 2004 scenario](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Frias-DeathGratitude.pdf)).

### What it does

The findings: "mortality salience led highly extrinsic participants to manifest greed, whereas death reflection again generated intrinsic, unselfish behavior" ([Cozzolino, Staples, Meyers & Samboceti 2004, PSPB](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167203260716)). On gratitude, "the death reflection induction showed the greatest increase in state gratitude, followed by the traditional mortality salience treatment and the controls," and the study's conclusion: "when one confronts their own death in a specific and vivid manner, their feelings of gratitude tend to increase" ([Frias et al. 2011](https://greatergood.berkeley.edu/images/uploads/Frias-DeathGratitude.pdf)). The claims are scoped to these studies; later replication attempts have not uniformly reproduced the greed effect.
