> **Principle 107** — Trust your own nature

Chapter 8 of the Zhuangzi makes the point with a duck and a crane:

> "A duck's legs, for instance, are short, but if we try to lengthen them, it occasions pain; and a crane's legs are long, but if we try to cut off a portion of them, it produces grief." (Zhuangzi 8, [Legge](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf))

The chapter frames the rule before the image: the perfectly correct path is not to lose the real character of the nature with which we are endowed, so what is long should not be considered too long, nor what is short too short ([Zhuangzi 8, Legge](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)). It closes the case the same way: where a part is by nature long, we are not to amputate, or where it is by nature short, we are not to lengthen it ([Zhuangzi 8, Legge](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)).

The reading the compendium keeps: the question to ask of any self-improvement plan is whether it develops what is there or amputates it.

(Zhuangzi 8.)

**Plain words**
The duck is not a failed crane. Each has legs that work for its life, and each would be injured by being edited toward the other. Before you change yourself, ask which kind of change this is: practice that builds on your nature, or surgery that removes part of it. Hard training can still be right; the question is whether the change serves the life you actually live or an image of a different animal.

*For example:*
Suppose you are an introvert in a job built on constant cold-calling. The chapter's question is whether the problem is your nature or the job's shape, and its answer: a mismatch is not a defect in the legs.
