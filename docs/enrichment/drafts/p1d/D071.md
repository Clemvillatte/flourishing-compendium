> **Principle 71** — Practice undivided attention

In the Zhuangzi's nineteenth chapter, a hunchback catches cicadas as if picking them up with his hand, and explains the training behind it to Confucius: five or six months practising with two pellets till they never fell, then three, then five, until he missed only one cicada in ten, and then caught them as if gathering them ([Zhuangzi 19, Legge](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)).

> "My body is to me no more than the stump of a broken trunk, and my shoulder no more than the branch of a rotten tree. Great as heaven and earth are, and multitudinous as things are, I take no notice of them, but only of the wings of my cicadas; neither turning nor inclining to one side." (Zhuangzi 19, [Legge](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf))

Confucius draws the lesson for his disciples: "Where the will is not diverted from its object, the spirit is concentrated" — this might have been spoken of this hunchback gentleman ([Zhuangzi 19, Legge](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)).

The compendium's reading: undivided attention is trained, not willed. The pellets make it a skill built by graded practice, the trunk-and-rotten-branch image makes the bodily side of it, and the cicada wings make the single object.

(Zhuangzi 19.)

**Plain words**
The hunchback's answer has three parts, and each is something you can do. First the training: months of graduated practice with pellets before cicadas. Then the body: settled so completely that it stops competing for notice. Then the object: everything else, however large, drops out, leaving the one thing. Divided attention is the untrained version of this, and the story treats it as trainable.

*For example:*
Practise the pellet stage before the cicada stage: write one paragraph with the phone in another room before attempting a whole report with the phone face-down.
