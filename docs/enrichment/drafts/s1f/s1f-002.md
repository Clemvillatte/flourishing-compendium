Historical exchange · New York and Boston, April 1929; documented in Gerald Holton, Einstein's Third Paradise (Dædalus, 2003), with Einstein's 1930 essay Religion and Science

A Boston cardinal called Einstein's relativity "befogged speculation producing universal doubt about God and His Creation", and a New York rabbi wired Einstein a paid question: "Do you believe in God? Stop. Answer paid 50 words." Einstein answered in twenty-five German words that he believed in Spinoza's God, "Who reveals Himself in the lawful harmony of the world, not in a God Who concerns Himself with the fate and the doings of mankind." Beside P187, the exchange separates the religious quality from religion: the awe at the world's harmony is kept, the intervening God and the institutional formula are declined ([Holton 2003](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/essay-Einsteins-Third-Paradise.pdf)).

The charge came from Cardinal O'Connell of Boston, who warned of "the ghastly apparition of atheism". Alarmed, Rabbi Herbert S. Goldstein sent the telegram; the reply, for which Holton notes Einstein "needed but twenty-five (German) words", stated his beliefs succinctly ([Holton 2003](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/essay-Einsteins-Third-Paradise.pdf)). Goldstein read the reply as evidence that Einstein was not an atheist, and declared that "Einstein's theory, if carried to its logical conclusion, would bring to mankind a scientific formula for monotheism"; Holton adds that Einstein wisely remained silent on that point ([Holton 2003](https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/essay-Einsteins-Third-Paradise.pdf)).

Einstein's own account the next year names what the reply kept: a third stage of religious experience he called cosmic religious feeling, in which "The individual feels the futility of human desires and aims and the sublimity and marvelous order which reveal themselves both in nature and in the world of thought", with "there is no anthropomorphic conception of God corresponding to it" ([Einstein 1930](https://sacred-texts.com/aor/einstein/einsci.htm)). He called it "the strongest and noblest motive for scientific research" ([Einstein 1930](https://sacred-texts.com/aor/einstein/einsci.htm)).

The compendium's reading for P187: the quality, awe at lawful harmony, survives the declension of the supernatural claims, and the exchange shows the separation in a single public act; the quality in Einstein's account is not contingent on the intervening God. The page does not carry Dewey's positive program of cultivating the quality in ordinary experience, and it makes no claim about the truth of theism.
