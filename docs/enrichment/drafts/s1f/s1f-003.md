Roundtable interview · 2004; Salzberg, Goldstein, and Kornfield in Tricycle: The Buddhist Review, with the founding drawn from Insight Meditation Society's own history and the principle's defining sutta

The Tricycle introduction places the meeting: "Returning to America, they met in 1974 at the first session of Naropa Institute, catching the great wave of interest of a generation hungry for spiritual guidance." ([Tricycle 2004](https://tricycle.org/magazine/roundtable-through-good-times-and-bad/)) The center's own history page says: "In 1975, a group of young teachers – Joseph Goldstein, Sharon Salzberg and Jack Kornfield – decided to start a meditation retreat center," opening on February 14, 1976. ([IMS History](https://www.dharma.org/about-us/ims-history/))

The principle's founding text turns on a correction. When Ānanda offers that admirable friendship is half of the holy life, the Buddha answers: "Don't say that, Ananda. Don't say that. Admirable friendship, admirable companionship, admirable camaraderie is actually the whole of the holy life." ([SN 45.2, Thanissaro](https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn45/sn45.002.than.html)) The correction turns on a claim about how practice actually works.

Goldstein, describing the working arrangement: "The three of us, and whoever else the team might be, actually make one teacher. We balance each other and bring different perspectives, which has been helpful." ([Tricycle 2004](https://tricycle.org/magazine/roundtable-through-good-times-and-bad/)) The balance holds disagreement inside it. Kornfield: "What makes IMS and Spirit Rock unique, though, is that we don't all agree and yet we work together in the same place. We use different language, and our approaches differ. Yet we all agree on the most fundamental principles quite firmly—suffering and freedom from it." ([Tricycle 2004](https://tricycle.org/magazine/roundtable-through-good-times-and-bad/))

Goldstein names the friction as a discipline: "Over the thirty years of our teaching, I think we got more tolerant of each other [all laugh]" ([Tricycle 2004](https://tricycle.org/magazine/roundtable-through-good-times-and-bad/)) Kornfield: "One of the great things about team teaching or having a collective community is that we really learn from one another. Another beautiful thing is that in our community, although we are teachers, we are still also students." ([Tricycle 2004](https://tricycle.org/magazine/roundtable-through-good-times-and-bad/)) Salzberg adds the counterweight: "Well, there have been good times and bad. But maybe we're more relaxed nowadays because we have more confidence that the dharma will actually survive us." ([Tricycle 2004](https://tricycle.org/magazine/roundtable-through-good-times-and-bad/))

The Buddha's dependence passage states the causal form: "It is in dependence on me as an admirable friend that beings subject to birth have gained release from birth, that beings subject to aging have gained release from aging, that beings subject to death have gained release from death, that beings subject to sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, & despair have gained release from sorrow, lamentation, pain, distress, & despair." ([SN 45.2, Thanissaro](https://accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/sn/sn45/sn45.002.than.html)) The roundtable does not prove that passage.

The compendium's reading for B037: the roundtable carries the principle as a relationship you can point at. Ānanda's half is wrong, the Buddha's whole is right, and Goldstein's one-teacher line is the same correction restated as observed working practice. The page makes no causal claim that the friendship produced awakening; the coverage gap is named.
