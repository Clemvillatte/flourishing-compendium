Documented case, reported in the sources · Nickel Mines, Pennsylvania, October 2006; five Amish girls killed at a one-room school, and the community's response

On the morning of October 10, 2006, a milk truck driver entered the one-room Amish school and shot ten girls; five died. Kraybill opens his essay on the event with the response: "The blood was hardly dry on the bare board floor of the Nickel Mines School when Amish parents sent words of forgiveness to the family of the killer who had executed their children." ([Kraybill, EMU News](https://emu.edu/now/news/2006/why-the-amish-forgive-tales-of-redemption-at-nickel-mines/))

The review of Amish Grace records what the families said. To Amy Roberts, the killer's widow, an Amish delegation said: "Stay in your home here," and "We have forgiven your husband, . . . and we share in your sorrow." At the grave of one of the girls, the grandfather warned the children: "We shouldn't think evil of the man who did this," and "He, too, is a child of God." ([Ruth, The Christian Century](https://www.christiancentury.org/reviews/2007-11/miracle-forgiveness)) The review records that half of the seventy people at the killer's burial were Amish. ([Ruth, The Christian Century](https://www.christiancentury.org/reviews/2007-11/miracle-forgiveness))

Kraybill's account carries the practice alongside the pain. He writes that the Amish have "an enormous capacity to absorb adversity" and that "Meals are brought to grieving families. Neighbors milk cows and care for other daily chores." ([Kraybill, EMU News](https://emu.edu/now/news/2006/why-the-amish-forgive-tales-of-redemption-at-nickel-mines/)) He also writes: "Make no mistake-death is painful. Many tears are shed." ([Kraybill, EMU News](https://emu.edu/now/news/2006/why-the-amish-forgive-tales-of-redemption-at-nickel-mines/))

Kraybill names the theology the practice comes from: "A frequent phrase in Amish life is \"forgive and forget.\"" And: "Beyond his example, the Amish try to practice Jesus' admonitions to turn the other cheek, to love one's enemies, to forgive 70 times 7, and to leave vengeance to the Lord. Retaliation and revenge are not part of their vocabulary." ([Kraybill, EMU News](https://emu.edu/now/news/2006/why-the-amish-forgive-tales-of-redemption-at-nickel-mines/)) He closes on what the response was: "Forgiveness is woven into the fabric of Amish faith. And that is why words of forgiveness were sent to the killer's family before the blood had dried on the schoolhouse floor. It was just the natural thing to do, the Amish way of doing things." ([Kraybill, EMU News](https://emu.edu/now/news/2006/why-the-amish-forgive-tales-of-redemption-at-nickel-mines/))

The review records the basis in the Amish reading of the Lord's Prayer: "If you do not forgive others, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses" ([Ruth, The Christian Century](https://www.christiancentury.org/reviews/2007-11/miracle-forgiveness)).

**Principles:**
- [[B162 — Hatred never ceases by hatred|B162 Hatred never ceases by hatred]]: the Amish response at Nickel Mines illustrates the principle in a real-world case — words of forgiveness sent to the killer's family the same day, a grandfather warning children not to think evil, and half the killer's burial attendance made up of Amish, with the pain carried alongside the response; the page does not claim the response matched the Buddha's refusal of an insult, and does not treat the community's words as evidence of what the forgiveness produced. ([Ruth, The Christian Century](https://www.christiancentury.org/reviews/2007-11/miracle-forgiveness))
