Real person, first-person account · Richard Feynman, "The Dignified Professor," in Surely You're Joking, Mr. Feynman! (1985).

After Los Alamos, Feynman took a post at Cornell and felt "burned out." He decided to treat physics the way he had as a boy: "I used to play with it." He remembered watching water from a faucet narrow, working out its curve, and not caring that someone else had already done it. So he gave himself a rule: he would play with physics "whenever I want to, without worrying about any importance whatsoever" ([Feynman, The Dignified Professor](https://www.chem.fsu.edu/chemlab/isc3523c/feyn_surely.pdf)).

Within a week, in the cafeteria, someone threw a plate into the air. Feynman noticed the red Cornell medallion going around as the plate wobbled, and began to work out the motion. He then tried to understand it "in a more fundamental way," by looking at the forces on the particles. When he showed Hans Bethe, Bethe asked what the importance was. "There's no importance whatsoever. I'm just doing it for the fun of it." Bethe's reaction did not discourage him ([Feynman](https://www.chem.fsu.edu/chemlab/isc3523c/feyn_surely.pdf)).

From wobbles he went to electron orbits in relativity, then the Dirac equation, and then quantum electrodynamics. "It was like uncorking a bottle: Everything flowed out effortlessly." He ends: "The diagrams and the whole business that I got the Nobel Prize for came from that piddling around with the wobbling plate" ([Feynman](https://www.chem.fsu.edu/chemlab/isc3523c/feyn_surely.pdf)).

The compendium sets this beside P053 and P120. The activity is its own reason in the Bethe exchange. What changes is how Feynman treats his own seriousness: he no longer asks whether the work is important, and the work becomes serious again.

Limits: this is Feynman's own account, written decades after the event, and he says he does not remember how he solved the particle problem. The claim that the Nobel-winning diagrams came from the plate is his own hindsight. A 2007 paper in the American Journal of Physics, "Feynman's wobbling plate," offers an elementary explanation for "the two-to-one ratio of wobble to spin frequencies," which puts the faster motion in the wobble ([AJP abstract](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2007AmJPh..75..240T/abstract)). A later physics paper, quoting Feynman's line that "the medallion went around faster than the wobbling," says: "the situation is just opposite: the wobbling is twice as fast as the rotation" ([Opatrný, Richterek and Opatrný](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1708.07764)). So the story's best-known detail looks reversed in Feynman's telling, and the page cannot settle how much else was shaped by hindsight.

- [[P120 — Make activities autotelic — worth doing for their own sake]]: "I'm just doing it for the fun of it."
