Biographical account · Benjamin Franklin, from the Autobiography, Part II (1784).

Franklin decided to treat his own character as a project. "It was about this time I conceived the bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection," he writes, listing thirteen virtues from temperance to humility, each with a short definition ([Franklin, Autobiography, Part II](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20203/20203-h/20203-h.htm)). He understood at once that preaching the list to himself would do nothing. What was needed was practice, structured so that nothing depended on how he happened to feel.

So he built an instrument. "I made a little book, in which I allotted a page for each of the virtues," ruled with columns for each day of the week. Each evening he examined the day and marked, by "a little black spot, every fault I found upon examination to have been committed respecting that virtue upon that day." He then cycled the virtues: "I determined to give a week's strict attention to each of the virtues successively," so that over thirteen weeks each virtue got its turn in the front of the line, and the whole course could be repeated four times a year ([Franklin, Autobiography, Part II](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20203/20203-h/20203-h.htm)).

The result he reports is a change measured across months rather than in a moment. "I was surpris'd to find myself so much fuller of faults than I had imagined; but I had the satisfaction of seeing them diminish" ([Franklin, Autobiography, Part II](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20203/20203-h/20203-h.htm)). He also reports, with the speckled-axe story of a customer too tired of turning the grindstone, that a partially polished blade is what most people settle for, and with the confession that a man who believed he had conquered pride would probably be proud of his humility. The faults never reached zero; the disposition changed anyway.

Limits: everything here is Franklin's own self-report, written decades later. His list of thirteen virtues is his own invention rather than a received catalogue, and the scheme's earnest bookkeeping is part of the joke the older Franklin tells on his younger self. The mechanism, not the perfection, is what carries the principles.

**Principles:**
- [[A108 — Practise habituation through repeated action]]: the little book is habituation as engineering, a fixed rotation of attention in which each virtue is exercised daily until the marks thin out.
- [[A042 — Recognise that virtue is a hexis, not a feeling]]: the measure of progress is a settled condition observed over months, "seeing them diminish," rather than any single day's resolution or inspiration.
