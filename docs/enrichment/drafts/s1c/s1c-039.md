Biographical account · Benjamin Franklin, from the Autobiography, Part II (1784).

Franklin decided to treat his own character as a project. "It was about this time I conceived the bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection," he writes, listing thirteen virtues from temperance to humility, each with a short definition ([Franklin, Autobiography, Part II](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20203/20203-h/20203-h.htm)). He had already concluded that conviction alone would not carry it: "the mere speculative conviction that it was our interest to be completely virtuous, was not sufficient to prevent our slipping; and that the contrary habits must be broken, and good ones acquired and established, before we can have any dependence on a steady, uniform rectitude of conduct" ([Franklin, Autobiography, Part II](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20203/20203-h/20203-h.htm)).

So he built an instrument. "I made a little book, in which I allotted a page for each of the virtues," ruled with columns for each day of the week. Each evening he examined the day and marked, by "a little black spot, every fault I found upon examination to have been committed respecting that virtue upon that day." He then cycled the virtues: "I determined to give a week's strict attention to each of the virtues successively," so that over thirteen weeks each virtue got its turn in the front of the line, and the whole course could be repeated four times a year ([Franklin, Autobiography, Part II](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20203/20203-h/20203-h.htm)).

The result he reports is a change measured across months rather than in a moment. "I was surpris'd to find myself so much fuller of faults than I had imagined; but I had the satisfaction of seeing them diminish" ([Franklin, Autobiography, Part II](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/20203/20203-h/20203-h.htm)). He also reports, with the speckled-axe story of a customer too tired of turning the grindstone, that a partially polished blade is what most people settle for, and with the confession that a man who believed he had conquered pride would probably be proud of his humility. The faults never reached zero; the disposition changed anyway.

Limits: everything here is Franklin's own self-report, written decades later. His list of thirteen virtues is his own compilation rather than a received catalogue: "In the various enumerations of the moral virtues I had met with in my reading, I found the catalogue more or less numerous," he writes. and the scheme's earnest bookkeeping is part of the joke the older Franklin tells on his younger self. The mechanism, not the perfection, is what carries the principles.

**Principles:**
- [[A108 — Practise habituation through repeated action]]: the little book is habituation as engineering — a week's strict attention to each virtue in turn, with daily examination and fault-marking, cycled until the marks thin out.
- [[A042 — Recognise that virtue is a hexis, not a feeling]]: the compendium reads the months of diminishing marks, "seeing them diminish," as its comparison for hexis — a settled condition rather than a passing state — with the speckled-axe and pride passages marking what a temporary state looks like next to it.
