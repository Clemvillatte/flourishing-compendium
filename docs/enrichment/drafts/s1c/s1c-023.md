Documented experiment · Frederic Bartlett's 1920 paper on reproducing folk stories, Psychological Laboratory, University of Cambridge.

Bartlett gave readers at Cambridge a folk tale, The War of the Ghosts, which he describes as "slightly adapted" from a translation by Franz Boas. Subjects read it twice, wrote it out after fifteen minutes, and in a serial version each person worked on the previous person's version, with no access to the original ([Bartlett 1920](https://home.iscte-iul.pt/~fgvs/Bartlett,%20Experiments.pdf)). In the tale a young man joins warriors in a canoe, hears them say that he has been hit, feels no hurt, and thinks "Oh, they are ghosts." The next morning at sunrise "something black" comes from his mouth and he is dead.

The ghosts go first. "In every one of the series of reproductions all mention of ghosts drops out almost immediately, and this in spite of the fact that ghosts appear in the original title." That leaves the painless wound unexplained. Bartlett shows one chain of nine. The warrior's line "Go back to the canoe, for you are wounded by an arrow" is gradually reshaped until the young man falls "pierced through the heart" and asks to be taken home to a named village. In the ninth version a warrior says, "You are not going to die."

The rest is rebuilt as well. Across his reproductions "boats" invariably replaced "canoes," and "something black" became "a black thing," then a "great black thing" that "flew" out of his mouth, which gave one subject "a suggestion of a soul passing from a dead body" ([Bartlett 1920](https://home.iscte-iul.pt/~fgvs/Bartlett,%20Experiments.pdf)).

Bartlett reports that at the beginning of the series "every person said that the whole business of the wound bothered him very much. At the end the narrative was simply taken without question." No one gave reasons for the change; the process was "a gradual process" toward familiarisation. He says the tendency produces "a pleasant mood of unquestioning acceptance."

The compendium reads the case beside P098. The subjects were not careless. Each one had a coherent memory in hand, and each one built on the last.

Limits: this is a short paper from one laboratory, and the chain shown is a single one, as Bartlett says: "I take one chain of reproductions only." He promises fuller analysis "later." The tale is presented here without its own community's voice, and Bartlett describes the source communities as "relatively primitive," which this compendium does not adopt. The study does not show how ordinary autobiographical memory behaves.

**Principles:**
- [[P098 — Know memory as living reconstruction]]: each reteller rebuilds the story from the last version.
