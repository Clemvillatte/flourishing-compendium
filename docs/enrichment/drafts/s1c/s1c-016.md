Real people, documented experiment · Tim Gowers's Polymath1 project, 2009, on his and Terence Tao's blogs.

On 27 January 2009 the mathematician Tim Gowers asked on his blog whether a hard problem could be solved by many people posting short comments, instead of by a few people working alone. His rule for the forum was that you "would contribute ideas even if they were undeveloped and/or likely to be wrong" ([Gowers, 27 January 2009](https://gowers.wordpress.com/2009/01/27/is-massively-collaborative-mathematics-possible/)). He thought the worry about being scooped could be turned around: because the whole record is public, "you would be afraid not to share it in case someone beat you to that particular idea." He picked a problem he cared about but that was not the "holy grail" of his field, and offered one preliminary approach to it, which he expected to hit a wall quickly.

Six weeks later, on 10 March, he wrote that "for me personally this has been one of the most exciting six weeks of my mathematical life." The project had moved away from his starting approach, and he declared: "I hereby state that I am basically sure that the problem is solved (though not in the way originally envisaged)" ([Gowers, polymath1 posts, 10 March 2009](https://gowers.wordpress.com/category/polymath1/)). One example of what the format produced is that Randall McCutcheon wrote useful comments in the language of ergodic theory, which Gowers says he understands only in a very limited way. Terence Tao translates between combinatorics and ergodic theory, so Gowers could use them through Tao.

The result was a new proof of the density Hales-Jewett theorem. On 22 October 2009 Tao reported that the project had uploaded the paper "A new proof of the density Hales-Jewett theorem" to the arXiv, with "special thanks" to Ryan O'Donnell for the lion's share of the write-up and to Gowers for running "a highly successful online mathematical experiment" ([Tao, 22 October 2009](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/2009/10/22/a-new-proof-of-the-density-hales-jewett-theorem/)).

The compendium uses it for P131 as a worked example of Dewey's point that public discussion can be improved by method. The method here is explicit: short contributions, a visible record and shared credit.

Limits: Gowers himself says the problem may have suited a smaller group, because it helped to know related results, so "the pool of potential collaborators was not as large as it might have been" ([Gowers, 10 March 2009](https://gowers.wordpress.com/category/polymath1/)). The people involved were professional mathematicians. The story shows nothing about whether the method works for less technical problems, or for disagreements about values.

**Principles:**
- [[P131 — Pool intelligence — solve problems together]]: a rule that rewards short, possibly wrong, public contributions turned a blog thread into a proof.
