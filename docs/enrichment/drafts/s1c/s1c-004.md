Daoist funeral scene with a commentary · Zhuangzi, chapter 3, in Legge's and Eno's translations.

When Lao Dan died, his friend Qin Shi went to the house, cried out three times and left. Legge gives the name as Qin Shi, and Eno as Qin Yi. The dead man's disciples asked whether this was a friend's proper way to offer condolences ([Legge, ch. 3 §5](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)). He said it was.

His reason was what he had seen inside: old people wailing as if they had lost a son, young people as if they had lost a mother. In Legge's rendering, the master had hidden his Heaven-nature from himself and allowed "an excessive indulgence of his (human) feelings" ([Legge](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)). Eno puts the fault on the mourners: they are "disobeying Heaven and turning their backs on natural feelings" ([Eno, §3.5](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/iuswrrest/api/core/bitstreams/5dc89a23-c6bf-4190-a1e4-6762dc5359cc/content)). The two readings do not agree on who is at fault. Both treat grief that nobody can stop as a departure from a natural measure.

Then Qin Shi gives the other half. Lao Dan came when it was time and left in the same order. Legge: "Quiet acquiescence in what happens at its proper time, and quietly submitting (to its ceasing) afford no occasion for grief or for joy" ([Legge](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)). Eno: "When one is at ease with time and dwells in compliance, how can sorrow or joy find a way in?" ([Eno](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/iuswrrest/api/core/bitstreams/5dc89a23-c6bf-4190-a1e4-6762dc5359cc/content)).

The scene ends on firewood. The wood is consumed, but "it passes the flame along and who can tell when it will ever be truly extinguished?" ([Eno](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/iuswrrest/api/core/bitstreams/5dc89a23-c6bf-4190-a1e4-6762dc5359cc/content)).

The compendium reads this beside D095. The Neiye names sorrow, happiness, joy and anger as what drains vitality, and Daodejing 55 offers the infant that cries all day without going hoarse. Zhuangzi supplies a person in a room. The point is not to avoid grief but to avoid being swept past what the occasion calls for. Qin Shi's three cries are a measured response.

Limits: the disciples' question is a fair one, since Qin Shi looks cold. The passage does not say he feels nothing, and it gives no after-story for the mourners. It also mixes a view of death with a view of emotion, so it may not carry over to losses that are not part of an old person's natural end. For the other side, see [[ST031 — Zhuangzi Drums on a Basin]], where Zhuangzi admits he grieved first.

**Principles:**
- [[D095 — Moderate emotion — extremes injure vitality]]: the three cries and the "no room for sorrow or joy" show moderation as a stance toward the occasion rather than as suppression.
