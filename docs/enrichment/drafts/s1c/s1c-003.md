Chan lamp-record story · Fayan Wenyi (885-958) and Luohan Guichen, from the Transmission of the Lamp (Whitfield's translation) and John C. H. Wu's retelling.

Fayan Wenyi was already a learned monk, versed in the scriptures and the Confucian classics, when he left a Chan master in Fuzhou whose teaching had not opened his mind ([John C. H. Wu, The Golden Age of Zen, ch. XIII](https://terebess.hu/zen/fayan.html)). On the road, bad weather stopped him at the temple where Luohan Guichen was abbot. The two accounts differ on the weather: Wu has a snowstorm, while Whitfield has a sudden downpour that flooded the mountain streams ([Whitfield, Transmission of the Lamp](https://terebess.hu/zen/fayan.html)).

Guichen asked where he was going. On pilgrimage, Fayan said, and what was the pilgrimage for? In Whitfield's translation the answer is "Don't know," and Guichen replies, "'Don't know' is the most intimate," at which "The master suddenly opened into awakening" ([Whitfield](https://terebess.hu/zen/fayan.html)). Wu's version has the same exchange, which he calls a cryptic remark, but Fayan is not awakened by it. The real turn comes later ([Wu](https://terebess.hu/zen/fayan.html)).

When the snow had stopped, Guichen walked him to the door and reminded him of the teaching that the three realms are nothing but Mind. Then he asked: was that stone in the courtyard inside his mind or outside? Inside, said Fayan. Guichen answered: "what makes it so necessary for you to travel with a stone on your mind?" ([Wu](https://terebess.hu/zen/fayan.html)). Fayan put his bag down and stayed. For a month he brought Guichen new views and new reasons, and each time was told the Buddha Dharma is not like that. At last he said, "I have exhausted my stock of words and reason." Guichen answered, "As regards the Buddha Dharma, everything is a present reality," and Fayan was greatly enlightened ([Wu](https://terebess.hu/zen/fayan.html)).

The compendium reads the two versions as two sides of B140. The first shows the plain admission of not knowing met as closeness. The second shows what the don't-know mind is resting from: a good doctrine carried around as if it answered a live question. The month of explanations is the practice of finding that the explaining has run out.

The caution is in the tradition itself. Wansong's commentary, as quoted from Cleary's Book of Serenity, says that on hearing this phrase people "immediately go over to just not knowing, not understanding" ([Zen Studies Podcast, ep. 255](https://zenstudiespodcast.com/medicine-of-emptiness/)). That quotation comes from a podcast transcript, not the Cleary volume. The story does not give an everyday method for resting in don't-know, and it does not say that learning is the problem.

**Principles:**
- [[B140 — Rest in don't-know mind]]: Fayan's "I don't know" is received as closeness, and the stone and the month of failed explanations show what the don't-know mind lets go of.
