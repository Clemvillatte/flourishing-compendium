Real people, documented letters · Charles Darwin, Charles Lyell, Joseph Hooker and Alfred Russel Wallace, June and July 1858.

On 18 June 1858 Darwin forwarded to Lyell an essay Wallace had sent him from the Moluccas. "I never saw a more striking coincidence." He wrote that Wallace's terms "now stand as Heads of my Chapters," and added: "So all my originality, whatever it may amount to, will be smashed" ([Darwin to Lyell, 18 June 1858](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/letter/DCP-LETT-2285.xml)). Wallace had not said he wanted it published. Darwin said he would offer to send it to any journal.

A week later, with a baby sick in the house, he wrote again, this time to ask whether he could publish a sketch of his own views honourably, now that Wallace had sent his outline. "I would far rather burn my whole book than that he or any man shd. think that I had behaved in a paltry spirit." He said he had "entire confidence in your judgment & honour," asked that Lyell's answer also go to Hooker so that he would have the opinion of "my two best & kindest friends," and ended: "This letter is miserably written" ([Darwin to Lyell, 25 June 1858](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/letter/DCP-LETT-2294.xml)).

Lyell and Hooker took the decision out of his hands. Their letter to the Linnean Society of 30 June says they were "not solely considering the relative claims to priority of himself and his friend, but the interests of science generally" ([Hooker and Lyell, 30 June 1858](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/view/letters/DCP-LETT-2299)). Wallace's essay was read with extracts from Darwin's 1844 essay and his 1857 letter to Asa Gray at an extra meeting on 1 July. Neither author was present ([Linnean Society](https://www.linnean.org/news/2018/07/01/1st-july-2018-160th-anniversary-of-the-presentation-of-on-the-tendency-of-species-to-form-varieties)). Darwin, exhausted, wrote "It is miserable in me to care at all about priority"; after the reading he said he was "much more than satisfied at what took place at Linn. Socy" ([Darwin Correspondence Project](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/letters/darwins-works-letters/origin-big-book)). In January 1859, the Correspondence Project notes, a now-lost letter from Wallace expressed satisfaction ([Darwin Correspondence Project, Origin](https://www.darwinproject.ac.uk/letters/darwins-works-letters/origin)).

The compendium sets this beside P026. The decision about who owns a discovery was made inside a small community of colleagues, and it gave the claim to the shared question.

Limits: Lyell and Hooker arranged the reading while Wallace was in the Moluccas, and his expression of satisfaction came afterwards. Darwin's own self-judgment in the letters is harsh ("trumpery"), and the pages opened do not settle whether the arrangement was fair to Wallace. The story is also a chapter in a long friendship, and the letters show one worried man on two particular days.

**Principles:**
- [[P026 — Make inquiry communal]]: a priority dispute settled by colleagues with the question of science in front of them.
