Real people, autobiographical account · Jane Addams, Hull-House, Chicago; from Twenty Years at Hull-House (1910).

One early spring day Jane Addams walked down Polk Street, worried that the Italian women of the neighbourhood so often lost their hold on their Americanised children. She saw an old woman sitting on a tenement step with a distaff, spinning thread by hand. The woman called out that when she had spun a little more yarn she would knit stockings for her goddaughter. Addams took it as a clue. Young people working in the factories could see where machine spinning came from if they saw it done by their own parents. That, she thought, might begin what Dewey defines as "a continuing reconstruction of experience" ([Addams, ch. XI](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1325/pg1325.txt)). Hull-House set up a room for neighbours with old crafts, and an "overmastering desire to reveal the humbler immigrant parents to their own children lay at the base of what has come to be called the Hull-House Labor Museum" ([Addams](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1325/pg1325.txt)).

Angelina came every Saturday evening to a cooking class in the same building where her mother spun in the museum. She always left her mother at the front door and went round to a side door, so as not to be linked with a woman in a kerchief and short petticoats. One evening visitors from the School of Education admired the spinning, and Angelina overheard that her mother was "the best stick-spindle spinner in America." She asked Addams whether it was true ([Addams](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1325/pg1325.txt)).

Addams described her mother's village, the free life there, and the hardship of exchanging it for a two-room tenement. Two things followed: "she allowed her mother to pull out of the big box under the bed the beautiful homespun garments which had been previously hidden away as uncouth; and she openly came into the Labor Museum by the same door as did her mother" ([Addams](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1325/pg1325.txt)).

In another scene some Russian women from a nearby sewing room came one Christmas week for a party that was not on, and were "greatly disappointed" by the residents' impromptu music and refreshments. Shown the Labor Museum, "the thirty sodden, tired women" were transformed. "They knew how to use the spindles," and "from having been stupidly entertained, they themselves did the entertaining" ([Addams](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/1325/pg1325.txt)).

The compendium reads both scenes beside Dewey. The craft was in the box under the bed as something the daughter was ashamed of. It became a resource once it was shown as the past of her mother's present life, and once the visitors started from what they could already do.

Limits: the account is Addams's alone, written ten years later, and Angelina's and her mother's voices are not in it. Addams admits there was something she "could not convey to the child."

**Principles:**
- [[P100 — Treat the past as resource, not prison]]: the homespun garments hidden in a box become something to be proud of once the craft is shown in its setting.
- [[P108 — Learn from where the learner is]]: the improvised entertainment missed the Russian women; the museum started from what they already knew how to do.
