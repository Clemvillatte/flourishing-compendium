Real people, documented correspondence · Gottlob Frege and Bertrand Russell, June 1902, in the van Heijenoort translations, with Russell's own later account.

Gottlob Frege spent years on a two-volume work, the Grundgesetze der Arithmetik, that aimed to build arithmetic on logic alone. In June 1902, with the second volume at the printer, he received a letter from the young Bertrand Russell. Russell began with praise: he found himself "in complete agreement with you in all essentials." Then he gave one difficulty. Let w be the predicate "to be a predicate that cannot be predicated of itself." Can w be predicated of itself? "From each answer its opposite follows" ([Russell to Frege, 16 June 1902, van Heijenoort translation](https://philarchive.org/archive/KANFFC)).

Frege answered within a week. "Your discovery of the contradiction caused me the greatest surprise and, I would almost say, consternation, since it has shaken the basis on which I intended to build arithmetic." He ended, all the same: "In any case your discovery is very remarkable and will perhaps result in a great advance in logic, unwelcome as it may seem at first glance" ([Frege to Russell, 22 June 1902](https://philarchive.org/archive/KANFFC)). In an appendix added to the volume, he opened: "Hardly anything more unwelcome can befall a scientific writer" than to have a foundation shaken "after the work is finished" ([Furth translation, as quoted](https://philarchive.org/archive/KANFFC)).

Russell described his own side in 1956. "I wanted certainty in the kind of way in which people want religious faith," he wrote, and expected to find it in mathematics. But "I found the elephant tottering, and proceeded to construct a tortoise to keep the elephant from falling." The tortoise was no more secure, and "after some twenty years of very arduous toil, I came to the conclusion that there was nothing more that I could do in the way of making mathematical knowledge indubitable." After that his attention went to the First World War and "human misery and folly" ([Russell, quoted at idealectic.com](https://idealectic.com/BRANotes.pdf)).

The Stanford Encyclopedia says Frege "responded with both dismay and admiration, and never recovered from the blow dealt to his life’s work" ([SEP, Russell's paradox](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/russell-paradox/)).

The compendium uses the pair as a concrete picture of P047. Russell names what the quest for certainty felt like, a hunger for religious-level assurance, and what it produced after twenty years: another repair. Frege shows a mind taking the failure of a certainty without hiding it, in six days and in print.

Limits: Dewey's argument in P047 is about judgments in action, not about mathematical proof, and the story does not show either man adopting it. Russell stopped; he did not conclude that certainty was a mistaken goal.

**Principles:**
- [[P047 — Give up the quest for certainty]]: Frege and Russell show a search for secure foundations that fails, one man owning the loss quickly and the other counting the repairs.
