Real person, first-person account · Barry Marshall's Nobel Lecture (2005), on the 1984 self-experiment at Fremantle Hospital, Perth.

In January 1984 Marshall tried to meet Koch's postulates, the classic test for whether a germ causes a disease, by infecting four piglets. The Helicobacter "did not take." Without an animal model he could not show that the bacterium caused disease, and his colleagues were unconvinced. He writes that "the medical understanding of ulcer disease was akin to a religion": ulcers were caused by stress, diet, smoking, alcohol and genes, and a bacterial cause "was preposterous" ([Marshall, Nobel Lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/marshall-lecture.pdf)).

He decided to be the subject. "The only person in the world at that time who could make an informed consent about the risk of swallowing the Helicobacter was me." He judged that an ethics committee might refuse and that he would then have done it anyway and been unable to publish, so he went ahead on a “don’t ask don’t tell” basis. First he asked his gastroenterology chief for a baseline endoscopy, without saying why. On the morning of the experiment he took cimetidine to lower his stomach acid, and a colleague scraped a heavily inoculated culture plate into a broth. "I drank it down in one gulp then fasted for the rest of the day."

For three days he had no symptoms. Then came an over-full feeling, several mornings of vomiting, poor sleep, tiredness and what his wife called "a putrid breath." On day ten a second endoscopy showed spiral bacteria on the first biopsy, and the next day a pathology specimen "teeming with Helicobacter and pus cells." "The experiment had succeeded – Helicobacter was a proven pathogen." Then his own correction to the legend: he did not take antibiotics on his wife's orders, as many reports say. "This was not really the case." The infection had cleared without them, and he took tinidazole only after the day-14 endoscopy ([Marshall](https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/marshall-lecture.pdf)).

The compendium uses it for P022 and P017. A belief was held on one side by an entire field and on the other by one man, and the man chose a test that could have shown him wrong. If nothing had happened, he writes, "my whole hypothesis could be wrong."

Limits: it is one subject, narrated by that subject, 21 years later. He did not tell his wife until the evening of the day he swallowed the culture. He calls it "rather selfish," and says he did not consider that he might pass the bacteria to his family. The story is not a template for skipping ethical review.

**Principles:**
- [[P022 — Verify in experience — test beliefs by living them]]: Marshall tests the hypothesis on himself, and reports what happened, including the failed piglets and the unsupported legend about his cure.
- [[P017 — Suspend dogma — practice systematic open-mindedness]]: his own phrase for the establishment view, "akin to a religion," names the dogma he was working against.
