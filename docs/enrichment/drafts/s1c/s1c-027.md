Early Buddhist discourse, a short narrative · Samyutta Nikaya 3.13, in Bhikkhu Sujato's translation.

At Savatthi, King Pasenadi of Kosala "used to eat boiled rice by the bucketful." One day, after a meal, he goes up to the Buddha huffing and puffing. The Buddha, knowing why, speaks a verse: "When a man is always mindful, knowing moderation in eating, his discomfort fades, and he ages slowly, taking care of his life" ([Sujato, SN 3.13](https://suttacentral.net/sn3.13/en/sujato)).

The student Sudassana is standing behind the king, and Pasenadi asks him for help: "Please, dear Sudassana, memorize this verse in the Buddha’s presence and recite it to me whenever I am served a meal." He sets up a daily allowance for the service. Sudassana learns the verse, and at every meal he says it ([Sujato](https://suttacentral.net/sn3.13/en/sujato)).

The change is gradual: "Then the king gradually got used to having at most a cup of rice." After some time the king's body slims right down. Stroking his limbs with his hands, he says: "In both ways the Buddha has sympathy for me: in the good of this life and the good of lives to come" ([Sujato](https://suttacentral.net/sn3.13/en/sujato)).

The compendium uses this beside B169. The mindfulness here is moved out of the king's own head and placed in a habit with another person in it. The verse is spoken when a meal is served, and it names the benefit in the king's own terms: less discomfort, slower ageing, taking care of one's life.

Limits: only a king could pay a man to recite a verse at every meal, and the sutta does not say how many weeks it took. This page used only Sujato's text. The sutta praises the slim body as a result, but the verse itself speaks of discomfort and care for life, and this page does not read it as a rule about weight.

**Principles:**
- [[B169 — Eat mindfully]]: a verse recited at the moment of serving, and a gradual change of habit.
