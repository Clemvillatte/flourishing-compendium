### Does a harmful act weigh more in the body or in the mind?

The shared question: when someone does harm, where does the weight of the wrong lie, in what the body did or in what the mind intended? The Buddhist discourse called With Upāli stages the contest as a conversation. The Jain ascetic Dīgha Tapassī reports his teacher's view:

> "The Jain Ñātika describes the physical rod as being the most blameworthy for performing bad deeds, not so much the verbal rod or the mental rod." ([MN 56, Sujato translation](https://suttacentral.net/mn56/en/sujato))

The Buddha answers with the opposite ranking:

> "I describe mental deeds as being the most blameworthy for performing bad deeds, not so much physical deeds or verbal deeds." ([MN 56, Sujato translation](https://suttacentral.net/mn56/en/sujato))

The same stress on intention appears in the Buddha's account of kamma in the Nibbedhika Sutta: "Intention, I tell you, is kamma. Intending, one does kamma by way of body, speech, & intellect." ([AN 6.63, Thanissaro translation](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an06/an06.063.than.html))

The three restraints in the definition of right action, which this page opens with, name acts of the body. The discourse does not say that the body does not matter; it says where the weight of the wrong is carried. The Jain side is known here only as the Buddhist text reports it, and the translator's note adds a caution, citing Hiralal Jain, that the Jain position is not that physical action matters while intention does not, since all three kinds of action must be intentional, and that intentional action through the body carries greater weight than through speech or thought alone (same source).

My lean, which is mine and not a finding of the sutta, is that if the translator's note is right, the two sides agree that intention is required and differ over how much weight the body's act adds to it. The sutta's own question, which of the three is heaviest, cannot be tested from these texts. The limits are that I did not read any Jain text, so the first position rests on one hostile report and one translator's note, and that I read only the opening exchange of the discourse, not Upāli's later debate with the Buddha.
