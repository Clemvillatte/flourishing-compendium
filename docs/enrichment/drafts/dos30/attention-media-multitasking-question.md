### Does heavy media multitasking cost attentional control?

The shared question: whether people who often use several media at once filter distraction less well than people who do not.

Ophir, Nass and Wagner's [2009 study in PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/pdf/10.1073/pnas.0903620106) reported that "heavy media multitaskers are more susceptible to interference from irrelevant environmental stimuli and from irrelevant representations in memory," and that they "performed worse on a test of task-switching ability."

Wiradhany and Nieuwenstein's [replication and meta-analysis](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5662702/) found that of 14 tests, "only five yielded a statistically significant effect in the direction of increased distractibility," and that their meta-analysis of 39 effect sizes found "a weak but significant association" that "turned nonsignificant after correction for small-study effects." They write that the findings "lead us to question the existence of an association between media multitasking and distractibility in laboratory tasks of information processing."

A later [three-level meta-analysis](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10648-023-09746-0.pdf) of 43 studies and 118 effect sizes reports "a moderate mean negative association between media multitasking and cognitive control," with worse inhibitory control and working memory in heavy multitaskers but "no significant difference in cognitive flexibility."

Comparison: the three results can all hold if the association is real for some control functions (inhibition, working memory) and absent for others (flexibility, filtering in lab distractor tasks). The studies are correlational, so none says that multitasking causes the difference.

My lean, which is mine: treat the original filtering claim as unsettled and the broader negative association as small and uncertain.

Limits: abstracts only; the questionnaires differ across studies; no causal design.
