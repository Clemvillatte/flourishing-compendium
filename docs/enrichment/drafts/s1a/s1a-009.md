Philosophical parable · Zhuangzi, chapter 18, section 5

A marquis welcomes a seabird with the food and music of a lavish human banquet. The bird takes neither food nor drink and dies within three days: the Zhuangzi uses the episode to ask what happens when one creature's standard of good is imposed on another. For D080, it adds a practical consequence to disputes over perspective: someone else's needs may be harmed by what we call kindness. ([Zhuangzi 18.5](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/perfect-enjoyment))

In the chapter's dialogue, Confucius worries that Yan Yuan's words will bring him into danger at the court of Qi. He tells Zi-gong about a seabird that once alighted outside Lu; the marquis went out to meet it and brought it to the ancestral temple for a banquet. ([Zhuangzi 18.5, dialogue frame and seabird episode](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/perfect-enjoyment))

The marquis has the Jiu-shao music performed and an ox, a sheep and a pig killed for the meal. The bird looks on with dim eyes and is very sad. In the English text at the Chinese Text Project, "It did not venture to eat a single bit of flesh, nor to drink a single cupful; and in three days it died." ([Zhuangzi 18.5, banquet and death](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/perfect-enjoyment))

The explanation follows immediately: "The marquis was trying to nourish the bird with what he used for himself, and not with the nourishment proper for a bird." The passage describes a different provision: forests in which to perch, sandy plains, rivers and lakes, eels and small fish, flight and undisturbed rest. ([Zhuangzi 18.5, explanation after the bird's death](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/perfect-enjoyment))

The comparison widens. Music that draws people together would send birds, beasts and fish away; fish live where a person would drown. "They are different in constitution, and therefore differ in their likes and dislikes." The passage concludes that the ancient sages did not demand the same abilities or performances from everyone, but gave approval where it was fitting. ([Zhuangzi 18.5, concluding comparisons](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/perfect-enjoyment))

Philosopher Karyn Lai reads the marquis's failure as the assumption that his views are universal. That gives this story its particular use beside D080: before declaring something good for another being, look at how that being lives and responds. The passage still judges a response by its suitability to a creature's circumstances. ([Lai, “Debating the Correct View”](https://unsworks.unsw.edu.au/bitstreams/0c9fd908-f28e-46fd-be66-e746f2eab0be/download); [Zhuangzi 18.5](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/perfect-enjoyment))
