Ise, Japan, since 690 CE · the Shikinen Sengu rebuilding cycle at Ise Jingu · a ritual narrative, opened web sources (AP News 2025; Nippon.com 2013)

Every twenty years since the first recorded rebuilding in 690, the Ise Jingu shrines have been torn down and built again, and the cycle now in progress is the 63rd ([AP News](https://www.ap.org/news-highlights/spotlights/2025/japans-most-sacred-shinto-shrine-has-been-rebuilt-every-20-years-for-more-than-a-millennium/)). A shrine that is rebuilt on purpose, forever, is a narrative object for P153's claim that the world is never finished and that a contribution belongs to an unfolding situation rather than to a completed thing.

The scale is documented ([AP News](https://www.ap.org/news-highlights/spotlights/2025/japans-most-sacred-shinto-shrine-has-been-rebuilt-every-20-years-for-more-than-a-millennium/)):

> "a massive, $390 million demolition and construction job that takes about nine years"

A Shinto priest whose family crafts miniature replicas of the shrine, Yosuke Kawanishi, described what twenty years means to the people inside the cycle ([AP News](https://www.ap.org/news-highlights/spotlights/2025/japans-most-sacred-shinto-shrine-has-been-rebuilt-every-20-years-for-more-than-a-millennium/)):

> "Twenty years from now, the older generation — our grandfathers — will likely no longer be here. And those of us who are still young now will then see our grandchildren involved in the next" version of Ise.

> "After 20 years, the shrine we are building will have deteriorated quite a bit. But instead of thinking, 'It's a shame to tear down something we worked so hard to build,' we think, 'It's been 20 years, so we want the deity to move into a beautiful, fresh, new shrine.'"

A writer who attended the 2013 rebuilding put the paradox of the practice plainly ([Nippon.com](https://www.nippon.com/en/nipponblog/m00001/)):

> "The shikinen sengū rebuild takes place every 20 years, and is believed to represent the agelessness and eternal youth of the Ise Shrine and the gods who dwell there."

> "Because they are rebuilt on a regular basis to exactly the same specifications, the shrine buildings manage to be almost literally timeless: both unimaginably old and obviously new at the same time."

My reading: P153's source text describes nature as never complete, always passing beyond itself. Ise is that description built in wood. No building at Ise is allowed to be the final one; the completed shrine is immediately the deteriorating one, and the next is already due. The unity is not any single building but the cycle that the generations pass through: Kawanishi's line about grandfathers who will not be here and grandchildren who will carry the next rebuild is the principle's conjunctive unity in family terms. His second remark shows the deliberate refusal of preservation: the hard-built thing is given up, on purpose, so that what continues is fresh. What the story does not carry is Whitehead's theory, the categoreal scheme and the technical account of process; it carries the pattern, a thing kept alive by never being finished, with each build answering the last and handing the work on.

- [[P153 — Participate in the world's becoming|153. Participate in the world's becoming]] — Ise's shrine is torn down and rebuilt every twenty years by design, the finished building refused as final, generations passing through one unending cycle of renewal
