*A canonical Buddhist sutta · the Akkosa Sutta, Saṁyutta Nikāya 7.2, in Thanissaro Bhikkhu's translation (Access to Insight)*

The Blessed One was staying near Rajagaha, in the Bamboo Grove, the Squirrels' Sanctuary, when the brahman Akkosaka Bharadvaja, "Insulter" of the Bharadvaja clan, heard that a clansman of his had gone forth into homelessness in the Buddha's presence. "Angered & displeased, he went to the Blessed One and, on arrival, insulted & cursed him with rude, harsh words."

The Buddha's reply was not a rebuke and not a lecture. He asked the brahman about hospitality:

> What do you think, brahman: Do friends & colleagues, relatives & kinsmen come to you as guests? ... And what do you think: Do you serve them with staple & non-staple foods & delicacies? ... And if they don't accept them, to whom do those foods belong?

> If they don't accept them, Master Gotama, those foods are all mine.

> In the same way, brahman, that with which you have insulted me, who is not insulting; that with which you have taunted me, who is not taunting; that with which you have berated me, who is not berating: that I don't accept from you. It's all yours, brahman. It's all yours.

Food offered and refused stays with the giver. The Buddha then names what acceptance would have looked like:

> Whoever returns insult to one who is insulting, returns taunts to one who is taunting, returns a berating to one who is berating, is said to be eating together, sharing company, with that person. But I am neither eating together nor sharing your company, brahman. It's all yours. It's all yours.

The brahman answers that "[t]he king together with his court know this of Master Gotama — 'Gotama the contemplative is an arahant' — and yet still Master Gotama gets angry," and the Buddha answers in verse: "Whence is there anger for one free from anger, tamed, living in tune... You make things worse when you flare up at someone who's angry. Whoever doesn't flare up at someone who's angry wins a battle hard to win." The exchange converts him: "Magnificent, Master Gotama!" — he asks for the going forth, is admitted, and, dwelling alone, secluded, heedful, ardent, & resolute, in no long time reaches the goal, knowing: "Birth is ended, the holy life fulfilled, the task done. There is nothing further for the sake of this world."

The compendium draws a comparison between this scene and B074, and the comparison needs its label. The sutta teaches non-retaliation toward abuse from another person; B074 concerns the status of one's own thoughts. What the two share is the structure of the second act: in both cases something arrives, an insult in the Buddha's case, a thought in the reader's, and the event's occurrence does not settle the question of assent. The Buddha does not dispute that he was cursed; he declines the delivery, and the curse stays with the speaker. A thought that arrives loudly ("I am a failure") has likewise occurred, and the practice B074 points to is seeing that occurrence is not verdict.
