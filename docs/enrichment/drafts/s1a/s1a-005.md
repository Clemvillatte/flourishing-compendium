*India, 5th century BCE · the Akkosa Sutta, Saṁyutta Nikāya 7.2, in Thanissaro Bhikkhu's translation*

A brahmin named Akkosaka ("Insulter") hears that a clansman of his has gone forth under the Buddha, is angered, and comes to where the Buddha is wandering in the Kosalan countryside. He "insulted & cursed him with rude, harsh words." The Buddha's reply is not a rebuke and not a lecture. He asks the brahmin about hospitality:

> What do you think, brahmin? Do you give food, garnish, & illumination to guests? — Yes, Master Gotama. — And when they don't accept them, to whom do those foods belong? — Those foods are all mine. — In the same way, brahman, that you have insulted & cursed me... that I don't accept from you. It's all yours, brahman. It's all yours.

The exchange takes seconds and the logic is complete. Food offered and refused stays with the giver. An insult offered and declined stays with the speaker. The words still happened, the rudeness still landed in the air, and the Buddha does not dispute that he was cursed. What he refuses is the second act, the act of reception. He spells out what acceptance would look like:

> If you return insult for insult, or strike back at those who strike you, or return anger for those who speak harsh words to you, that I don't accept from you.

The sutta closes with a verse that names the trap on both sides: "You make things worse when you flare up at someone who's angry. Whoever doesn't flare up at someone who's angry wins a battle hard to win."

The compendium reads the sutta as B074 performed in real time. The principle says thoughts are not facts: they are events that arise in awareness, and treating them as verdicts is a separate act of believing them. The brahmin's curses are the loudest possible thoughts arriving in another man's awareness, insistent, insulting, felt, and the Buddha's move is to see them as an offering that can be declined. Nothing is suppressed; the words are fully registered and fully refused. The practice for an ordinary reader is the same move at lower volume: the thought "I am a failure" arrives like Akkosaka, and the question is not whether the sound occurred but whether to sign for the delivery. What the story does not carry is the toolbox side of B074, the five practices of the Vitakkasanthana Sutta for working thoughts once they are seen; that needs its own stories.

- [[B074 — Thoughts are not facts|B074 Thoughts are not facts]] — the Buddha declines the insult as an offered event; acceptance is a separate act.
