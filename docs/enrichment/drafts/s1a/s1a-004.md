*Japan · five-story pagodas; the structural account from NIPPONIA, the Japanese government's culture magazine (June 2005)*

Japan is earthquake country, and Japan's old temples hold five-story wooden towers that have outlived more than a millennium of shaking. The structural engineer Ueda Atsushi explained the secret in a government culture-magazine article, and the secret is not strength. The material itself refuses to be rigid:

> When wood is subjected to a force it may bend and warp, but it does not break easily. And when the force is removed the wood returns to its former shape.

The joints are built on the same logic. "The timbers are fastened together, with hardly a nail at all, by inserting carved thinner and narrower ends into slots. So if the ground begins to shake, the wood surfaces in these joints twist and rub against each other." There are "about a thousand large mortise joints in a five-story pagoda, making the entire structure practically as flexible as *konnyaku*," the wobbling jelly of Japanese cooking.

The floors of a pagoda are not one tower but five stacked boxes, each free to wobble on its own; the article's author calls the arrangement his "Bowls of Columbus," upside-down bowls held on a chopstick. The chopstick is the *shinbashira*, the thick central pillar:

> If one of the box layers tries to slide off to the side, the sturdy pillar returns it to center. During an earthquake, the central pillar will sway a little, like an upside down pendulum, counteracting the seismic force.

All three- and five-storied pagodas in Japan have such a pillar, a feature very few surviving wooden towers in China or Korea share. The whole design, the article concludes, "resembles a willow tree in the way it sways and withstands an earthquake," and its last paragraph makes the contrast explicit:

> Older, stone buildings were made sturdy and unyielding to withstand earthquakes, like an oak tree. New ones are designed to be flexible, to sway just enough to negate seismic forces, like a willow tree — and like a five-story pagoda.

Modern Japanese towers still borrow the pagoda's tricks, flexible dampers and counter-swaying mechanisms, to ride out the same shocks.

The compendium reads the pagoda as D156 built in wood. The principle quotes Laozi's Daodejing 76: the living body is "supple and weak," the dead body "firm and strong," for "the soft and yielding is the disciple of life" and the stiff and unbending "the disciple of death." The pagoda is the principle as engineering: the rigid structure transfers the earthquake's full force until it cracks; the flexible one absorbs, moves, and returns to center, and it is still standing after a thousand years of ground that will not stop moving. What the story does not carry is the psychological side of the principle, the loosening of fixed views and self-images; that needs its own stories.
