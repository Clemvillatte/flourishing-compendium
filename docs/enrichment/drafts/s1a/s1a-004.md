*Japan · five-story pagodas; the structural account from NIPPONIA (web-japan.org), the Japanese government's culture magazine (June 15, 2005), written by the architect Ueda Atsushi*

Japan is earthquake country, and its temples hold five-story wooden towers. The five-storied pagoda at Hōryū-ji Temple stands among buildings the article's caption calls "the oldest wooden structures in the world." The article notes "there is almost no record of a pagoda being knocked over" in an earthquake, and that the 1995 Great Hanshin Awaji Earthquake "brought down many tall modern buildings in the Kobe area, but not one of the 13 three-story pagodas in surrounding Hyogo Prefecture was damaged." In Ueda Atsushi's account, the stability comes from complementary mechanisms: the parts flex, and the central pillar restrains them.

The first is the material. Every structural part is wood, which, as Ueda describes it, may bend and warp under force without breaking and returns to its former shape when the force is removed, absorbing seismic stresses. The joints work with that flexibility: timbers are fastened together, in his description, "with hardly a nail at all," by carved ends fitted into slots, so that when the ground shakes the wood surfaces in the joints twist and rub against each other, which in his account keeps the energy from traveling far up the tower. There are "about a thousand large mortise joints in a five-story pagoda, making the entire structure practically as flexible as *konnyaku*."

The next mechanism is the layered structure: the pagoda is basically a number of box-like structures laid one on top of another, and when the ground shakes, each box layer sways slowly and independently of the others. A carpenter who watched a pavilion through a major earthquake long ago reported that when the bottommost box layer swayed to the left, the one above it swayed to the right, and so on: "The tower was doing a kind of snake dance!"

The final mechanism is the thick central pillar, the *shinbashira*. If one of the box layers tries to slide off to the side, in Ueda's description the sturdy pillar returns it to center, and during an earthquake the central pillar sways a little, like an upside-down pendulum, counteracting the seismic force. All three- and five-storied pagodas in Japan have such a pillar, his article states, while very few similar wooden towers still standing in China or the Republic of Korea have one. The whole design, he concludes, resembles a willow tree in the way it sways and withstands an earthquake, and its last paragraph draws the contrast:

> Older, stone buildings were made sturdy and unyielding to withstand earthquakes, like an oak tree. New ones are designed to be flexible, to sway just enough to negate seismic forces, like a willow tree — and like a five-story pagoda."

(Ueda Atsushi, NIPPONIA, 2005.)

Read alongside D156, Ueda's account illustrates the principle. The principle quotes Laozi's Daodejing 76: "Man at his birth is supple and weak; at his death, firm and strong. (So it is with) all things. Trees and plants, in their early growth, are soft and brittle; at their death, dry and withered." The pagoda lives on the supple side of that contrast: it moves, snake-dances, and the pillar keeps the movement from becoming collapse.
