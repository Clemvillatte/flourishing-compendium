*Japan · five-story pagodas; the structural account from NIPPONIA, the Japanese government's culture magazine (June 2005)*

Japan is earthquake country, and its temples hold five-story wooden towers built on a design that, in the account of a government culture-magazine article by Ueda Atsushi, "has existed in Japan for well over a thousand years," with "Five-story pagodas still stand at some old temples in Japan today." What keeps them standing is a combination, not a single secret: the parts flex, and the central pillar restrains them.

The material itself refuses to be rigid:

> When wood is subjected to a force it may bend and warp, but it does not break easily. And when the force is removed the wood returns to its former shape.

The joints are built on the same logic. "The timbers are fastened together, with hardly a nail at all, by inserting carved thinner and narrower ends into slots. So if the ground begins to shake, the wood surfaces in these joints twist and rub against each other." There are "about a thousand large mortise joints in a five-story pagoda, making the entire structure practically as flexible as *konnyaku*," the wobbling jelly of Japanese cooking.

The floors of a pagoda are not one tower but five stacked boxes, each free to wobble on its own; the article's author calls the arrangement his "Bowls of Columbus," upside-down bowls held on a chopstick. The chopstick is the *shinbashira*, the thick central pillar, and this is where the design's other half lives:

> If one of the box layers tries to slide off to the side, the sturdy pillar returns it to center. During an earthquake, the central pillar will sway a little, like an upside down pendulum, counteracting the seismic force.

All three- and five-storied pagodas in Japan have such a pillar, a feature very few surviving wooden towers in China or Korea share. The whole design, the article concludes, "resembles a willow tree in the way it sways and withstands an earthquake," and its last paragraph contrasts it with the older way of building:

> Older, stone buildings were made sturdy and unyielding to withstand earthquakes, like an oak tree. New ones are designed to be flexible, to sway just enough to negate seismic forces, like a willow tree — and like a five-story pagoda.

Modern Japanese towers, the same paragraph notes, still borrow the pagoda's tricks, flexible dampers and counter-swaying mechanisms.

The compendium draws on Ueda's account as an analogy for D156, and the analogy runs through the article's own closing contrast. The principle quotes Laozi's Daodejing 76: the living body is "supple and weak," the dead body "firm and strong," for "the soft and yielding is the disciple of life" and the stiff and unbending "the disciple of death." The pagoda is the principle as engineering: it does not resist the shaking by standing rigid against it; it moves, and the pillar keeps the movement from becoming collapse. What the story does not carry is the psychological side of the principle, the loosening of fixed views and self-images; the coverage matrix names it for another story.
