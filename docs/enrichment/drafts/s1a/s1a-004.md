*Japan · five-story pagodas; the structural account from NIPPONIA (web-japan.org), the Japanese government's culture magazine (June 15, 2005), written by the architect Ueda Atsushi*

Japan is earthquake country, and its temples hold five-story wooden towers. The five-storied pagoda at Hōryū-ji Temple stands among buildings the article's caption calls "the oldest wooden structures in the world." The article notes "there is almost no record of a pagoda being knocked over" in an earthquake, and that the 1995 Great Hanshin Awaji Earthquake "brought down many tall modern buildings in the Kobe area, but not one of the 13 three-story pagodas in surrounding Hyogo Prefecture was damaged." Ueda's account runs through five "secrets," and they all work the same way: the parts flex, and the central pillar restrains them.

The first secret is the material: "Every structural part of the five-story pagoda is made of wood."

> When wood is subjected to a force it may bend and warp, but it does not break easily. And when the force is removed the wood returns to its former shape.

Because it is flexible, "it can absorb seismic stresses." The second secret works the joints. "The timbers are fastened together, with hardly a nail at all, by inserting carved thinner and narrower ends into slots. So if the ground begins to shake, the wood surfaces in these joints twist and rub against each other — which, in his account, "helps prevent the seismic energy from traveling far up the tower." There are "about a thousand large mortise joints in a five-story pagoda, making the entire structure practically as flexible as *konnyaku*.

The third secret is the layered structure: the pagoda is "basically a number of box-like structures laid one on top of another," and "when the ground shakes, each of these box layers sways slowly and independently of the others." A carpenter who watched a pavilion through a major earthquake long ago reported that "when the bottommost box layer swayed to the left, the one above it swayed to the right, the one above that one to the left, and so on. The tower was doing a kind of snake dance!"

The fifth secret is the thick central pillar, the *shinbashira*:

> If one of the box layers tries to slide off to the side, the sturdy pillar returns it to center. During an earthquake, the central pillar will sway a little, like an upside down pendulum, counteracting the seismic force.

All three- and five-storied pagodas in Japan have such a pillar, while "very few similar wooden towers still standing in China or the Republic of Korea have one." The whole design, the article concludes, "resembles a willow tree in the way it sways and withstands an earthquake," and its last paragraph draws the contrast: "Older, stone buildings were made sturdy and unyielding to withstand earthquakes, like an oak tree. New ones are designed to be flexible, to sway just enough to negate seismic forces, like a willow tree — and like a five-story pagoda."

Read alongside D156, Ueda's account illustrates the principle. The principle quotes Laozi's Daodejing 76: "Man at his birth is supple and weak; at his death, firm and strong. (So it is with) all things. Trees and plants, in their early growth, are soft and brittle; at their death, dry and withered." The pagoda lives on the supple side of that contrast: it moves, snake-dances, and the pillar keeps the movement from becoming collapse.
