*A canonical Buddhist parable · the Tittha Sutta, Udana 6.4, in Thanissaro Bhikkhu's translation*

The Buddha tells of a king in Sāvatthī who orders a man to "gather together all the people in Sāvatthī who have been blind from birth." The man rounds them up, and the king gives an instruction that sets the story in motion: "Very well then, I say, show the blind people an elephant." To some of them the man shows the elephant's head, to some the ear, the tusk, the trunk, the body, the foot, the hindquarters, the tail, and to some the tuft at the end of the tail, telling each group, "This, blind people, is what an elephant is like."

Then the king asks them what an elephant is, and each group answers from what its hands actually held:

> The blind people who had been shown the elephant's head said, "The elephant, your majesty, is just like a jar."
> Those who had been shown the elephant's ear said, "The elephant, your majesty, is just like a winnowing basket."
> Those who had been shown the elephant's tusk said, "The elephant, your majesty, is just like plowshare."
> Those who had been shown the elephant's trunk said, "The elephant, your majesty, is just like the pole of a plow."
> Those who had been shown the elephant's body said, "The elephant, your majesty, is just like a granary."

And the rest answer from the foot, the hindquarters, the tail, the tuft: a post, a mortar, a pestle, a broom. Each group encounters a real part, then makes an inadequate claim about the whole. And then comes the sentence that makes the parable a story:

> Saying, "The elephant is like this, it's not like that. The elephant's not like that, it's like this," they struck one another with their fists. That gratified the king.

The Buddha's coda names the quarrel as the point: the wanderers of other sects are "blind & eyeless," and "they keep on arguing, quarreling, & disputing, wounding one another with weapons of the mouth, saying, 'The Dhamma is like this, it's not like that.'" His verse ends with a four-word diagnosis: "people seeing one side."

The compendium draws on the parable as an illustration of D080, and the comparison is drawn by this page. The principle dissolves rigid right and wrong: the qiwu insight is that a partial truth gripped as the whole turns into a fist. The parable stages exactly that turn, and the sutta's own frame keeps it from sliding into a relativism it does not teach: the Buddha's coda still speaks of the Dhamma and of sectarians who are wrong about it, so the point is not that all claims are equally true, but that fighting over a part as though it were the whole is a category of suffering with a cure. The elephant is what it is regardless of the quarrel; the king ordered the gathering, and it was his man who showed each group its part.
