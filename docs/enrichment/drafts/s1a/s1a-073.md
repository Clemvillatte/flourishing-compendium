Biographical account · Vedran Smailović, principal cellist of the Sarajevo Opera Orchestra, via a Sarajevo city account of the 1992 episode.

On 27 May 1992 a mortar shell fell on the queue outside a bakery on Vase Miskina Street in Sarajevo and killed twenty-two people ([Explore Sarajevo](https://exploresarajevo.com/stories/2026/09/sarajevo-cellist-smailovic-albinoni/)). The next afternoon Smailović "in full evening dress carried his instrument and a stool to the crater, sat down, and began to play. He came back at the same time the following day. And the day after. Twenty-two consecutive days, one for each of the dead" ([Explore Sarajevo](https://exploresarajevo.com/stories/2026/09/sarajevo-cellist-smailovic-albinoni/)). The piece was the one the world knows as Albinoni's Adagio in G minor ([Explore Sarajevo](https://exploresarajevo.com/stories/2026/09/sarajevo-cellist-smailovic-albinoni/)).

The reason the article reports, from his interviews with the BBC and The Irish Times, is a plain one: "he did not play in the crater to make a statement about war. He played to keep himself from despairing, and because, as he put it in a 2002 interview, 'a cellist plays. That is what a cellist does'" ([Explore Sarajevo](https://exploresarajevo.com/stories/2026/09/sarajevo-cellist-smailovic-albinoni/)).

The connection with B156 is offered as a cross-tradition illustration, with no historical link claimed between this cellist and the engaged-Buddhist tradition the page takes its name from. The page's argument is that a practice that only works on the mind leaves the suffering it faces in place; this story shows a man taking his practice into the crater itself, twenty-two afternoons in the open, in the clothes of his profession. I read the daily return as that engagement in its smallest and most exact form: the music placed where the deaths happened. The draft claims only the engaged response; the page's fuller structure, calm enough to be useful in the room where the work happens, is not carried here and remains the principle page's own.
