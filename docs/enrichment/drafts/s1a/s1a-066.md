Traditional Daoist parable · from the Huainanzi, chapter 18, "In the World of Man" (2nd century BCE), via the Wikipedia article's printed Larre translation.

A man near the border loses his horse for no reason, and everyone feels sorry for him; his father asks, "Who knows if that won't bring you good luck?" Months later the horse returns with a group of noble barbarian horses, and everyone congratulates him; his father asks, "Who knows if that won't bring you bad luck?" The house is now rich in horses, the son rides with joy, falls, and breaks his leg; everyone feels sorry for him, and his father asks again, "Who knows if that won't bring you good luck?" A year later the barbarians invade, the adult men string up their bows and go to battle, and nine out of ten border residents are killed, except the son, because of his broken leg; father and son both survive ([Wikipedia, Larre et al. translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_old_man_lost_his_horse)). The parable opens with a line that frames it: "Good luck and bad luck create each other and it is difficult to foresee their change" ([Wikipedia, Larre et al. translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_old_man_lost_his_horse)).

The connection with D110 is the father's three replies doing the holding. The neighbours arrive with the verdict ready, first sorry, then glad, then sorry again, and each time he declines to let the verdict settle. He is not predicting; the story never says the father knows what comes next. He keeps each event lighter than the reaction around it, and the closing line names why: bad luck brings good luck and good luck brings bad luck, "without end and nobody can estimate it" ([Wikipedia, Larre et al. translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_old_man_lost_his_horse)). The story survives as the chengyu Sai Weng Shi Ma, "the old man lost his horse, but it all turned out for the best," which the tradition uses to mean, how could one know it was not good fortune ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_old_man_lost_his_horse)).

Not carried: the page's childlike vitality and opposites co-creating facets belong to their own principles; the parable carries the refusal to settle the verdict, and stops there.

**Principles:**
- [[D110 — Hold events lightly — the horse story]]: the father's three replies to the neighbours' three verdicts, and the parable's closing line that the changes run without end.
