Autobiographical narrative · Henri Poincare, “Mathematical Creation” (1908)

Henri Poincare describes a mathematical investigation repeatedly interrupted by other activities. Ideas arrive while he is travelling or walking, but he returns to deliberate work to check them and finish his memoir. As a comparison with D039, his account gives intervals away from direct effort a place inside the work, before its completion. ([Poincare, pp. 88-90](https://www.paradise.caltech.edu/ist4/lectures/Poincare_Reflections.pdf))

For fifteen days, he tries to prove that a certain kind of mathematical function cannot exist. He sits at his table for an hour or two each day and gets nowhere. Then a sleepless night brings ideas that lead him to establish the existence of such functions, later called Fuchsian functions; he writes out the result the next morning and develops it further. ([Poincare, pp. 88-89](https://www.paradise.caltech.edu/ist4/lectures/Poincare_Reflections.pdf))

A geological excursion takes him away from Caen, and the travel makes him forget his mathematics. At Coutances, as he steps into an omnibus, he suddenly recognises a connection between transformations he has been using and non-Euclidean geometry. He feels certain, but has no time to verify it; he takes his seat and resumes a conversation. Back in Caen, he checks the result. ([Poincare, p. 89](https://www.paradise.caltech.edu/ist4/lectures/Poincare_Reflections.pdf))

Later, arithmetic questions resist his efforts. He writes, "Disgusted with my failure, I went to spend a few days at the seaside, and thought of something else." Walking on a bluff one morning, he finds another connection to non-Euclidean geometry. On his return, he works through its consequences and makes a systematic attempt to extend the theory. ([Poincare, p. 89](https://www.paradise.caltech.edu/ist4/lectures/Poincare_Reflections.pdf))

One difficulty remains. Military service interrupts the investigation again, and a solution comes to him while he is walking along a street. He leaves detailed examination until after his service; then he arranges the elements and writes the final memoir. The story reaches its outcome at the desk, after the walks and journeys. ([Poincare, p. 89](https://www.paradise.caltech.edu/ist4/lectures/Poincare_Reflections.pdf))

Poincare interprets these experiences as evidence of unconscious work during apparent rest. He stresses both the conscious effort beforehand and the work afterwards: "above all is verification necessary." He also warns that a vivid feeling of certainty can deceive, and ends his wider account by calling his proposed explanations hypothetical. ([Poincare, pp. 90 and 94](https://www.paradise.caltech.edu/ist4/lectures/Poincare_Reflections.pdf))

For D039, the useful comparison is with leaving space before a task is finished. Poincare's intervals included other demanding activities, so this account concerns release from one problem rather than bodily inactivity. It records what happened in his investigation; it gives no guarantee that taking a break will produce an answer. ([Poincare, pp. 89-90](https://www.paradise.caltech.edu/ist4/lectures/Poincare_Reflections.pdf))
