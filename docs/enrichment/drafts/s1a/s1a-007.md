*California, early 1970s · W. Timothy Gallwey, tennis coach, later author of The Inner Game of Tennis (1974)*

Tim Gallwey, who had captained the tennis team at Harvard, was coaching in California when a student arrived with a stuck backhand. "The guy had a backhand problem," Gallwey remembered four decades later. "This time, instead of telling him what he was doing wrong, I let him swing away at it for a while. Pretty soon I noticed that his backhand was getting better without any instruction at all."

That was the crack in the whole idea of coaching. The improvement was happening when Gallwey stopped talking, which meant something inside the student had been doing the work all along, and something else had been getting in its way. In the book he built from the discovery, Gallwey names the two parties in the inner argument:

> Obviously, the "I" and the "myself" are separate entities or there would be no conversation, so one could say that within each player there are two "selves." One, the "I," seems to give instructions; the other, "myself," seems to perform the action. Then "I" returns with an evaluation of the action. For clarity let's call the "teller" Self 1 and the "doer" Self 2.

In the model, Self 1 is the voice that instructs, evaluates, and worries about the score while the ball is in the air; Self 2 is the doer that performs the action. Gallwey's problem was to keep the teller from strangling the doer, and his solution became his most famous drill:

> "Say the word bounce out loud the instant you see the ball hit the court and the word hit the instant the ball makes contact with the racket—either racket."

The exercise coordinates the words with the events, and that is the whole job it gives the teller. "It's hard to be saying 'bounce-hit' and at the same time overinstructing yourself, trying too hard or worrying about the score," Gallwey explains; as the tennis.com account puts it, the drill serves "to keep Self 1 busy so it couldn't distract Self 2 with other thoughts," while the hearing of the rhythm "seemed to hold the attention for longer periods of time." In an interview four decades on he gave the method's summary with its own conditions attached: "Before you try to change something, become more aware of the way it is. Awareness itself will cause the change if you're clear about the desired outcomes, and you don't try too hard."

The compendium reads the story as P182 caught in the act, and the reading runs through Gallwey's model rather than through any claim about how brains work. The principle says self-preoccupation inhibits performance: when attention folds back on itself as instruction and evaluation, the machinery clamps. Gallwey's discovery is what unclamping looks like in practice, give the chatterer a small job coordinated with what is actually happening ("bounce," "hit"), and the doer, attended to without being supervised, plays. The trial runs in the mind ("you always choke here") are not information about the stroke; in the model they are the interference itself.
