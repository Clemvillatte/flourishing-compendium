New York and Boston, 1888-1903 · John Macy's supplementary account in Helen Keller, The Story of My Life · a biographical account, Project Gutenberg raw text

Helen Keller could not hear music, and the account of how she enjoyed it anyway is P105's claim, that time is felt in the body, told at full stretch. The rhythm reached her as vibration, through things she touched and through the air itself ([The Story of My Life](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2397/pg2397.txt)).

The account of her musical experience ([The Story of My Life](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2397/pg2397.txt)):

> "Her enjoyment of music, however, is very genuine, for she has a tactile recognition of sound when the waves of air beat against her."

> "Part of her experience of the rhythm of music comes, no doubt, from the vibration of solid objects which she is touching: the floor, or, what is more evident, the case of the piano, on which her hand rests. But she seems to feel the pulsation of the air itself."

The organ carries the same point further ([The Story of My Life](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2397/pg2397.txt)):

> "When the organ was played for her in St. Bartholomew's, the whole building shook with the great pedal notes, but that does not altogether account for what she felt and enjoyed. The vibration of the air as the organ notes swelled made her sway in answer."

And another voice put her in touch with rhythm directly ([The Story of My Life](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2397/pg2397.txt)):

> "Sometimes she puts her hand on a singer's throat to feel the muscular thrill and contraction, and from this she gets genuine pleasure."

My reading: P105's plain words ask whether you are hurrying, holding still, or moving without taking in what is happening, and they treat tempo as something you can examine rather than assume. Keller's case is that examination run at the limit. Deprived of hearing, she received beat and pulsation through touch: the piano's case under her hand, the floor, the throat of a singer, the air beating against her. The organ at St. Bartholomew's is the clearest image: the notes swell, and she sways in answer, the tempo of the music becoming the tempo of her body. The account is honest about the limits of the report, saying that no one knows just what her sensations are, and it refuses the magazine claim that she had an intelligent appreciation of composers; what it stands on is narrower and firmer, that rhythm was available to her bodily and that her body answered it. The story does not carry Dewey's theory of experience as intakings and outgivings, which the principle page supplies, nor the rehearsal exercise; it carries the felt fact.
