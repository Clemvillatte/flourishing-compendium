Traditional Daoist parable · from Zhuangzi, chapter 2, "The Adjustment of Controversies", in Legge's translation as printed on the Chinese Text Project.

A keeper of monkeys tells them: "In the morning I will give you three (measures) and in the evening four." This makes them all angry, and he says: "Very well. In the morning I will give you four and in the evening three." The monkeys were all pleased. The two proposals were substantially the same, "but the result of the one was to make the creatures angry, and of the other to make them pleased - an illustration of the point I am insisting on" ([Zhuangzi 2, Legge, ctext](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/adjustment-of-controversies)).

The connection with D124 is the keeper doing what the page asks before opposing: he takes the monkeys' side of the same total and lets it stand. Nothing changes but the arrangement, and the anger changes completely. The chapter's frame names what the monkeys' anger and pleasure both miss: "When we toil our spirits and intelligence, obstinately determined (to establish our own view), and do not know the agreement (which underlies it and the views of others), we have what is called 'In the morning three'" ([Zhuangzi 2, Legge, ctext](https://ctext.org/zhuangzi/adjustment-of-controversies)). I read the keeper's re-arrangement as the small, workable version of that knowing: before fighting a position, find the form of it the other side can live with, and see that the substance has not moved.

Not carried: the page's exercise of stating the opposing position's strongest point in your own words; the parable keeps to the acorns.

**Principles:**
- [[D124 — Look for the right in the other side]]: the monkey keeper's second arrangement as the other side's reading accommodated, with the chapter's own line that the two proposals were substantially the same.
