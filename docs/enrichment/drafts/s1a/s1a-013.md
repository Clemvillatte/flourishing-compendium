Autobiographical nature narrative · Sierra Nevada, December 1874

John Muir spends hours near the top of a tree during a Sierra windstorm, moving with it as it bends and circles. He chooses the tree carefully and trusts its elasticity; elsewhere in the forest, trees are falling. Beside D156, this is a physical image of remaining rooted while yielding under pressure, with survival depending on more than suppleness alone. ([Muir, chapter 10](https://vault.sierraclub.org/john_muir_exhibit/writings/the_mountains_of_california/chapter_10.aspx))

Muir is staying with a friend in a tributary valley of the Yuba River when the storm begins. He goes into the woods early in the morning. Pine sprays fly through the sunlight, and for hours he hears a tree fall every two or three minutes: some uproot in the wet ground, while others break where fire has weakened them. ([Muir, the December 1874 outing](https://vault.sierraclub.org/john_muir_exhibit/writings/the_mountains_of_california/chapter_10.aspx))

By midday he reaches a ridge and decides to climb for a wider view and to hear the topmost needles. The choice matters. One tree looks liable to fall or be struck by another; a second has no accessible lower branches. He settles on the tallest of a close-growing group of what he calls Douglas Spruces, each about a hundred feet high. He judges that one is unlikely to fall without the rest. ([Muir, tree selection](https://vault.sierraclub.org/john_muir_exhibit/writings/the_mountains_of_california/chapter_10.aspx))

He climbs to the top, where the slender growth swishes, bends and turns in the wind. Muir says he "clung with muscles firm braced" as the treetop swept through an arc of twenty to thirty degrees. He had seen the same species bend almost to the ground under snow without breaking, and writes, "I felt sure of its elastic temper." That is his judgement of this tree, in these conditions. ([Muir, climb and widest sweeps](https://vault.sierraclub.org/john_muir_exhibit/writings/the_mountains_of_california/chapter_10.aspx))

He stays aloft for hours, sometimes closing his eyes to listen or notice the scents passing through the woods. The moving trees make him think of them as travellers, too. When the storm begins to weaken, he climbs down and walks through the calming forest in the setting sun. ([Muir, hours aloft and descent](https://vault.sierraclub.org/john_muir_exhibit/writings/the_mountains_of_california/chapter_10.aspx))

The useful image for D156 is the moving tree with its hold intact. Muir's account also keeps that image from becoming an absolute rule: the same chapter describes rigid junipers as storm survivors, and names anchorage, nearby trees and previous damage among the conditions that matter. Extending this particular tree's movement to an opinion or a plan remains a comparison for the reader to consider. ([Muir, alpine trees, forest damage and tree selection](https://vault.sierraclub.org/john_muir_exhibit/writings/the_mountains_of_california/chapter_10.aspx))
