Bangladesh, 1974-1983 · a first-person account of a revision of ends, told in Muhammad Yunus's Nobel Peace Prize lecture (2006)

In 1974, an economics professor in Bangladesh found the theories he taught feeling empty in front of him. A famine had begun, and the classroom could not be turned toward it. The end he set himself shrank to what one person could act on that day, and the sequence that followed, a list, then twenty-seven dollars, then a guarantee, then a bank, is a public demonstration of what P041 calls an end-in-view: a target set up to give present action direction, and moved when the action produces information the target did not expect. ([Yunus, Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/2006/yunus/lecture/))

Yunus tells it this way ([Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/2006/yunus/lecture/)):

> "In 1974, I found it difficult to teach elegant theories of economics in the university classroom, in the backdrop of a terrible famine in Bangladesh."

> "Suddenly, I felt the emptiness of those theories in the face of crushing hunger and poverty. I wanted to do something immediate to help people around me, even if it was just one human being, to get through another day with a little more ease."

The new end was deliberately small, sized to one day of action. The village next door then gave him information his end had not expected ([Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/2006/yunus/lecture/)):

> "I was shocked to discover a woman in the village, borrowing less than a dollar from the money-lender, on the condition that he would have the exclusive right to buy all she produces at the price he decides. This, to me, was a way of recruiting slave labor."

He made a list of the money-lender's victims. It came to 42 people and a total of 27 dollars. He offered the 27 dollars from his own pocket, and the reaction prompted him to expand the effort ([Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/2006/yunus/lecture/)):

> "If I could make so many people so happy with such a tiny amount of money, why not do more of it?"

The rest is the same pattern. The campus bank refused ("The bank said that the poor were not creditworthy"), so he tried a new means, becoming guarantor himself, and the means produced new information: "I was stunned by the result. The poor paid back their loans, on time, every time!" Repayment worked, the existing banks still would not expand the program, and the pivot came at the level of the institution itself: "That was when I decided to create a separate bank for the poor, and in 1983, I finally succeeded in doing that. I named it Grameen Bank or Village bank." In his 2006 lecture, Yunus reported that Grameen Bank gave loans "to nearly 7.0 million poor people, 97 per cent of whom are women, in 73,000 villages in Bangladesh" ([Nobel lecture](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/peace/2006/yunus/lecture/)).

My reading: Dewey's line about targets fits the sequence. "Men do not shoot because targets exist, but they set up targets in order that throwing and shooting may be more effective and significant" ([Dewey, Human Nature and Conduct](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/41386/41386-0.txt)). Yunus's broad concern with poverty remained; what experience supplied was increasingly workable immediate objectives: help nearby borrowers, secure access to bank credit, and, when the existing banks would not support expansion, establish a separate institution. These provisional aims directed subsequent action and changed as obstacles and results became apparent, which is the connection to P041. Each objective was equipment for the next stretch of present action, what Dewey calls a redirecting pivot. What the story does not carry is the inner face of goal revision, the felt disengagement from a cherished plan; the lecture reports public moves, not deliberation.

**Principles:**
- [[P041 — Set ends-in-view, and revise them|41. Set ends-in-view, and revise them]] — the sequence shows ends being sized to present action and redirected by what each action returns, from the classroom famine to the founding of Grameen Bank
