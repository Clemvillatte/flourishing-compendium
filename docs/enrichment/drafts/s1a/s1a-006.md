*Akron, Ohio, 1935–1939 · the founding of Alcoholics Anonymous by Bill Wilson and Dr. Bob Smith; program text from the Big Book (1939)*

In May 1935 a newly sober New York stockbroker named Bill Wilson found himself in Akron, Ohio, on a failing business trip. He was tempted to drink, and he saw the danger clearly enough to do something odd about it: he realized he must talk to another alcoholic to stay sober. He phoned local ministers to ask if they knew any alcoholics, and one of them directed him to Henrietta Seiberling, an Oxford Group member whose group had been trying to help a desperate alcoholic named Dr. Bob Smith, an Akron surgeon. The meeting that was meant to be brief lasted long into the evening, and Smith's last drink was on June 10, 1935 (a beer to steady his hand for surgery), which AA members consider the founding date of Alcoholics Anonymous.

Two realizations came out of the two men's work together in Akron. The first was that, to remain sober, an alcoholic needed another alcoholic to work with. The second was the concept of the "24 hours":

> that if the alcoholic could resist the urge to drink by postponing it for one day, one hour, or even one minute, he could remain sober.

Four years later those discoveries, and everything learned from living them, were written down in the book Alcoholics Anonymous, whose fifth chapter, "How It Works," sets out the program in twelve steps. The chapter's flat sentence about half-way measures, "Half measures availed us nothing. We stood at the turning point. We asked His protection and care with complete abandon," sits beside a first step that names the enemy without euphemism: "We admitted we were powerless over alcohol — that our lives had become unmanageable." The later steps keep the work going: "Continued to take personal inventory and when we were wrong promptly admitted it," "Sought through prayer and meditation to improve our conscious contact with God as we understood Him," and finally carrying the message to other alcoholics. The book's accounting of what sobriety is closes the design:

> We are not cured of alcoholism. What we really have is a daily reprieve contingent on the maintenance of our spiritual condition.

The compendium's comparison to B026 runs through those pieces, and it is a comparison drawn by this page between the Akron program and the Buddha's formula for the training of effort. The postponement practice of the "24 hours" works the first task, guarding against a state before it takes hold: the craving is not fed, and the day is gotten through without the first drink. The first step works the second, abandoning what has taken hold: the drinking is named as unmanageable and stopped. The continuing steps work the third and fourth, developing the good and keeping it: inventory, meditation, service to other alcoholics, and the "daily reprieve" sentence, which makes the keeping explicitly not an achievement but a daily maintenance. What the story does not carry is the meditative-concentration side of right effort, the jhāna context of the original formula; the coverage matrix names it for another story.
