*Akron, Ohio, 1935–1939 · the founding of Alcoholics Anonymous by Bill Wilson and Dr. Bob Smith; program text from the Big Book (1939)*

In May 1935 a newly sober New York stockbroker named Bill Wilson found himself in Akron, Ohio, on a failing business trip. He was tempted to drink, and he saw the danger clearly enough to do something odd about it: he realized he must talk to another alcoholic to stay sober. He phoned local ministers to ask if they knew any alcoholics, and one of them directed him to Henrietta Seiberling, an Oxford Group member whose group had been trying to help a desperate alcoholic named Dr. Bob Smith, an Akron surgeon. The meeting that was meant to be brief lasted long into the evening, and Smith's last drink was on June 10, 1935 (a beer to steady his hand for surgery), which AA members consider the founding date of Alcoholics Anonymous.

Two realizations came out of the two men's work together in Akron. The first was that, to remain sober, an alcoholic needed another alcoholic to work with. The second was the concept of the "24 hours":

> that if the alcoholic could resist the urge to drink by postponing it for one day, one hour, or even one minute, he could remain sober.

Four years later those discoveries, and everything learned from living them, were written down in the book Alcoholics Anonymous, whose fifth chapter, "How It Works," sets out the program in twelve steps, beginning with one that names the enemy without euphemism: "We admitted we were powerless over alcohol — that our lives had become unmanageable." The sixth chapter, "Into Action," then turns the steps into daily practice. Step Ten watches for what has arisen: "Continue to watch for selfishness, dishonesty, resentment, and fear. When these crop up, we ask God at once to remove them. We discuss them with someone immediately and make amends quickly if we have harmed anyone." Step Eleven develops what is not yet there: "On awakening let us think about the twenty-four hours ahead... Before we begin, we ask God to direct our thinking, especially asking that it be divorced from self-pity, dishonest or self-seeking motives." And the book's accounting of what sobriety is closes the design:

> We are not cured of alcoholism. What we really have is a daily reprieve contingent on the maintenance of our spiritual condition.

Read alongside B026, those practices map the four efforts with unusual directness, and the mapping is this page's. Guarding against the unarisen is the morning practice: the day's thinking is asked to be "divorced from self-pity, dishonest or self-seeking motives" before the motives take form, and the founding "24 hours" postpones the first drink rather than fighting the craving. Abandoning the arisen is Step Ten's "when these crop up" passage, which handles selfishness, resentment and fear exactly as arisen states to be removed at once. Developing the good continues the same step — "Then we resolutely turn our thoughts to someone we can help" — and runs through Step Eleven's prayer and meditation and the twelfth step's carrying the message. Maintaining it is the "daily reprieve" sentence, which makes the keeping explicitly not an achievement but a daily maintenance, contingent, as the book says, "on the maintenance of our spiritual condition."
