*Akron, Ohio, 1935–1939 · the founding of Alcoholics Anonymous by Bill Wilson and Dr. Bob Smith; program text from the Big Book (1939)*

In May 1935 a newly sober New York stockbroker named Bill Wilson found himself in Akron, Ohio, on a failing business trip. He was tempted to drink, and he saw the danger clearly enough to do something odd about it: he realized he must talk to another alcoholic to stay sober. Through a local minister's wife he found Dr. Bob Smith, a surgeon whose drinking had already cost him his hospital practice. The meeting that was meant to be brief lasted long into the evening, and Smith's last drink came on June 10, 1935, a beer to steady his hand for surgery. That date became the founding of Alcoholics Anonymous.

Two realizations came out of the two men's work together in Akron. The first was that, to remain sober, an alcoholic needed another alcoholic to work with. The second was the concept of the "24 hours":

> that if the alcoholic could resist the urge to drink by postponing it for one day, one hour, or even one minute, he could remain sober.

Four years later those discoveries, and everything learned from living them, were written down in the book Alcoholics Anonymous, whose fifth chapter, "How It Works," opens with a confidence earned in the field:

> Rarely have we seen a person fail who has thoroughly followed our path. Those who do not recover are people who cannot or will not completely give themselves to this simple program, usually men and women who are constitutionally incapable of being honest with themselves.

The program the chapter lays out is a design for the four efforts. The first effort, preventing the unarisen, is the "24 hours" itself: the craving is postponed rather than fought, and the first drink is simply not taken today. The second, abandoning the arisen, is Step One: "We admitted we were powerless over alcohol — that our lives had become unmanageable." The chapter is blunt about what half-measures do: "Half measures availed us nothing. We stood at the turning point. We asked His protection and care with complete abandon." The third and fourth, developing and maintaining the wholesome, run through the rest of the steps: "Continued to take personal inventory and when we were wrong promptly admitted it," "Sought through prayer and meditation to improve our conscious contact with God as we understood Him," and finally carrying the message to other alcoholics, and through the book's sober accounting of what sobriety is:

> We are not cured of alcoholism. What we really have is a daily reprieve contingent on the maintenance of our spiritual condition.

The compendium reads the Akron story as the four efforts of B026 reinvented from the inside, without a sutta in the room. Two drunks worked out, in the grammar of their own illness, what the Buddha had named: guard against the state that has not yet arisen, abandon the one that has, grow the good and keep it alive, and the keeping is not an achievement but a daily practice. Not carried here: the meditative-concentration side of right effort, the jhāna context of the original formula; that needs its own stories.
