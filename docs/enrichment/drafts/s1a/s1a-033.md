Reported Tang-dynasty anecdote · China, early 8th century · retold from later descriptions by Imre Hamar and Taigen Dan Leighton

Fazang, third patriarch of the Huayan school of Chinese Buddhism, is said to have built a hall of mirrors for Empress Wu Zetian so she could see his school's teaching in one lit room: a single Buddha image surrounded by mirrors, every reflection carrying all the others ([Hamar](https://www.harrassowitz-verlag.de/dzo/artikel/201/4_201.pdf); [Leighton](https://www.upaya.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Flower-Ornament-Sutra-Taigen-D-L.pdf)). The room was the sermon. Fazang taught the Flower Garland Sutra at court, and the IEP's account of his lecturing is that "the principles he expounded upon were so abstruse that the listeners were utterly dumbstruck," so he reached for images instead ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/fazang/)).

The accounts differ in their details, and each names its own arrangement. Hamar, a Huayan scholar, gives the cautious version: "According to later descriptions eight mirrors were placed in the four cardinal directions and four secondary directions, and one on the top, and one on the bottom. In the middle of the ten mirrors facing to one another, a Buddha statue was installed, along with a lamp or a candle to illuminate it. This setting produced an infinite number of Buddha reflections in the mirrors" ([Hamar](https://www.harrassowitz-verlag.de/dzo/artikel/201/4_201.pdf)). Leighton's essay describes a hall with "mirrors on the ceiling, floor, four walls, and four corners of a room" and a lamp beside the Buddha image ([Leighton](https://www.upaya.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Flower-Ornament-Sutra-Taigen-D-L.pdf)). The IEP adds a torch: "an endless web of reflected light crisscrossed, creating an infinite series of images within images, each containing the entire Buddha" ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/fazang/)).

What the empress saw is Leighton's sentence: "the reflection in any one mirror clearly reflected the reflections from all of the other mirrors, including the specific reflection of the Buddha image in each one" ([Leighton](https://www.upaya.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Flower-Ornament-Sutra-Taigen-D-L.pdf)). No mirror held a self-made image. Each one borrowed its Buddha from every other mirror at once, and lent its own back to them.

The story has a modern echo. Hamar reports visiting the Huayan monastery on Zhongnanshan, where the abbot keeps a reconstruction of the Mirror Hall, ten metal plates and a Buddha image at the center; "lighting up the candle, infinite Buddha images became reflected on the metal plates" ([Hamar](https://www.harrassowitz-verlag.de/dzo/artikel/201/4_201.pdf)). The demonstration still runs.

The compendium reads the room as B143's relational emptiness made visible: every reflection in it exists through its relations to all the others, and nothing in the room has an image it owns. The fullness matters as much as the emptiness: the relations multiply the Buddha rather than subtracting it. Two gaps stay open. The sources present a Huayan demonstration of interpenetration, a cousin of Nagarjuna's emptiness rather than the same argument, so the mapping is the compendium's cross-tradition reading. And the anecdote reports a teaching, with nothing recorded of the empress's response, so it does not carry the principle's caution that recognizing dependence does not by itself improve a relationship.

**Principles:**
- [[B143 — Emptiness is relational fullness|B143 Emptiness is relational fullness]] — the mirror hall shows each reflection existing only through its relations to all the others while the room stays full, carrying the principle's dependence-to-emptiness set-up, its tracing operation, and its fullness reading; the Huayan framing is marked as the compendium's cross-tradition reading. ([Hamar](https://www.harrassowitz-verlag.de/dzo/artikel/201/4_201.pdf); [Leighton](https://www.upaya.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Flower-Ornament-Sutra-Taigen-D-L.pdf))
