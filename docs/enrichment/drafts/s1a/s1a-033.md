Reported Tang-dynasty anecdote · China · retold from later descriptions by Imre Hamar and Taigen Dan Leighton

Fazang, third patriarch of the Huayan school of Chinese Buddhism, is said to have built a hall of mirrors for Empress Wu Zetian so she could see his school's teaching in one lit room: a single Buddha image surrounded by mirrors, every reflection carrying all the others ([Hamar](https://www.harrassowitz-verlag.de/dzo/artikel/201/4_201.pdf); [Leighton](https://www.upaya.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Flower-Ornament-Sutra-Taigen-D-L.pdf)). Fazang taught the Flower Garland Sutra, and the IEP's account of his lecturing is that "the principles he expounded upon were so abstruse that the listeners were utterly dumbstruck," so he reached for images instead ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/fazang/)).

Hamar, a Huayan scholar, gives the cautious version of the demonstration: "According to later descriptions eight mirrors were placed in the four cardinal directions and four secondary directions, and one on the top, and one on the bottom. In the middle of the ten mirrors facing to one another, a Buddha statue was installed, along with a lamp or a candle to illuminate it. This setting produced an infinite number of Buddha reflections in the mirrors" ([Hamar](https://www.harrassowitz-verlag.de/dzo/artikel/201/4_201.pdf)). The IEP's account gives the illumination as a torch: "an endless web of reflected light crisscrossed, creating an infinite series of images within images, each containing the entire Buddha" ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/fazang/)).

What the empress saw is Leighton's sentence: "the reflection in any one mirror clearly reflected the reflections from all of the other mirrors, including the specific reflection of the Buddha image in each one" ([Leighton](https://www.upaya.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Flower-Ornament-Sutra-Taigen-D-L.pdf)). The direct image in each mirror needed only the statue and the light. Leighton describes each mirror reflecting the other mirrors and their Buddha images, and that is the nesting the demonstration showed.

The story has a modern echo. Hamar reports visiting the Huayan monastery on Zhongnanshan, where the abbot showed him a reconstruction of the Mirror Hall, a small building with ten metal plates in place of mirrors and a Buddha image at the center; with the candle lit, the plates filled with reflected images of the Buddha ([Hamar](https://www.harrassowitz-verlag.de/dzo/artikel/201/4_201.pdf), paraphrase of his account of the visit).

The compendium pairs this Huayan demonstration with B143 because the displayed images depend on the statue, the light, and the mirrors, and each mirror reflects the other mirrors and their Buddha images while the room stays full: the relations multiply Buddha images rather than subtracting them. The pairing is the compendium's comparison between Huayan interpenetration and B143; the demonstration does not supply Nagarjuna's argument for emptiness. The opened accounts do not report the empress's response, so the story also does not carry the principle's caution that recognizing dependence does not by itself improve a relationship.
