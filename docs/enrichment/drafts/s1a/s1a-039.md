In October 1802, Ludwig van Beethoven sat down in the village of Heiligenstadt and wrote the fear out. He was thirty-one, and his hearing was going. The letter to his brothers, now known by that village's name, was written to be read after his death, with instructions that his physician describe his malady and add these pages to it ([Beethoven's Letters, trans. Lady Wallace](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/13065/13065-h/13065-h.htm)).

What he wrote in it is the strange part. He did not write slogans. He wrote down what had happened, and then, separately, what he feared might happen, and the two are not mixed up in the letter. He recorded the fact, in the Wallace translation: "six years ago I was attacked by an incurable malady, aggravated by unskilful physicians, deluded from year to year, too, by the hope of relief, and at length forced to the conviction of a lasting affliction" ([Beethoven's Letters](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/13065/13065-h/13065-h.htm)). He recorded the humiliation, the observation that stung the most: "what humiliation when any one beside me heard a flute in the far distance, while I heard nothing, or when others heard a shepherd singing, and I still heard nothing!" ([Beethoven's Letters](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/13065/13065-h/13065-h.htm)). And he recorded the thing the fear had pushed him toward, plainly: "Such things brought me to the verge of desperation, and wellnigh caused me to put an end to my life. Art! art alone, deterred me. Ah! how could I possibly quit the world before bringing forth all that I felt it was my vocation to produce?" ([Beethoven's Letters](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/13065/13065-h/13065-h.htm))

A year earlier he had put the defiance in writing to his friend Wegeler: "i want to reach into the throat of fate, it shall certainly not bend me down completely -" ([Beethoven-Haus Bonn](https://www.beethoven.de/en/g/schicksal)). The Heiligenstadt letter is what that sentence looks like on a bad day. Not the fist shaken at fate, but the ledger: what is true, what is feared, what is still owed. His own conclusion was neither despair nor cure. It was patience, chosen on purpose: "It is decreed that I must now choose Patience for my guide! This I have done." ([Beethoven's Letters](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/13065/13065-h/13065-h.htm))

### Action

When a fear starts to run the show, write the Heiligenstadt version. One column: what has actually happened. Another: what you are afraid might happen. A third: what you would still owe the world if the second column came true.
