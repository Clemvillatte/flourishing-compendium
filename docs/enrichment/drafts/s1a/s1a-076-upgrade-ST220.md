Autobiographical philosophical-practice account · Rome, 1st century CE · Seneca, De Ira III.36 · quotations from the Aubrey Stewart translation (1900), opened at [Standard Ebooks](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/seneca/dialogues/aubrey-stewart/text/on-anger)

Seneca attributes a nightly self-examination to the philosopher Sextius, then describes his own practice. "The spirit ought to be brought up for examination daily," he writes, recording that it was Sextius's custom, when the day was over, to inquire of his spirit: "What bad habit of yours have you cured today? what vice have you checked? in what respect are you better?" ([De Ira III.36, Stewart](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/seneca/dialogues/aubrey-stewart/text/on-anger)). The passage gives the practice its teeth: "Anger will cease, and become more gentle, if it knows that every day it will have to appear before the judgment seat" ([De Ira III.36, Stewart](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/seneca/dialogues/aubrey-stewart/text/on-anger)).

Seneca's own version is domestic in its setting: "when the lamp is taken out of my sight, and my wife, who knows my habit, has ceased to talk, I pass the whole day in review before myself, and repeat all that I have said and done: I conceal nothing from myself, and omit nothing" ([De Ira III.36, Stewart](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/seneca/dialogues/aubrey-stewart/text/on-anger)). The self-reprimands he records are specific, including one about speech: "In that dispute you spoke too contentiously: do not for the future argue with ignorant people: those who have never been taught are unwilling to learn" ([De Ira III.36, Stewart](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/seneca/dialogues/aubrey-stewart/text/on-anger)). And he records why the audit is sustainable rather than punishing:

> for why should I be afraid of any of my shortcomings, when it is in my power to say, "I pardon you this time: see that you never do that anymore?" ([De Ira III.36, Stewart](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/seneca/dialogues/aubrey-stewart/text/on-anger))

The reward is printed in the same breath: "how sweet is the sleep which follows this self-examination? how calm, how sound, and careless is it when our spirit has either received praise or reprimand, and when our secret inquisitor and censor has made his report about our morals?" ([De Ira III.36, Stewart](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/seneca/dialogues/aubrey-stewart/text/on-anger)).

My reading: the review's Stoic content is the direction of the audit — not what happened to me today, but what I did with what happened, which is the only thing on the list that was ever mine. The chapter closes with "A good man delights in receiving advice: all the worst men are the most impatient of guidance" ([De Ira III.36, Stewart](https://standardebooks.org/ebooks/seneca/dialogues/aubrey-stewart/text/on-anger)) — Seneca's own maxim about receiving correction. That the nightly self-audit is also what trains a person to receive it is my inference, not the chapter's claim; Seneca's own application is narrower, about weighing whether the person reprimanded can bear the truth.
