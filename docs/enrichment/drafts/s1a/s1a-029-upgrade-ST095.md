*Biographical narrative, cross-cultural · India/Burma, 20th century · [Dipa Ma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipa_Ma); quotations from [dipama.com, "Teachings"](https://dipama.com/teachings/) and [Tricycle, "Enlightenment in This Lifetime" (Spring 2004)](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)*

Dipa Ma, a Bengali housewife, lost her husband and two of her three children, and developed heart trouble that reached the stage where, in her words, "my doctors expected me to die at any time" ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)). "When I realized I was dying, I knew I had to begin to practice", she recalled, asking herself, "What can I take with me when I die?" ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)). She recovered, and within years she was recognised as a master of the deepest concentration states; Wikipedia records her first formal student in Calcutta as "her neighbor, Malati Barua, a widow trying to raise six young children alone" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipa_Ma)). Her instructions to such students were made for interstices: "Whatever you are doing, be aware of it" ([dipama.com](https://dipama.com/teachings/)).

She is the modern tradition's clearest case against the excuse of circumstance. No monastery, no leisure, a damaged heart, and a child. The practice, she demonstrated, fits inside an ordinary life because an ordinary life is made of moments, and moments are all it requires. Her own summary of a lifetime of it: "The practice never leaves me" ([dipama.com](https://dipama.com/teachings/)).
