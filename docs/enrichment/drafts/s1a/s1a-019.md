Buddhist commentarial narrative · Dhammapada Atthakatha, Ciñcamanavikavatthu, trans. Burlingame, Buddhist Legends XIII.9

A woman near her term walks into the Buddha's teaching hall at Savatthi and accuses him before the crowd: he made her pregnant, she says, and provides nothing for her confinement. He stops the discourse and gives her one sentence: whether her words are true or false is known only to her and to him. The deception then unmasks itself before everyone; the crowd's fury swings from doubt to spitting; the Buddha does not move. ([Buddhist Legends XIII.9](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/English-Texts/Buddhist-Legends/13-09.htm))

The frame is reputation weather. As the Buddha's gain and honor grow, the heretics lose theirs, "even as fireflies lose their brilliance before the coming of the sun", and they plot in secret. A wandering nun named Ciñcā Māṇavikā takes the job. For weeks she lets herself be seen walking toward Jetavana at dusk and back at dawn; asked where she has spent the night, she answers, "at Jetavana alone with the monk Gotama in the Perfumed Chamber". After three or four months she binds her belly and goes about saying, "I have conceived a child by the monk Gotama." ([Buddhist Legends XIII.9](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/English-Texts/Buddhist-Legends/13-09.htm))

In the ninth month she fastens a disk of wood beneath her cloak, pounds her hands and feet with an ox's jawbone to swell them, and stands before the Buddha as he preaches: "Mighty monk, mighty is the throng to which you preach the Law; sweet is your voice, soft are your lips. Nevertheless you are the one by whom I have conceived a child, and the time of my delivery is near at hand." The Tathāgata stops his discourse and answers, "Sister, as to whether what you have said be true or false, that is something which only you and I know." ([Buddhist Legends XIII.9](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/English-Texts/Buddhist-Legends/13-09.htm))

The machinery then turns. Sakka's seat grows warm; he and four deities become mice, and "with one bite of their teeth these little mice severed the cords with which the disk of wood was fastened to the belly of the woman"; the disk falls and cuts off her toes, the crowd spits on her head and drives her from Jetavana, and the earth opens and swallows her to the Avīci hell. The next day the monks discuss her ruin; the Buddha's recorded teaching is about lying, not composure: "If a man break one commandment, if he speak lies, If he abandon the next world, there is no evil deed he will not commit." ([Buddhist Legends XIII.9](https://ancient-buddhist-texts.net/English-Texts/Buddhist-Legends/13-09.htm))

Read for B054, the story stages the worldly winds. The plot is manufactured blame aimed at standing; the crowd is blown first into doubt and then into fury; the Buddha's one sentence neither argues nor retaliates, and the frame records no change in him across both weathers. The verse he teaches is about lying; the equanimity reading is this page's interpretation. ([B054 body, AN 8.6](https://www.accesstoinsight.org/tipitaka/an/an08/an08.006.than.html))
