Traditional Chan narrative · the Flower Sermon, via the Wikipedia article on the story.

The Buddha gives a wordless sermon to his disciples by holding up a white flower, and no one in the audience understands it except Mahakasyapa, who smiles ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower_Sermon)). That is the whole of the action: a flower held up, a room that does not understand, and one smile. The words that follow in the account name what the flower carried: "I possess the true Dharma eye, the marvelous mind of Nirvana, the true form of the formless, the subtle dharma gate that does not rest on words or letters but is a special transmission outside of the scriptures. This I entrust to Mahakasyapa" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower_Sermon)).

The connection with D135 is the sermon working as the principle's extreme case: the teaching is delivered with no words at all, and the reception is a smile rather than an answer. Even the words that follow do not carry the content; they point at a gate that does not rest on words or letters. The story is told as an origin of Chan and Zen, the direct transmission of wisdom to Mahakasyapa ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower_Sermon)), and it appears as Case 6 of the Gateless Barrier ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower_Sermon)). The account belongs to the tradition's own record rather than to the earliest texts: it appears to have been recorded by Chinese Chan Buddhists, with the earliest known version dated 1036 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flower_Sermon)).

Not carried: the page's everyday practice of teaching without words in ordinary life, the flower sermon's silence carried into conduct; the parable keeps to the transmission scene.
