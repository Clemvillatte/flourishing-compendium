Massachusetts, 1845 · Henry David Thoreau, Walden (1854), the house ledger in "Economy" · a memoir, Project Gutenberg raw text

Before Thoreau moved to the pond he built the house, and the account of building it is a working version of D035's question, which everyday needs can be met nearby and what the distant ones cost. His materials were local, second-hand and cheap, and he wrote down what each one cost to the half-cent ([Walden](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-0.txt)).

The house, as he describes it ([Walden](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-0.txt)):

> "I have thus a tight shingled and plastered house, ten feet wide by fifteen long, and eight-feet posts, with a garret and a closet, a large window on each side, two trap doors, one door at the end, and a brick fireplace opposite."

And why he gives the ledger at all ([Walden](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-0.txt)):

> "The exact cost of my house, paying the usual price for such materials as I used, but not counting the work, all of which was done by myself, was as follows; and I give the details because very few are able to tell exactly what their houses cost, and fewer still, if any, the separate cost of the various materials which compose them:"

The total ([Walden](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-0.txt)):

> "In all, $28.12½"

The items run from boards at $8.03½, "mostly shanty boards," to chalk at one cent, with his own marginal notes beside them, "That was high" beside the lime and "More than I needed" beside the hair; beside transportation, $1.40, he notes that he "carried a good part on my back." The timber, stones and sand he "claimed by squatter's right" ([Walden](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-0.txt)):

> "These are all the materials excepting the timber stones and sand, which I claimed by squatter's right."

The chapter's argument about needs and costs is stated plainly ([Walden](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-0.txt)):

> "Most of the luxuries, and many of the so called comforts of life, are not only not indispensable, but positive hindrances to the elevation of mankind. With respect to luxuries and comforts, the wisest have ever lived a more simple and meagre life than the poor."

> "Superfluous wealth can buy superfluities only. Money is not required to buy one necessary of the soul."

My reading: D035's source image, Daodejing 80, is a small state whose people find their coarse food sweet and their plain clothes beautiful, and the principle asks which needs can be met nearby. Thoreau's ledger is that question answered with a hammer. The boards came from shanties, the windows were second-hand, the brick was old, the timber and sand were claimed on the spot, and the largest standing cost on the list, transportation, was cut by carrying the goods himself. The ledger's point is not the smallness of the total but the knowing: he says few people can tell what their house actually costs, item by item, and the accounting is itself the practice. The contentment side of the principle is in the two later passages: the wisest living a simpler life than the poor, and superfluous wealth buying superfluities only. The story does not carry Daodejing 80's isolation ideal, which the principle page sets aside, and Thoreau's pond life was famously sociable.
