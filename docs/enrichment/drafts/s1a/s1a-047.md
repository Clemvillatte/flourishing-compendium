Warring States China · Zhuangzi, Book XIX · a canonical anecdote, James Legge translation

A woodcarver made a bell stand so fine that the ruler asked what art produced it, and the carver's answer was that art had nothing to do with it. What produced the bell stand was a fast: three days shedding the thought of reward, five days shedding praise and blame, seven days shedding himself. Only when his mind had gone still did he enter the forest and see the stand already standing in the trees. The anecdote is the narrative form of what D041 calls purity and stillness. ([Zhuangzi XIX, Legge](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf))

The carver's reply, as Legge has it ([Zhuangzi XIX](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)):

> "When your servant had undertaken to make the bell-stand, I did not venture to waste any of my power, and felt it necessary to fast in order to compose my mind. After fasting for three days, I did not presume to think of any congratulation, reward, rank, or emolument (which I might obtain by the execution of my task); after fasting five days, I did not presume to think of the condemnation or commendation (which it would produce), or of the skill or want of skill (which it might display). At the end of the seven days, I had forgotten all about myself—my four limbs and my whole person. By this time the thought of your Grace's court (for which I was to make the thing) had passed away; everything that could divert my mind from exclusive devotion to the exercise of my skill had disappeared."

The fast ends, and the seeing begins ([Zhuangzi XIX](https://metis.ethz.ch/wp-content/uploads/2023/03/Zhuangzi_EnglishLegge.pdf)):

> "Then I went into the forest, and looked at the natural forms of the trees. When I saw one of a perfect form, then the figure of the bell-stand rose up to my view, and I applied my hand to the work. Had I not met with such a tree, I must have abandoned the object; but my Heaven-given faculty and the Heaven-given qualities of the wood were concentrated on it. So it was that my spirit was thus engaged in the production of the bell-stand."

My reading: the anecdote gives D041 its mechanism in a single arc. The Classic of Purity states the condition, that the mind "loves stillness" but the desires "draw it away," and that when the desires are sent away "the mind would of itself become still" ([Classic of Purity, Legge](http://www.sacred-texts.com/tao/sbe40/sbe4019.htm)). The carver's fast is that sending away, staged: reward first, then the audience's judgment, then the self that would be judged. What is left when the distraction is gone is not emptiness for its own sake but capacity: the still mind can see the bell stand in the tree, and the work follows the seeing. The scripture's promise, that he who has this purity "enters gradually into the (inspiration of the) True Tâo," is the same claim at the scale of a life. Read alongside D041, the story carries the stillness, the cleaning and the consequence whole; it does not carry purity as a moral register of ordinary conduct, which needs a different story.

**Principles:**
- [[D041 — Practice purity and stillness (qingjing)|41. Practice purity and stillness (qingjing)]] — the fast shows desires being sent away in stages until the mind stills itself, and the stillness shown as the condition of seeing the work before doing it
