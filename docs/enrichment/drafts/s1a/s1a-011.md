Autobiographical narrative · Chicago; Jane Addams's 1916 account

A rumour brings visitors to Hull-House to see a Devil Baby that is not there. Jane Addams grows tired of the commotion, but older women's eagerness draws her into conversations about their lives; one bedside visit ends in disappointment. Beside P136, the episode asks what a spectator misses when a belief has been dismissed without understanding what it means to its holder. ([Addams, chapter I, pp. 2-21](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/69234/69234-h/69234-h.htm))

Three women arrive demanding to see the child. They know its supposed appearance and powers, and denials fail to persuade them. For six weeks, visitors and telephone calls disrupt the settlement's work; Addams confesses her irritation at the spectacle. Yet she keeps leaving her work to listen when she hears older women's eager voices. ([Addams, chapter I, pp. 2-7](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/69234/69234-h/69234-h.htm))

Those conversations reveal lives she has not fully understood. The women describe family violence, bereavement and households whose younger members barely include them in conversation: "More than half the time I can’t tell what they are talking about," is a complaint Addams records. The rumour gives them familiar material to discuss, and the prospect of having something to tell at home. ([Addams, chapter I, pp. 8-12 and 21-22](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/69234/69234-h/69234-h.htm))

Then Addams visits a bedridden woman who will accept that there is no baby only if Addams herself says so. Once a prosperous second-hand dealer, the woman now lives with her grandson, passing long days alone apart from short visits. On her way upstairs, Addams is tempted to supply a convincing description of the baby: she thinks a story confirmed by her would attract neighbours and restore some of the woman's former social importance. ([Addams, chapter I, pp. 16-20](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/69234/69234-h/69234-h.htm))

At the doorway, she hesitates. In Addams's account, that hesitation gives the answer away: the woman's eager expression fades and her shoulders sag. Addams sees the anticipated larger life close off; she does not tell a comforting lie, and the visit offers no happy resolution. ([Addams, chapter I, pp. 20-21](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/69234/69234-h/69234-h.htm))

"I never encountered that particular temptation again," Addams writes. She goes on to say that she gradually lost her impression that the old people wanted a second chance to live their lives over again. She came to understand the tale as a possible way for their experience to secure a hearing among younger people who otherwise disregarded them. ([Addams, chapter I, pp. 21-22](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/69234/69234-h/69234-h.htm))

The comparison with P136 concerns Addams's revised understanding of her neighbours. A false report had brought forward experiences her prior acquaintance with them had left partly unseen. The women's disclosures, and Addams's difficulty in answering them, give the story its continuing claim on the reader. ([Addams, chapter I](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/69234/69234-h/69234-h.htm); [University of Illinois Press, account of the women's disclosures](https://www.press.uillinois.edu/books/?id=p070242))
