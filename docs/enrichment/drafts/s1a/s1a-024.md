First-person interview testimony, primary-adjacent · Dipa Ma, in Jack Engler's account of his 1977 meetings with her (Tricycle, Spring 2004); teaching instructions via dipama.com; biographical frame via Wikipedia

Dipa Ma described insight as a method of watching: "insight practice is a method of observation", and stray thoughts are not fought: "Just watch them arise and pass away and come back to the feeling of the breath" ([dipama.com](https://dipama.com/teachings/)). Her testimony of what that watching did, told to Jack Engler in 1977, is the story below ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)).

The backdrop was grief and illness: she lost a daughter at three months and a son at childbirth, then developed heart trouble that reached the stage where "my doctors expected me to die at any time" ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)). Her husband died suddenly, and she recalls: "When I realized I was dying, I knew I had to begin to practice", asking herself, "What can I take with me when I die?" ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)). Wikipedia records her first retreat at the Kamayut Meditation Center in Rangoon, and a later course at Thathana Yeiktha, the center of the Mahasi Sayadaw, where "she experienced the first stage of enlightenment" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipa_Ma)). of that course she says "It took about six days" ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)).

What she describes is not belief but observation, and the instructions her students preserved are blunt about the method: "Whatever is happening is to be noticed, then that will go away, and another thing will come" ([dipama.com](https://dipama.com/teachings/)). Asked about desire and anger, the pattern is the same. On sense-desire: "It came up for me too. When it arose, I knew it—that's the way to overcome it" ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)). Asked about anger: "As soon as it comes, at the very start, I'm aware of it", so that "It doesn't get any nourishment"; "Anger is a fire, but I don't feel any heat. It comes and dies right out" ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)). Her reports of what seeing changed cover impermanence (above), the sense of self ("Any question of a permanent self became meaningless") and clinging ("There is nothing ultimately desirable in this world, nothing to cling to") ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)). Her summary: "Enlightenment brings purity and liberation and understanding" ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)).

She kept the practice inside an ordinary life: "Whatever you are doing, be aware of it" ([dipama.com](https://dipama.com/teachings/)). In 1967 she moved to Calcutta; Wikipedia records her first formal student was "her neighbor, Malati Barua, a widow trying to raise six young children alone" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipa_Ma)).

Read beside B043, the story carries the principle's centre: insight is not agreeing with a doctrine but watching one's own experience, a desire or an anger arising, being seen, and dying out, until impermanence and the emptiness of clinging are things she reports observing rather than asserting. The MN 10 frames of reference are not taught frame by frame here, and the draft claims no doctrinal lineage for them; that teaching is left to the principle's own practice section.
