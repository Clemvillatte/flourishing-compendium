First-person interview testimony, primary-adjacent · Dipa Ma, in interview with Amy Schmidt (Tricycle, 2004); teaching instructions via dipama.com; biographical frame via Wikipedia

Dipa Ma, a Bengali woman living in Rangoon, lost a daughter at three months and a son at childbirth, then developed heart trouble that reached the stage where, in her words, "my doctors expected me to die at any time" ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)). Her husband died suddenly, and she recalls: "When I realized I was dying, I knew I had to begin to practice", asking herself, "What can I take with me when I die?" ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)). Wikipedia records her first retreat at the Kamayut Meditation Center in Rangoon, and a later course at Thathana Yeiktha, the center of the Mahasi Sayadaw, where "she experienced the first stage of enlightenment" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipa_Ma)). Of that course of practice she says simply: "It took about six days" ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)).

What she describes is not belief but observation. Her instructions to students, preserved by her students, are blunt about the method: "insight practice is a method of observation"; thoughts that pull attention away are not fought: "Just watch them arise and pass away and come back to the feeling of the breath" ([dipama.com](https://dipama.com/teachings/)). Her own report of anger works the same way: "When it arose, I knew it—that's the way to overcome it"; "As soon as it comes, at the very start, I'm aware of it", so that "It doesn't get any nourishment"; "Anger is a fire, but I don't feel any heat. It comes and dies right out" ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)). The same seeing reached further: "Any question of a permanent self became meaningless", and "There is nothing ultimately desirable in this world, nothing to cling to" ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)). Her summary of the point of it all: "Enlightenment brings purity and liberation and understanding" ([Tricycle](https://tricycle.org/magazine/enlightenment-lifetime-meetings-remarkable-woman/)).

She kept the practice inside an ordinary life: "Whatever you are doing, be aware of it" ([dipama.com](https://dipama.com/teachings/)) — and in 1967 moved to Calcutta, where Wikipedia records her first formal student was "her neighbor, Malati Barua, a widow trying to raise six young children alone" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipa_Ma)).

Read beside B043, the story carries the principle's centre: insight is not agreeing with a doctrine but watching one's own experience, an anger arising, being seen, and dying out, until impermanence and the emptiness of clinging are things observed rather than asserted. The MN 10 frames of reference are not taught frame by frame here; the draft names their lineage and leaves that teaching to the principle's own practice section. ([B043](https://github.com/Clemvillatte/flourishing-compendium/blob/main/content/principles/B043%20%E2%80%94%20Develop%20insight%20(vipassana).md))
