Biographical account · John Milton (1608-1674), poet, via the Morgan Library & Museum's Paradise Lost pages and the poem's text (Project Gutenberg ebook #20).

Blind by 1652, Milton composed Paradise Lost orally and dictated it to others; in Book III its speaker lists the sights he has lost and then asks celestial light to "Shine inward" ([Morgan](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/John-Miltons-Paradise-Lost); [PL III.51, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20/pg20.txt)). The blindness was probably glaucoma, and it arrived before the epic was made: the Morgan dates composition of the ten books to 1658-1663, from a plan Milton had held as early as 1640 ([Morgan](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/John-Miltons-Paradise-Lost)). Because he could not write, he composed at night or in the early morning, held the lines in memory until someone was available to write them down, and revised as the text was read back to him; a day's work amounted to twenty lines of verse ([Morgan](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/John-Miltons-Paradise-Lost)).

The Book III invocation names the condition plainly. The speaker counts himself into a lineage of blind poets and prophets, "Blind Thamyris and blind Maeonides, / And Tiresias and Phineus Prophets old" ([PL III.35-36, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20/pg20.txt)), then sets the year's turning against his own: "Thus with the Year / Seasons return, but not to me returns / Day, or the sweet approach of Ev'n or Morn, / Or sight of vernal bloom, or Summers Rose, / Or flocks, or herds, or human face divine; / But cloud in stead, and ever-during dark / Surrounds me" ([PL III.40-46, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20/pg20.txt)). After the list of what is gone, the request follows: "So much the rather thou Celestial light / Shine inward, and the mind through all her powers / Irradiate, there plant eyes" ([PL III.51-53, Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20/pg20.txt)).

I read the invocation as a limited illustration of how absence sharpens the description of what is desired: the daylight, flowers and faces are named in the same breath as the light asked for, and the asking comes after the losing. Carried this far, the story serves D015's set-up and its narrow operation. It does not carry the principle's full reciprocal claim, that each pole holds the other up; the sources show a man adapting to a condition and describing his desire inside it, and they stop there. Whether the illustration earns its place on D015 is for the supervisor to rule on.
