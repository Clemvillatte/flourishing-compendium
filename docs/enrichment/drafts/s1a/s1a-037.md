Between 1906 and 1915, Hilma af Klint produced The Paintings for the Temple, which eventually comprised 193 works. According to the Guggenheim, she kept her experimental paintings largely private and rarely exhibited them. In 1932 she wrote in one of her notebooks that she did not want her work to be seen for twenty years after her death. The Guggenheim's account says she was "convinced the world was not yet ready to understand her work", and that the work was all but unseen until 1986.

What she was keeping private was not minor work. The Guggenheim describes the Temple paintings as her first major group of largely nonobjective work. In her notebooks, as the Guggenheim's education resource prints them, she described how they were made:

> "The pictures were painted directly through me, without any preliminary drawings and with great force. I had no idea what the paintings were supposed to depict; nevertheless I worked swiftly and surely, without changing a single brushstroke."

A line recorded in the same notebooks reads:

> "Don't expect that the signals and symbols that you developed with much effort will be understood by the brothers that you meet, but work hard for the future."

The Daodejing has an image for a person like this: "The sage wears a coarse cloth and carries jade in his bosom." The value is real, and it is carried close, and it is not put in the surface where the world can appraise it. Af Klint spent her life as a working painter while the paintings she considered her real task stayed folded away, waiting for the future the notebooks told her to work for.

### Action

Before showing your best work, ask what the showing is for. If the answer is mostly that you want the world's appraisal now, consider carrying the jade a while longer and letting the work wait for the reader it is actually for.
