Hilma af Klint finished her greatest work in 1915, and then made sure almost no one would see it. The Paintings for the Temple, produced between 1906 and 1915, eventually comprised 193 works. She exhibited her landscapes and portraits, but the Temple cycle stayed private. In 1932 she wrote in one of her notebooks that she did not want her work to be seen for twenty years after her death, and the Guggenheim's account of her life says she was "convinced the world was not yet ready to understand her work". Her work was all but unseen until 1986.

What makes the story strange is that the paintings were not apprentice work she had reason to hide. They were large, deliberate, and unlike anything being made in Europe at the time. In her notebooks she described how they came:

> "The pictures were painted directly through me, without any preliminary drawings and with great force. I had no idea what the paintings were supposed to depict; nevertheless I worked swiftly and surely, without changing a single brushstroke."

A note she recorded from her inner instruction reads like a prophecy aimed at her own posthumous career:

> "Don't expect that the signals and symbols that you developed with much effort will be understood by the brothers that you meet, but work hard for the future."

She obeyed it to the letter. The world did eventually come, four decades after her death, and by then the paintings had waited long enough to be understood.

Daodejing chapter 9 closes with a rule about completed work: when the work is done and the name is becoming distinguished, to withdraw into obscurity is the way of Heaven. Af Klint ran the rule in an unusual direction. Her name never became distinguished, so she withdrew the work instead, and on the same logic: the thing was finished, the world was not ready, and showing it early would not have improved it.

### Action

Before you push a finished thing into view, ask what the showing is for. If the answer is mostly that you want to be seen, consider af Klint's version: finish it, store it, and let it wait for a world that can receive it.
