In January 1941, Henri Matisse, then seventy-one, underwent surgery for cancer. He survived, but the operation left him bedridden and later confined to a wheelchair, unable to stand at an easel for the long hours oil painting demanded. He had prepared himself to die. A year later he wrote: "J'avais tellement préparé ma sortie de la vie, qu'il me semble être dans une seconde vie" — I had so prepared my departure from life that I seem to be in a second life.

What he did with the second life is the part worth telling. Too weak to paint as before, he took up scissors and sheets of paper painted in flat colour, and cut. He came to call the technique by a phrase that has outlived him: "Drawing with scissors. Cutting directly into vivid colour reminds me of the direct carving of sculptors."

The temptation is to read the cut-outs as an old man's compromise, art reduced to what a failing body could manage. Matisse's own account refuses that reading. "Paper cut-outs allow me to draw in colour," he explained. "For me it is a question of simplification. Instead of drawing the outline and establishing colour within it, I draw directly in the colour, which is more exact for not being transposed." The scissors were not a substitute for the brush. They were the last distillation of everything the brush had taught him, and the Louisiana Museum's exhibition text describes the late burst of expression as "the culmination of half a century of work".

There is a line from him that could serve as the caption for his whole career: "I have needed all that time to reach the stage where I can say what I want to say." The great vessel is the slowest made. The cut-outs he kept making until his death in the autumn of 1954, the work of a man who could no longer stand up to paint, are not a late style bolted onto a career. They are what the career was quietly building toward all along, the vessel finished only because the making took fifty years.

### Action

When your progress looks like drawing back, take the long view on purpose. Ask what your slow years were building, and judge the vessel by what it is becoming rather than by how finished it looks today.
