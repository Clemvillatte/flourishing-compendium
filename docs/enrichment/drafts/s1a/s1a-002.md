*Russia, 1879–1882 · Leo Tolstoy's A Confession, written in his early fifties after the crisis; William James made it his central exhibit of the "sick soul" in The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902)*

At fifty, Count Tolstoy was famous, rich, healthy and loved. He hid a rope so he would not hang himself while alone at night, and stopped going out with a gun. This is his own account, as James quotes it in his 1902 lectures:

> Behold me then, a man happy and in good health, hiding the rope in order not to hang myself to the rafters of the room where every night I went to sleep alone; behold me no longer going shooting, lest I should yield to the too easy temptation of putting an end to myself with my gun.

Nothing outside him had failed. Two of the greatest novels ever written stood behind him, his estate grew, his family was well. The failure was inner, and it was total:

> My life came to a standstill. I could breathe, eat, drink, and sleep, and I could not help doing these things; but there was no life, for there were no wishes the fulfillment of which I could consider reasonable. ... The truth was that life is meaningless.

He told the condition as an Eastern fable, a man fleeing a beast who drops into a dry well, finds a dragon waiting at the bottom, and hangs from a twig that two mice, one black and one white, day and night, gnaw through; and he insisted the fable was "not a fable but the real unanswerable truth intelligible to all." The honey on the leaves no longer consoled the hanging man. Reason offered him four exits: animal blindness, refined pleasure, suicide, or weak clinging, and his intellect settled on suicide as the consistent course. Something else in him refused it, a craving he could call "by no other name than that of a thirst for God," which came from the heart and ran against the whole movement of his thought.

The turn came from below, not from the books that had failed him. The labouring people around him, who had nothing he had, lived by a faith that made sense of labour, suffering and death:

> The simplest laboring people around me were the Russian people, and I turned to them and to the meaning of life which they give. ... This meaning was clear to me and near to my heart.

What he came back to was not his old inherited faith. "My relations to faith before and after were quite different": what he had once dismissed as unnecessary gibberish he now knew to be the only thing that gave life a meaning. That is the second birth. Same man, same world, a meaning consciously held instead of unconsciously received.

The compendium reads the story as the twice-born route told from inside. James built his contrast between the once-born, who start from life's goodness and need no second start, and the sick souls, who find peace only through the fall; his summary of this family of religions is that "the man must die to an unreal life before he can be born into the real life." Tolstoy's story carries that route whole: the fall, the search, the refusal of the easy exits, the turn toward those who suffer well, the rebirth. It does not carry the once-born route, the unbroken confidence of a Whitman; that route needs its own stories, and nothing in this page ranks one above the other.
