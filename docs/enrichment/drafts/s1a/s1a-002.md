*Russia, 19th century · Leo Tolstoy's A Confession, written after the crisis of his late forties; William James used the case in The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902)*

In his own account Tolstoy was "not yet fifty," with, as he tells it, a good wife, good children, a large estate that improved and increased without much effort on his part, and a famous name; none of it explained the crisis. The page tells a collapse and a reconstruction, and the reconstruction is the point. In Part 4 he describes the man he was:

> I, a man favored by fortune, hid a cord from myself lest I should hang myself from the crosspiece of the partition in my room where I undressed alone every evening, and I ceased to go out shooting with a gun lest I should be tempted by so easy a way of ending my life.

The standstill was inner and total, in his words (Part 4):

> My life came to a standstill. I could breathe, eat, drink, and sleep, and I could not help doing these things; but there was no life, for there were no wishes the fulfillment of which I could consider reasonable. ... The truth was that life is meaningless.

He told it as an Eastern fable: a man drops into a dry well to escape a beast, finds a dragon at the bottom, and hangs from a twig that two mice gnaw through; it was, he insisted, "not a fable but the real unanswerable truth intelligible to all" (Part 4). The honey on the leaves, family and art, no longer consoled him. In Part 7: "I found that for people of my circle there were four ways out": ignorance, epicureanism while it lasts, suicide, or weak clinging. Reason, he thought, pointed to suicide: "I saw that this was the worthiest way of escape and I wished to adopt it." What stopped him was "some dim consciousness of the invalidity of my thoughts," and a craving he could call "by no other name than that of a thirst for God" (James's quotation), which ran against the whole movement of his reasoning.

The turn came from below. He turned to the lives and faith of the Russian working people, finding there a meaning his own reasoning had not supplied. As he writes in Part 13:

> The simplest laboring people around me were the Russian people, and I turned to them and to the meaning of life which they give. ... This meaning was clear to me and near to my heart.

What he came back to was not his old inherited faith: "My relations to faith before and after were quite different" (Part 13). In James's telling (Lecture VIII), the returning energy "was nothing new. It was my ancient juvenile force of faith, the belief that the sole purpose of my life was to be BETTER"; he gave up "the life of the conventional world, recognizing it to be no life, but a parody on life," and has "felt right and happy, or at least relatively so, ever since."

The compendium reads the story as the twice-born route told from inside. James contrasts people whose basic confidence in life remains intact with those who must rebuild a way of living after that confidence breaks. Tolstoy illustrates the second route: the fall, the four exits, the turn, and a second birth in which the renewed willingness to live did not erase his awareness of suffering. In James's terms, the once-born live in a "one-storied" world and the twice-born in a "double-storied" one, where "renunciation and despair of it are our first step in the direction of the truth." These are contrasting outlooks, not fixed kinds; James calls actual people "intermediate varieties and mixtures." The once-born temperament, the unbroken confidence of a Whitman, is not carried here; it needs its own stories.
