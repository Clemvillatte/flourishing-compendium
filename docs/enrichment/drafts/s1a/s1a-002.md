*Russia, 1879–1882 · Leo Tolstoy's A Confession, written after the crisis of his late forties; William James made the case his central exhibit in The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902)*

In his own account Tolstoy was "not yet fifty," with a good wife, good children, a large estate that grew without effort and a famous name, and none of it explained the crisis. In Part 4 he describes the man he was:

> I, a man favored by fortune, hid a cord from myself lest I should hang myself from the crosspiece of the partition in my room where I undressed alone every evening, and I ceased to go out shooting with a gun lest I should be tempted by so easy a way of ending my life.

The standstill was inner and total:

> My life came to a standstill. I could breathe, eat, drink, and sleep, and I could not help doing these things; but there was no life, for there were no wishes the fulfillment of which I could consider reasonable. ... The truth was that life is meaningless. (Part 4)

He told it as an Eastern fable: a man drops into a dry well to escape a beast, finds a dragon at the bottom, and hangs from a twig that two mice gnaw through day and night; it was, he insisted, "not a fable but the real unanswerable truth intelligible to all" (Part 4). The honey on the leaves, family and art, no longer consoled him. In Part 7: "I found that for people of my circle there were four ways out": ignorance, epicureanism while it lasts, suicide, or weak clinging. Reason, he thought, pointed to suicide: "I saw that this was the worthiest way of escape and I wished to adopt it." What stopped him was "some dim consciousness of the invalidity of my thoughts," and a craving he could call "by no other name than that of a thirst for God" (James's quotation), which ran against the whole movement of his reasoning.

The turn came from below. The labouring people around him, who had none of what he had, lived by a faith that made sense of labour, suffering and death:

> The simplest laboring people around me were the Russian people, and I turned to them and to the meaning of life which they give. ... This meaning was clear to me and near to my heart. (Part 13)

What he came back to was not his old inherited faith: "My relations to faith before and after were quite different" (Part 13), and what he had once dismissed as gibberish he now knew to be what gave life meaning. In James's telling, the returning energy "was nothing new. It was my ancient juvenile force of faith, the belief that the sole purpose of my life was to be BETTER"; he gave up "the life of the conventional world, recognizing it to be no life, but a parody on life," and has "felt right and happy, or at least relatively so, ever since." The outcome was real but not rosy: "the sadness was preserved as a minor ingredient in the heart of the faith by which it was overcome."

The compendium reads the story as the twice-born route told from inside. James's contrast, in his own terms: for the once-born, "the world is a sort of rectilinear or one-storied affair"; for the twice-born, "the world is a double-storied mystery" where "renunciation and despair of it are our first step in the direction of the truth." Tolstoy carries that second route whole: the fall, the four exits, the turn, the second birth. James judged the twice-born outlook the wider one; the principle this page serves keeps the two routes level, noting with James that actual people are "intermediate varieties and mixtures." The once-born temperament, the unbroken confidence of a Whitman, is not carried here; it needs its own stories.
