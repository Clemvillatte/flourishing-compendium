Puritan spiritual autobiography · John Bunyan, Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners (1666)

He writes from prison, a tinker by his own aside, of "a low and inconsiderable generation; my father's house being of that rank that is meanest, and most despised of all the families in the land." His inner life was verbal fire: the temptation would come, "Sell Christ for this, or sell Christ for that," not once but "not so little as a hundred times together," and resistance took his body: "my very body would be put into action or motion, by way of pushing or thrusting with my hands or elbows; still answering, as fast as the destroyer said, Sell Him; I will not, I will not, I will not." ([Grace Abounding, Project Gutenberg #654](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/654))

An old copy of Luther's comment on Galatians fell into his hand, and "I found my condition in his experience so largely and profoundly handled, as if his book had been written out of my heart." Still the weather ran its own course: "my peace would be in it, and out, sometimes twenty times a day; comfort now, and trouble presently; peace now, and before I could go a furlong, as full of fear and guilt as ever heart could hold." ([Grace Abounding, Project Gutenberg #654](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/654))

The change did not arrive as a verdict he reached. One day he was "passing in the field, and that too with some dashes on my conscience," when the sentence fell upon his soul: "Thy righteousness is in heaven." He saw his standing located outside himself, "wherever I was, or whatever I was doing," and the clause he keeps is exact: "it was not my good frame of heart that made my righteousness better, nor yet my bad frame that made my righteousness worse." Then: "Now did my chains fall off my legs indeed; I was loosed from my afflictions and irons; my temptations also fled away." The graces still green on him were "but like those cracked groats and fourpence-halfpennies that rich men carry in their purses, when their gold is in their trunks at home: Oh! I saw my gold was in my trunk at home." ([Grace Abounding, Project Gutenberg #654](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/654))

This is the twice-born route as P184 defines it through James's contrast: peace sought through confronting suffering and inner conflict, rather than a confidence that begins with life's goodness. Bunyan's before-state shows the weakness of peace tied to inner weather, swinging "twenty times a day"; his resolution is not a better mood but a standing located outside the mood, in his words a frame of heart that made his standing neither better nor worse. The story illustrates the route without prescribing it: P184's plain-words stance asks what someone is experiencing rather than assigning types or ranking worth by suffering, and the prison is circumstance, not the source of the peace.

**Principles:**
- [[P184 — Distinguish the once-born from the twice-born: two routes through suffering|P184 Distinguish the once-born from the twice-born]]: a documented twice-born resolution; the standing moves from inner weather to something outside it, and the clause "not my good frame of heart" states the route's mechanism in the narrator's own words.
