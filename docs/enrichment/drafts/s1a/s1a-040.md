Victorian England · John Stuart Mill, Autobiography (1873), chapter V · a memoir account, Project Gutenberg ebook #10378

In the autumn of 1826, twenty years old and famous in radical circles for his education, John Stuart Mill put one question to himself: if every reform he lived for were granted that instant, would he be happy? Something in him answered before the trained mind could edit the reply. That answer is the hinge of the crisis Mill called a turning point in his mental history, and it reads as a case study in what P165 calls opening the gate. ([Mill, Autobiography ch. V](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10378/10378-0.txt))

Mill had been built as a machine for association: his father's doctrine held that feelings attach to things by education, and the instrument that kept those attachments clean was analysis. His education, he wrote, had made "premature analysis the inveterate habit of my mind." His own theory implied that "the habit of analysis has a tendency to wear away the feelings," a "perpetual worm at the root both of the passions and of the virtues." ([Mill, Autobiography ch. V](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10378/10378-0.txt))

The question and its answer arrived in that frame ([chapter V](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10378/10378-0.txt)):

> "Suppose that all your objects in life were realized; that all the changes in institutions and opinions which you are looking forward to, could be completely effected at this very instant: would this be a great joy and happiness to you?" And an irrepressible self-consciousness distinctly answered, "No!"

> At this my heart sank within me: the whole foundation on which my life was constructed fell down.

What followed was "the dry, heavy dejection of the melancholy winter of 1826-7": he kept working by the mere force of habit, "when all the spirit had gone out of it." ([Mill, Autobiography ch. V](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10378/10378-0.txt))

The recovery came through poetry. Byron was exactly wrong for him; Wordsworth proved "the precise thing for my mental wants at that particular juncture," because the poems worked as "not mere outward beauty, but states of feeling, and of thought coloured by feeling," and "seemed to be the very culture of the feelings, which I was in quest of." "I felt myself at once better and happier as I came under their influence." ([Mill, Autobiography ch. V](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10378/10378-0.txt)) The recovery was gradual: "Thus the cloud gradually drew off, and I again enjoyed life; and though I had several relapses, some of which lasted many months, I never again was as miserable as I had been." The creed changed too: "The cultivation of the feelings became one of the cardinal points in my ethical and philosophical creed." ([Mill, Autobiography ch. V](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/10378/10378-0.txt))

My reading: Rogers's account in P165 has defensiveness as distortion at the gate, experience translated to fit a pre-conceived self-structure. Mill's gate was analysis itself: every feeling was inspected for its usefulness before it could register as experience. The crisis was the moment the gate failed open: the "No" was one stimulus that got through undistorted, and it dismantled the structure it contradicted. The recovery ran the other direction, by letting states of feeling in and being changed by them. Read alongside P165, the story carries the whole arc: a defended mind, the unedited message that breaks the defense, and the slow practice of keeping the gate open after it has been forced.
