Poems with biographical context · United States, 20th century · Poetry Foundation, Poets.org, Britannica, and the Annals of Thoracic Surgery

William Carlos Williams was born in Rutherford, New Jersey in 1883, took his M.D. at the University of Pennsylvania in 1906, and, in Britannica's phrase, "returned in 1910 to a lifetime of poetry and medical practice in his hometown of Rutherford, New Jersey" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/William-Carlos-Williams)). That lifetime ran forty years of family practice and an estimated 2,000 deliveries from an office at 9 Ridge Road, and he kept making house calls ([Carter](https://www.annalsthoracicsurgery.org/article/S0003-4975(99)00293-3/fulltext)). Craig Morgan Teicher's account has him composing "the tiny poems that would define his career" on the backs of prescription pads, in the gaps between house calls and deliveries ([Teicher](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/articles/68731/william-carlos-williams-the-red-wheelbarrow)).

His maxim, Teicher notes, was "No ideas but in things," and the most famous of the things was a wheelbarrow: "so much depends / upon // a red wheel / barrow // glazed with rain / water // beside the white / chickens" ([Teicher](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/articles/68731/william-carlos-williams-the-red-wheelbarrow)). Nowhere, Teicher observes, does Williams tell us why so much depends; "he leaves us to ask, and answer, that question" ([Teicher](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/articles/68731/william-carlos-williams-the-red-wheelbarrow)).

Another signature poem is a note left by its speaker, who has eaten the plums that were in the icebox and that the note says were probably being saved for breakfast; it closes by asking forgiveness and praises the plums for being "so sweet / and so cold" ([Poets.org](https://poets.org/poem/just-say)). Someone's breakfast food, eaten and confessed, praised in the poem's last words for being cold as well as sweet.

In his Autobiography, as quoted by Carter, Williams describes medicine and writing as complementary: "they are two parts of a whole, that it is not two jobs at all, that one rests the man when the other fatigues him" ([Carter](https://www.annalsthoracicsurgery.org/article/S0003-4975(99)00293-3/fulltext)). Britannica reads his whole career the same way, crediting his "clarity and discreteness of his imagery" for making "the ordinary appear extraordinary" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/William-Carlos-Williams)).

The compendium pairs him with D180, the Daoist delight in the ordinary, as a cross-tradition reading of its own; it does not attribute Daoist commitments to Williams. What the pairing rests on is the operation the principle describes, attentive sensory pleasure in ordinary things: the plums are tasted as "so sweet and so cold," the wheelbarrow is seen "glazed with rain water." What the story does not establish is the principle's consequence: the Autobiography passage speaks to work rhythm, and the plum poem's speaker acts on appetite and eats food someone else may have reserved, so contentment and the end of chasing remain outside what the opened sources show. The principle's classical frame, DDJ 80's village and Zhuangzi's paradox, also stays outside this story.
