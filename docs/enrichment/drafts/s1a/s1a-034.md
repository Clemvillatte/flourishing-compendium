Biographical account · United States, 1883-1963 · Poetry Foundation, Poets.org, Britannica, and the Annals of Thoracic Surgery

William Carlos Williams was born in Rutherford, New Jersey in 1883, took his M.D. at the University of Pennsylvania in 1906, and, in Britannica's phrase, "returned in 1910 to a lifetime of poetry and medical practice in his hometown of Rutherford, New Jersey" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/William-Carlos-Williams)). That lifetime ran forty years of family practice and an estimated 2,000 deliveries from an office at 9 Ridge Road, and he kept making house calls when most doctors had stopped ([Carter](https://www.annalsthoracicsurgery.org/article/S0003-4975(99)00293-3/fulltext)). His poems came out of that life. Craig Morgan Teicher's account has him composing "the tiny poems that would define his career" on the backs of prescription pads, in the gaps between house calls and deliveries ([Teicher](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/articles/68731/william-carlos-williams-the-red-wheelbarrow)).

The tiny poems took the smallest possible subjects. His maxim, Teicher notes, was "No ideas but in things," and the most famous of the things was a wheelbarrow: "so much depends / upon // a red wheel / barrow // glazed with rain / water // beside the white / chickens" ([Teicher](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/articles/68731/william-carlos-williams-the-red-wheelbarrow)). Nowhere, Teicher observes, does Williams tell us why so much depends; "he leaves us to ask, and answer, that question" ([Teicher](https://www.poetryfoundation.org/articles/68731/william-carlos-williams-the-red-wheelbarrow)).

The other signature poem is a note about plums: "I have eaten / the plums / that were in / the icebox / and which / you were probably / saving / for breakfast // Forgive me / they were delicious / so sweet / and so cold" ([Poets.org](https://poets.org/poem/just-say)). Breakfast food, stolen and admitted, praised in the last three words for being cold as well as sweet.

Williams himself described the two jobs as one job. Questioned about how he managed both, he answered in the Autobiography: "they are two parts of a whole, that it is not two jobs at all, that one rests the man when the other fatigues him" ([Carter](https://www.annalsthoracicsurgery.org/article/S0003-4975(99)00293-3/fulltext)). Carter's closing line for his medical colleagues reads him as the poet who "portrayed the ordinary as extraordinary in verse" ([Carter](https://www.annalsthoracicsurgery.org/article/S0003-4975(99)00293-3/fulltext)), and Britannica opens the same way, crediting his "clarity and discreteness of his imagery" for making "the ordinary appear extraordinary" ([Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/William-Carlos-Williams)).

The compendium reads him for D180, the Daoist delight in the ordinary, as a cross-tradition pairing: Williams was not a Daoist. What he carries is the operation the principle describes, the noticing that lands on coarse food and finds it sweet: the plums are tasted as "so sweet and so cold," the wheelbarrow is seen "glazed with rain water," and the delight arrives without escalation past the object. The consequence is carried only partially: the poems refuse to reach past their objects, but the opened sources speak to his work rhythm, and none proves contentment or the end of chasing in his life. The principle's classical frame, DDJ 80's village and Zhuangzi's paradox, stays outside this story.
