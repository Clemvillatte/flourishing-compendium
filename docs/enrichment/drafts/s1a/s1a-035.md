In late 1967, Agnes Martin burned all of the art left in her New York studio, gave away her art supplies, and left. The move bewildered the art world: she was at the height of a career she had spent two decades pursuing. Asked why, she never gave one straight answer, only "I thought I would experiment in solitude, you know. Simple living." Years later she wrote, "I left New York in 1967 because every day I suddenly felt I wanted to die and it was connected with painting. It took me several years to find out that the cause was an overdeveloped sense of responsibility."

She spent the proceeds of a $5,000 arts grant on a Dodge pickup and an Airstream trailer, then spent a year and a half camping across Canada and the American West. "I drove all in the West and up in Canada and couldn't make up my mind where to stop," she recalled. What stopped her was odder than any plan: "I had a vision of an adobe brick. And I thought, that means I should go to New Mexico." She leased fifty acres on a remote mesa, lived in the trailer, and built an adobe house with her own hands.

Martin had a reason for the quiet, and she wrote it down. In her essays on the artist's life, she put it as a rule of her craft: "To live and work by inspiration you have to stop thinking. You have to hold your mind still in order to hear inspiration clearly."

Read that against her doctrine of where inspiration actually lives: "Inspiration is there all the time. For everyone whose mind is not clouded over with thoughts whether they realize it or not." And against what she called the natural rhythm of the thing: "Of course we know that an untroubled state of mind cannot last. So we say that inspiration comes and goes but really it is there all the time waiting for us to be untroubled again."

For Martin, the sediment was a sense of duty. The cure was not more effort, more exhibitions, more responsibility. It was a pickup truck, a trailer, a mesa, and years of letting the water go still. In 1974 she built a thirty-five-foot-square log studio on the property and began to paint again. Her first exhibition after the silence opened in March 1975, and she kept painting for thirty more years.

### Action

When your work goes dark, ask first whether the water has been stirred. Before adding effort, try two minutes of doing nothing at all, and see what becomes audible.
