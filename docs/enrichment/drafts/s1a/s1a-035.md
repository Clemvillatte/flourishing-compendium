Agnes Martin is usually told as an abstract painter who loved the desert. In 1967, at the height of her career, she stopped instead. She gave up her studio in New York, got rid of her possessions, and drove west in a pickup truck and camper. "I gave up all my possessions and put my pictures in the hands of a friend who tried to sell them," she later recalled, and she settled on a mesa in New Mexico to build a house with her own hands. She would not exhibit for seven years.

Her neighbours thought she had quit. She had not. Martin insisted that the pause was the work. In her short book The Untroubled Mind, drawn from the experiences of those years, she set out her position:

> "Awareness of stillness in our mind is an important step toward realization. Only when there is stillness can we see clearly and be more alert to all the information around us."
>
> "...an untroubled state of mind cannot last. So we say that inspiration comes and goes but really it is there all the time waiting for us to be untroubled again."

Notice what she is claiming. Inspiration is not a rare visitor who arrives if you work hard enough. It is the ordinary condition of a mind that is no longer clouded over, and it waits. The mud does not clear because you stir the water faster. It clears because the stirring stops.

Martin was ruthless about what stillness required. "Inspiration is there all the time," she wrote. "For everyone whose mind is not clouded over with thoughts whether they realize it or not." Thoughts here are not insight; they are sediment. When she resumed painting in 1974, she did so on the strength of what had settled in the quiet: grids and bands of colour that she made for the next thirty years, most of them in the same adobe house.

Her students wanted to know how to live like that, and she gave them a plain answer. "So we all enjoy awareness of stillness in our mind," she wrote, as if it were the most ordinary thing in the world. For her it was.

### Action

Before your next hard task, sit for two minutes and do nothing but let the water go still. Do not use the time to plan. When the task begins, notice whether the mud has to be stirred again before anything can be seen.
