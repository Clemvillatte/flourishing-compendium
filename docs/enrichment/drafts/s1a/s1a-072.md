Ecological account · the wolves, elk, willows, beavers and streams of Yellowstone National Park, via National Geographic Education and the International Wolf Center's 2024 review of the science.

Wolves were hunted out of Yellowstone, and the effects ran through everything downstream of them. "The elk's main predator was gone, and their population more than doubled", and the elk, grazers and browsers, "overgraze the entire park" ([NatGeo Education](https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/wolves-yellowstone/)). "Perhaps the elk's most devastating impact was how they affected the park's riverbanks": gathered in herds on the lush banks with no predator to fear, "the massive elk's hooves eroded the riverbanks, so the rivers and streams clouded with soil" ([NatGeo Education](https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/wolves-yellowstone/)). Wolves were reintroduced in 1995, and the popular account of what followed is a cascade: elk numbers down, "just the elk's fear of wolves gives the riverbank trees, like aspen and willow, a chance to regenerate", songbirds returning, stronger riverbanks, and "the return of the beaver dams creates new habitats for fish, amphibians, reptiles, and even otters" ([NatGeo Education](https://education.nationalgeographic.org/resource/wolves-yellowstone/)).

The connection with B015 is the web itself: no strand in this story exists alone. The willows are the elk's food and the beaver's material; the beaver's dams hold the water that the willows need; the streams' shape follows all of them; the wolves are in the elk's feeding habits. The removal of one animal reaches the fish and the color of the water.

The research record keeps the story honest. The International Wolf Center's review reports the popular narrative of 41 wolves reintroduced from 1995-1997 causing a dramatic recovery, and then the 2024 experimental work: plots fenced, dammed, both, or neither, in which willows in the unaltered ground "were still being suppressed by intense ungulate browsing and limited by available water", so that "willows had not yet recovered from prior degradation". The conclusion holds both halves: "recovery of aspen and willow communities in Yellowstone since wolf reintroduction has not been as strong or widespread as claimed. Still, there has been some recovery, suggesting a trophic cascade has unfolded" ([International Wolf Center](https://wolf.org/wolf-info/international-wolf-magazine/do-wolves-really-change-rivers-2/)). I read the pairing as interbeing with its history kept in: the connections are real, and so is the fact that a web once broken does not simply rewind.
