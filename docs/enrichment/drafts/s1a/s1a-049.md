Massachusetts, 1845-1847 · Henry David Thoreau, Walden (1854), chapters "The Bean-Field", "Sounds" and "Solitude" · a memoir, Project Gutenberg raw text

In his first summer at the pond, Thoreau kept a bean field, seven miles of rows when added together, and he also kept something rarer: whole mornings with nothing in them. The memoir's account of those days offers a companion picture for what P089, drawing on Dewey's language of outgoing and incoming energy, calls the rhythm of activity and receptivity. ([Walden](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-0.txt))

The doing side, as he tells it ([Walden, "The Bean-Field"](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-0.txt)):

> "Meanwhile my beans, the length of whose rows, added together, was seven miles already planted, were impatient to be hoed"

> "I came to love my rows, my beans, though so many more than I wanted. They attached me to the earth, and so I got strength like Antæus."

> "I cherish them, I hoe them, early and late I have an eye to them; and this is my day's work."

The undergoing side comes from the same first summer, and he ranks it above the work ([Walden, "Sounds"](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-0.txt)):

> "I did not read books the first summer; I hoed beans. Nay, I often did better than this. There were times when I could not afford to sacrifice the bloom of the present moment to any work, whether of the head or hands. I love a broad margin to my life."

> "I sat in my sunny doorway from sunrise till noon, rapt in a revery, amidst the pines and hickories and sumachs, in undisturbed solitude and stillness, while the birds sing around or flitted noiseless through the house, until by the sun falling in at my west window, or the noise of some traveller's wagon on the distant highway, I was reminded of the lapse of time. I grew in those seasons like corn in the night, and they were far better than any work of the hands would have been. They were not time subtracted from my life, but so much over and above my usual allowance. I realized what the Orientals mean by contemplation and the forsaking of works."

The two poles meet on a rainy day. The rain stops the hoeing, and he rules the interruption the better half ([Walden, "Solitude"](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/205/205-0.txt)):

> "The gentle rain which waters my beans and keeps me in the house to-day is not drear and melancholy, but good for me too. Though it prevents my hoeing them, it is of far more worth than my hoeing."

My reading: the two poles of the day sit side by side in the chapters. The field is outgoing energy: the rows attached him to the earth, "and so I got strength like Antæus." The doorway is incoming energy: he introduces it with "Sometimes, in a summer morning," and closes it with the claim that those seasons "were not time subtracted from my life, but so much over and above my usual allowance." The rain passage resists treating an interrupted workday simply as a loss; it rules the reception worth more than the hoeing it stopped. Taken together, the passages illustrate making room for receptivity inside a life of work. They do not document a fixed daily schedule, and they do not show him revising his next action in response to what he took in; Dewey's fuller account, in which an experience is made by the union of doing and undergoing, is drawn on by P089 rather than carried here.

**Principles:**
- [[P089 — Ride the rhythm of activity and receptivity|89. Ride the rhythm of activity and receptivity]] — the memoir pairs the bean field's doing with the doorway's undergoing and rules an interrupted day of reception worth more than the work it stopped
