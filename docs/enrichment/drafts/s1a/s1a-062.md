Tuscumbia, Alabama, 1880-1887 · Helen Keller's own account and Anne Sullivan's letters in The Story of My Life · a biographical narrative, Project Gutenberg raw text

The child who could not make herself understood kicked and screamed, and the teacher who arrived in 1887 left an account of what happened to that force. Between the two documents sits P074's claim, that impulses are raw material whose arrangement decides what they become ([The Story of My Life](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2397/pg2397.txt)).

Keller's own memory of the frustration ([The Story of My Life](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2397/pg2397.txt)):

> "I could not understand, and was vexed. I moved my lips and gesticulated frantically without result. This made me so angry at times that I kicked and screamed until I was exhausted."

Her teacher saw the same energy from outside ([The Story of My Life](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2397/pg2397.txt)):

> "She has tyrannized over everybody, her mother, her father, the servants, the little darkies who play with her, and nobody had ever seriously disputed her will, except occasionally her brother James, until I came; and like all tyrants she holds tenaciously to her divine right to do as she pleases."

> "Every thwarted desire was the signal for a passionate outburst, and as she grew older and stronger, these tempests became more violent."

Two weeks of work later, Sullivan reported the turn ([The Story of My Life](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/2397/pg2397.txt)):

> "The wild little creature of two weeks ago has been transformed into a gentle child."

> "The little savage has learned her first lesson in obedience, and finds the yoke easy. It now remains my pleasant task to direct and mould the beautiful intelligence that is beginning to stir in the child-soul."

My reading: P074's plain words treat an impulse as a starting point rather than a verdict, and the question as what organizes it. The Keller of 1886 is a case study in an impulse meeting no arrangement at all: the desire to be understood, with no language to carry it, became fury, and the fury ruled the household. Sullivan's letters do not describe a war on the child's nature. She frames her work as obedience, and the story does not claim she held Dewey's view of redirection; what the record shows is that the force was never removed. The will that made a tyrant of the house is the same tenacity that, once it had a channel, drove the learning: the thwarted desire that once became outbursts became the hunt for words. Sullivan's last quoted line is the principle's whole claim in her own voice: the task is "to direct and mould the beautiful intelligence," material to be shaped, not an enemy to be broken. The story does not carry Dewey's fear example, which the principle page supplies, nor the outlet exercise.

**Principles:**
- [[P074 — Treat impulses as raw material, not enemies|74. Treat impulses as raw material, not enemies]] — Keller's thwarted will, expressed in tantrums, becomes under Sullivan's direction the tenacity that learns, the same force redirected rather than removed
