Biographical account with primary text · Albert Einstein, "Religion and Science" (1930), the 1929 Spinoza telegram, and Autobiographical Notes (quoted via Wikipedia)

In his 1930 essay "Religion and Science", Einstein distinguished three human impulses behind religious belief, fear, moral need, and a third he named: "I shall call it cosmic religious feeling", a kind of feeling that "knows no dogma and no God conceived in man's image" ([Religion and Science](https://merrell-wolff.org/sites/default/files/Religion%20and%20Science.pdf)). His own life gives the distinction a story. His account of his childhood religion ends abruptly: "... I came—though the child of entirely irreligious (Jewish) parents—to a deep religiousness, which, however, reached an abrupt end at the age of twelve. Through the reading of popular scientific books I soon reached the conviction that much in the stories of the Bible could not be true" ([Autobiographical Notes, quoted in Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_and_philosophical_views_of_Albert_Einstein)). The container broke; the question is what, if anything, was inside it.

On 24 April 1929, Einstein cabled Rabbi Herbert S. Goldstein in German: "I believe in Spinoza's God, who reveals himself in the harmony of all that exists, not in a God who concerns himself with the fate and the doings of mankind" ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_and_philosophical_views_of_Albert_Einstein)). A year later he published the essay "Religion and Science" (1930), where the distinction is spelled out: cosmic religious feeling "knows no dogma and no God conceived in man's image" ([Religion and Science](https://merrell-wolff.org/sites/default/files/Religion%20and%20Science.pdf)). What the individual feels, in his description, is "the futility of human desires and aims and the sublimity and marvelous order which reveal themselves both in nature and in the world of thought", and he "wants to experience the universe as a single significant whole" ([Religion and Science](https://merrell-wolff.org/sites/default/files/Religion%20and%20Science.pdf)).

The essay then makes the claim that gave his position its edge: "I maintain that the cosmic religious feeling is the strongest and noblest motive for scientific research", and "it is the most important function of art and science to awaken this feeling and keep it alive in those who are receptive to it" ([Religion and Science](https://merrell-wolff.org/sites/default/files/Religion%20and%20Science.pdf)). The feeling needs no doctrine to be had; science, of all things, is one of its keepers.

Read beside P187, Einstein is one documented case of the principle's core distinction: a religious quality of experience, awe at what his essay calls the "sublimity and marvelous order", carried by a man who had dropped the doctrinal container of his childhood. The essay runs the relationship both ways: art and science awaken and keep alive the cosmic religious feeling, and that feeling is "the strongest and noblest motive for scientific research". The draft draws no wider conclusion than his own account; it does not claim Dewey's specific proposals or that everyone leaving a tradition keeps the goods.
