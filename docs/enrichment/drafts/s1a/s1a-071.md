Biographical account · Aron Ralston (b. 1975), outdoorsman, via the Wikipedia article on his 2003 accident.

On April 26, 2003, in a solo descent of Bluejohn Canyon in Utah, Ralston dislodged a boulder that pinned his right wrist to the canyon wall. He spent five days rationing roughly 350 ml of water and two burritos, "repeatedly trying to extricate his arm", and after three days of trying to lift and chip away at the boulder he prepared to amputate the trapped arm to escape ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aron_Ralston)). The turn came as an epiphany: "he could break his radius and ulna using torque against his trapped arm". He did so, then amputated his forearm with the dull two-inch knife and pliers of his multi-tool; the process took an hour, with a CamelBak tube for a tourniquet and the major arteries left until last ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aron_Ralston)).

The connection with B072 is offered with its register named: this is relinquishment at the scale of the body, and the page's practice is quieter. What the story carries is the shift itself. For five days the effort went to freeing the trapped thing, and the escape arrived only when the effort turned to releasing it. Ralston later said that if he had amputated earlier he would have bled to death before being found, and that if he had not done it he would have been found dead days later ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aron_Ralston)), which the story's own account documents in his autobiography Between a Rock and a Hard Place and the film made from it ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aron_Ralston)).

Not carried: the page's breath-paced practice of letting a thought or mood run without servicing it; the sutta's relinquishment is trained one breath at a time, and no part of the canyon story shows that training.
