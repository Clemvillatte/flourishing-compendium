Scientific monograph · Duchenne de Boulogne, Mecanisme de la physionomie humaine, Paris, 1862

In a Paris hospital in the 1850s, a doctor holds electrodes to the face of an old patient and photographs what appears: smiles, grief, terror, on a face whose owner feels none of them. One muscle refuses the procedure. The old man's mouth can be made to smile on command; the ring of muscle around his eyes cannot, and Duchenne concludes that this one is moved only by the feeling itself. His 1862 monograph turns on that refusal. ([Duchenne, Mecanisme de la physionomie humaine, NLM full-text OCR](https://collections.nlm.nih.gov/ocr/nlm:nlmuid-60221710RX1-mvpart))

Because facial stimulation is disagreeable, he explains that "j'ai choisi, pour sujet principal de mes expériences, un homme chez lequel la sensibilité de la face était peu développée" — he chose as the principal subject of his experiments a man in whom the sensitivity of the face was little developed. ([Duchenne 1862, pp. 33-34](https://collections.nlm.nih.gov/ocr/nlm:nlmuid-60221710RX1-mvpart))

Duchenne saw the modern objection himself: might the artificial contraction work backwards and produce a feeling? "La contraction partielle d'un muscle qui préside à une expression ne pourrait-elle pas réagir sur l'âme, et produire sympathiquement une impression intérieure" — might the contraction of a muscle that governs an expression react upon the soul and produce an inner impression? His answer came from the recently dead: the same movements could be produced on faces whose souls had left, so a muscle's contraction, by itself, carries no feeling with it. An expression imposed from outside is a mask. ([Duchenne 1862, p. 34](https://collections.nlm.nih.gov/ocr/nlm:nlmuid-60221710RX1-mvpart))

The book's most quoted finding sits in the aesthetic section. Among the muscles of joy, the great zygomatic draws the mouth into a smile and obeys the will. But "le muscle de la bienveillance (l'orbiculaire inférieur, E, fig. 1), est également rebelle à volonté, et n'obéit qu'à ce doux mouvement de l'âme qui rend le regard sympathique": the muscle of benevolence, the lower orbicular of the eye, is equally rebellious to the will, obeying only that sweet movement of the soul which makes the gaze sympathetic. And "la joie fausse, le rire menteur, ne sauraient provoquer la contraction de ce dernier muscle" — false joy, the lying laugh, cannot call this last muscle into play. ([Duchenne 1862, p. 183](https://collections.nlm.nih.gov/ocr/nlm:nlmuid-60221710RX1-mvpart))

For P181 the story marks both ends of the lever. What the will commands, the mouth's part of a smile, the set of the shoulders, is regulable, and the direction of causality can start there. What the will cannot command answers only to the feeling itself, which is why a commanded smile can accompany a mood without fabricating the deepest part of it. The old man's electric smiles show the limit from the other side: an expression that is not the person's own action produces nothing felt. French quotations are verbatim from the opened 1862 text; English renderings are this page's own translations. ([Duchenne 1862](https://collections.nlm.nih.gov/ocr/nlm:nlmuid-60221710RX1-mvpart))

**Principles:**
- [[P181 — Regulate feeling by regulating expression|P181 Regulate feeling by regulating expression]]: Duchenne's own boundary case — the mouth's smile obeys the will and can act as the lever, the eye's smile does not, and an expression imposed from outside moves no feeling at all.
