Scientific monograph · Duchenne de Boulogne, Mecanisme de la physionomie humaine, Paris, 1862

A doctor holds electrodes to the face of an old patient and photographs the muscles of expression raised one at a time, joy and sorrow and fear assembled on a living face. In his 1862 monograph Duchenne draws a distinction among the muscles of joy that turns on the will: the muscle that pulls the mouth into a smile obeys it, and the muscle around the eyes, which he names the muscle of benevolence, does not. ([Duchenne, Mecanisme de la physionomie humaine, NLM full-text OCR](https://collections.nlm.nih.gov/ocr/nlm:nlmuid-60221710RX1-mvpart))

Because facial stimulation is disagreeable, he explains "j'ai choisi, pour sujet principal de mes expériences, un homme chez lequel la sensibilité de la face était peu développée" — he chose as the principal subject of his experiments a man in whom facial sensitivity was little developed. ([Duchenne 1862, pp. 33-34](https://collections.nlm.nih.gov/ocr/nlm:nlmuid-60221710RX1-mvpart))

Duchenne asked the question himself: "La contraction partielle d'un muscle qui préside à une expression ne pourrait-elle pas réagir sur l'âme, et produire sympathiquement une impression intérieure" — might the contraction of a muscle that governs an expression react upon the soul and produce an inner impression? His answer came from the recently dead: the same movements, he reports, could be produced on faces whose souls had left. On his argument this showed that the movement of an expressive muscle does not require an inner emotion. ([Duchenne 1862, p. 34](https://collections.nlm.nih.gov/ocr/nlm:nlmuid-60221710RX1-mvpart))

In the aesthetic section, among the muscles of joy, the great zygomatic draws the mouth into a smile and obeys the will. But "le muscle de la bienveillance (l'orbiculaire inférieur, E, fig. 1), est également rebelle à volonté, et n'obéit qu'à ce doux mouvement de l'âme qui rend le regard sympathique": the muscle of benevolence, the lower orbicular of the eye, is equally rebellious to the will, obeying only that sweet movement of the soul that makes the gaze sympathetic. And "la joie fausse, le rire menteur, ne sauraient provoquer la contraction de ce dernier muscle": false joy, the lying laugh, cannot call this last muscle into play. ([Duchenne 1862, pp. 183-184](https://collections.nlm.nih.gov/ocr/nlm:nlmuid-60221710RX1-mvpart))

For P181 the story is a historical comparison rather than a demonstration. Duchenne distinguished movements he regarded as voluntary from expressions he associated with felt joy, and the distinction gives the principle a documented account of where the will reaches in the face. The monograph does not test whether changing expression changes feeling in a living person, and this page claims no demonstration of either the effectiveness or the limits of expression-based mood regulation. French quotations are verbatim from the opened 1862 text; English renderings are this page's own translations. ([Duchenne 1862](https://collections.nlm.nih.gov/ocr/nlm:nlmuid-60221710RX1-mvpart))
