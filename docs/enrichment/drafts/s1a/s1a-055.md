Philadelphia, 1730s-1780s · Benjamin Franklin, Autobiography, the moral-perfection project and the civic section · a memoir, Project Gutenberg raw text

In his twenties Benjamin Franklin set out on what he called the bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection, and the instrument he built for it is one of the earliest working versions of P179's daily loop: a morning question that opens the day, an evening question that closes it, and a little book in between where the day's evidence is marked ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt)).

The method was designed around daily examination ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt)):

> "Conceiving then, that, agreeably to the advice of Pythagoras in his Golden Verses, daily examination would be necessary, I contrived the following method for conducting that examination."

> "I made a little book, in which I allotted a page for each of the virtues. I rul'd each page with red ink, so as to have seven columns, one for each day of the week"

> "I might mark, by a little black spot, every fault I found upon examination to have been committed respecting that virtue upon that day."

His page for the virtue of Order carried a schedule for the whole day, and its two questions mark the loop's ends ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt)):

> "The Morning. Question. What good shall I do this day?"

> "Evening. Question. What good have I done to-day?"

The results came back as evidence, not as feelings ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt)):

> "I was surpris'd to find myself so much fuller of faults than I had imagined; but I had the satisfaction of seeing them diminish."

The other half of the principle, savoring, Franklin stated in his own words, later in the book ([Autobiography](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/20203/pg20203.txt)):

> "Human felicity is produced not so much by great pieces of good fortune that seldom happen, as by little advantages that occur every day."

My reading: P179 takes Dewey's definition of reflective thought, the consideration of grounds and consequences, and applies it to a day: examine what happened and what it tends toward. Franklin's scheme is that application, run as a schedule. The day opens with a question about the good to be done and closes with a question about the good actually done, and the little book between them records the examination's findings as black spots, which let him watch his faults diminish. The savoring half has the same shape in his account: happiness is produced by little advantages that occur every day, and his example of the young man who learns to shave, escaping the barbers' dull razors, ends with someone who "enjoys daily the pleasure" of what he has learned to do. The loop Franklin ran is honest about failure too; he reports intermissions, and he says he always carried the little book with him. The story does not carry Dewey's definition of reflective thought, which the principle page supplies itself; it carries the loop, opened and closed by questions.

- [[P179 — Close the loop with savoring and review|179.
