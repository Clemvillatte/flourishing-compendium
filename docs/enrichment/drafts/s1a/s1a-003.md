*Warsaw, Frankfurt, London, New York, 1907–1972 · Abraham Joshua Heschel, The Sabbath (1951); the biography from the Jewish Theological Seminary's timeline*

In October 1938 the Gestapo came for Abraham Joshua Heschel, a thirty-one-year-old rabbi and scholar living in a rented room in Frankfurt. The seminary's timeline records the scene plainly: "He had one hour to pack two suitcases," then a three-day train journey to Warsaw, standing the whole way, the passengers denied entry into Poland and with "not enough food to sustain the overcrowded passengers." Six weeks before the German invasion he got out through London, and in March 1940 he arrived in the United States. His sister Esther was killed in the German bombing of Warsaw; his mother was murdered by Nazis who broke into her flat in the Warsaw Ghetto, where his sister Gittel also died; his sister Devorah was deported from Vienna to Auschwitz, where she perished.

In 1951 he published The Sabbath, a short book about a day. Its argument is an inversion of the working week. The Jewish sanctuary, he wrote, is not in space but in time:

> The meaning of the Sabbath is to celebrate time rather than space. Six days a week we live under the tyranny of things of space; on the Sabbath we try to become attuned to holiness in time. It is a day on which we are called upon to share in what is eternal in time, to turn from the results of creation to the mystery of creation; from the world of creation to the creation of the world.

And:

> The Sabbaths are our great cathedrals; and our Holy of Holies is a shrine that neither the Romans nor the Germans were able to burn.

The inversion the book builds on is this:

> The work on weekdays and the rest on the seventh day are correlated. The Sabbath is the inspirer, the other days the inspired.

The compendium's comparison to D039 runs through that sentence. The principle says make rest the root of activity: not a refueling pause inserted between stretches of work, but the center the week travels from, the way the Daodejing's prince marches all day while staying near his baggage waggons. Heschel's Sabbath states the same structure with its religious meaning intact: in his account the seventh day is not the week's recovery period but its source, the inspirer that the six days express, and a week with no such center is, on his terms, unrooted activity. The comparison, not the productivity point, is what this page records. What the story does not carry is the body-level side of Daoist rest, the stillness and nourishing-life practices; the coverage matrix names it for another story.
