Literary narrative · a scene from Victor Hugo's Les Misérables (1862), via the Hapgood translation (Project Gutenberg ebook #135).

Jean Valjean, the released convict who has just stolen his host's silver, is brought back by gendarmes, and the bishop answers them with the cover the thief had already told: "And he told you... that it had been given to him by a kind old fellow of a priest with whom he had passed the night? I see how the matter stands. And you have brought him back here? It is a mistake" ([Hugo, Gutenberg #135](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/135/pg135.txt)). Then, with the man freed, the bishop gives him more than was stolen: "Well, but how is this? I gave you the candlesticks too, which are of silver like the rest, and for which you can certainly get two hundred francs. Why did you not carry them away with your forks and spoons?" ([Hugo, Gutenberg #135](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/135/pg135.txt)). The gift is put into his hands — "My friend... before you go, here are your candlesticks. Take them" ([Hugo, Gutenberg #135](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/135/pg135.txt)) — and the reason follows in the novel's most quoted speech: "Jean Valjean, my brother, you no longer belong to evil, but to good. It is your soul that I buy from you; I withdraw it from black thoughts and the spirit of perdition, and I give it to God" ([Hugo, Gutenberg #135](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/135/pg135.txt)).

The connection with D125 is the policy's shape: the bishop's goodness arrives before any deserving, survives the theft, and then doubles the gift. Nothing in the exchange conditions the kindness on what the man becomes, and the novel spends a thousand pages reporting what the man becomes. That report is Hugo's invention, and the draft claims nothing from it about the world; what it carries is the shape of the policy itself, good to the good and the not-good alike, asking nothing first.
