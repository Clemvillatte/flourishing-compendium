Grasmere and Ullswater, 1802-1807 · Dorothy Wordsworth's journal, William Wordsworth's Preface and Poems in Two Volumes · a composition narrative, Project Gutenberg raw texts

The best-known poem in English about joy has a paper trail, and the trail shows the difference P113 draws between venting a feeling and shaping it. On April 15, 1802, William and Dorothy Wordsworth walked the shore of Ullswater; that evening Dorothy recorded the day in her journal, and two years later William made the poem. Nothing was discharged anywhere in the process; the feeling was kept and worked ([Journals of Dorothy Wordsworth](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/42856/pg42856.txt), [Poems in Two Volumes](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8824/pg8824.txt)).

Dorothy's entry, written the same day ([Journals of Dorothy Wordsworth](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/42856/pg42856.txt)):

> "When we were in the woods beyond Gowbarrow Park we saw a few daffodils close to the water-side. We fancied that the sea had floated the seeds ashore, and that the little colony had so sprung up."

> "I never saw daffodils so beautiful. They grew among the mossy stones about and above them; some rested their heads upon these stones, as on a pillow, for weariness; and the rest tossed and reeled and danced, and seemed as if they verily laughed with the wind, that blew upon them over the lake; they looked so gay, ever glancing, ever changing."

The poem, published in 1807 ([Poems in Two Volumes](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/8824/pg8824.txt)):

> "I wandered lonely as a Cloud / That floats on high o'er Vales and Hills, / When all at once I saw a crowd / A host of dancing Daffodills; / Along the Lake, beneath the trees, / Ten thousand dancing in the breeze."

> "For oft when on my couch I lie / In vacant or in pensive mood, / They flash upon that inward eye / Which is the bliss of solitude, / And then my heart with pleasure fills, / And dances with the Daffodils."

And Wordsworth's own account of how such poems come to be, from the Preface ([Prose Works](https://www.gutenberg.org/cache/epub/16550/pg16550.txt)):

> "I have said that poetry is the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings: it takes its origin from emotion recollected in tranquillity: the emotion is contemplated till, by a species of re-action, the tranquillity gradually disappears, and an emotion, kindred to that which was before the subject of contemplation, is gradually produced, and does itself actually exist in the mind."

My reading: P113 quotes Dewey's line that to discharge is to get rid of and to express is to stay by and carry forward in development. The composition of this poem is that sentence with dates on it. The journal entry is the raw material: weather, stones, a guess about seeds, flowers resting their heads "for weariness." The poem, made two years later, selects and reshapes. It drops the mossy stones and the seed theory, keeps the dancing and the lake, adds the "inward eye" and the return visits on the couch. The Preface explains what happened in the gap: the emotion is contemplated, and a kindred emotion is "gradually produced" in the mind before composition begins. The final stanza is not the day itself but the day worked into a form that can be re-entered. Nothing in the story promises relief or a timetable; what it shows is a feeling given a shape that holds it available. The affect-labeling and expressive-writing research the principle page lists as parallels is not carried here.
