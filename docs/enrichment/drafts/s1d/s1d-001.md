China, 9th century · Chan story preserved in Dōgen's Shōbōgenzō fascicle Shin Fukatoku (1241); Diamond Sutra, section 18; John C. H. Wu's retelling in The Golden Age of Zen (1967)

Deshan Xuanjian was the recognized master of the Diamond Sutra in ninth-century China, and he set out for the south to destroy a rival school whose claim, in John C. H. Wu's retelling of his indignation, was that "those little devils of the south are bragging of pointing directly at the mind of man, of seeing one's self-nature and attaining Buddhahood immediately!" ([Wu, pp. 150-154](https://terebess.hu/zen/deshan.html)) On the road he stopped to buy a snack from an old woman, and a question about eating stopped his career as a scholar.

The Chinese word for the snack, diǎn xīn, means "to point at the mind" ([satyori, section 18 note](https://satyori.com/source-texts/diamond-sutra/18/)). Asked what he carried, Deshan answered as the fully qualified expert: "I am the Fully Perfected Lord of the Diamond-Cutting Scripture. I have so mastered this Scripture that there is nothing in It that I do not understand" ([Shin Fukatoku, pp. 189-190](https://www.thezensite.com/ZenTeachings/Dogen_Teachings/Shobogenzo/017shinFukatoku.pdf)). Her question used his own text against him: "I once heard the part in the Diamond-Cutting Scripture where it says, 'The mind of the past cannot be held onto; the mind of the present cannot be held onto; the mind of the future cannot be held onto.' Which mind do you think you will refresh with these rice cakes? If the reverend monk is able to say, I will sell you some rice cakes. If the reverend monk is unable to say, I will not sell you any." ([Shin Fukatoku, pp. 189-190](https://www.thezensite.com/ZenTeachings/Dogen_Teachings/Shobogenzo/017shinFukatoku.pdf)) The sutra she quoted is the one he carried. The question was aimed at the mouth about to eat, and his commentaries had nothing to say about that mouth. Dōgen reports that the expert "was so flabbergasted that he did not recall how he would have customarily responded" ([Shin Fukatoku, p. 190](https://www.thezensite.com/ZenTeachings/Dogen_Teachings/Shobogenzo/017shinFukatoku.pdf)).

She pointed him to the master Longtan. One evening Deshan asked for a light to leave by. "Ryutan then took a paper candle and proffered it to Tokusan. Just as Tokusan touched it, Ryutan blew out the flame, whereupon Tokusan suddenly had a great awakening and immediately bowed in respect" ([Shin Fukatoku, pp. 193-194](https://www.thezensite.com/ZenTeachings/Dogen_Teachings/Shobogenzo/017shinFukatoku.pdf)). The next day, telling himself that "[a]n exhaustive discussion of the abstruse is like a hair thrown into the infinite void", he burned the commentaries he had carried ([Wu, pp. 150-154](https://terebess.hu/zen/deshan.html)).

Read against B129, the story turns on the gap between knowing about a text and standing in front of the thing the text describes. ([B129](https://github.com/Clemvillatte/flourishing-compendium/blob/main/content/principles/B129%20%E2%80%94%20Point%20directly%20at%20experience.md)) One question about buying food reached past every line of the commentaries because it was pointed at Deshan's experience at that stall. The lamp scene repeats the move without words: the teaching arrives as an event, in the dark, as his hand reaches out.

**Principles:**
- [[B129 — Point directly at experience|B129 Point directly at experience]]: the old woman's question bypasses commentary and points at Deshan's present act of eating, which his complete textual mastery cannot answer; the lamp scene delivers the teaching as a direct event rather than as text.
