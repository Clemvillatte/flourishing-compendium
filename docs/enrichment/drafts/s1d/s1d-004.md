Columbia River, Pacific Northwest · ceremonial account of the Wyam people and neighboring tribes at Celilo Falls; sources: the Northwest Power and Conservation Council, the Columbia River Inter-Tribal Fish Commission, National Geographic, Indian Country Today and the U.S. National Library of Medicine

At Celilo Falls the fishing year began with a stop. The first salmon of the spring was met with a ceremony, and "the fishing couldn't begin, however, until the salmon had been shown the proper respect" ([National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/searching-for-wild-pacific-northwest-salmon-from-river-to-table)). Lewis and Clark "witnessed a first-salmon ceremony at Celilo Falls in the spring of 1806 on their return journey" ([NW Council](https://www.nwcouncil.org/reports/columbia-river-history/firstsalmonceremony/)), and the falls were a gathering place where "as many as 5,000 would come in on horseback from as far away as the Plains" ([Indian Country Today](https://ictnews.org/archive/celilo-falls-gone-and-forgotten-part-one/)). In 1957 the falls vanished under a reservoir in four and a half hours, and the feast still happens every spring.

The ceremony's order of operations carried the meaning. "The salmon chief of the tribe would select a fisher to catch the first salmon", a fisher who "would undergo a blessing or a purification" before entering the river; the fish was "carefully prepared, cooked and distributed to the people"; "the head of the fish would be kept pointed upriver to show the salmon's spirit the way home"; and "the bones would be carefully cleaned and returned to the river, where it was believed the salmon would reconstitute itself and continue its journey" ([NW Council](https://www.nwcouncil.org/reports/columbia-river-history/firstsalmonceremony/)). The feasts honor Salmon "for being the first to volunteer when Creator asked all the plants and animals for a gift to help humans survive" ([CRITFC](https://critfc.org/2013/04/01/honoring-first-salmon/)), and the ritual had a job to do: "the ambassador from the salmon tribe can return to its underwater kin and tell of the respect it received from the human beings living upstream, so that more of its kind will ascend and nourish the people" ([National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/searching-for-wild-pacific-northwest-salmon-from-river-to-table)).

The ending is the part that tests the image. "At 10 a.m. on March 10, 1957, its inch-thick reinforced steel gates crash shut, forcing a ferocious back flow that in 4½ hours floods everything in its way, including the site of the ancient village Celilo and the rocks from which tribal fishermen netted salmon" ([NLM Native Voices](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/nativevoices/timeline/632.html)). The falls are gone; "the first salmon feast still happens in the spring" ([Indian Country Today](https://ictnews.org/archive/celilo-falls-gone-and-forgotten-part-one/)), at the village longhouse in mid-April ([National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/travel/article/searching-for-wild-pacific-northwest-salmon-from-river-to-table)).

The compendium's reading for D006: the ceremony is return treated as fulfillment, not loss. Daodejing 16 says the return to the root is "a reporting that they have fulfilled their appointed end" ([D006](https://github.com/Clemvillatte/flourishing-compendium/blob/main/content/principles/D006%20%E2%80%94%20Move%20by%20return%20(fu)%20%E2%80%94%20everything%20renews%20in%20cycles.md)), and the fishery's whole year was arranged around that reporting: wait for the return, honor it, send it back so it can continue. The dam is the counter-image, a plan with no stopping in it, and the story keeps both.

**Principles:**
- [[D006 — Move by return (fu) — everything renews in cycles|D006 Move by return (fu): everything renews in cycles]]: the First Salmon ceremony arranges the fishing year around the salmon's return as fulfillment, holding the return open by sending the bones back to the river; the 1957 flooding is the counter-image of a plan with no stopping, and the feast's continuation the renewal that outlasts it.
