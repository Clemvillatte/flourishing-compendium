United States, 1878 · essay from Charles Sanders Peirce's "How to Make Our Ideas Clear", the second of the six Illustrations of the Logic of Science papers in Popular Science Monthly, with the text at peirce.org and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy

In January 1878 the Popular Science Monthly printed the second of six papers by Charles Sanders Peirce under the title Illustrations of the Logic of Science ([Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2004/entries/peirce/)). In it Peirce proposed a rule for getting an idea truly clear. A belief, he had argued, is a habit, and "what a thing means is simply what habits it involves" ([Peirce 1878, Section II](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p119.html)). So the test of a conception is what its object would do: "Consider what effects, that might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then, our conception of these effects is the whole of our conception of the object" ([Peirce 1878, Section II](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p119.html)).

His first example was almost embarrassingly small. "Let us ask what we mean by calling a thing hard", he wrote, and answered that the whole conception lies in its effects: "There is absolutely no difference between a hard thing and a soft thing so long as they are not brought to the test" ([Peirce 1878, Section III](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p119.html)). Then the question that still gets asked in philosophy classes: "Suppose, then, that a diamond could be crystallized in the midst of a cushion of soft cotton, and should remain there until it was finally burned up. Would it be false to say that that diamond was soft?" Peirce's answer is deliberately deflating. Nothing would be false in such a way of speaking, only clumsy; the facts would be the same facts, arranged badly ([Peirce 1878, Section III](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p119.html)).

The second example was picked to offend everyone equally. Catholics hold that the consecrated elements are literally flesh and blood, though they keep all the sensible qualities of wafer and wine. Peirce, dropping the theology as soon as he has used it, rules the dispute verbal: "to talk of something as having all the sensible characters of wine, yet being in reality blood, is senseless jargon", and "It is foolish for Catholics and Protestants to fancy themselves in disagreement about the elements of the sacrament, if they agree in regard to all their sensible effects, here and hereafter" ([Peirce 1878, Section II](https://www.peirce.org/writings/p119.html)).

The compendium's reading for P012: the maxim works as a refusal to let words carry weight they cannot cash. If two people agree on every conceivable difference in practice, on what would be seen and done, their remaining disagreement is about nothing. The story stays inside the 1878 paper; Peirce's later quarrel with William James over what pragmatism had become, and his renaming of his own view as pragmaticism, is not carried here.

**Principles:**
- [[P012 — An idea's meaning is its practical effects|P012 An idea's meaning is its practical effects]]: Peirce's own two illustrations of the maxim, the diamond that was never scratched and the wine that is called blood, show the test doing its work; a conception is cashed out in the effects its truth would make conceivable.
