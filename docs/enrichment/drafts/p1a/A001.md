# A001 — Make eudaimonia the organising end of your life (candidate, pending advisor QC)

---

Chapter 7 of Book I states the definition the principle carries:

> "human good turns out to be activity of soul in accordance with virtue, and if there are more than one virtue, in accordance with the best and most complete."
> (NE I.7, [W. D. Ross's translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.1.i.html))

The activity is [[Virtue|in accordance with virtue]]. Chapter 13 draws the consequence: "Since happiness is an activity of soul in accordance with perfect virtue, we must consider the nature of virtue" (NE I.13, Ross). And the life has to be whole: "one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day; and so too one day, or a short time, does not make a man blessed and happy" (NE I.7). The compendium's practical reading: when a choice is hard, ask what kind of human life it is helping you become capable of living. (NE I.7, I.13.)

**Plain words** Two lives can look identical from the outside and differ completely: one is a sequence of pleasant weekends, the other is building toward something. The test Aristotle offers is the complete-life one. A choice that feels good now might close doors you will want in ten years, and a hard one might open them. Ask what the choice contributes to the whole, because the whole is what gets judged.

*For example:* Taking the job with the better title but a commute that eats your evenings with your kids: the real question is what that trade does to the life you're building.
