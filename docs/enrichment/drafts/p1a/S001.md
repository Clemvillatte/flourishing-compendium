Chapter 1 of the Enchiridion draws the line:

> "Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions. Things not in our control are body, property, reputation, command, and, in one word, whatever are not our own actions."
> (Ench. 1, [Elizabeth Carter's translation](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/epicench.html))

The chapter's warning is that misclassifying is what costs you: "if you suppose that things which are slavish by nature are also free, and that what belongs to others is your own, then you will be hindered. You will lament, you will be disturbed, and you will find fault both with gods and men." Chapter 5 gives the mechanism: "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things" (Ench. 5). For harsh appearances the chapter prescribes an examination: test them "whether it concerns the things which are in our own control, or those which are not; and, if it concerns anything not in our control, be prepared to say that it is nothing to you". The compendium's practical reading: sort each concern into the two lists before spending effort on it. (Ench. 1; Disc. I.1, I.22.)

**Plain words** Disturbance, on this account, is what misclassification feels like from inside. When you treat a verdict, an outcome, or a body as yours to command, you carry a weight that does not answer to you, and the carrying shows up as lament and fault-finding. The exercise is to notice which of the two lists a concern actually belongs to before spending effort on it. Nothing in the exercise requires pretending the second list doesn't matter; it only requires not commanding it.

*For example:* Before an exam, the questions are not in your control; your preparation and how you respond to a bad start are. The exercise sorts the worry into those two piles before the day arrives.
