> **Principle 44** — Treat the WEIRD critique as a standing corrective

The critique comes from a 2010 review by Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine and Ara Norenzayan. Behavioral scientists, they observed, "routinely publish broad claims about human psychology and behavior in the world's top journals based on samples drawn entirely from highly educated segments of Western societies" (Henrich et al. 2010, author manuscript, long abstract). Their comparison of experimental results across populations found standard subjects to be frequent outliers on measures from visual perception to fairness.

> The findings suggest that members of Western, educated, industrialized, rich, and democratic societies, including young children, are among the least representative populations one could find for generalizing about humans. (Henrich et al. 2010, [author manuscript](https://www2.psych.ubc.ca/~ara/Manuscripts/Weird_People_BBS_Henrichetal.pdf), long abstract)

The fairness experiments show the pattern. In the Dictator Game, one anonymous player divides a sum of real money and the other simply receives it, so the split measures fairness with no rejection to fear. In the authors' Phase II comparison across 15 societies, the U.S. adult sample made the highest mean offer, nearly double those of the Hadza of Tanzania and the Tsimane of Bolivia; the projects overall spanned 23 small-scale societies (§2.2). The remedy they draw is proportionate: generalizing from industrialized populations to humans broadly is, in their words, problematic "in the absence of supportive empirical evidence" (§2.6). The critique does not invalidate psychology; it makes cultural generality an empirical question.

**Plain words**
Suppose a study tests students at one university. Before using its result to advise someone of a different age or background, look for evidence involving people like them. The study shows something about its participants; extending the conclusion further needs more support. The corrective is that check, applied every time: in whom was this shown?

*For example:* In the Ultimatum Game, subjects from industrialized populations typically offer between 40% and 50% of the total and reject offers below about 30%. The same games run across small-scale societies produced widely varying offers, with industrialized samples at the extreme end (§2.2). A reader who asks "in whom was this shown?" applies the corrective; one who takes it as settled human nature generalizes past the data.
