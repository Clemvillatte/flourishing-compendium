Epictetus sets out the curriculum in Discourses III.2:

> "There are three things in which a man ought to exercise himself who would be wise and good. The first concerns the desires and the aversions, that a man may not fail to get what he desires, and that he may not fall into that which he does not desire. The second concerns the movements (toward) and the movements from an object, and generally in doing what a man ought to do, that he may act according to order, to reason, and not carelessly. The third thing concerns freedom from deception and rashness in judgement, and generally it concerns the assents."
> (Disc. III.2, [Long's translation, Internet Classics Archive](https://classics.mit.edu/Epictetus/discourses.3.three.html))

He adds a priority: "Of these topics the chief and the most urgent is that which relates to the affects; for an affect is produced in no other way than by a failing to obtain that which a man desires or a falling into that which a man would wish to avoid" (Disc. III.2). The compendium's practical reading: when a reaction is running, sort which of the three is in play before acting on it. (Disc. III.2; Ench. 1-3.)

**Plain words** Each of the three names a different question. What do I want here, and is it mine to get? What is the right thing to do? What am I telling myself happened? A strong reaction can involve any of the three, and the repair depends on which one is doing the work. The exercise is to sort which question is live before acting on it.

*For example:* Feeling jealous? That is desire. Unsure what to do about a late colleague? Action. Convinced they did it out of spite? Judgement.
