Biographical account · Andrew Wiles (b. 1953), mathematician, from his own account in the 1997 documentary The Proof.

Seven years of secret work on Fermat's Last Theorem, and he describes them in the currency of creation, not reward: "The first seven years I'd worked on this problem, I loved every minute of it... it was a kind of private and very personal battle I was engaged in" ([NOVA, The Proof](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/transcripts/2414proof.html/)). He announced the proof in Cambridge in 1993 — "a wonderful feeling after seven years... I'd finally done it. Only later did it come out that there was a problem at the end" ([NOVA](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/transcripts/2414proof.html/)). The referee's innocent-seeming question became "a fundamental error" ([NOVA](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/transcripts/2414proof.html/)), and the year that followed was spent trying to mend what would not mend.

Then the morning of September 19, 1994. He was not hoping; he was doing the opposite: "convincing myself that it didn't work, just seeing exactly what the problem was, when suddenly, totally unexpectedly, I had this incredible revelation" ([NOVA](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/transcripts/2414proof.html/)). What had been blocking the failed method was exactly what his abandoned approach of three years earlier needed: "So, out of the ashes seemed to rise the true answer to the problem" ([NOVA](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/transcripts/2414proof.html/)).

The joy is the thing to notice, and its timing. "It was the most important moment of my working life. It was so indescribably beautiful; it was so simple and so elegant, and I just stared in disbelief for twenty minutes. Then, during the day, I walked around the department. I'd keep coming back to my desk and looking to see if it was still there. It was still there" ([NOVA](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/transcripts/2414proof.html/)). That is Bergson's joy, not pleasure: nothing had been published, no one knew, the world still believed the proof broken. He went down and told his wife, "I've fixed my proof. I've got it," and "it was so unexpected, I think she thought I was talking about a children's toy or something and said, 'Got what?'" ([NOVA](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/transcripts/2414proof.html/)). The joy was the felt knowledge that something new existed — a proof, invisible to everyone else — and it needed no witness.

Not carried: Bergson's biological contrast case (pleasure as function performed); the mother-and-child example on the principle page; the story carries the artist's side of Bergson's examples, the realized thought.

**Principles:**
- [[P191 — Joy is the felt signature of creation; distinct from pleasure]]: Wiles's September 19 joy arrived with the created proof itself — indescribably beautiful, checked again and again because he could not believe it — while no recognition existed yet, which is the principle's test of joy over pleasure.
