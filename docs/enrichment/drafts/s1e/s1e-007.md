Biographical account · Eugen Herrigel (1884-1955), German philosopher, from his memoir of six years learning kyudo, Japanese archery, from the master Awa Kenzo.

A professor of philosophy arrives at the archery hall with a will, and the will is the problem. He cannot draw the bow "in the manner prescribed," and the master's diagnosis is not about the arms: "'You cannot do it,' explained the Master, 'because you do not breathe right'" ([Herrigel, 1953](https://users.wfu.edu/moran/PDFs/eugen-herrigel-zen-in-the-art-of-archery.pdf)). The cure is a gathering before it is a shooting: "'Concentrate entirely on your breathing, as if you had nothing else to do!'" ([Herrigel, 1953](https://users.wfu.edu/moran/PDFs/eugen-herrigel-zen-in-the-art-of-archery.pdf)). The instruction has the shape of the training intention in the Anapanasati sequence: the mind collected on one thing, before anything is done with it.

The master's account of the breath is the principle's own psychology. "The breathing in, the Master once said, binds and combines; by holding your breath you make everything go right; and the breathing out loosens and completes by overcoming all limitations" ([Herrigel, 1953](https://users.wfu.edu/moran/PDFs/eugen-herrigel-zen-in-the-art-of-archery.pdf)). And the drift-return loop is described exactly: "The more one concentrates on breathing, the more the external stimuli fade into the background... one soon feels oneself shut in by impermeable layers of silence. One only knows and feels that one breathes" ([Herrigel, 1953](https://users.wfu.edu/moran/PDFs/eugen-herrigel-zen-in-the-art-of-archery.pdf)).

The consequence is the collected mind, and it arrives as a report, not an achievement. "I learned to lose myself so effortlessly in the breathing that I sometimes had the feeling that I myself was not breathing but − strange as this may sound − was being breathed" ([Herrigel, 1953](https://users.wfu.edu/moran/PDFs/eugen-herrigel-zen-in-the-art-of-archery.pdf)). On the day the release finally came, the master broke off the lesson and bowed: "You remained this time absolutely self-oblivious and without purpose in the highest tension, so that the shot fell from you like a ripe fruit" ([Herrigel, 1953](https://users.wfu.edu/moran/PDFs/eugen-herrigel-zen-in-the-art-of-archery.pdf)). That is B095's arc: the gathering trained for its own sake, until the task falls out of it.

Not carried: the book frames kyudo as Zen training; Yamada Shoji's study argues that Awa Kenzo never practiced Zen and that the famous "It shoots" may have grown from a mistranslation of "that's it" ([Yamada, 2001](https://archive.org/stream/YamadaShojiTheMythOfZenInTheArtOfArchery/Yamada-Shoji-The-Myth-of-Zen-in-the-Art-of-Archery_djvu.txt)). The breath passages this story uses are Herrigel's plain report of instruction; the Zen frame is the author's, and the story carries it as such.

**Principles:**
- [[B095 — Collect the mind (samadhi as gathering)]]: the master treated the scattered professor's breath as the collection point — bind on the in-breath, loosen on the out, the stimuli fading as attention holds — until the mind was gathered and the shot fell like a ripe fruit.
