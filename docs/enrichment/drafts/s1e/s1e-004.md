Biographical account · Marva Collins (1936-2015), founder of Westside Preparatory School in Chicago, told through a 1982 Reason interview, a 2007 NPR interview, and the National Endowment for the Humanities' biographical note.

After fourteen years teaching in Chicago's public school system, Collins used her $5,000 pension to open Westside Preparatory School in her inner-city home in 1975, starting with four children ([NEH](https://www.neh.gov/about/awards/national-humanities-medals/marva-collins); [AAM-US](https://www.aam-us.org/2015/10/29/marva-collins-education-futurist/)). Her student body consisted of children labeled problem or learning-disabled ([NEH](https://www.neh.gov/about/awards/national-humanities-medals/marva-collins)) — the children the sorting had already decided about. In the public schools she had ignored the curriculum and been "constantly being called into the office because I did not follow the guidelines" ([Reason, 1982](https://reason.com/1982/01/01/interview-with-marva-collins/)).

What she built at Westside took the teacher out of the center of the room. She gave the children the choice and the authority: "We tell every student in our school it is their right to be average, to be a failure, and that is one of their choices. But here is another way, if you choose to partake of it" ([NPR, 2007](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/14178874)). Her stance toward their steps was availability, not control: "I can't test the ground for you and tell you that's a safe step there" ([Reason, 1982](https://reason.com/1982/01/01/interview-with-marva-collins/)). What she supplied was belief: "All they want, all they've ever asked, is for someone to believe in them. Then they try much harder" ([Reason, 1982](https://reason.com/1982/01/01/interview-with-marva-collins/)). And the day's direction came from the students: "Every student in our school must begin the day by telling us what they will do that's going to be phenomenal that day" ([NPR, 2007](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/14178874)).

The consequence is the DDJ 17 outcome. There were no substitute teachers in her school: "When teachers were absent, students simply taught themselves and developed early skills in teambuilding and peer-to-peer learning" ([AAM-US](https://www.aam-us.org/2015/10/29/marva-collins-education-futurist/)). Her students argued in Shakespeare: "Speak the speech trippingly on the tongue" ([Reason, 1982](https://reason.com/1982/01/01/interview-with-marva-collins/)), and by the end of the first year, "every child had surpassed his or her expectations" ([NEH](https://www.neh.gov/about/awards/national-humanities-medals/marva-collins)). After the 1979 60 Minutes visit, Morley Safer called the results "astounding" ([AAM-US](https://www.aam-us.org/2015/10/29/marva-collins-education-futurist/)). The work succeeded, and the ones doing it were the children, the classroom version of "we are as we are, of ourselves."

Not carried: DDJ 66's river-and-sea mechanics of humility; Collins was a vivid, high-energy presence, so the story carries the behind-the-leader effect without the invisible-leader image of DDJ 17's first rank.

**Principles:**
- [[D115 — Lead from behind]]: Collins led by handing the school's agency to the children — the choice, the ground-testing, the day's phenomenal thing — so that the school continued without her in the room, which is the chapter's test of successful undertakings.
