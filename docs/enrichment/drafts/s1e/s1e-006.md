Biographical account · Ramana Maharshi (born Venkataraman, 1879-1950), from the standard account of his 1896 awakening and his own instructions in Who am I? (Nan Yar?).

In mid-1896, a sixteen-year-old schoolboy was sitting alone on the first floor of his uncle's house in Madurai, in his usual health, when "a sudden and unmistakable fear of death took hold — he felt he was going to die" ([Arunachala Ashrama, Life](https://archive.arunachala.org/ramana/life/)). What he did next made the fear into a search. He lay down, "stretching his limbs out and holding them stiff as though rigor mortis had set in," held his breath, and kept his lips tightly closed so that no sound could escape, so that "to all outward appearance his body resembled a corpse" ([Life](https://archive.arunachala.org/ramana/life/)). Then the inquiry: "'Now, death has come. What does it mean? What is it that is dying? This body dies.'... 'Well, this body is now dead. It will be carried to the burning ground and there burnt and reduced to ashes. But with the death of this body am I dead? Is the body I? This body is silent and inert. But I feel the full force of my personality and even the voice of the "I" within me, apart from it'" ([Life](https://archive.arunachala.org/ramana/life/)).

The search is the principle's own: look for the one behind the thoughts, and no examiner turns up. What he found instead of a thinker was the bare "I" sense, "very real, the only real thing," and the fear of death "vanished once and for all." From then on, the account says, "'I' continued like the fundamental sruti note that underlies and blends with all the other notes" ([Life](https://archive.arunachala.org/ramana/life/)): thoughts still came and went, but no one stood behind them.

His later teaching made the search a method for every thought, not only for death. "When other thoughts arise, one should not pursue them, but should inquire: 'To whom do they arise?'... The answer that would emerge would be 'To me'. Thereupon if one inquires 'Who am I?', the mind will go back to its source; and the thought that arose will become quiescent" ([Who am I?, Q11](https://www.gururamana.org/Teachings/who_am_I.pdf)). And the question itself is the same kind of thing as the thoughts it destroys: "like the stick used for stirring the burning pyre, it will itself in the end get destroyed" ([Who am I?, Q10](https://www.gururamana.org/Teachings/who_am_I.pdf)). That is B075's plain words as a practice: keep looking, and notice that the looking itself is another thought.

Not carried: the Milindapanha's designation logic and the aggregates enumeration on the principle page; and Ramana's conclusion (a positive Self, "the Spirit transcending the body") is Vedanta's answer, not the Buddhist page's negative one. The story carries the search, which is what the principle asks you to run.

**Principles:**
- [[B075 — The thinker is another thought]]: the death-drama at sixteen ran the elimination search on his own person and found no examiner behind the thoughts, only the "I" sense continuing like a sruti note under them; the teaching turned it into a method — each thought traced to "to me," and the "me" traced back to its source.
