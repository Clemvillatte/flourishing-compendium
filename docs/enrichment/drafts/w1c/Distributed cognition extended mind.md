Distributed cognition and the extended mind are two related research programmes that deny the skull as the boundary of thinking. Clark and Chalmers's 1998 paper calls its position "an active externalism, based on the active role of the environment in driving cognitive processes": "the human organism is linked with an external entity in a two-way interaction, creating a coupled system that can be seen as a cognitive system in its own right". Its parity principle: "If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is... part of the cognitive process" ([Clark and Chalmers, 1998](https://consc.net/papers/extended.html)).

### Otto's notebook

The paper's famous case: Inga remembers that the Museum of Modern Art is on 53rd Street; Otto, who has Alzheimer's disease, looks the address up in his notebook. "The information in the notebook functions just like the information constituting an ordinary non-occurrent belief; it just happens that this information lies beyond the skin." What earns notebook entries the status of belief is "a high degree of trust, reliance, and accessibility": the notebook is a constant in Otto's life, directly available, and automatically endorsed.

### Cognition spread across a crew

Hutchins's distributed cognition makes the same move at group scale, in studies of ship navigation. As the Stanford Encyclopedia summarizes it: "The cognitive load required to navigate a large Navy vessel exceeds the capacity of any single individual, but can be distributed across a number of specialists, each with his or her own task". The general point: "The cognitive 'load' that a task requires can be reduced when the agent embeds herself within an appropriately designed physical or social environment."

### The objection

The thesis has critics. Adams and Aizawa argue that notebook entries and written numerals carry only derived content, unlike the "intrinsic, non-derived content" of thought, and that a science of brain-plus-tools "would have to cover a genuine motley". Rupert asks why implements should count as constituents of a cognitive system rather than as tools: "An axe does not become part of a person when she uses it to chop down a tree". The encyclopedia notes the constitution thesis is not committed to the absurd versions.

### In the compendium

P144, "Let cognition extend into the environment," and CO006, "Recognise that thinking is socially distributed — you think with others," carry the two halves of the thesis, and SO034, "Treat knowledge as social," is its epistemic version. ST132, the Blind Man's Cane story, illustrates the perceptual version: the cane as part of the seeing system. D178, "Use tools without belonging to them," is the productive Daoist counterweight. The extended-mind thesis says the cane is part of you; D178 says it is not you; the disagreement locates the self's boundary, and that tension is a comparison, not a documented debate.
