The Sabbath is the weekly day of rest instituted in Genesis: "On the seventh day God finished the work that had been undertaken, ceasing on the seventh day from doing any of the work. And God blessed the seventh day and declared it holy" ([Genesis 2:2-3, Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Genesis.2.2?lang=bi)). The concept's modern philosopher is Abraham Joshua Heschel, whose The Sabbath (1951) reads the day as Judaism's core teaching about time.

### A palace in time

Heschel's famous formulation: "The seventh day is like a palace in time with a kingdom for all. It is not a date but an atmosphere." The day reverses the week's orientation: "Six days a week we wrestle with the world, wringing profit from the earth; on the Sabbath we especially care for the seed of eternity planted in the soul... Six days a week we seek to dominate the world, on the seventh day we try to dominate the self" ([excerpts](https://www.thecjm.org/learn_resources/348)).

The stakes are diagnostic, not decorative: "Life goes wrong when the control of space, the acquisition of things of space, becomes our sole concern", because "there is a realm of time where the goal is not to have but to be, not to own but to give, not to control but to share, not to subdue but to be in accord". Judaism, Heschel writes, "is a religion of time aiming at the sanctification of time".

The day also has emotional rules: "The Sabbath is no time for personal anxiety or care, for any activity that might dampen the spirit of joy... It is a day for praise, not a day for petitions. Fasting, mourning, demonstrations of grief are forbidden." Rest as a covenant, not a luxury.

### In the compendium

The compendium's principles read the Sabbath as the root of activity, not its reward. D039, "Make rest the root of activity," is the Daoist version of the same architecture; D113, "Rest deeply," and D154, "Treat sleep as sacred," carry its depth; AT046's digital sabbath and AT037's attention recovery are the modern applications. The rhythm the Genesis text blesses is the compendium's rhythm: work, cease, sanctify.

Heschel's palace also reads as attention self-defense: in a world engineered to never let you stop, a pre-technological institution that mandates stopping is the counter-practice the attention economy has yet to defeat. That reading is interpretation, not Heschel's words.
