## Page body (for content/concepts/Brahman.md)

In Śaṅkara's Advaita Vedānta, Brahman is the nondual ground of all things, "one without a second," held to be identical with the innermost self, [[Atman]] ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/); [Menon, IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/advaita-vedanta/)). The world of plurality is its appearance through [[Maya (illusion)|māyā]]; removal of ignorance ([[Avidya]]) reveals that nothing is other than Brahman ([Menon, IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/advaita-vedanta/)).

### What the texts say

Śaṅkara's Advaita reads the Upaniṣads as revealing Brahman, the fundamental principle of nonduality and the reality of all things ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)). The anchors Dalal cites are the Chāndogya proposition that existence is "one only, without a second" (6.2.1), the identity "you are that" (6.8.7), and the Bṛhadāraṇyaka's "I am Brahman" (1.4.10) ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)). Brahman is described as self-established, irreducible, immutable, free of space, time and causation, pure formless existence ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)). Consciousness is its intrinsic nature: self-illuminating, requiring no second awareness in order to be known ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)).

### Appearance and its levels

The experienced world has no existence apart from Brahman: Brahman appears as the manifold through its creative power māyā, without intrinsic change, a superimposition rather than a transformation ([Menon, IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/advaita-vedanta/)). The universe is not nonexistent like a hare's horn; it is an objective but less-than-real appearance, transient and dependent ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)). Menon distinguishes absolute, worldly and illusory existence, the latter two functions of māyā ([Menon, IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/advaita-vedanta/)). Dalal's account of Śaṅkara centres on conventional and ultimate reality, and separately notes disputes over attributing developed later theories of māyā to Śaṅkara ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)).

### Liberation as recognition

Mokṣa is the direct recognition that one's self is nondual Brahman; the SEP article characterizes it as self-knowledge, not a newly produced attainment ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)). Understanding the identity statements generates the knowledge "I am Brahman," which removes root ignorance and negates superimposition ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)). A liberated person may continue to experience the world while embodied, living liberation (jīvanmukti) ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)). For Śaṅkara, action can prepare the mind for knowledge, but knowledge alone is held to liberate ([Menon, IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/advaita-vedanta/)).
