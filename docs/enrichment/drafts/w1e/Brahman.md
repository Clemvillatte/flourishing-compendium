## Page body (for content/concepts/Brahman.md)

Brahman is the nondual ground of all things — "one without a second, infinite existence" — which Advaita Vedānta holds to be identical with the innermost self, [[Atman]] ([Śaṅkara, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)). The world of plurality is its appearance through [[Maya (illusion)|māyā]]; removal of ignorance ([[Avidya]]) reveals that nothing is other than Brahman ([IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/advaita-vedanta/)). Cross-tradition: resonates with the Daoist [[Dao]] as the unnameable source, Spinoza's [[Deus sive natura]], and the Stoic [[The logos common to all|logos]].

### What the texts say

Śaṅkara's Advaita reads the Upaniṣads as revealing Brahman, the fundamental principle of nonduality and the reality of all things ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)). The anchors are the Chāndogya proposition that existence is "one only, without a second" (6.2.1), the identity "you are that" (6.8.7), and the Bṛhadāraṇyaka's "I am Brahman" (1.4.10) ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)). Brahman is described as self-established, irreducible, immutable, free of space, time and causation, pure formless existence ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)). Consciousness is its intrinsic nature: self-illuminating, requiring no second awareness in order to be known ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)).

### Appearance and its levels

The experienced world has no existence apart from Brahman: Brahman appears as the manifold through its creative power māyā, without intrinsic change, a superimposition rather than a transformation ([Menon, IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/advaita-vedanta/)). The universe is not nonexistent like a hare's horn; it is an objective but less-than-real appearance, transient and dependent ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)). The IEP gives three planes of existence: absolute, worldly, and illusory appearances like mirages, the latter two functions of māyā ([Menon, IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/advaita-vedanta/)). The SEP article presents two orders, conventional and ultimate, and notes that the fuller later-Advaita accounts of māyā are contested for Śaṅkara himself ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)).

### Liberation as recognition

Mokṣa is the direct recognition that one's self is nondual Brahman; the SEP article characterizes it as self-knowledge, not a newly produced attainment ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)). Understanding the identity statements generates the knowledge "I am Brahman," which removes root ignorance and negates superimposition ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)). A liberated person may continue to experience the world while embodied, living liberation (jīvanmukti) ([Dalal, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/spr2026/entries/shankara/)). The IEP account adds that knowledge of Brahman removes errors and brings liberation from transmigration; action purifies the mind but is not itself the route ([Menon, IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/advaita-vedanta/)).

### In the compendium

Y092 "Read the great statements as prompts, not doctrines" treats the mahavakyas, including "that thou art" and "I am the absolute," as meditation prompts rather than debate propositions. Y093 "Know your field and its knower" carries the Gita's version of the witness: the body-and-mind is the field, the awareness of it the field-knower, the same in kind across fields (BG 13.1-3).
