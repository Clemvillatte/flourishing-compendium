## Page body (for content/concepts/Capacity to be alone (Winnicott).md)

The capacity to be alone is D. W. Winnicott's name for a developmental achievement, one he calls "one of the most important signs of maturity in emotional development" ([Winnicott 1958](https://web.english.upenn.edu/~cavitch/pdf-library/Winnicott_Capacity_To_Be_Alone.pdf)). The concept belongs to object relations theory, the school in which internalised early relationships become templates through which later relationships are lived. Its core claim is a paradox: "the basis of the capacity to be alone is a paradox; it is the experience of being alone while someone else is present" ([Winnicott 1958](https://web.english.upenn.edu/~cavitch/pdf-library/Winnicott_Capacity_To_Be_Alone.pdf)).

### The paradox

The basic experience is "being alone, as an infant and small child, in the presence of mother" ([Winnicott 1958](https://web.english.upenn.edu/~cavitch/pdf-library/Winnicott_Capacity_To_Be_Alone.pdf)). Winnicott names the relationship at work ego-relatedness: "the relationship between two people, one of whom at any rate is alone; perhaps both are alone, yet the presence of each is important to the other" ([Winnicott 1958](https://web.english.upenn.edu/~cavitch/pdf-library/Winnicott_Capacity_To_Be_Alone.pdf)). He adds that id-relationships strengthen the ego when they occur in a framework of ego-relatedness ([Winnicott 1958](https://web.english.upenn.edu/~cavitch/pdf-library/Winnicott_Capacity_To_Be_Alone.pdf)). In time "the individual introjects the ego-supportive mother and in this way becomes able to be alone without frequent reference to the mother or mother symbol" ([Winnicott 1958](https://web.english.upenn.edu/~cavitch/pdf-library/Winnicott_Capacity_To_Be_Alone.pdf)).

### What the capacity rests on

The capacity "depends on the existence of a good object in the psychic reality of the individual," an internal object well enough established that the person can "rest contented even in the absence of external objects and stimuli" ([Winnicott 1958](https://web.english.upenn.edu/~cavitch/pdf-library/Winnicott_Capacity_To_Be_Alone.pdf)). "I am alone" is a development from "I am," dependent on the infant's awareness of the continued existence of a reliable mother ([Winnicott 1958](https://web.english.upenn.edu/~cavitch/pdf-library/Winnicott_Capacity_To_Be_Alone.pdf)). The payoff is discovery: "It is only when alone (that is to say, in the presence of someone) that the infant can discover his own personal life" ([Winnicott 1958](https://web.english.upenn.edu/~cavitch/pdf-library/Winnicott_Capacity_To_Be_Alone.pdf)).

### In the compendium

SE058 "Recognise self and solitude: the self needs alone time" treats solitude as a necessary condition for self-reflection and self-renewal, not isolation. B173 "Be alone well" gives the practice: solitude without collapse or distraction, citing MN 122 and Dhammapada 61-62, a Buddhist cousin of the same achievement. P134 "Grow in solitude and community together" schedules both phases: the self is social but needs the pause of solitude to consolidate, citing Dewey and James.
