## Page body (for content/concepts/Boulesis (rational desire).md)

Boulesis (βούλησις) is Aristotle's term for wish: the species of desire that aims at what is seen as good, as opposed to appetite (epithumia), which aims at pleasure, and spirit (thumos), the retaliatory desire ([Lockwood, review of Pearson](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2013/2013.09.24)). De Anima II.3 gives the division: "appetite is the genus of which desire, passion, and wish are the species," in Smith's translation, where "appetite" renders orexis, the generic desiring faculty, and "desire" renders epithumia ([De Anima II.3](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Aristotle/De-anima/de-anima2.htm)).

### Wish and choice

Nicomachean Ethics III.2 separates wish from choice (prohairesis): choice cannot relate to impossibles, while there may be a wish even for impossibles such as immortality, and wish may relate to things outside one's own power, such as an athlete's winning ([NE III, Ross trans.](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.3.iii.html)). Wish relates to the end and choice to the means: we wish to be healthy, but we choose the acts that will make us healthy ([NE III](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.3.iii.html)). Choice is "deliberate desire of things in our own power" ([NE III](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.3.iii.html)).

### For the good or the apparent good

Nicomachean Ethics III.4 asks whether wish is for the good or the apparent good, and resolves: "absolutely and in truth the good is the object of wish, but for each person the apparent good" ([NE III](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.3.iii.html)). In truth, what is wished for is wished for by the good man, who "judges each class of things rightly," being "as it were the norm and measure of them" ([NE III](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.3.iii.html)). The Ross translation adds: "In most things the error seems to be due to pleasure; for it appears a good when it is not" ([NE III](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/nicomachaen.3.iii.html)). De Anima III.10 says the object that originates movement "may be either the real or the apparent good" ([De Anima III](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Aristotle/De-anima/de-anima3.htm)).

### How rational is it

De Anima III.9 mentions that some would put wish in the calculative part and desire and passion in the irrational, while Aristotle argues against breaking up the desiring faculty into separate parts of the soul ([De Anima III](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Aristotle/De-anima/de-anima3.htm)). Book III.10 then says that movement according to calculation also involves wish, while appetite can produce movement contrary to calculation, since wish is a form of desire ([De Anima III](https://psychclassics.yorku.ca/Aristotle/De-anima/de-anima3.htm)). The Lockwood review identifies a difficulty with treating wish as invariably deliberative: it includes hopes and idle wishes that appear to lack a deliberative basis ([Lockwood, review of Pearson](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2013/2013.09.24)).

### In the compendium

A007 "Let reason (logos) guide desire (orexis)" carries the pairing: desire trained to follow reason, not extinguished, citing NE I.13, III.12 and De Anima III.7-10. MO001 "Understand that motivation is a system, not a feeling" is the compendium's psychological cousin: motivation as a system of needs, values, goals and habits rather than a single feeling.
