## Page body (for content/concepts/Buddhist emptiness of inherent nature.md)

Buddhist emptiness of inherent nature (śūnyatā) is the Madhyamaka claim that no phenomenon possesses svabhāva, a nature it has independently ([Westerhoff, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)). It has been rendered as "inherent existence," "intrinsic nature," "substance" and "essence" ([Westerhoff, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)). Nāgārjuna's summary verse reads: "Whatever is dependently co-arisen / That is explained to be emptiness. / That, being a dependent designation, / Is itself the middle way" (MMK 24.18, Garfield trans.) ([Garfield](https://kokyohenkel.weebly.com/uploads/1/2/7/4/127410773/nagarjuna_-_mulamadhyamakakarika_ch_24__garfield_.pdf)).

### What svabhāva means

Westerhoff distinguishes an ontological dimension, a way objects exist, from a cognitive dimension, a way human beings conceptualise them ([Westerhoff, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)). His analysis distinguishes essence, substance and absolute reality within the ontological dimension; as essence, the svabhāva of fire would be to be hot and of water to be wet ([Westerhoff, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)). Westerhoff notes that when Nāgārjuna argues things are empty of svabhāva, it is not this notion of essence he is concerned with, but svabhāva as substance: independent existence, being part of the basic furniture of the world ([Westerhoff, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)). The rejected svabhāva is a superimposition mistakenly projected onto objects, a cognitive default rather than a theoretical posit ([Westerhoff, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)).

### Emptiness and the path

The opponent's worry is nihilism: "If all of this is empty, / Neither arising, nor ceasing, / Then for you, it follows that / The Four Noble Truths do not exist" (MMK 24.1) ([Garfield](https://kokyohenkel.weebly.com/uploads/1/2/7/4/127410773/nagarjuna_-_mulamadhyamakakarika_ch_24__garfield_.pdf)). The rejoinder reverses it: "If all this were nonempty, as in your view, / There would be no arising and ceasing. / Then the Four Noble Truths / Would become nonexistent" (MMK 24.20) ([Garfield](https://kokyohenkel.weebly.com/uploads/1/2/7/4/127410773/nagarjuna_-_mulamadhyamakakarika_ch_24__garfield_.pdf)). Because existents arise in mutual dependence, suffering can arise and be caused to cease; if suffering were the essential state of affairs, it could not be interrupted and the Buddhist path would be impossible ([Arnold, IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/madhyamaka-buddhist-philosophy/)).

### Emptiness itself empty

On Westerhoff’s reading, emptiness is not a theory describing reality independently of human interests and conceptual resources; it corrects the mistaken attribution of svabhāva ([Westerhoff, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)). The chapter gives the doctrine a two-truths frame: "The Buddha’s teaching of the Dharma / Is based on two truths: / A truth of worldly convention / And an ultimate truth," and that without conventional truth the ultimate's significance cannot be taught (MMK 24.10) ([Garfield](https://kokyohenkel.weebly.com/uploads/1/2/7/4/127410773/nagarjuna_-_mulamadhyamakakarika_ch_24__garfield_.pdf)). Arnold connects this with verse 18’s dependent designation: emptiness is not another independently existing foundation ([Arnold, IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/madhyamaka-buddhist-philosophy/)). Westerhoff notes that intellectual conviction does not make phenomena stop appearing to have svabhāva; that change is attributed to realisation ([Westerhoff, SEP](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/nagarjuna/)).
