## EXEMPLAR: Dao
---

Dao (道) is the term Laozi uses for the way the world works — the source and pattern of all things, which can be pointed at but not named ("a dao that can be spoken is not the constant Dao", Daodejing 1). It is not a god or a creator: the Dao does not command, it nourishes without possessing (Daodejing 51). The image family in the Daodejing is of water (yielding yet carving canyons), of the valley (empty yet generative), of the mother (source without interference).

The practical reading carried by the compendium's principle D001: the Dao is what the world's own patterns are — and the practice is to align your effort with those patterns rather than against them. This makes Dao the root of wu wei (act without forcing) and ziran (be what you are of itself).

The Dao is also the Chinese member of a family of concepts that name the self-organising pattern of the world: Heraclitus' Logos and Panta rhei (everything flows), the stream of consciousness in James' psychology, and flow as Csikszentmihalyi describes it in experience — the felt alignment of a person with what is happening.

## EXEMPLAR: De
---

De (德) is the Daoist term for the power or virtue a thing has when it is aligned with the Dao — not moral goodness in the rule-following sense, but the characteristic vitality and effectiveness of a thing living out its own nature. Chapter 51 of the Daodejing gives the canonical chain: "The Dao gives birth to them, de nurtures them" (道生之，德畜之) — where the Dao is the way the world works, de is what that way looks like instantiated in a particular being: the way water's de is flowing and yielding, or a tree's de is growing. The same chapter ends with the formula of "profound virtue" (玄德): giving life without possessing, acting without presuming, leading without dominating.

In the Zhuangzi, de becomes more psychological. Inner Chapter 5, "The Seal of Virtue Complete" (德充符), is the sustained meditation on it: a gallery of the mutilated, the deformed and the ugly — men who have lost their feet, men whose appearance frightens the world — in every case the wholeness of their inner de draws people to them more powerfully than beauty, rank or eloquence. The chapter's title announces the paradox: the completeness of the spirit may be most perfectly sealed in the body that is broken. De, in other words, is not dependent on conventional standing, appearance, or physical completeness.

The compendium's principle D004 ("Cultivate de: your embodied character-power") carries the practical reading: de is what your character looks like when it stops being a performance and becomes an embodied capacity — what you do well and naturally, without the interference of self-conscious effort.
